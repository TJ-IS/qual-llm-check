# A domain selection and evaluation framework for introducing knowledge-based systems in smaller businesses

Maris G. Martinsons

Faculty of Business, City Polytechnic University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

Abstract. As the use of knowledge-based systems becomes increasingly viable, small business managers face decisions related to the introduction and integration of this information technology into their organizations. This article provides a framework to evaluate domains for knowledge-based applications development.

Keywords: domain evaluation, knowledge-based systems, small business.

## INTRODUCTION

Over the last decade, knowledge-based technology has been successfully commercialized, with thousands of applications now being used in the business world. The vast majority of users, however, have been larger companies (Harmon, 1989a). In the 1990s, the product of this technology — knowledge-based systems (KBS) — has the potential to offer great benefits across a broader organizational spectrum (Monger, 1988).

The typical smaller business can derive significant value from integrating KBS into its information technology arsenal (Hui & So, 1988; Osins, 1988; Harmon, 1989b). To date, however, the potential has seldom been realized. Many small business managers perceive KBS to be either an over-exalted, over-promised answer to all computer needs or a suspicious, unproven technology without value (Helton, 1990). They are reluctant to consider its application to their operations.

Southeast Asia, with its scarcities or professional and managerial staff, provided a near-ideal environment for expanding use of this technology in the 1980s. Even there, however, KBS growth, after considerable enthusiasm in the middle of the decade, has been markedly slow among the smaller business sector (Chow et al., 1989).

Among those who have attempted to introduce KBS into their small business, disappointing results (and hence a hesitation to try again) are common. They can often be attributed to inadequate planning or improper implementation of the initial knowledge-based systems.

A common cause of failure has been the imprudent selection and evaluation of initial application areas (Waterman, 1986). The presence of a simple yet formalized framework would enable managers to make an informed and appropriate selection of the introductory domain for KBS development.

The first application of knowledge-based systems in a given organization is generally one of two types. Some business managers choose to begin with projects which lead to the development of stand-alone KBS applications, probably using a personal-computer delivery platform. For others, introduction involves work which attempts to improve the effectiveness of the conventional medium for large applications by building intelligent 'front ends'.

In either case, the pace of future KBS development and integration is greatly determined by the success of these pioneering projects. Top managers are unlikely to allocate additional resources to a technology where an initial investment has produced clearly unsatisfactory results.

Using KBS technology in a judicious manner (Beerel, 1987) offers competitive advantages for businesses in many industries, especially in economies where high-skilled labour is scarce. Several factors, however, are critical to achieving success with knowledge-based systems. During introduction, these critical success factors (CSFs) include appropriate choices of where and how to apply this increasingly population technology.

A framework for answering the question of 'where' to begin knowledge-based system development is required as managers ponder the introduction of KBS technology to their organizations. This must be consistent with established systems planning methods. A business opportunities ('market pull') approach, rather than one based on technical interest ('technology push') should be employed. In this way, an organization proceeds with development only if suitable domains, clearly deriving benefits from KBS, are identified.

The decision to introduce KBS technology is significantly different from deciding to purchase a new machine or to recruit a new member of staff. It is the beginning of a long process which requires a substantial investment. Unfortunately, the consequences for an organization are often difficult to assess in advance. Launching an organization into the world of KBS is a decision which can be only partly based on traditional cost-benefit analyses. It also reflects the ambitions of the organization, and to some extent, represents an act of faith by management.

The proposed transformation of existing business organizations, through progressive introduction and integration of KBS, involves careful feasibility explorations. Many businesses are over-eager to seize on this new technology and consequently choose ill-suited applications. It is necessary to identify and evaluate specific business-domains which would derive benefit from the technology and to plan appropriate developments.

A sophisticated methodology, such as that associated with IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP), specifically the business process definition stage (Lu & Guinaraes, 1989), is appropriate for larger-sized organizations. Recognizing, however, that managers in smaller businesses require something that is both simple and meaningful when considering their first KBS domains, this paper describes such a framework and considers some of the issues related to the initial KBS planning in these organizations.

## DOMAIN CANDIDATE SELECTION

The integration of personal computer-based KBS (PC-KBS) into existing operations offers many potential advantages for smaller businesses, including the following (Martinsons & Ramsey, 1988): reduction in the scarcity of human expertise, increased decision-making consistency, increased cost-effectiveness of staff training and human-resources development, and the capability of retaining in-house expertise (even after human expertise leaves the organization).

The selection of the first applications for KBS are rarely obvious. The technology's broad applicability and potential to being significant benefits to various parts of the entire organization begs the question 'where to begin?'. This problem is often compounded by the fact that KBS is not readily embraced by traditional systems people; its constituents, e.g. decision support, interactivity and non-procedural programming, are inconsistent with the habits of conventional systems activities (Benchimol, 1988).

Individual managers through the organization are commonly required to take the initiative for KBS. After being briefed on the nature, potential benefits and limitations of KBS, their role is to create a wish-list of possible domains within their own activity areas. Once such a list has been formulated, the framework outlined in this article can be used to evaluate these knowledge-based system development candidates.

In general terms, a potential business-domain for knowledge-based systems technology must:

(a) be constrained in scope;

(b) promise substantial improvement over existing systems or operations; and

(c) provide a reasonable pay-off relative to the total resources invested (Martinsons, 1989).

A business domain with some or all of a high level of field-acquired (as opposed to book-acquired) knowledge, a significant learning curve and no available simulation environment is suitable for the application of KBS technology. It is also very important to have available during the development project, an on-going source of domain expertise, preferably a human expert who can articulate his or her methods of problem-solving or decision-making in the domain.

## DOMAIN CANDIDATE EVALUATION

Based on considerable personal experience in the development and implementation of knowledge-based systems as well as a review of successful knowledge-based systems, 13 separate criteria have been identified, to judge opportunities. As not all criteria are equally important, the following relative weighting scheme is offered to rank the criteria:

(a) 'A' means criterion is essential;

(b) 'B' means it is important but not essential; and

(c) 'C' means it is useful, but neither essential or important. The criteria can be grouped into three categories related to

(a) problem domain COMPLEXITY;

(b) possibility of IMPROVEMENT; and

(c) problem IMPORTANCE and SOLVABILITY.

## Complexity

There are limits to the complexity of problems which can be effectively handled by today's knowledge-based technology. Furthermore, small businesses are usually restricted, by economic or operational considerations, to personal computer (PC) hardware. This bounds the viable set of application domains. An opportunity may be considered to exist the following apply.

1 There is a clearly defined and well-understood problem. Managers must be able to describe the nature and fundamentals of their problems and identify their objectives in seeking to apply a KBS solution. A large domain should be divisible into smaller problems that may be addressed by KBS development projects in a modular fashion. To fully develop each of these modules, the project should require no more than one person-year of professional resource commitment (e.g. 8 months dedicated time by a developer and 8 months at half-time for the expert equals a one person-year). (Rank: A).

2 The scope of the problem area is bounded and sufficiently narrow. A manager should be able to clearly identify whether a particular issue is or is not within the problem area. This allows the number of issues and resulting combinations of solutions to be estimated. A total number of solutions, depending on their individual complexity, which significantly exceeds 100 tends to make the problem inappropriate for solution by PC-KBS technologies. (A).

3 Sufficient task-specific information is available on the problem so that an individual or a group could, through application of appropriate expertise and logical inferencing, solve the problem. In other words, the use of both specific knowledge and judgment is necessary and sufficient for a solution in the domain. Often, it is appropriate to have one or more typical problems solved by humans, in order to evaluate this criteria. (C).

4 Information associated with the domain is detailed and specific, enabling creation of both a knowledge base and a set of heuristic rules for using that base in solving problems within the specified domain. (C).

It is imperative to keep the scope of the problem sufficiently narrow. Experience has shown that the subject domains for developments in this field have a natural tendency to grow very quickly in both size and complexity, as projects proceed. A project that starts big will quickly get bogged down. Therefore, at the outset, a problem should be broken into sub-components that can be addressed independently within a reasonable timeframe.

## Improvements possible

Major improvements are possible if the conditions that follow apply.

5 Agreement (or, at a minimum, a consensus) among the experts can be reached on the knowledge domain and access options. (B).

6 Current procedures (or, at a minimum, general methodologies) exist to solve the problem. (B).

7 However, information bottlenecks exist or are foreseen because of a scarcity in the number of available experts. A degradation in the quality of solutions through the unavailability of expertise or the inability to disseminate appropriate information/knowledge in a timely manner are symptoms of such a bottleneck. (B).

Improvements are not possible if resolution of the problem cannot be facilitated through the use of known rules and data and the application of judgement by a domain expert.

## Problem importance and solvability

The final set of criteria are related to the expected return on the investment necessary to develop first the conceptual (or logical) model and then the actual software application. Critical success factors are listed below.

8 One or more experts available and willing to co-operate in supplying information for the knowledge base. Only in cases where there is significant existing documentation or defined knowledge sources will it be possible to develop a knowledge-based application without at least one human expert. (B).

In satisfying both conditions 7 and 8, it is recognized that the list of potential domains will be significantly shortened.

9 The end-product can achieve a performance which significantly benefits the organization (i.e. a performance level close to that of the existing expert, at a minimum, in a subset of the domain, if not the entire domain). (B).

10 The project is ‘manageable’ based on current or forecast organizational capabilities and it can be completed within a one-year time period. The use of existing internal human and system resources is preferred, although readily available contracted resources from the immediate environment have also proven to be quite suitable. (A).

11 Go/no-go decisions can be made at regular intervals during the course of the development. These intervals may be based on time-units or money spent. The presence of such decision points limits the risk borne by business and enables sound project management practices to be utilized. Top management's attitude toward risk and level of commitment to the project will determine the length of interval between go/no-go decisions. (C).

12 The solution must promise significant business-process improvement or enable the creation of an important backup capability (in the context of risk insurance or contingency planning). The cost/benefit ratio (or surrogate financial measure) must be acceptable to the organization (based on its economic feasibility evaluation methods).

Benefits may be both tangible and intangible. They may include improved product/service quality and delivery, decreased turnaround times, labour-cost savings, training benefits in both the applications and technology areas as well as increased client satisfaction. These benefits are most evident when expertise is needed simultaneously by a variety of people or in a variety of business locations, or when the human expert is bogged down by routine or semi-routine work at the expense of high-payoff specialized tasks. (A).

13 The project has a high degree of top-management support. This may be manifested through the initiation of the project by a top manager, the explicit approval of resources for this pioneering project by one or more top managers or the presence of top management on a project-steering group. Experience has shown that this support takes on various degrees of importance based on the organization's culture and the degree of centralization in its decision-making. (B).

## EVALUATION USING THE FRAMEWORK

A quantitative scheme should be used when evaluating a particular potential domain based on these 11 criteria. A simple scoring system, such as one based on a scale from 0 to 3 is appropriate in that it provides a systematic, if somewhat inexact, way of evaluating the relative merits of each candidate.

Each of the criteria which are essential ('A') receive three points, those important but not essential ('B') are given two points, while useful ones ('C') are allocated one point. Next, each domain is evaluated relative to each criteria and point values are assigned: '0' denotes a criteria definitely not satisfied; '1' implies a criteria unlikely to be satisfied; '2' and '3' indicate that the domain or applications area, is likely or would definitely satisfy the criteria, respectively.

Next, the satisfaction-score for the application relevant to a criteria is multiplied to the value for the importance of that criteria; Adding up all the criteria scores for the domain, gives a 'total score' for each domain candidate.

The organization can then plan an appropriate development schedule for domains which meet or exceed a pre-set acceptability threshold-score. Based on several post-implementation reviews, it has been found that a total score above 55 or 60 correlates well with a developed KBS application which the organization deems ‘satisfactory’, ‘beneficial’ or ‘a success’.

The influence of the development project on the delivery of an acceptable end-product cannot be overstated. If however, none of the candidates measure up in this domain evaluation, management may conclude that KBS development is not suitable for the organization at present.

Recently, the managers of a small manufacturing company in Hong Kong were asked to identify potential KBS domains in their organization. After considerable thought and deliberation, three domains were short-listed for further consideration. The candidates for KBS development were a dynamic job-shop production scheduler, a financial planning and forecasting assistant, and a consumer market analyser. The evaluation results, which were obtained using the framework, are shown in Table 2.

In this situation, the production scheduling domain received the top score of 66, largely due to an appropriate level of complexity for a PC-KBS solution and a consensus that such a solution would improve performance by alleviating an organizational bottleneck.

The market analyser received a marginal score of 58. Although the problem was deemed important and had management support, it was not clear that the domain could be sufficiently bounded or sub-divided to enable ‘manageable’-sized projects.

The financial planner received the lowest tally, below the acceptability threshold. There was an absence of experts available for the project and management support for the development of this KBS application was lukewarm.

As a result, it was recommended to proceed immediately with development of a knowledge-based production scheduler, to defer work on the market analyser (until after the developed and implemented scheduling application could be reviewed) and to consider the financial planning domain unsuitable for KBS development.

The actual development priority of an application may be based on independent merit, closely related to the score obtained using the previously considered evaluation framework. Such priorities should, however, be established with recognition of the importance of presenting core operations and/or future strategic directions of the organization and the relative risk level associated with the related systems-development project.

Finally, just as the impacts on productivity will be different in each case, the effect of PC-KBS introduction on organizational structure varies greatly from one application to another, and from one organization to another. If a company intends to introduce significant PC-KBS applications, especially if they are large scale projects over a short time period, the whole organizational structure may have to be reassessed and modified.

## Biography

Maris G. Martinsons is a Senior Lecturer (Faculty of Business) at the City Polytechnic University of Hong Kong. He is actively engaged in research and consulting work. His interests include management decision modelling, business and political risk analysis, technology transfer as well as Asian-Ballic business development. Dr Martinsons has a successful track record of planning and managing KBS projects in both North America and Asia. He has written extensively in both the popular press and management journals and is on the editorial board of the Journal of Applied Sciences and Strategy.
