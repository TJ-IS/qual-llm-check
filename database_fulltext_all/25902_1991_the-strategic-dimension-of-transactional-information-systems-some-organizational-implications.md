# The strategic dimension of transactional information systems: some organizational implications

R Andreu, JE Ricart and J Valor

Information Systems Department, I.E.S.E., Av. Pearson, 21 08034 – Barcelona, Spain

Abstract. Often information systems (IS) are classified in three groups: (a) transactional, used mainly for co-ordination and resource allocation purposes at the operational level of a company; (b) tactical, often employed to support the resource procurement activities typical of middle management; and (c) information systems for strategic decision making, designed to help in the planning and strategy design processes which are the direct responsibility of top management. In general, the amount of care and management attention that companies give to these different types of systems is proportional to their position in this hierarchy: little attention is devoted to the mundane transaction-pushing systems and exquisite care is put into developing the sophisticated decision making aid for the CEO and his/her staff.

The IS/IT literature has been reporting quite commonly cases in which companies have attained or lost great competitive advantages by way of their transactional information systems [for example, Emery Worldwide, Baxter Healthcare ASAP system, and Frontier Airlines]. The aim of this paper is to identify actions that companies can take to realize potential benefits of their IS, in particular from their low-level, transactional IS.

Among other actions, we will conclude that companies would be better off if they: (a) have the IS department at the right place in the organization, staffed with people knowledgeable about the basic nature of the business in which the company is engaged; (b) are sensible to what can be called ‘strategic maintenance’ of systems, (c) set up a formal procedure for IS planning to ensure coherence between IS plans and business plans, derived, in turn, from business strategy, and (d) keep abreast of the relevant technology.

Several examples taken from European companies are used to illustrate these conclusions.

Keywords: information systems planning, organizational implications of IS, strategic information systems, transactional information systems.

## INTRODUCTION

IS researchers and practitioners alike have often used the framework depicted in Figure 1 to classify information systems within a company. The message in it is that as one moves higher in the organization, the need is for more elaborated and aggregate information, which consequently becomes more critical for the success of the company.

The first set of systems, those designed to support strategic decisions in a company, often build on two sets of information: (a) information extracted from company operations, and (b) information gathered specifically for this purpose from outside the organization. Such systems are used at the top management level of the company, and no one questions their strategic dimension.

The distinction between Transactional and Management Control Systems (see Anthony, 1965; Gorry & Scott Morton, 1971) refers to the fact that the base of the pyramid contains the most disaggregate information available in the company. The corresponding systems usually involve just the mechanization of existing processes and their development typically does not require the participation of high ranking executives. Examples of transactional IS are accounting, payroll, and billing systems. Usually, these transactional systems are considered routine systems and their operation and maintenance are supervised at low levels in the organization.

The information processes by transactional systems, once aggregated and adequately elaborated, provides the basis for most of the Management Control Systems in use in many companies. Examples of these kinds of systems include production planning, financial planning, etc. It is not unusual that such systems take the form of Decision Support Systems (DSS, see Scott Morton, 1971; Keen & Scott Morton, 1987). They are used by middle and top management, and it is the responsibility of these managers to make sure that they serve the purposes for which they were designed. It is also accepted that DSSs can, sometimes, achieve strategic goals for the company. There are cases reported in which a well designed DSS has given the company competitive advantage. For example, the case of Pinsos Galofré (IESE, 1989), where a DSS to support the purchase decisions of Pinsos Galofré's clients — which depend upon certain characteristics of the clients' own activities — was used to get new clients and to increase the switching costs of all of them.

One of the tasks that companies perform to keep systems operationally up to date is software maintenance. In general, companies devote resources to this end proportionally to the perceived importance of the system, usually implying great care for 'strategic' information systems and relative disregard for transactional 'mundane' systems.

We found that the perennial disregard of transactional systems, which in many cases is even compounded by the belief that information systems are a simple support for administrative tasks, greatly handicaps the competitive position of the company. In contrast, other companies have excelled in the use of their transactional systems and have been able to use them for competitive advantage.

There are four types of actions taken by those companies that have excelled in the use of their transactional IS for competitive advantage: (a) putting the IS department in the right place in the organization, with the right people, and the right assignment, (b) being sensible to 'strategic maintenance' of software, (c) setting a formal process for developing IS plans and strategy, and (d) keeping abreast of the relevant technology. They are discussed in the following sections.

## Putting the IS department in the right place in the organization, with the right people, and the right assignment

In many companies the IS department comes from the evolution of a 'Data Processing Department', in which people with a strong technological background had as their initial mission to mechanize payroll and accounting — that is, the typical transaction processing systems justified mainly in terms of cost savings. As a result, many companies still have the IS department located at second-level, often reporting to the administrative/financial head. With such a set-up it is difficult to maintain a perspective wide enough to perceive all the strategic potential of information systems, specially if they are merely transactional.

A large European oil company had its IS department (still named DPD) dependent of the financial officer. Using this officer's words, the DPD processed his data and any information system that had to be developed had to be approved by him. Furthermore, the company-wide control systems and culture reinforced this kind of attitude. Under these circumstances, the company was unable to build the systems needed to compete in the fast-evolving refining and distributing industry. To use information systems effectively, even if purely transactional, it is essential for the organization to have a high-level officer responsible for them, closely controlling their coherence with the strategic goals of the company and, even further, making sure that information systems contribute actively to the business strategy.

Often, a problem found in organizations is that the heat of the IS department has suffered the same evolution that the department has, and he or she has a DP-biased vision of the company. Although technologically competent or even brilliant, (s)he is very often illiterate regarding the company's business. When information systems are used to achieve competitive differentiation, it is imperative for the executive in charge of them to be solidly knowledgeable of the business his/her company is in. Probably (s)he needs to be more knowledgeable about that than about the technology on which the systems will eventually be based. Although this conclusion is not new at all, we like to emphasize again that it also applies to environments where the systems are 'just transaction processing systems'.

The issue of where to locate the IS Department in the organizational chart (and, in fact, the deeper issue behind it, of how to organize the IS-strategy interplay) is also not new or scantily treated in the literature. On the contrary, it has been one of the classic topics in the IS field (see, for example, Cash et al., 1983; Sprague & McNurlin, 1986; Wysocki & Young, 1990, for different reviews of it). Our comment here is that this controversy is also potentially relevant when the vast majority of the IS used in a firm are ‘purely transactional’.

## Being sensible to 'strategic maintenance' of software

In many IS/IT departments, software maintenance is considered to be at the lower end of the tasks to be performed by IS staff. Maintaining old systems is time consuming, demotivating, and unrewarding.

One of the largest savings banks in Spain was the first to introduce a new product based on its transaction processing system a few years ago. The product is a typical example of 'an old product with an information add-on'. The 'old product' is a savings account. The 'information add-on' is a sort of 'revenues and expenses' account, issued normally monthly, where all the account's deposits and withdrawals are shown, classified by type and date.

The attractiveness of such a product for the bank's client comes from the added 'reporting service', while for the bank it motivates the client to put all his or her payments and receipts—including payroll—through the account, allowing the bank to increase the size of its deposits. The cost of producing the report is very small, as it is almost a direct by-product of the account movements' processing that has to be done anyway, precisely because it is one of the basic transaction processing systems of a bank. This same reason makes the product easily imitable, but first mover advantages can be very important; in this case the brand name used by the pioneer has pretty much become the generic product name.

In addition, even if what is to be imitated is based on not very sophisticated transaction processing systems, imitating is not so easy. In connection with the above example, some savings banks had difficulty in getting their imitations to market in time because the necessary upgrading of their transactional systems was considered to be 'maintenance of the old system', and put, with not high enough priority, in the maintenance queue. Drawbacks of this kind can create serious implementation problems in the process of having the necessary systems up and running on time.

Another interesting example is a bank in Spain that has a data base containing information about almost all operating companies in the country. This data base allows the bank to perform very fast credit scoring. The access system and data-base management is efficient enough given the purpose it was designed for. At some point, however, the bank decided that it could sell information about companies to other institutions. The software needed to allow such use of the data base was considered maintenance of the existing software and entered the 'maintenance queue' at the DPD. It took more than 6 months, and a concrete action by the bank's CEO, to get the project started. Thus, the attitude of the DPD delayed the introduction of this new product more than half a year.

When transactional systems become the processing plant of the company, software maintenance may have to do with a lot more than just 'patching up' the old systems. As a consequence, the relevance of a maintenance request cannot be judged from the IS perspective alone, and certainly not if the IS department is not at the proper level in the organization and/or its people are not knowledgeable about the business of the company. Thus the term 'strategic maintenance', which at first might seem self-contradictory. A similar conclusion is reached by Swanson & Beath in their book (Swanson & Beath, 1989).

Our conclusion is coherent with classical strategic analysis. For example, in the context of the valued added chain framework (Porter, 1985) it is clear that if the IS is critical for an important value-chain step, then the IS is critical for the business and should be considered strategically important. The fact is, according to our experience, that this is often overlooked in practice — in particular when the IS in question is transactional.

## A formal process for setting IS strategy

Many companies do have in place formal procedures to design their business strategies. Strategic planning and strategic management are common words in today's executive vocabulary. Less common are information systems planning and information systems strategic planning. However, if a company has to compete effectively by way of its information systems, it is necessary that information systems strategic actions be designed in the same manner as other strategic actions are developed in the company. To continue with the tone of this paper, this is also true for actions involving transaction processing systems.

No airline executive would question the fact that the company should plan airplane purchases well in advance, in the context of the lines it plans to cover and the type of service it wants to offer. This is so even though airplanes are going to be used in operations — it's clear that with no operations there is no company. Much in the same way, when IS are essential for the business (be it at the operational or transaction processing level or at any other level), investments in all aspects of information systems (hardware, systems infrastructure, application design and development, staff placement and training, etc.) should also be planned in the light of the competitive strategy designed for the company. Again this sounds obvious. But, again, in our experience companies seem to forget . . . The following examples illustrate it.

A large savings bank in Spain has its client databases accessed in real-time via a communications system which is incompatible with batch programs that require heavy database access.

This restriction forces the bank to shut down teleprocessing (and to have all its ATMs off-line) every evening as batch updates and backups are performed. This transactional system, although not perfect, was sufficient for the saving bank's needs, as the off-line operation of the ATMs has proved to be secure enough. Problems started however, when its major competitor offered to large department stores and supermarkets the possibility of connecting their point-of-sale terminals to the bank's databases so that clients could pay with debit cards, with the limit of the purchase being the balance in their accounts. Assuming that the bank had planned to enter the point-of-sale debit-card business in advance, it should have planned, also in advance, the migration of its current database and teleprocessing system to a system allowing multiple access for both real-time teleprocessing and massive batch updates and searches.

There are a number of stories that could be told to illustrate the lethal effect of the lack of IS planning in the line of Frontier Airlines or People's Express. There are also some European examples, for example, the savings bank of Hobbitown. Hobbitown Savings Bank is a local bank in Hobbitown, Middle Europe. It has about 60 branch offices and has a very strong market share in its county. Some time in the early 1970s, the bank started its credit-card business. When evaluating the data processing needs that this would generate (customer tracking, billing, etc.), the head of the data processing department wrote a report to high management stating that developing all the software necessary would be extremely expensive; he suggested to subcontract all the processing to Mordor Bank, a larger bank in a different county. Because of regulation, Mordor Bank could not operate Hobbitown's area. When debit cards came about a few years later, their printing and mailing was also subcontracted to the same bank. At this point, Mordor Bank could know everything about Hobbitown's credit-card clients and the names and addresses of all debit-card users (almost every client has a debit card for the ATM). A few months ago, Mordor Bank bought a small bank in Hobbitown that was having financial difficulties. No one at Hobbitown Bank doubts which mailing list Mordor will use for its approaching campaign of financial services. At this point, Hobbitown Bank is spending very large amounts of money to set up a system in a hurry that will allow it to process all its credit and debit card business. Making myopic decisions regarding transactional systems denotes lack of business-based planning for IS; putting too much emphasis on the economics of the situation can be thus grossly inappropriate and have far reaching consequences for the competitive positioning of the company.

IberCharter (IESE, 1984) a small Spanish tour operator founded in the early 1980s, was in fact created around its transactional information system, which was designed and implemented from the very beginning with the clear goal of its being the differentiating factor for the company. At the time, the tour operator industry in Spain was characterized by sluggish administrative processes that ended up generating cumbersome and inefficient reservation procedures and great delays in billing. In such an environment, traditional tour operators couldn't offer early payment discounts to their clients (mainly travel agents). When they finally billed the travel agents these had to perform the time-consuming and boring task of physically matching the bills to actual reservations which were already filed away, as billing was typically done after each flight had flown, while reservations could be done at any time. Knowing all these drawbacks very well, the founders of IberCharter, old time veterans of the tour operator industry, put special care on the design of the transactional information system. The results were many-fold: on the one hand, it dramatically improved the reservation procedures used at the time, but also it allowed the issue of bills early in the process (and thus the offer of discounts). On the other hand, it permitted to do away with the time-consuming bill-reservation matching process — something which, by the way, was a solution to a client's problem. It is clear that, by way of its transactional system, IberCharter achieved clear distinctive treats. But it went further, in fact, than that: by being able to offer discounts, and to simplify the administrative procedures of its customers, it effectively began to change the competitive standards of the industry. With such far-reaching implications, few would deny the strategic role that the transactional system had for IberCharter. It was the experience of the company's founders, who effectively planned the system with very clear business objectives in mind, that produced a transactional system enabling the company to compete effectively, turning traditional industry's practices into advantages for IberCharter.

Thus, a methodology for ensuring that IS (even transactional IS) is planned in accordance with the business strategy is needed. In general terms, such a methodology should at least make sure that IS implications derived from the business strategy in effect, at any point in time, are explicitly taken into account in the development of the corresponding IS plan. Several such methodologies have been proposed in the literature (see, for example, IBM, 1984; Lincoln, 1990; Earl, 1988). In some cases it may even be necessary that the business strategy itself be designed taking explicitly into account the strategic possibilities of IS — which implies a different, more proactive approach to IS planning (see Andreu et al., 1989b, 1991).

Finally, although out of the scope of this paper, it is worth saying that in order to plan effectively for IS it is often necessary to let the organization learn about IS planning, developing the appropriate culture for it. In our experience (see, for example, Andreu et al., 1989a), the very same IS planning process can (and in many cases should) be designed with definite organizational learning goals in mind.

## Keeping abreast of the relevant technology

Sometimes, being fully aware of the capabilities and characteristics of the technology available for IS implementation can be at the origin of IS developments with strategic potential. The Thomson's TOP system described below is a good example, involving a transactional system and a relatively new technology. For this reason, being at least in tune with the latest advances of the technology may be essential.

Even a system covering reasonably well the basic needs that motivated its design may become obsolete in front of the very eyes of its users not because it is not serving them, but because it will not be able to perform in the future or to supersede the systems of the company's main competitors. Once again, overlooking this circumstance is probably more likely when it comes to transactional systems, because they tend to be seen just as efficiency improving systems for which a better technological base is only justified if there is a clear, tangible and short-term economic benefit.

Thomson Holidays, the well-known tour operator from Great Britain developed a viewdata based reservations system (TOP) in 1982. The technology upon which it was based (a communications network owned by Thomson, fully compatible with the Prestel network) made it possible for all travel agents to gain cheap access to it. As Prestel terminals were available at low prices and could even be rented, the risk averse travel agents could try the system before making a definite commitment and investment. The TOP system was perceived to be very convenient by the travel agents, and a large share of the vacation travel business in Great Britain (mainly in the lucrative, late-availability segment) switched to Thomson the first season TOP was fully available. Thomson's sales grew, and so did its market share. TOP has had many imitators, but none has really been as successful as the original, in part due to the fact that the travel agents' operators were already used to the operational characteristics of TOP, which contributed to making it 'the operational standard'. Putting the reservation system (a transaction processing system) to work on the basis of a cheap, affordable and easy to use technology, has given Thomson clear early mover advantages in the marketplace which it has been able to exploit during the following years. Being aware of the technology available for this purpose was instrumental to achieve the competitive goal.

The case of the savings bank which has to have its databases off-line for a few hours is relevant here too. Being technologically incapable of offering 24-hour service, it is in clear disadvantage in front of the bank that can offer the debit-card service.

Thus, in connection with strategically valuable information systems it is extremely important to know 'what's out there' as far as the relevant technology is concerned. As a consequence, although in general it is not necessary to specifically create a post in the organization for someone to be in touch with the latest advances in information technology, it is essential to have this function explicitly stated as one of the responsibilities of someone in the organization, possibly in the IS/IT department.

Summarizing all these examples, we could say that a strategic dimension can be present in any kind of system, be it transactional or otherwise. In other words: something was missing in Figure 1. Perhaps a more accurate representation of the hierarchy of information systems in companies would thus be as in Figure 2. The message is clear: any system can be of strategic value, including transactional systems. As it turns out, it is very important to recognize this fact explicitly, as its implications can be far-reaching from an organizational point of view.

## CONCLUSION

It has been the main contention of this paper that all kinds of information systems in organizations may in fact have a strategic dimension, as even transaction processing systems can provide firms with competitive advantages in the marketplace.

The implications of such a strategic dimension are many, and all of them very relevant to management. In this paper we have argued that at least the following should be explicitly considered in order not to run into drawbacks that could seriously damage a company's ability to compete effectively, mainly when IS is directly used for this purpose:

1 Have the IS department in the right place in the organization, staffed with people knowledgeable about the basic nature of the business in which the company is engaged. Don't be fooled by thinking that 'all our systems are operational and don't need top management attention'; this could lead you to overlook precisely the strategic opportunities of such operational systems.

2 Be sensible of what can be called 'strategic maintenance'. The maintenance of a strategically important system has strategic importance. So, treating it as 'routine maintenance' is grossly inappropriate, and can be at the root of strategic disadvantages.

3 Use a procedure for strategic IS planning which at least ensures coherence between IS plans and business strategies. Even for operational systems planning.

4 Keep abreast of the relevant technology. 'Keeping an eye' on what is in store technologically can bring advantages though pioneering the usage of new technological developments in the implementation of IS. Some companies have even set up a specific organizational post to perform this function. The message in this paper is that this can be justified for transactional systems as well.
