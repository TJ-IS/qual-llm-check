# End-user software training: an experimental comparison of methods to enhance motivation

L Olfman and R P Bostrom\*

Programs in Information Science, The Claremont Graduate School, Claremont, California 91711 and \*Department of Management, College of Business Administration, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602

Abstract. Effective training is a key factor in determining the success of end-user computing (EUC) in organizations. Software training research often focuses on one outcome of training — understanding. The experimental study reported here examines understanding, motivation, and use as outcomes of software training. Two methods of training, applications-based and construct-based, that focused on different levels of personal relevance were compared. The experiment utilized subjects in a 'real' training setting. The effects of previous experience with software were strong. There is some evidence to suggest that applications-based training is best for novices.

Keywords: end-user training, learning, learning style, novice users, previous computer experience.

## INTRODUCTION

A worker with access to computer software applications can often decide to what extent he/she will use these applications on the job. For example, a clerical worker with access to a personal computer and Lotus 1-2-3 may be able to automate some of his/her tasks. The worker's decision to adopt the software, their extent of usage and subsequent effectiveness of using the software will depend on factors such as training, organizational support and work-group support (Cheney et al., 1986). Training, and more importantly proper training, is the first step.

Traditionally, the majority of software training has been aimed at 'product features and mechanics' (Karten, 1986). The goal of this type of training is to send the trainee away with a knowledge of what the software does. Karten calls this the 'I know how it works, but I don't know how to use it syndrome' (p. 10). This conventional type of training focuses on development of the user's knowledge of the software. The user's knowledge is one component that determines rational user behaviour. Moran (1981) describes rational user behaviour as follows:

The user is engaged in goal-oriented activity. He[/she] attempts to accomplish his[/her] goal as effortlessly as possible, within the constraints imposed upon him[/her] by the structure of the task, by what he[/she] knows, and by his[/her] own information-processing limits . . . (p. 3)

However, this description excludes motivation. Motivation is also a key determinant of behaviour in learning (Chaiken & Stangor, 1987; Pittman & Heller, 1987). Similarly, conventional training methods seem to avoid the issue of motivating trainees to use the software on the job.

A simple way of enhancing motivation in software training is to address the issue of how to use the software in specific job situations. Linking learning to solving job-related problems should make training more relevant for the trainee. Personal relevance is the meaningfulness that a particular situation holds for the person experiencing it. This concept is important in the learning process and in the formation and change of attitudes (Petty & Cacioppo, 1981; Ross et al., 1986) because a learning environment that is more personally relevant can be expected to enhance both the user's knowledge and their motivation to use the software.

In the experimental study reported here, a conventional training method (referred to as construct-based training) is compared to a more personally relevant training design (referred to as applications-based training). The experiment focuses on measuring users' knowledge and motivation to use software after training, and on their subsequent use of software. The experiment used the Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheet application.

Research in the field of information systems has generally ignored the (explanatory) process that transforms inputs to outputs (Benbasat & Nault, 1988). Experimental research designs are constructed around the manipulation of independent variables (inputs), and results are based in the measurement of dependent variables (outputs). This research follows the same model, however its goal is to utilize a process variable — personal relevance — to provide an explanation of why inputs should lead to specific outcomes.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next section describes a framework for conducting software training research, defines the nature of a software training seminar, and outlines previous research in the area. This is followed by a section which details the experimental methods, hypotheses and results. The final section provides a discussion of results, implications for researchers and practitioners, and conclusions.

## PREVIOUS RESEARCH

## A model for end-user training research

Research on end-user training must be predicated, in part, on the extensive amount of research done in the field of education. A number of key findings in that domain are significant for conducting training research. It is recognized that both motivation and ability are important factors in learning (Keller, 1983). It is also recognized that different training methods can differently affect different types of learners (Cronbach & Snow, 1977). Moreover, factors such as the instructor, the support materials used, and the training setting will influence learning outcomes (Włodkowski, 1982).

The inputs to training that directly influence training outcomes are: the software to be learned, the individual differences, and the training method. Software can directly influence the design of the training method. Individual differences can impact outcomes directly and indirectly via an interaction with software and/or method.

Training outcomes are shown as motivation to use and understanding. An additional long-term outcome is that of the use of the software on the job. Although these three outcomes are represented as interdependent in this research model, the purpose of the research reported here is to explore the impact of training methods on each of the specific outcomes.

A person's understanding of a software package can be of two types. The first type is called language understanding; it refers to knowledge of the architecture, syntax, and function of software (Olfman et al., 1986). The second type is called problem-solving understanding; it refers to knowledge about how the software can be utilized to solve a particular problem.

The few researchers that have focused on motivation outcomes have used related measures such as anxiety and attitude toward computers and software (Galletta, 1985; Howard, 1986; Harrington et al., 1987). The following sections describe a model for designing software training methods that can be varied and tested experimentally.

## Design of training sessions

Olfman & Bostrom (1988) have identified four components of a software training session delivered in a seminar/classroom setting. These include overview, presentation material, exercises, and documentation/handouts.

The overview consists of introductory information, presentation of basic concepts of the software to be learned, and a brief demonstration of the software capabilities. There is evidence to indicate that a conceptual model — a framework of the basic concepts of the software to be learned — will produce better levels of understanding for learners of software packages (Mayer, 1981; Galletta, 1985; Sein, 1988).

Presentation material encompasses specific procedural and usage information about the software, and is delivered by the trainer during the seminar. Presentation material is usually followed by exercises. Exercises allow the trainee to practice the material to be learned. These could be performed with or without the aid of the computer. However, research by Howard (1986) and Harrington et al. (1987) shows that hands-on use of computers should be employed in software training because this reduces computer anxiety.

Documentation and handouts are provided to assist the trainee during the seminar, and for later reference. Carrol et al. (1985) found that self-directed manuals that emphasize active learning lead to more positive understanding of software.

## Personal relevance

Bikson & Gutek (1984) classified software training methods as either narrow or broad. Broad training is that which focuses on getting trainees to understand the capabilities of a software package. Narrow training is aimed at a learner's own application, that is, a specific job junction to which the learner can relate. Bikson & Gutek (1984) found that the narrow approach was often used in training office workers to use software, and they argue that it constrains a person's abilities to utilize the software for other applications. They recommended using the broad approach which focuses on the range of capabilities that a software package can perform. This is the current common mode of training in industry, by vendors, in textbooks, and in educational settings.

The argument for maintaining a narrow focus is related to the concept of personal relevance. In three experiments, Ross and colleagues found evidence that high personal relevance exercises can have a positive impact on understanding learning materials (Ross, 1983; Ross et al., 1986). These studies did not address the motivation outcome. However, motivation is hypothesized to be enhanced in settings where personally relevant information can be processed. Petty & Cacioppo (1984) present the elaboration-likelihood model of attitude change. In the model, only a person who is motivated to process a persuasive communication can be expected to have an enduring attitude change. The motivation is derived in part from high personal relevance and direct involvement in the message being evaluated. Thus, a narrow approach such as one that focuses on trainees learning by solving their own problems could be expected to produce higher training outcomes in comparison to a broad approach (Gruhn & Hohl, 1979).

As each component of the training session can be designed to have a specific level of personal relevance, it follows that training sessions can be designed to have a specific overall level of personal relevance. For a given training design and trainee, each component could then be placed along a continuum spanning a scale from 'high personal relevance' to 'low personal relevance'.

Table 2 shows how the different components of a training session can differ in terms of the level of personal relevance. A session on the high end of the personal relevance continuum would have a overview that was related to a trainee's job-related needs, would have presentations that focused on filling these needs, would have hands-on exercises that help to fill the needs, and would have documentation to help in this process. By contrast, a session on the low end of the personal relevance continuum would have an overview that showed software features, would have presentations that focused on these features, would not have hands-on exercises related to these features, and would have supporting documentation of the features.

## DESCRIPTION OF EXPERIMENTAL STUDY

## Purpose

A field experiment was conducted to compare two training methods that were different in terms of personal relevance. The methods were called applications-based training and construct-based training, with the former being considered to have a higher level of personal relevance than the latter. The difference in level of personal relevance was predicated on an analysis of a typical seminar/classroom software training session.

## Subjects

The subjects for this study were volunteers who were interested in taking a beginner's Lotus 1-2-3 training session. At the time of the study, Lotus 1-2-3 was the most popular end-user personal computer software package (Nelson & Cheney, 1986), and so was chosen as the target software for this experiment. Subjects were currently employed in managerial or staff positions with two organizations in the State of Indiana. The two organizations were a state university and a manufacturing firm. In the university, the sessions were advertised in the personnel training bulletin. In the manufacturing firm, subjects were recruited with the aid of those responsible for EUC software training within the organization.

A total of 70 subjects participated in 12 training sessions. In the university, 58 subjects attended 10 training sessions; in the manufacturing firm, 12 subjects attended two training sessions. All but two sessions were attended by six trainees. In each of the two remaining sessions, five trainees attended due to last minute cancellations. In all, 62 of the subjects completed all dependent measures.

Subjects were not randomly assigned to treatments, but instead signed up for training without knowledge about possible differences in sessions. A check of a number of personal demographics variables such as age, sex, highest level of education and total years of employment showed no significant differences between the groups that received the two types of training treatments. This group of subjects had an average age of approximately 38 years, with about 16 years of employment experience. Subjects were mainly female, had varying degrees of education and job titles. Only six had been told to attend the training by a supervisor. The subject pool may be considered fairly typical of adults who seek further education (Cross, 1981; Amador, 1986).

## Procedure

Prior to the training session, subjects were sent a pre-training package that consisted of instructions and questionnaires. The questionnaires included an instrument to measure perceived usefulness and previous experience with computers, and to capture the demographic data mentioned above.

Each training session was conducted over an 8-hour period during 1 work day. This included 7 hours of training and 1 hour for lunch. The session classrooms were set up so that each trainee was able to have individual hands-on access to the Lotus 1-2-3 software. The setup allowed the instructor to demonstrate and present materials using projections directly from the personal computer to an overhead projector. Lotus 1-2-3 Release 2.01 was used in the university, and Release 1A was used in the manufacturing firm. However, the training package was designed to demonstrate only commands that were compatible to both Releases.

All sessions were conducted by the first author of this paper. An associate was present for 10 of the 12 sessions. Two different persons acted in this role, and reported that the researcher's conduct of the training sessions was similar for both applications-based and construct-based sessions. Subjects completed a post-training evaluation which showed no differences between groups in terms of the conduct of the instructor.

The training methods were similar to those used in a previous experiment but included some changes to accommodate a different type of software (Lotus 1-2-3 versus IFPS), and a different type of presentation mode (personal computer projection versus static overhead projection). At the beginning of the training session, each trainee was given the session agenda, and a training manual. The session began with a 30 min overview that included a brief introduction, presentation of a conceptual model, and a software demonstration. At the end of the overview, trainees were given about 15 min to explore Lotus 1-2-3 on their own. Then, presentations on different aspects of Lotus 1-2-3 and associated exercises were alternated up to the lunch break, and continued for the first $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours after the lunch break.

With 1 hour left in the training session, subjects were asked to complete the perceived usefulness measure and a training evaluation. Then, the instructor gave each trainee a blank work diskette and the problem to solve. After 30 min, subjects were asked to save their work, and to quit Lotus 1-2-3. Then they were given the language quiz. On completion of the quiz, trainees had completed the session, and were thanked for their participation.

Eight weeks after the training session, subjects were interviewed by the instructor over the telephone about their use of Lotus 1-2-3, and about their perceptions of how training had influenced usage.

## Independent variables

## Level of Lotus 1-2-3 experience

Trainees with previous experience in Lotus 1-2-3 were distinguished from those without such experienced based on the data from the pre-training questionnaires. In a previous experiment, the broader criterion of previous spreadsheet experience was used because none of the subjects had previously used the target software (IFPS). It was determined for this experiment, that because some subjects had used Lotus 1-2-3, a more specific criterion than previous spreadsheet experience should be applied.

## Training methods

Two training methods were used; applications-based and construct-based. Each training method was designed to create a different level of personal relevance. Table 3 presents a summary of each of the components of applications-based and construct-based training. The goal was to create high personal relevance in applications-based training by aiding 'own' problem solving. On the other hand, construct-based training was designed to be lower on the personal relevance continuum because it contained generic problem-solving and language related teachings. Both methods were designed to include equivalent levels of hands-on individual usage of the computer problem-solving exercises, and individual tutorial assistance. Specific differences in the four training session components are described below.

The overview in applications-based training included a demonstration of how to set up a problem using Lotus 1-2-3. The overview in construct-based training demonstrated various features and commands of the software. In both cases the overview included presentation of a conceptual model of Lotus 1-2-3 as an accounting spreadsheet.

The exercises in construct-based training were designed to allow the trainees to build the budgeting model (i.e. the same one used for demonstration purposes in the applications-based session). The exercise first requires the creation of a current year statement of revenues, expenses, and net income. Then it asks trainees to develop a 5-year projection. Other requirements included doing 'what if' manipulations and changing the budget statement to conform to a specific format. The applications-based trainees worked on a problem they had brought to the training session. In a pre-training handout, students who were to receive applications-based training were asked to prepare a problem beforehand and were given some suggestions on what this problem might be.

Applications-based trainees brought a variety of problems to the training session. These included simple budgeting applications (similar to the construct-based training exercise), comparisons of two or more alternatives, and tabulation and summation of various items (e.g. quantity × price). Trainees were guided to develop a simple model, and then expand it. They also were urged to try 'what if' scenarios and formatting.

Presentations in applications-based training were geared to facilitate problem-solving, while presentations in construct-based training explained how to create formulas and to use commands. In fact, the problem used in construct-based training was demonstrated in the applications-based training sessions. It should be noted again that although the presentations in applications-based training were on problem solving, exercises performed by the subjects were focused on problem-solving in both training methods.

Applications-based trainees were given a minimalist manual (based on Carroll et al., 1986) for documentation and to guide their problem solving, while construct-based trainees were given a manual that specified how to perform various commands, as well as the functions of the commands. Applications-based trainees were specifically advised that they should work through certain sections of the manual to reinforce the presentation material. The manual in construct-based training was a typical language reference manual. Both manuals were organized around the training session topics.

## Dependent measures

## Language understanding: quiz score

Language understanding was measured using a closed-book, handwritten, and untimed quiz. The quiz consisted of nine short-answer questions that were designed to examine basic information about the software architecture, syntax, and function that was covered in the training session. An example question (on syntax) was: 'What exact command sequence must you use, and what prompts must you answer to . . . resave an existing worksheet?' A maximum of 9.5 points could be obtained on the quiz.

## Problem-solving understanding: task score

Problem-solving understanding was measured through the score on a task that consisted of one short problem concerning a decision about buying one of two cars. It was open-book and timed for 30 min. It was to be entered into the computer using the target software (Lotus 1-2-3). The problem was designed to test simple model-building skills, as well as an understanding of how to develop a general model. On the task, six basic items (such as creation of parameters)

were scored for a possible sub-total of 7 points. Up to a maximum of three bonus points (for example for formatting) could also be earned, so that the maximum problem solving score was 10.

## Motivation to use: perceived usefulness of Lotus 1-2-3

Perceived usefulness was used as a surrogate for motivation to use the software. Perceived usefulness was measured using an instrument developed by Davis (1985, 1989) to assess the likelihood that prospective users of a software package would use the software. The instrument was found to be highly reliable. It contained six items (see Appendix A). Each statement was a 7-point Likert scaled item, with a 1 indicating high perceived usefulness and a 7 indicating low perceived usefulness. It was scored by summing the values for each of the six items, and then averaging. Change in perceived usefulness was measured by a simple gain score calculation. For example, for a given trainee, if average perceived usefulness before training was 3, and after training was 2, then the change would be -1.

## Usage: self-reported hours of use

Use of Lotus 1-2-3 after training was measured during a telephone interview conducted by one of the researchers. Each interview was based on a structured set of questions that asked trainees to estimate the average number of times they used Lotus 1-2-3 per week and the average length per session for job-related and non-job related functions. Totals were calculated by multiplying the average number of sessions by the average length per session and by the number of weeks of reported use. Trainees were also asked about influences they perceived to have affected their usage since training. The structured interview form was designed to minimize bias associated with the interviewer's knowledge of the method of training given to the interviewees.

A number of researchers have attempted to estimate usage of end-user software. There are two basic methods that can be utilized by the researcher to capture this data. The first method relies on the computer itself to monitor use. Kublanow et al. (1985) applied this methodology in their studies of end-users at IBM. A second method is to rely on self-reports via interview or survey. A recent study using this method investigated the usage of personal computers in organizations (Lee, 1986). The decision about which method to choose depends to a great extent on the target machine. The drawback of using self-report measures is that they are less reliable than machine-based measures (Ettema, 1985). As users in this study were from diverse organizations, who used personal computers in conjunction with others who were not in the study, self-reports of use were the most viable alternative. Actual use reported in this study may not be accurate, but the comparison of usage hours between treatments should be considered to be reliable.

## Hypotheses

Of the 12 hypotheses to be tested (two main effects and one interaction effect for each of four dependent variables), five were expected to show a difference and the others were expected to show no difference. The following hypotheses state the five expected main or interaction effects for the experiment.

It could be expected that differential training methods would produce different outcomes right after training. As the focus of construct-based training is on the low end of the personal relevance continuum, it emphasizes learning about the software as a language. Therefore, it is hypothesized that this form of training will enhance language understanding. As the focus of the applications-based training is on solving a personally relevant problem, it is hypothesized that this form of training will lead to better performance on a problem-solving task.

H1 Subjects who receive applications-based training will score higher on problem-solving understanding than subjects who receive construct-based training.

H2 Subjects who receive construct-based training will score higher on language understanding than subjects who receive applications-based training.

However, it was not plausible to expect that the training method could explain enough of the variance in the subject population, especially in terms of language understanding.

H3 Subjects who have previous experience with Lotus 1-2-3 will score higher on language understanding than subjects who have not.

The change in perceived usefulness is expected to be greatest for individuals who receive applications-based training because they learn in an environment where they can directly evaluate how the software can be used. At first sight, it should be expected that such changes would be positive. However, another view would suggest that the applications-based training method can enable an individual to determine that the target software will not be useful, and thus the training could produce negative changes.

H4 Subjects who receive applications-based training will have larger positive changes (after score minus before score) in perceived usefulness scores than subjects who receive construct-based training.

Actual use will be dependent on a number of factors including training outcomes. Lee (1986) found that individuals with higher levels of previous computer experience had higher levels of personal computer usage. Assuming that other factors are equally likely to affect usage for either applications-based trainees or construct-based trainees, it can be expected that the former will have higher usage levels than the latter because of higher motivation. This is because it is also hypothesized that at least the naive applications-based trainees will have higher perceived usefulness compared to construct-based trainees.

H5 Subjects who receive applications-based training will report spending more hours using the Lotus 1-2-3 on the job than subjects who receive construct-based training.

Cronbach's alpha was used to measure the reliability of the perceived usefulness, problem-solving task, and language quiz instruments. Perceived usefulness scores before and after training were highly reliable with alpha's of 0.97 and 0.96, respectively. The alpha for the problem-solving task was 0.55 and the alpha for the language quiz was 0.72. The low value of alpha on the task is difficult to interpret as the subjects completed the task as a project and not as a set of individual test items. The test items used to calculate reliability were scored post hoc by the grader. Low instrument reliabilities can be tolerated in test of group means (Mueller, 1986).

H1 stated that applications-based trainees would score significantly higher on the problem-solving task because of the orientation of the training method toward individual problem-solving. The problem-solving task scores were higher for applications-based trainees, but the difference was not significant ( $F_{1.58}=2.1$ , P=0.16; see Table 5). Instead, previous experience with Lotus 1-2-3 was a highly significant predictor ( $F_{1.58}=17.9$ , P=0.00; see Table 5).

H2 stated that construct-based trainees would score significantly higher on the language quiz because the training method focused on teaching constructs. Again, the difference between groups was in the expected direction, but not significant ( $F_{1,58}=1.2$ , P=0.28; see Table 5). H3 stated that those with previous Lotus 1-2-3 experience would do better on the language quiz than those without such experience because of the results of previous research findings. The difference was highly significant ( $F_{1,58}=14.8$ , P=0.00; see Table 5).

H4 proposed that perceived usefulness change (after training minus before training) would be significantly higher for naive applications-based trainees. These expectations were based on the fact that applications-based training was designed to be more personally relevant in order to enhance motivational outcomes. The perceived usefulness change difference was not significant $F_{1,58}=0.5, P=0.49$ ; see Table 5). In fact, directional means favoured construct-based training. In the change analysis, construct-based trainees had a mean increase in perceived usefulness, while applications-based trainees had a mean decrease.

H5 stated that applications-based trainees would use Lotus 1-2-3 for significantly more hours after training than construct-based trainees because it was designed to have higher personal relevance. While differences were again not significant ( $F_{1,58}=3.3$ , P=0.07; see Table 5), the directional difference was very large. However, the effect was significant in terms of level of Lotus 1-2-3 experience, with those having previous experience reporting much greater usage ( $F_{1,58}=8.1$ , P=0.01; see Table 5). These results should be interpreted with some caution because over 40% of the trainees (26 of 62) did not report using Lotus 1-2-3 in the first 8 weeks after training.

The results show previous Lotus 1-2-3 experience main effects with high power: 95% of the problem-solving task and the language quiz, and 75% for usage. Power levels for all other tests were small, and indicate that results of these tests are inconclusive.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

## Discussion

The experiment demonstrated the importance of previous experience in training outcomes because those with previous Lotus 1-2-3 experience performed better on understanding tasks. No significant statistical results were found regarding differences in training methods outcomes measured directly after training. Applications-based trainees used the software more after training than did construct-based trainees.

The lack of main effect significance for training method may be the result of the two training methods being too similar. Both training methods featured ample hands-on use, one-on-one tutorial assistance, and problem-solving exercises. All of these factors are important in facilitating learning. (Of course, in applications-based training, exercises were chosen by each trainee; while in construct-based training exercises were generic.) In retrospect, it is evident that neither training method focused on teaching constructs and syntax. Trainees who had negative comments about the training, regardless of method, identified a need for this type of instruction. This lack of focus may also be reflected in the rather low language quiz scores in both experiments.

The subjects came to training with a preconceived idea that it was necessary to learn Lotus 1-2-3. There is a strong possibility that beliefs about the software were already well-formed prior to the training session. This being the case, the perceived usefulness instrument may not have been useful in detecting changes in previously formed beliefs. Giving subjects some time to integrate information from and muse about the training session may have led to more reliable perceived usefulness measurements.

Post-training support can be an effective tool for giving users information about technical issues, and also for helping them find applications. It is surprising that neither those who use Lotus 1-2-3 nor those who did not use it mentioned factors related to availability (or lack) of user support in the follow-up interview. Post-training support was not explicitly offered as part of the experiment, and it is possible that this may have been a factor in the overall low level of usage.

Low power levels suggest that a conclusion of no differences between methods is not warranted. In exploratory research, it is difficult to determine what magnitudes of differences in dependent measures, and what levels of type I and type II error are appropriate (Cook & Campbell, 1979; Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1989). Thus, we are reluctant to conclude that there are no differences in the two types of training methods. Although the statistical results did not provide a clear decision regarding the strengths of one method over another, in the next section we briefly outline some plausible reasons why a practitioner, faced with a choice of two possibly equally effective methods, might wish to choose an applications-based approach over a construct-based approach.

## Implications for practitioners

Practitioners should consider the possibility of treating differently those with and without experience in a specific software package. One possible design would be to hold separate sessions for those with and without such experience. A drawback to this design is that many trainers are able to utilize the knowledge of some trainees to help others learn about the software. Mixing experience levels would make it more difficult to focus on applications-based versus construct-based approaches. Although not conclusive, the evidence derived from the experimenta! study suggests that applications-based training would be the better choice. Moreover, it is possible to have trainees use self-directed manuals on the job to learn the appropriate software constructs.

Use of software on the job is a highly desirable goal of software training. Applications-based trainees reported higher post-training usage hours than those in the construct-based training design partly because users could better understand how to apply the target software to their job. During the post-training interviews, trainees were asked to assess the role of training in their use of the software. Of 12 users who perceived the effect of training to include an understanding of how to apply the target software (Lotus 1-2-3) on the job, nine (75%) received applications-based training. An important benefit that can be derived from motivation is that it leads to experimentation. Experimentation will facilitate the user in developing language understanding. Better language understanding will be reflected in more use and more effective use. More use will lead to further motivation and experimentation, and so on. More effective use can be expected to increase productivity and to decrease the number of errors that can lead to negative organizational consequences.

## Research implications

The research findings in this paper represent snapshots of training outcomes. A more comprehensive design would involve longitudinal follow-ups on all three training outcomes at more regular intervals, and for a longer post-training period. In addition, during a longitudinal study, use on the job should be measured in more ways than just hours of use. Factors such as quality and correctness of worksheets (for example) should also be included in a measure of post-training usage.

Further research that operationalizes other methods along the personal relevance continuum should be undertaken. This would provide more tests of the importance of using personal relevance in designing software training. A study that further analyses and refines training components could be embedded in a study that varies training designs. The use of problem-solving guidance versus teaching of syntax should be explicitly explored for non-procedural software. It should be noted that the personal relevance continuum is not independent of the training setting. For example, a one-on-one training method would be seen as more personally relevant than a classroom setting. However, it is also important to compare the effectiveness of training methods across different settings, including computer-based training.

There is still much to be learned about the design of effective end-user training programs. This paper contributes to this learning by reporting on the design of software training methods that pay attention to a user's motivation to use the software on the job. Moreover, the reported experiments assessed multiple training outcomes (that is, understanding, motivation, and usage) in a field setting. There are other factors that can lead to more highly motivated trainees. Webster (1989) has shown that the concept of playfulness, both in terms of the software and the trainees, is an important dimension to consider.

Researchers also need to focus their attention on the process of learning. This process is not consistent across individuals. What is required is an understanding of how the learning process occurs, both during and after training. This understanding will come as researchers discover more about how different types of individuals learn in different training environments.
