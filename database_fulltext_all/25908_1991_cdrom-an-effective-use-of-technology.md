# CD-ROM: an effective use of technology?

CJ Armstrong

Information Automation Limited, Bronant, Aberystwyth, Wales, UK

Abstract. The correct use of information systems implies, in part, the effective delivery and use of information. In situations where, as has frequently been the case in recent years, CD-ROMs form a part of an information system, there is very little that users can do to regulate or control the quality of the information being supplied. Poor information cannot be effectively used. This paper discusses the effective use of the medium and questions whether all publishers are paying sufficient attention to quality.

Keywords: CD-ROM, data quality, information retrieval, information system, optical publishing, quality management.

One of the accepted definitions of the discipline of information systems mentions the effective analysis, design, delivery and use of information and information technology in organizations and society. Despite the specific division between information per se and information technology, the term information systems is still frequently viewed as being technology oriented — to do with the computer department. Information systems, like information management, actually spans the complete organization or institution and has to do with information in any form — whether generated from within (such as memoranda, internal reports or computer databases), purchased (external reports, conference proceedings or databases such as those supplied on diskette and CD-ROM), or leased (remote online databases, for example).

The library or information unit within any organization frequently has responsibility for only some aspects of the total information spectrum. These are traditionally those which have evolved from conventional library-based services — reference work, for instance. Thus, the library has, over the last 20 or so years, been the prime source for access to remote online databases. Now, and for the last 4–5 years, their portable counterpart, the CD-ROM has replaced the library in this function. CD-ROMs (Compact Disc-Read Only Memory) are optical discs — exactly similar in size and construction to conventional music compact discs — carrying hugh quantities of data in any form that is required (facts, statistics, references, text, images, sound, etc.). Again like music discs, the data carried cannot be altered, over-written or erased: they are a publishing medium which can be distributed easily. Clearly their ready availability means that the library is not the sole area in which CD-ROMs are used, but because CD-ROMs originally emerged as locally available equivalents of online information sources, the library has tended to be a chief centre for their use. By virtue of the apparently user-friendly interfaces and the fact that searching is not charged by time as with online systems, even in libraries the CD-ROM often becomes an end-user tool, i.e., used mostly by the library patrons and less frequently by the library staff. CD-ROM is often seen as placing the information directly in the hands of those who need it.

It is the devolved aspect which is the most worrying. While, as with conventional printed reference works and with online systems, a specialist controls use — guides users to the correct source, only purchases reputable reference works, studies and uses the most effective ways of locating the correct, best or most information, and even teases from users the appropriate question to ask in order to find the knowledge they lack — it is less important if one particular work is flawed because of its coverage or if one particular database fails by virtue of time span or data quality, because the specialist information worker would know both of the limitations and of alternate sources.

Now that raw information can be placed in and used by 'untutored' hands — even if the source is a product selected by the librarian — there is considerable risk of users being fooled by the information found: biased, inaccurate or incomplete data can be dangerously misleading. CD-ROMs carry data which cannot be altered, over-written or erased and as has been said, they are a publishing medium. This very stasis invests them with an even greater assumed authority than that often attributed to other material read from a computer or terminal screen. End-user searchers of any system are often naive. They are prone to enter simple searches and on receiving partial or even null answers, return to their work happy for instance, in the knowledge that no-one has written on the search topic. Almost certainly, what is obtained from the CD-ROM will be accepted as correct even if the user is sufficiently knowledgeable as to look for further information.

Where there is a controlling force, as within a library or information unit, use of notices and in-house training of end-users may ameliorate the dangers to some extent. Where CD-ROMs are purchased directly by end-users, the information system becomes fragmented to such an extent that there is very little that can be done within the organization to prevent potential information deterioration at outlying nodes. The onus must rest with the publishers and information providers. And the number of CD-ROMs published is rising, and is expected to continue rising, exponentially.

A recently quoted report suggested that as about 80% of databases currently available online are of a size which would allow them to fit onto a single CD-ROM, there is considerable scope for further CD-ROM products (Lobeck, 1990). Already there is a considerable amount of work underway in the areas of multimedia CD-ROM publishing including that in DVI (Microsoft's Digital Video Interactive), CD-ROM XA (CD-ROM eXtended Architecture), and CI-I (Compact Disc Interactive, which requires dedicated hardware). All of these products taken together with the recent launch announcements of Commodore's CDTV (Commodore Dynamic Total Vision) which it is claimed, will have over 140 products in place within 6 months of launch) seems to indicate that the consumer world — for many of these products do not seem to be intended for industry — is to be faced with an ongoing explosion comparable to that which took place with the advent of vacuum cleaners, washing machines and microwave cookers: every home will shortly have a CD-ROM capability of one sort or another. And clearly, no major library can afford to be without a range of products.

Or are we guilty of being taken in by the hype? Are there really users out there who want to read Sherlock Holmes or the Bible on-screen; or who, in the comfort of their homes, 'will take a walk round the Victoria and Albert Museum' — or even Egypt — via CDTV? Indeed, should we not ask if it is desirable for the producers to generate this kind of need simply because the technology is there?

Putting aside such moral and philosophical niceties, as well as the magic of CDI, CDTV etc., some comment is called forth by the 'hard-core' CD-ROM market. Over the last 3 years the number of titles has increased from around 300 to about 1,600 and new titles continue to be announced at a surprisingly high rate. If the conversion of online databases to CD-ROM continues as it has been suggested it might, keeping track of what is available is set to become an industry in its own right! There are now regular reviews of CD-ROM products, (for example, Armstrong & Large, 1990, 1991). While many of the reviews are done by our panel of reviewers — especially those which require subject knowledge — this author has personally looked at more than 40 CD-ROMs containing databases which are bibliographic, statistical, cartographic, textual, factual, encyclopaedic and directory in format.

Is this explosion of information on CD-ROMs really desirable? Who are all the users? Do these prognostications spell the end of the online industry? Very unlikely indeed, but even the present rapid growth of new products without the wholesale conversion which it seems is possible, begs these questions. And as the market burgeons, it becomes more important to question how much market research is done before new products are thrust upon unsuspecting users? As many of the reviews in CD-ROM Information Products: the evaluative guide, Volume 2 suggest, the answer might be all too revealing. Some have the appearance of not being targeted at all. The question of who uses all the CD-ROM versions of the Bible and who reads their favourite Sherlock Holmes tales from the screen was raised in Volume 1; the review of one of the two Shakespeare CD-ROMs, written in part by a senior academic in a department of drama, emphasizes only too well its lack of utility (Williams & Armstrong, 1991). Similar comments could be made about other products reviewed, i.e. the CIA World Factbook, the World Atlas, Pravda 1987, and to a lesser extent the Oxford Textbook of Medicine and even Harrap's Multilingual Dictionary.

CD-ROM has always been sold as a publishing medium and not simply — although initially it was certainly viewed in this way by the information community — as an extension of online information services. Indeed several pundits have made much in the past of how ill-suited a CD-ROM is to those databases which require regular updates. It is only as the cost of production has reduced that information providers have been able to produce discs with sufficient regularity to answer these critics. The variety of form and content in CD-ROMs now available and exemplified in the reviews mentioned above indicates that publication on disc has now reached virtually every sector. Discs cover astronomy to zoology, administration and business to veterinary medicine; and vary in form from the bibliographic database, through textual, factual and statistical databases to patents, maps, and software.

It has often been stated that the medium is best suited to data which do not require constant or regular, short-term updating; it has been disappointing to see how few manuals, yearbooks, encyclopaedia and dictionaries have appeared. Perhaps the recent news that the Civil Aviation

Authority has given approval a CD-ROM of repair manuals and practice to be used without supporting hardcopy may boost the development of products in this area. Of those dictionaries and encyclopaedias which are available, it can only be said that not all were the most obvious candidates for this kind of publishing. The increasing 'multimedia' capability is beginning to allow the development of products which have a value added to their conventional print form. The Guinness Book of Records and the second edition of the Grolier Encyclopaedia are cases in point, although once again it is somewhat questionable as to who will use the former. Perhaps the best example of appropriate use of technology in this area lies with the Oxford English Dictionary on CD where the additional access points provided by the software allow much wider use than could be imagined for the print version (Grogan, 1990). Routes to information offered by the electric version and impossible without endless hours of study in the printed version include the ability to locate all words of a particular origin or connotation (perhaps, from the Chinese or to do with the sport of cricket); to discover words fitting a particular definition; to locate quotations which include a particular word; and the ability to pinpoint words which fit a number of criteria, for instance, 'words used in the East to describe a kind of litter carried on poles' or 'Jewish/Hebrew musical instruments'.

With this diversification of both publishing and forms, there is too often a lack of standards. For example, users all too frequently report problems with installation or with running several CD-ROM products from a single microcomputer. The problem may lie in poor documentation, poor installation routines or bad updating to a new version. Whatever the problem, it causes much unnecessary frustration and reflects badly on the information industry as a whole.

One approach to both standardization and quality control is through the foundation of such groups as the CD-ROM Standards and Practices Action Group (CD-ROM SPAG), an industry lead group in the UK, which has the avowed intention of making life easier for user and developer alike by identifying, developing and promoting standards and codes of practice such that CD-ROMs can be used with a minimum of effort in any circumstances. A similar initiative to standardize aspects of the CD-ROM interface has begun in the USA. CD-CINC (CD-ROM Consistent Interface Committee) aims to tabulate the basic functions of search and retrieval software and suggest a consistent set of terms for these functions (those of us originating in the library and information world may have thought this to be already in place) and like CD-ROM SPAG, CD-CINC intends to produce guidelines for publishers.

It would seem obvious that the publication of any item in a new medium must be justifiable, for example, paperbacks are cheaper than the hard cover equivalent. Unfortunately, CD-ROMs are often not cheaper than print equivalents so the justification must lie within the medium itself. Better access to the contents, the addition of useful sound or animation, or the ability to compare the text from different parts of the disc on screen are good examples; and there are products which offer this kind of interface and thus justify their existence alongside their print version. Unfortunately, there are also products which do not. One example is The Oxford Textbook of Medicine, which, for over twice the price of the printed version offers minimally better access to the text while robbing purchasers of all figures and tables.

Neither must the data themselves be forgotten amongst the excitement over interfaces and form. Multimedia and speedy or clever access notwithstanding, the information which users find on the CD-ROM is its raison d'être. Flawed information is useless; information which is only apparently valid could be dangerous. Shakespeare on Disc could have been improved by better editions of the plays and a complete critical apparatus — without these its use is limited; other CD-ROM have data which are inaccurate in a variety of ways. This is probably a criticism which is only levelled at nouveau databases; established products reproduced from an online parentage have had time to establish a natural order of things. No criticism can be levelled at, for instance, the CD-ROM Dissertations Abstracts database or the CD-ROM Books in Print, that is not equally true of the online version. In cases such as these, it is simply the greater availability which means that acknowledged problems will confront unsuspecting users. However, the data in the Postcode Address File on CD and in Computer Select, for instance, are new to the marketplace and both of these suffer from poor initial data input. In a review version of the former it would be necessary to enter five variations on one major bank's name in order to locate all branches, or to use considerable imagination to discover the 110 entries input with an initial ampersand; and to interpret 4950 and 9011 as 49–50 and 9–11. Nor was the coverage comprehensive — in a town known to the reviewer a number of major shops were absent. Similarly in the latter CD-ROM, the index was riddled with nonsense words generated by mis-keyed input or deficient editing/stop lists. The corollary to this is that in order to locate any item wholly, or precisely, or with certainty, users must frame their search requests with extreme care, and frequently at some length. Where products such as these are targeted for end-users, it is not sufficient to supply powerful software which could circumnavigate the data problems; end-users will probably never become sufficiently skilled in information handling to be aware of the problems let alone to discover the availability of ways which could overcome them, and then learn their use. In one product reviewed, the CIA World Fact Book, users are effectively discouraged from so doing, many of the software capabilities being hidden from users by the simple expedient of silence in the documentation. Obviously those seen as typical users are not deemed capable of the more complex search queries: the net result of this solution — a solution which also leaves three-quarters of the help pages unintelligible and contradicting the documentation — is to render the search software incapable of reducing search results to realistic numbers. After a search (for instance, on Hawaii finding 22 cards or on oil or bitumen or coal finding 132) users are left to browse through the top line/title of each data entry to discover its applicability. There is no way of limiting a country search to the country's main card — the result includes all those cards which simply mention the country. Publishers, and more particularly information providers, must be made aware that poor data integrity cannot be overcome with cosmetic disguise and turning a blind eye.

Apart from better quality control of data, a number of solutions suggest themselves. Poor quality data can take a number of forms: apparently good data may be either inaccurate or out-of-date, and this can be potentially a worse problem as there is nothing to indicate that something may be wrong. In one CD-ROM reviewed, TrEC (three databases on training and education) many of the records related to courses which had either not been run for years or had only ever been run as a one-off short course. The date-stamping of these records for both input and date-of-course would have given some indication of their provenance. In the same database, it was often not clear why some records had been displayed. The highlighting of search terms or the inclusion on the display of the indexing which had caused that record's selection would have been useful. The development or use of a thesaurus and a controlled indexing vocabulary can serve to reduce false drops and increase precision in some cases; although this is not necessarily the best or most appropriate solution with full-text databases, it should not be ruled out of court too speedily. It fails in this context if users cannot be channelled into using it automatically (see, for instance the remarks with respect to SilverPlatter's Medline in Whitsed, 1991) as most users will tend towards the default search option with little thought as to the intersection of their search terms (for example, a simple search on 'Heart' and 'Conditions' in a full-text database could locate an article with the incidental remark, 'had not the heart to explain to the staff' in an early paragraph of an article about economic conditions in hospitals). Coupled with a search interface which forces its use on the initial query and explains the progress and possibilities of the search at all stages it can answer or ameliorate a lot of search and access problems. A particularly good example of such an interface is CD Plus' Medline.

Discovering the validity of external data is never easy — there are too many interests involved. In the online world, there are the data provider and the host on whose computers the databases are mounted; information about individual databases — their structures, currency, retrospective coverage and updating, for instance — will be supplied by the data provider and distributed by the host as database chapters or database sheets in its documentation. The documentation itself will detail ways in which the software can be best utilized to retrieve appropriate data. In most cases the data provider will not only be named but be known by all parties — the National Library of Medicine, for instance, which provides Medline. CD-ROM, possibly because it is targeted at the end user, is frequently less than generous with documentation. Too weighty a tome would put searchers off and too much detail would, in any event, be either ignored or not understood. Thus searchers often have no database-specific information, and without this it is impossible to explain how to get the best from the software. Frequently it is not even clear from where the database originates. SilverPlatter were particularly poor in this respect with early versions of their documentation. As CD-ROM proliferates, more and more players enter the arena: in one directory of CD-ROMs (Desmarais, 1991) there are indexes for data provider, publisher (equates to an online host), software provider and distributors. And there are plenty of distributors. Many, although certainly not all, from a computer hardware- or software-product background; a pedigree which tends to a situation in which the distributors know very little more about the data, the value of the data, the application of the data or the provenance of the data than does the purchaser. Thus the amount of after-sales support available is likely to be strictly limited. The moral for the institution and the information systems' manager is to purchase from either the publisher (for example, SilverPlatter, Whitaker, University Microfilms International) or from an agent who has a library/information background (for example, Microinfo or Faxon).

As a guarantee-purchase is not always possible, it seems that the final answer to problems of data integrity must lie with the publishers of CD-ROM products. Just as Red Book and Green Book Standards and ISO 9660 have developed; there is a need for a standard which dictates a methodology for provenancing a database. Were the first data item on every CD-ROM to include information on the source of the data, the domain, the date at which the data was last revised, the frequency at which the data provider normally updates their own database, the frequency with which CD-ROMs are issued, database size (in records), date at which database commences (where relevant), number of records added per update, name of thesaurus, database structure, and print and online equivalents, and were this information to be automatically read and displayed whenever any CD-ROM is loaded, some of the problems inherent in an accessible data publishing medium would be removed.

Problems of data accuracy lie squarely at the doors of the data provider. The solutions suggested above simply allow users some acquaintance with the facts. This is perhaps most crucially important where the data as read from the screen take on a value to the user which is not wholly incidental. Springer-Verlag's Dangerous Goods CD-ROM can be cited as one example on which the data accuracy is of paramount importance. Covering dangerous chemicals and goods transported within Europe, it contains a series of databases of first recourse in the event of spillage or accident — lives could depend on the information gleaned. Here however, the data seems of excellent quality, although users should be aware that the seven databases from various information providers often contain data which do not match precisely. One solution which begins to provide a means by which data providers can vet their data as it will appear on the CD-ROM lies in the provision of proof-discs. While software which mimics the final product has been available for some time, it is usually a part of the design and mastering software and hence not easily available to third parties such as data producers. Lower development costs now mean that an actual CD-ROM can be produced and sent to the data provider for checking. One company offering this service is NIMBUS. It is perhaps slightly invidious to pick examples in which data accuracy is more important than others. Examples in the medical field could save lives, examples from the hundreds of business CD-ROMs could save millions of pounds, and so on: Micromedex produce the DRUGDEX database containing information on drugs; Bureau Marcel van Dijk publish the Jordan company database as Financial Analysis Made Easy (FAME), and these are just two examples from the more than 1 600 CD-ROM database currently available.

The strength of CD-ROM as a publishing medium lies in its vast capacity, minimal storage requirements and desk-top utility. Major databases can be located in the work place for use by the information seekers themselves. While any reference work is vested with a degree of authority by virtue of its publication, there is a tendency to view data provided directly to the computer or terminal screen as unimpeachable. Information workers are trained to question the provenance and validity of the sources they use, but this may not be the case with end-users. In any event, the expectation of user validation does not excuse the information providers and publishers from a duty to furnish users with products which can be used with confidence. Any database, whether on CD-ROM, diskette, tape or online, should contain data which have been validated and checked in terms of their origins, their accuracy, their scope and their precision. Where, as in the first two cases, search software is supplied with the database, care should be taken to ensure that the mechanisms by which information can be retrieved match the data itself. Software designed to retrieve bibliographic references is not ideal for full-text databases, and software for linear full-text databases such as The Oxford Textbook of Medicine is not suitable for multi-faceted databases such as Shakespeare on Disc, and certainly not for the product which Shakespeare on Disc should have been.

The speed with which the CD-ROM industry has grown is due in no small part to the number of companies which have discovered its potential. Those companies which have expanded their product range, or at least the range of media on which their products are available, from within the information industry for the most part provide a reliable and efficient service. In the early 1990s, however, it is still the case that considerable care must be exercised in evaluating products in terms of their content and utility before any information system can safely be deemed truly effective. It must be a part of the task of information systems managers to ensure that the variety of information sources available within the system are not only seen to match the needs of the users, but actually match and answer those needs so that the users can rely on the system.

## Biography

Chris Armstrong worked as a research officer at the (then) College of Librarianship Wales for about 10 years before leaving and setting up his own research and consultancy company. Information Automation Limited specializes in all aspects of online and CD-ROM information provision and handling with an emphasis on training. Since leaving the College of Librarianship Wales, work has included consultancy and subsequent training for the British Council in Indonesia, and training software development for two online hosts: the British Library and DIMDI in Germany. Chris Armstrong is also responsible for the compilation and production of CD-ROM Information Products: an Evaluative Guide and Directory and Information Studies Courses in the UK: a Directory and Guide.
