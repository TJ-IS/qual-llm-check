# The organizational fit of information systems

J. livari

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, Linnanmaa, SF-90570 Oulu, Finland

Abstract. One can expect that the fit, match or congruence between an information system and its organizational context will be of increasing importance as information systems become more and more integral parts of organizations. Existing IS research has largely neglected the theoretical analysis of the concept 'fit', however, even though it has been widely used in a 'pre-theoretical' sense. The analysis of the concept of 'organizational fit of an information system' put forward in this article is essentially based on the recent analysis of 'fit' in contingency theory, suggesting three interpretations for the concept: a selection approach, an interaction approach and a systems approach. A review of the IS literature on the organizational fit of information systems indicates a lack of any cumulative tradition in existing empirical contingency research into information systems and dominance of the selection approach. Because of the two limitations, this paper regards any definite assessment of the potential prospects of contingency research into information systems as overhasty and proposes a framework for future research into the situation dependence and organizational fit of information systems which integrates contingency research into information systems, contingency research into IS impacts and contingency research into the IS adoption (development/implementation) process.

Keywords: contingency theory, information system, information systems research, organizational fit, situation dependence.

## INTRODUCTION

Leifer (1988) recently stated that the 'match or 'fit' of CBIS design within organization design is one under-appreciated aspect of the equation determining CBIS success' (p. 63). In their review and assessment of the contingency theory of management information systems, Weill & Olson (1989) identify ill-defined concepts of fit and performance as one of their four major points of criticism, and take a highly critical attitude towards contingency theory research in MIS. They largely, though not altogether, advocate its abandonment arguing that it has a too narrow focus and naive meta-theory.

For reasons to be explained below, this paper takes a more positive attitude towards contingency theory research into the situation dependence of information systems. Its primary purpose is to analyse the concept of the 'organizational fit of information systems'. An information system is interpreted as a formal system which provides its users in a certain organizational context with information about a set of topics (cf. Gustafsson et al., 1982), covering both application information systems and organization-wide information systems. The analysis will take place at the levels of both the substance of theory and the structure of theory (Markus & Robey, 1988), the primary focus being at the latter. In the context of the organizational fit of information systems, the former concentrates on the characteristics of an information system and its organizational and social context, which are assumed to affect the success of the system interdependently in some sense whereas the latter focuses more specifically on the assumed form of the interdependence between the characteristics of an information system and its context, where this form defines the general concept of 'fit'.

Concentration on the organizational fit of information systems allows a more thorough analysis of the existing literature than was possible by Weill & Olson (1989), who review contingency theory research in two journals (Journal of Management Information Systems and MIS Quarterly) covering management of the MIS function, implementation of MIS, structure of the MIS function, and development of MIS as MIS variables. Weill & Olson do not identify IS characteristics as a separate category in their classification of MIS variables, but seem to include them in their category of development of MIS. $^{1}$ The distinction between IS characteristics and IS development characteristics is important however, as the former concern an information system as a product of IS development (cf. 'The information subsystem' in Ives et al., 1980) and the latter are process characteristics (cf. 'The development process' in Ives et al., 1980).

The composition of the paper is as follows. The following section discusses the concept of 'fit', taking as its starting point the distinction between a selection approach, an interaction approach and a systems approach as defined by Van de Ven & Drazin (1985) and Drazin & Van de Ven (1985), and suggests the analytical framework to be applied in the subsequent literature analysis of empirical research into the organizational fit of information systems.

The section following that summarizes the detailed literature analysis and yields two major findings. First, it indicates that existing contingency theory research into IS characteristics does not have any cumulative tradition at the level of the substance of theory. It is hard to find even a single pair of empirical studies, the results of which could reasonably be compared with each other. This suggests that Weill & Olson's (1989) critique of conflicting results is not valid in the case of the category of contingency theory research reviewed in this paper. Second, at the level of the structure of theory, the analysis shows that the concept of the 'organizational fit of information systems' has been dominated by the selection approach (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985;

Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). This implies a unidirectional causal model which assumes that the organizational context determines IS characteristics. With reference to Huber & McDaniel's (1986a) argument that the decision-making 'paradigm' is becoming increasingly central to organizational design, particularly in the post-industrial environment, and that any significant change in information technology seems to lead to new organizational design options (Huber & McDaniel, 1986b), the relationship between information systems and their organizational context is likely becoming more and more of a reciprocal matter. This means that unidirectional causalities expressing the fact that either the organizational context (environment, organizational technology, structure, etc) determines the characteristics of an information system, or that, vice versa, information technology and information systems determine organizational technology and structure, may be too simplistic.

Based on the two findings, this paper argues that it is too early to assess the prospects of future contingency theory research into the situation dependence of information systems based on a broader concept of fit and a more systematic research program. As the second major contribution of the paper, the final section outlines a framework for future research which largely avoids the meta-theoretical criticism of Weill & Olson, (1989). The framework distinguishes three major research areas, contingency research into information systems, contingency research into IS impacts and contingency research IS adoption process (IS development/implementation and related decision-making). The paper suggests, however, that the areas should form a structure in which the succeeding area serves as a source of empirical data for the preceding ones.

## THE CONCEPT OF 'FIT'

## Introduction

The idea of the organization fit, match, congruence or consonance of information systems has been quite common in IS research, at least as an implicit byproduct of its reference theories. Markus & Robey's (1983) article on organizational validity summarizes some of this tradition. It characterizes validity as the degree of fit or match between the information system and its organizational context, identifying four components in this fit: 'user-system fit', 'organization structure-system fit', 'power distribution-system fit' and 'environment-system fit'. It also reveals the conceptual confusion related to these. The four fits contain, on the one hand, a flavour of the concept 'organizational validity' as defined by Schultz & Slevin (1975) and on the other hand ideas from contingency theories of organizations. These two traditions are quite distinct, and it is by no means clear that 'fit' in the spirit of contingency theory corresponds to 'organizational validity' in the sense of Schultz & Slevin. They define it as 'a compatibility with the user organization' involving 'the behavioral congruence of the model and the organization' (p. 38) and suggest the degree of changes in individual, small group and organizational variables as one possible measure of organizataional validity (p. 40). Their interest also lies in successful implementation, which leads us to the dilemma between IS implementation success, i.e. planned institutionalization of the information system, and IS development success, expressing the desirability of the consequences of the whole IS development process (analysis, design and implementation) from the viewpoint of different interest groups (livari, 1985). As livari points out, maximization of the prospects of IS implementation success may detract from IS development success in the longer run. Recognizing the above dilemma, Markus & Robey (1983) note that increased organizational validity may ease implementation, but may have undesirable implications with regard to long-term organizational effectiveness. As fit is usually assumed in contingency theory to lead to high performance (organizational effectiveness), the two concepts are clearly distinct, even though interrelated.

This paper adheres to the concept of 'fit' in the spirit of contingency theory. Since Markus & Robey (1983), important structural criticism of the concepts of 'situation dependence' (Kickert, 1983) and 'fit' (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985; Venkatraman, 1989) have been published within the contingency theory of organizations. This criticism is evidently highly relevant in the context of the organizational fit of information systems, too. Consequently, the present article is heavily based on this quite recent criticism.

One can distinguish two traditions relevant to the concept of 'fit'. First, there is a set of conceptual frameworks with the governing idea that an organization consists of interacting subsystems, dimensions or sets of factors between which there should be a certain congruence as a condition for high performance. Consequently, these frameworks include an idea of 'fit', even though it may not be explicitly stated (cf. Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). Perhaps the most famous representative of this category is the framework proposed by Leavitt (1965), which has also widely influenced organization-oriented IS research (e.g. Hedberg & Mumford, 1975; Keen, 1981; Mumford, 1983; Davis & Olson, 1985) leading as its byproduct to an implicit concept of 'organizational fit of an information system'. We call these frameworks conceptual, as most of them are not empirically validated. The second tradition consists of the structural contingency theory of organizations, which emerged in the early sixties (Burns & Stalker, 1961; Woodward, 1965 etc.), explaining the organizational structure by means of various contextual factors, particularly environment, technology and size (see Child, 1975; Kilmann, 1977; Fry, 1982; Olerup, 1982 for reviews, and Child, 1977; Miller, 1981; Schoonhoven, 1981; Kickert, 1983; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985; Fry & Smith, 1987; Gutek, 1990 for criticism). In view of the vast literature on these two traditions, our intention is not to review them here, but we shall concentrate on recent criticism of the concept of 'fit' concerning both traditions (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). Finally, the section headed the analytical framework introduces the analytical framework to be used in the subsequent literature analysis of research into the organizational fit of information systems.

## Three perspectives on the concept of 'fit' in organization theory

The concept of 'fit' expresses an idea that the object of design (e.g. an organizational structure or an information systems) must match its context in order to be effective. Van de Ven & Drazin (1985) identify three more operational meanings of the concept 'fit' (see also Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985).

1 The selection approach, in which fit is an assumed premise that an organization must adapt to its context in order to survive or be effective. Fit is achieved by natural or managerial selection.

2 The interaction approach, in which fit is interaction between pairs of organizational context-structure factors regarding performance or conformity to a relationship of context and design.

Even though Van de Ven & Drazin do not state it directly, the name 'systems approach' is selected to emphasize the reaction against the reductionist consideration of pairs of structure and context factors in the interaction approach (Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). Consequently, the concept should not be confused with systems approaches in the sense of Churchmann (1979), Checkland (1981), etc.

Van de Ven & Drazin (1985) also propose test methods for the different fits. In the selection approach one can test coefficients of correlation or regression between context and structure. In the interaction analysis the context-structure interaction terms in MANOVA or regression analysis on performance should be significant or the residuals of context structure relations regressed on performance should be significant, while the systems approach predicts that deviations from a consistent structure should result in lower performance.

Kickert (1983) identifies five interpretations of situational dependence. If we denote the situation by z, the structure by x and the effectiveness by y, these cases can be depicted as follows: $^{1}$

$$
\begin{array}{l l}1.&z \rightarrow x \rightarrow y\\2.&z \rightarrow (x \rightarrow y)\\3.&z + x \rightarrow y\end{array}\quad\begin{array}{l l}4.&x, z \rightarrow y\\5.&(z <   \rightarrow x) \rightarrow y\end{array}
$$

In the first case, the situation is assumed to determine the structure, and the effectiveness, depends universally on the structure. In the second case, the situation influences the relationship between structure and effectiveness, in the third, the situation and structure have an independent effect upon effectiveness, whereas in the fourth, the influence is interactive. Finally, in the fifth case, the assumed specific congruence between situation and structure influences the effectiveness.

As a synthesis, we suggest the framework depicted in Figure 1. The selection approach includes the first case in Kickert's scheme. The dotted arrow from x to y indicates that research settings in this case usually omit the effectiveness variable (cf. Kickert, 1983; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). The interaction approach covers the second, fourth and fifth cases in Kickert's classification. As Kickert illustrates the concept of situation dependence in terms of the three variables, he does not clearly recognize the systems approach characterized by multiple contingencies and structural dimensions. The structural alternatives have been found to form basic patterns which are systematic configurations of the structural dimensions (Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985, p. 348; cf. gestalts in Miller, 1981). The alternative patterns, if they are not equifinal, differ in their effectiveness depending on the situation. Effective matching of the situational and structural dimensions can form quite a complex structure (cf. Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985, p. 349). $^{1}$

Figure 1. Three models for the concept of 'fit'.

More recently, Venkatraman (1989) published a more refined classification for the concept of fit, in which he distinguishes six interpretations of 'fit'; fit as moderation, fit as mediation, fit as matching, fit as gestalts, fit as profile deviation and fit as covariation. His article is methodologically oriented, concentrating on analytical issues and statistical methods for testing the fits. Interpretation of the six fits is complicated by the fact that he characterizes them only verbally, without defining them in any more precise mathematical terms. His discussion of the analytical and methodological issues suggests, however, that the moderator perspective reflects the interaction approach, including cases $z \rightarrow (x \rightarrow y)$ (strength of moderation) and $z \cdot x \rightarrow y$ (form of moderation) and the mediation perspective seems to have the mathematical form $z \rightarrow x \rightarrow y$ , corresponding in this respect to the selection approach. Observe, however, that the mediation perspective is criterion dependent (cf. Venkatraman, 1989), whereas the criterion $y$ is often omitted in the selection approach. The matching perspective in Venkatraman (1989) reflects the mathematical form $(x < \rightarrow x) \rightarrow y$ of the interaction approach, and the gestalt, and as profile deviation types-of-fit, reflects the systems approach. The difference between fit as a gestalt and fit as profile deviation in Venkatraman (1989) lies in their criterion dependence, the former being criterion-free and the latter criterion specific. Following Drazin & Van de Ven (1985), the two cases are included in the systems approach in this paper. The final case, the covariation perspective, suggests fit as a second-order factor in a two-stage factor analysis, and is not easily interpretable in the framework of Figure 1.

(d) Type of fit

Before proceeding to the major findings of the review, let us state the constraints of the analysis.

## Constraints of the review

In order to crystallize the focus of the paper, the scope of the review is restricted with regard to the following four aspects.

1 The 'user-system fit' (cf. Markus & Robey, 1983) is excluded. The major reason for this is the fact that research into this aspect is based on a quite different intellectual tradition from the remaining three 'fits' identified by Markus & Robey (1983), i.e., the 'organization structure-system fit', 'power distribution-system fit' and 'environment-system fit'. The level of analysis in 'user-system fit' lies at the micro level (Markus & Robey, 1988), focusing on individuals as units of analysis. Consequently, the research is very heavily based on various psychological theories, whereas the other three 'fits' focus on organizational variables and are based much more broadly on organization theory (cf. Srinivasan & Kaiser, 1987). The above restriction means that a major research tradition is excluded from further consideration in this article (for reviews see Dickson et al., 1977; Huber, 1983; Markus & Robey, 1983).

2 As this paper concentrates on information systems in the sense of organization-wide information systems and constituent application information systems, it does not consider contingency analyses of the MIS/IS function, its organization, management, etc. This excludes such contingency research as Wetherbe & Whitehead (1977), Ein-Dor & Segev (1978), Olson & Chervany (1980) and Tavakolian (1989). One reason for this exclusion is that the MIS/IS function, its organization and management should not be confused with IS characteristics, as they also act as environments for IS development.

3 Related to the previous point, IS development, operations and use processes are excluded. This rules out a considerable number of articles concerned especially with organizational factors related to user participation and involvement (Edström, 1975; De Brabander & Edström, 1977; Schonberger, 1980; Mahmood et al., 1983; Franz & Robey, 1986; Tait & Vessey, 1988). This exclusion is furthermore based on the assumption that user participation is quite independent of IS characteristics.

4 Research into the organizational impact of information systems and information technology of the type x -> z, in which x denotes the information system or technology and z the organizational context/structure, and of the type z + x -> y, in which the additional term y denotes effectiveness measures, is not included for two reasons. The major reason is that neither case implies any concept of the 'organizational fit of an information system'. The former type corresponds to the technological imperative characterizing much of existing impact research (Markus & Robey, 1988), and therefore excludes a considerable IS research tradition from our consideration (for reviews, see Attewell & Rule, 1984, and Swanson, 1987). It also excludes the contingency research tradition in which the degree of automation/computerization is used as a measure of organizational technology (e.g. Blau et al., 1976; Pfeffer & Leblebici, 1977; Reiman, 1980; Carter, 1984). The latter type of impact research refers to papers in which the effects of organizational and environmental factors are analysed independently of the IS characteristics (Cheney & Dickson, 1982; Sanders & Courtney, 1985). A second reason is that much of the impact research is based on a monolithic view of automation, computerization or technology as a determinant of the impact (Kraut et al., 1989). This kind of view is quite impotent from the viewpoint of the organizational fit of an information system.

## The major findings of the review

Table 1 indicates that existing research has identified a considerable number of contextual factors and IS characteristics, about a half of the IS characteristics (14 out of 30) having been studied empirically, even though their breakdown is quite detailed. The number of contextual factors and IS characteristics has nevertheless led to considerable dispersion and a lack of cumulative tradition in existing research, especially empirical research. As Table 1 indicates, it is hard to identify two empirical papers which include at least one pair of roughly equivalent contextual factors and IS characteristics, the only clear exceptions being Ein-Dor & Segev (1982) and Ahituv et al. (1989) both of which discovered the centralization of hardware to be positively related with the centralization of organization (decision-making).

A second clear finding is the fact that the selection approach has dominated existing contingency theory research into information systems. This is so particularly in the case of empirical research. Referring to our discussion at the very beginning of this paper, the unidirectional causality of the selection approach seems too simplistic. One should recognize, however, that as none of the three types of fit can be considered improper, researchers should aim at testing the different approaches to fit in order to obtain a more complete understanding of the context-IS characteristics-effectiveness relationship (cf. Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985, p. 358).

The two findings, the lack of a cumulative tradition and the dominance of the selection approach, have two important implications. First, one can conclude that Weill & Olson's (1989) criticism of conflicting results in contingency theory research is not valid in the case of research into the organizational fit of information systems. Secondly, the results of existing research do not justify their highly critical recommendation to largely abandon contingency theory research into MIS, albeit not altogether. Consequently, this paper takes a more positive attitude towards contingency theory research, arguing that its rejection may be overhasty. The following section outlines a framework for future contingency theory research into the organizational fit of information systems based on a broader concept of fit and a more systematic research program, a framework which largely avoids the criticism of Weill & Olson.

## A FRAMEWORK FOR RESEARCH INTO THE ORGANIZATIONAL FIT OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS

## Introduction

The dispersed nature of existing contingency theory research underlines the need for a reasonably common framework in the spirit of that of lves et al., (1980). Without such a framework, it is not realistic to expect essentially greater comparability in future research than in the past. Figure 2 presents a modification of lves et al. (1980), extending it slightly to include the impacts (effectiveness) of IS development.

Figure 2. A framework for research into the situation dependence of information systems (adapted from Ives et al., 1980).

As discovered above, existing contingency theory research into information systems is dominated by the selection approach, which is based on the assumption of natural or managerial selection and neglects the effectiveness aspect. The assumption implies that the IS development process is largely omitted as a determinant of IS characteristics. Research into IS impacts concentrates on the relationship between an information system (or more generally automation/computerization/technology) and its impacts. Research into IS development and implementation addresses the relationship between the IS development process and the resultant IS characteristics. This complementary nature of the three (or four) research traditions suggests that it might be worthwhile to try to integrate them into a more comprehensive research approach which covers the chain of relationships from contextual factors to IS effectiveness (cf. Figure 2).

The operations process of the original framework is omitted as a separate column, anticipating its decreased criticality in practice because of the increased operations capacity, the higher degree of autonomy of the operations process and the reduced need for distinct secondary users who just collect, enter, control and maintain data. Table 2 is a generalization, of course, as there are a few contingency studies into information systems, for example, which address the effectiveness aspect (e.g. Daft et al., 1987). Despite the exceptions, it describes the mainstreams of the traditions.

In spite of this history, one can recognize signs of converging tendencies underlying the three research traditions. First, one can anticipate that future contingency theory research will increasingly incorporate interaction and especially systems approaches (e.g., Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Gresov, 1989) covering the effectiveness aspect, which connects it with impact research. Secondly, partly as a consequence of the systems approach, one can identify efforts in contingency theory and related research within organization theory to incorporate the design process in the chain of factors influencing the organizational structure (see Barley, 1986; Gresov, 1989, and below). Thirdly, the situation dependence of IS development is widely agreed upon (e.g. Olle et al., 1982; Davis & Olson, 1985), even though it is emphasized in the context of IS development approaches rather than IS alternatives. Fourthly, impact research has clearly recognized that 'contextual variables [...] play an important mediating role in influencing the outcome of the introduction of the new technology' (Attewell & Rule, 1984, p. 1189) and the need for more refined views of technology and the process of technological change (Kraut et al., 1989).

## Three major areas of research into the organizational fit of information systems

The integration of the three research traditions does not mean their convergence into one, as each tradition has its own interests, but rather their extension to assure better comparability. The resultant research areas are described in Table 3. The first one, called 'contingency research into information systems', aims at understanding the fit between an information system and its contexts (external environment, organizational environment, user environment, development environment and operations environment), the second one, called 'contingency research into IS impacts', aims at understanding the impact of information systems and related organizational changes in different contexts, and the third one, called 'contingency research into the IS adoption process', is interested in the relationship between different IS characteristics, the IS adoption process (development, implementation and related decision-making) and its contexts. The rest of this section introduces the three research areas in more detail, emphasizing their quite different methodological implications.

## Contingency research into information systems

It is clear that contextual factors do not determine the information system directly, but the system is the result of an IS adoption process. If the existence of uncertainty related to IS adoption is accepted, it is by no means clear that the fit between the system and its contexts can be assured by the adoption process (management selection). It is also clear that potential natural selection operates at the level of populations of organizations and only in a longer time perspective. The two arguments suggest that the selection approach to the fit may not be a reasonable assumption from the viewpoint of an individual adoption process, but that the concepts of fit based on the interaction approach or the systems approach seem more appropriate (cf. Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985).

The interaction and systems approaches were introduced earlier in this paper and will not be repeated here. Our only comment is that methodologically they are based on variance research (Rogers, 1983), in which variance discovered in deviation should explain the variance in effectiveness. $^{1}$

## Contingency research into IS impacts

Contingency research into IS impacts forms a bridge between the other two research topics in Table 3. As both the interaction approach and the systems approach take effectiveness (impact) into account in their definitions of fit, they have a close relationship with IS impact research, which is interested in changes, changes in the values of impact criteria caused by changes in an information system brought about by the IS adoption process. This implies that the time dimension is essential in impact research, suggesting longitudinal research as a natural approach.

A recent article of Kraut et al. (1989) highlights the above points. First of all, it emphasizes the importance of individual and organizational contingencies that moderate the impact of technology, extending impact research to cover contexts related to IS adoption. One can expect impact research to include at least two measurements, pre- and post-IS adoption, which can potentially be used to validate alternative substantive theories (cf. Markus & Robey, 1988) about the fit, provided that the requirements imposed by the theories of fit are taken into account. Ceteris paribus, if the IS change is towards a better fit, as defined by the theory to be tested, the post-situation should be more effective than the pre-situation. In the alternative case, the prediction is just the opposite. In order to ensure the obvious synergy, contingency research into IS impacts should not be reduced to analysis of the impact of IS adoption only, but should be interested in whether the adopting organization is better off after the adoption in terms of alternative criteria of effectiveness and in explaining the change in effectiveness in terms of alternative theories of fit.

Kraut et al. (1989) also note that any technological configuration (information system) 'is itself an historical product of social, economic, and political decisions that shape it' and emphasize the significance of reinventions, 'modifications that occur as it is implemented and used' (p. 236). These points naturally underline that technology effects are not unidirectional, based on one-way causation (Kraut et al., 1989, p. 236), but that the IS adoption process as a determinant of the information system is an important modifying factor which influences the impacts. This connects impact research with research into the IS adoption process to be discussed below.

The points raised by Kraut et al. (1989) have implications concerning research methods. Without going into details, it seems that the required research starts to resemble process research as interpreted by Rogers (1983). Because of the longitudinality of IS adoption and its impacts, time-ordered sequences of relevant events become essential. The events may concern the evolution of the information system itself or changes in its contexts either as an immediate result of the introduction of the system or as exogenous changes. This kind of process research can be quite discrete, however, which distinguishes it from contingency research into IS adoption, to be discussed next.

## Contingency research into the IS adoption process

Contingency research into the IS adoption process is interested in various aspects of IS development and implementation which influence the process, its results and efficiency in different contexts. The results cover the quality of the information system implemented, for instance, the evolution of the system and its potential rejection. Efficiency naturally concerns the amount of resources (especially human resources and time) required in the process.

In terms of Table 3, contingency research into the IS adoption process can be seen as a synthesis of research into IS development and implementation. Research into IS development has mainly concentrated on various prescriptive methods (methodologies, methods, techniques, tools, etc.) suggested to support the process, largely neglecting contextual factors related to the external environment and organizational environment affecting the process (for an exception, see Davis, 1982). Because of its prescriptive nature, it has not paid attention to more emergent (non-prescribable) aspects of the events in the IS adoption process such as the actual flow of the development process (e.g. actual activities, intertwining, iteration, etc.), actors related to the process (their actual participation, motivation, commitment, resistance, etc.), actor relationships (communication, consensus, conflicts, coalitions, etc.) which may turn out to be highly critical in the process. Research into IS implementation is conventionally divided into two major categories: implementation factor research and implementation process research (Ginzberg, 1980; Lucas, 1981; Kwon & Zmud, 1987). $^{1}$ In the case of factor research, our focus here lies especially on factors which either directly concern the IS development/implementation process (e.g. top management support and user participation) or affect the IS development/implementation process (e.g. commitment to change).

Even though one should not exclude nomothetic research methods such as laboratory experiments, field experiments, field studies, etc., from contingency research into the IS adoption process, it is clear that idiographic methods such as case studies and action research have their most natural area of application here. They make it possible to analyse the process in a real situation and to recognize emergent and unanticipated contingencies and events which influence the process. It is essential, however, for case study and action research projects to also include a systematic collection of the contextual, information system, actor, actor relationship and IS adoption process characteristics. In a favourable case, the data based on one IS adoption process may be used to test, and especially falsify, existing theories as exemplified by Markus (1983) (cf. Lee, 1989). The major idea here, however, is to try to assemble a research data base derived from a number of in-depth case studies and action research projects to allow a more nomethetic analysis later (see below).

It also seems reasonable to incorporate research into IS impacts within research into the IS adoption process based on case studies and action research. First, it is good practice in IS development to have pre- and post-evaluations which evaluate the impacts of IS development. One could expect these evaluations to be integral parts of research projects in particular, studying the IS adoption process. The question is simply one of ensuring that the evaluations satisfy the standards of IS impact research. Secondly, one can expect considerable synergy, as many contextual and IS characteristics relevant to contingency theories of IS impacts are also relevant to contingency theories of the IS adoption process.

The reasoning above leads to the relationships between the three research areas depicted in Table 3, each successive area including the previous ones as subproblems. The resultant structure is also illustrated in Figure 3. For illustrative reasons, the figure includes only pairs of contextual factors and pairs of IS characteristics. Applying the illustration technique of Van de Ven & Drazin (1985), the asterisks denote theoretical fits and the ellipses contours of decreasing effectiveness. The shapes of the ellipses describe the sensitiveness of the fit with regard to the IS characteristics.

According to the systems approach to the concept of fit, contingency research into information systems, as illustrated in the upper part of Figure 3, assumes effective IS characteristics to be interdependent, forming patterns of fit (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). The centre of Figure 3 points out that IS adoption may lead to increased effectiveness with regard to some criteria and decreased effectiveness with regard to others. $^{1}$ The indisputable attribution of the effects to IS adoption naturally requires that the contextual factors potentially affecting the effectiveness should remain reasonably constant.

As pointed out above, the lower levels may provide at least one statistical case for a more nomothetic analysis to be performed at the upper levels. Consequently, the structure rejects the view that research methods representing the quantitative-nomothetic tradition on the one hand and the qualitative-idiographic tradition on the other are opposite or alternative to each other. Contingency research into the IS adoption process in particular should consider the two traditions complementary and combine them (cf. Kaplan & Duchon, 1988). Figure 3 also points out that the upper two levels may provide the IS development process with results concerning the fit and impacts of alternative information systems in different contexts. While this feedback information, if valid, can be expected to help IS development, it also changes the phenomenon to be studied. This emphasizes that IS research itself is one of the reasons for the fact that it is dealing with a moving target.

## On the meta-theory assumptions of future research into the organizational fit of information systems

Weill & Olson (1989) criticize contingency theory research into information systems on four main points (p. 67): (a) use of a naive meta-theory assuming completely informed and rational actors, a functional paradigm emphasizing social order, equilibrium and stability, objective and measurable reality and deterministic causality, (b) conflicting empirical results of existing research, with low correlations and little explanation of variance, (c) ill-defined concepts of fit and performance, and (d) the narrow perspective of researchers when confined to the contingency approach. In consequence, they recommend 'a program of research with a less naive meta-theory than contingency theory' (p. 78) and largely, albeit not altogether, advocate the abandonment of contingency theory research into MIS (p. 79). Their most fundamental criticism concerns the meta-theory assumptions of contingency theory research in MIS. The goal set for the following discussion is to point out that the meta-theory assumptions mentioned by Weill & Olson are not inherent to research into the situation dependence and organizational fit of information systems, even though they may well characterize existing contingency theory research into information systems.

Let us start with highly objectivist approach which, according to Weill & Olson, is characteristic of contingency theory research in MIS. This approach, reflecting the objective-subjective dimension in Burrell & Morgan (1979), assumes the existence of an objective, measurable and external reality, social reality being considered to be as concrete as physical reality (Weill & Olson, 1989, pp. 67, 76). Concerning organizational structure, efforts have been made to reconcile the objectivist 'structural' perspective and the subjectivist 'interactionist' perspective. Ranson et al. (1980) present a synthetic view in which organizational structures, constituting the organizational interaction, are constituted through an interactive structuring process and are constrained by organizational, environmental characteristics such as scale of operation (size), mode of technical production (technology), organizational resources, physical environment (e.g. the geography of the area), economic environment (e.g. markets), social environment (e.g. demographic patterns) and institutional environment. These constraints connect the view of Ranson et al. (1980) with the contingency theory tradition in organization theory. Partly based on the work of Ranson et al., (1980), Barley (1986) describes an interesting case of a structuring process in two radiology departments as a sequential interplay between institutional patterns guiding action and action modifying institutions. His findings, even though indicating that one of the two departments became far more decentralized, also showed that the structure of each department changed in a similar direction of increased decentralization (Barley, 1986, pp. 101, 105). Even though Barley's work differs considerably from mainstream contingency theory research, his work on how technology (in his case CT scanners in radiology) shapes organizational structure is a good example of research into situation dependence.

Barley's work leads us to the question of determinism criticized by Weill & Olson in the case of contingency research. His results indicate that technology was not able to determine the organizational structure uniquely, even though there was consistency in the direction of impact. Gresov's paper (1989) on multiple contingencies points out that contingency theory cannot be used to make a strong prediction about organizational design in the case of conflicting contingencies. He also recognizes that conflicting effectiveness criteria lead to similar design trade-off situations, as fit with one criterion may imply misfit with another. This example of recent contingency theory research illustrates that it is proceeding towards less deterministic models.

Gresov's paper also points out that the rationality assumption in the sense of non-political decisions by management based on accurate and plentiful information (Weill & Olson, 1989, p.67) cannot be considered characteristic of recent contingency theory research. Political decisions are still more inherent to the structuring theory in Barley's work (1986).

Weill & Olson's (1989) final point in their criticism of the naive meta-theory of contingency theory research in MIS concerns the functionalist paradigm. Burrell & Morgan (1979) describe this paradigm as an objectivist approach which is oriented towards social regulation rather than radical change, the former being characterized by the status quo, social order, consensus, etc. as an opposite to radical change, structural conflict, modes of domination, etc. As was point out above, one can identify less objectivist aspects in recent contingency theory or closely related research. It is not clear that the orientation of existing contingency theory research towards social regulation rather than radical change is inherent to contingency theory, even though one must admit that its empirical orientation may bias research towards actuality associated with social regulation rather than potentiality associated with radical change (cf. Burrell & Morgan, 1979, p. 18).

## Elements of future research into the organizational fit of information systems

The three IS research traditions in Table 2 imply that there exists a considerable body of results which provide a good starting point for future research. Existing contingency theory research, as summarized in Table 1, provides a useful classification of contextual factors related to the external and organizational environment, even though there are, of course, considerable definitional problems concerned with the conceptual content and operationalization of the factors. Research into ‘user-system fit’ obviously provides a similar list for the user environment.

Existing contingency theory also provides classifications for IS characteristics. The classification in Table 1, adapted from Olerups dissertation (1982), is one alternative. A second, quite different alternative, is to derive the IS characteristics from more IS design-oriented conceptual models for information systems. One can take as an example the level of resolution of IS characteristics in Arian & Ginzberg (1985) based on conceptual models of DSSs. Their approach suggests that future research should pay more attention to the artifact known as the 'information system' and to its relevant characteristics. Without going into details, we only take as an example the distinction between the level of an organization-wide information system and an application information system, as different architectures at the former level, such as hardware and data communication architecture, information architecture and information systems architecture, can be interpreted as macro-organizational switching rules (Van den Ven & Drazin, 1985) which constrain the development of individual application systems. $^{1}$ These macro-level switching rules may be either uniform or situational (cf. Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). Some characteristics of individual application information systems are determined by or constrained by the macro-organizational rules (architectures) and some by particularistic factors. This implies that we should have a contingency theory of information systems at two levels at least: the level of macro architectures and the level of application information systems. In the first case the architectures are design variables, whereas in the latter case they are contextual factors. Table 1 indicates that IS architectures are largely omitted both as IS characteristics and as contextual factors. Leifer's paper (1988) is the clearest exception to this.

This paper is essentially based on the criticism of the selection approach, which assumes that sooner or later natural or managerial selection will lead to the organizational fit of an information system (Van de Ven & Drazin, 1985). In cases of the interaction approach, and especially the systems approach, deviations from the organizational fit may be much more persistent, and where multiple, conflicting contingencies are concerned misfits may be likely or even inevitable (Gresov, 1989). Consequently, in the case of the interaction and systems approaches, knowledge about the effective combinations of organizational and IS characteristics with regard to alternative criteria of effectiveness and about the influence of deviations from the fit upon this effectiveness, becomes crucial for the organizational fit of information systems. If the goal of IS research into the situation dependence of information systems is to provide practically relevant results which are able to support situationally flexible IS development in practice, it is important to have a reasonably integrated understanding of IS development as a form of human and social action. This view is currently quite fragmented, however.

The organizational fit of information systems should be assessed in terms of organizational effectiveness rather than in terms of such measures as user information satisfaction, which is more appropriate at the level of user-system fit. This implies several problems. First, efforts to develop a general model for organizational effectiveness have not been very successful (Cameron, 1986; Lewin & Minton, 1986). topic in detail, but the generalizability of future research obviously requires that there should be a general typology of effectiveness criteria at different levels of organizations, e.g. at the organization-wide and work-unit levels, covering the differing viewpoints of major stakeholder groups. Research into organizational assessment (e.g. Van de Ven & Ferry, 1980; Seashore et al., 1982) provides a good source here. Secondly, there is the problem of 'causality' between the information system and the effectiveness criteria. Even though this problem should not be underestimated, there are examples in which they are addressed reasonably (e.g. Kraut et al., 1989).

To recapitulate on the major points above, future research should

1 Define the major levels of and contexts for the IS development process.

2 Define the relevant characteristics of the information system to be designed at each level.

3 Define the contextual factors relevant to each level.

4 Test the validity of the different approaches to the organizational fit of information systems in the above contexts.

5 Place more emphasis on the systems approach characterized by multiple, possibly conflicting contingencies, multiple IS characteristics to be considered and multiple effectiveness criteria.

If successful, this kind of research could produce scientifically justified IS design expertise for practical use.

## SUMMARY

This paper analyses the concept of the 'organizational fit of information systems'. A detailed review of the literature indicates that existing contingency theory research into information systems is almost totally based on the selection approach, and that empirical works are largely non-comparable. Consequently, it is too early to assess the results with regard to their cumulativeness.

Taking a positive attitude towards the prospects for future contingency theory research into information systems, the paper outlines a research framework, which distinguishes three major research areas: contingency research into information systems, contingency research into IS impacts and contingency research into IS adoption process. While contingency research into information systems may be based on variance research, the latter two are more oriented toward process research, contingency research into IS impacts requiring longitudinal analysis of the impacts and contingency research into the IS adoption process favouring a rich analysis of real adoption processes. The paper proposes a sort of 'hierarchy' for the three areas, based on the idea that each lower level takes into account the research problems of the upper levels in order to provide them empirical cases for more nomothetic analysis.

## Biography

Juhani livari is an Associate Professor of Information Processing Science at the University of Oulu, where he received his M.Sc. and Ph.D. degrees. He has been a visiting scholar at the State University of New York at Binghampton and the University of Houston. Dr livari has published in journals such as Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, and Information and Software Technology, as well as in a number of conference proceedings. His research has concentrated on IS development methodologies and approaches.
