# Knowledge elicitation as an inquiring system: towards a ‘subjective’ knowledge elicitation methodology

D. West

Department of Computing Science, Paisley College, Paisley, Renfrewshire, Scotland

Abstract. Many researchers in the field of knowledge elicitation (KE) have recognized the importance of the role of 'expertise' for the development of expert systems. This recognition has led to attempts to classify knowledge 'types' and define 'generic' task structures so as to reduce the complexity of the KE process (Gammack & Young, 1985; Breuker & Wielinga, 1987; Welbank, 1987). The success of such an approach has been debatable as expertise has proved difficult to classify (Burton et al., 1987), with different researchers adopting different catagorizations. The research reported in this paper attempts to develop a broad view of 'expertise' by drawing upon Vicker's notion of 'appreciation' (1965). This, it is argued, can help to provide a useful description of the way in which expertise is gained and practised. The model suggested also takes into account the more 'subjective' aspects of human expertise (i.e. it allows for such components as intuition, personal experience and belief, bias and even prejudice). The view of expertise put forward is developed as a theoretical basis for a practical subjective approach to KE. As a result of this model, the author suggests that instead of concentrating upon the process of extraction, KE may be more usefully thought of as a process of inquiry through which, as described by Churchman (1971), knowledge, or expertise is produced. Some ideas that deal with 'inquiring systems' are examined here as an appropriate framework for the KE process.

Keywords: inquiring systems, knowledge elicitation.

## KNOWLEDGE ELICITATION FOR EXPERT SYSTEMS

Knowledge elicitation (KE), which is the process of locating and extracting 'expertise' from a human expert, is frequently referred to in the expert systems literature as being one of the most difficult aspects of expert systems development (e.g. Buchanan et al., 1969, p. 256; Welbank,

1983, p. 129; Feigenbaum, 1984, p. 39; Gammack & Young, 1985, p. 105; Kidd, 1987, p. 1; Diaper, 1989, p. 11). Methods of eliciting 'expert' knowledge have been adapted from other disciplines that have attempted to deal with this problem, such as systems analysis and cognitive psychology. This approach has resulted in the development of a 'tool box' of techniques for KE, rather than a comprehensive KE 'methodology' that has been developed upon sound practical and theoretical foundations.

Elsewhere the author has suggested that one reason for the difficulties of KE expressed in the literature may be that few KE approaches can be said to be founded upon a clear and appropriate understanding (or model) of 'expertise' (West, 1990). Most current KE approaches fail to take into account what is referred to here as the 'subjective' aspects of human expertise. For example, Feigenbaum has referred to much of expertise as being 'judgemental, experiential, uncertain — generally private to an expert' (1984, p. 47); D'Agapeyeff talks about the 'subtle', 'contradictory', 'dynamic' nature of expertise which is usually 'prejudiced as a result of practical experience' (1988, p. 150). The heuristic and intuitive nature of expertise is well recognized by researchers and commentators (Basden, 1983, p. 463; Beerel, 1987, p. 118; Burton & Shadbolt, 1987, p. 3; Cross, 1988, p. 74), as is the notion of 'tacit' knowledge (Johnson, 1983, pp. 79–80; Collins et al., 1985; Bell & Hardiman, 1989, p. 5) and the experiential quality of expertise (Gaines, 1987; Beerel, 1987). The scope of this paper forbids a review of this argument but, as a summary, it is suggested that the models of expertise inherent in most current knowledge elicitation techniques are based upon a functionalist view of expertise which is in constant with the notion of 'expertise' as a subjective phenomenon (West, 1990).

Whilst accepting the point made by Breuker & Wielinga that there is a need for a more structured and better documented approach to expert system design (Breuker & Wielinga, 1987, p. 1), it seems unlikely that such a resource as 'expertise' can be understood and represented using technology-driven models and methods. As Winograd & Flores (1986) have explained, historically, technology has developed out of a 'rationalistic' view of investigation which rests upon the underlying principle of observation of rational behaviour. However, as discussed above, a unique quality of much of human expertise is its seeming irrationality and its dependence upon subjective experience and circumstance.

Vickers illustrated the potential dangers of technology imposed upon the way that 'expertise' is practised by professionals in his story concerning his doctor's diagnosis of his case of shingles (1987, p. 150). In this example, he describes a visit to the doctor who consequently diagnosed shingles and its successful treatment. An important aspect of this example is that the doctor was able to place his diagnosis within the context of the patient's plans to travel abroad for a week of hard work. Consequently, the advice the doctor gave his patient was not only about the medical prescription but also about cancelling his work-plans as the disease was contagious and would develop into an unpleasant-looking rash which would cause embarrassment to the patient and those around him and would possibly infect his hosts and colleagues. Through this example, Vickers illustrated the way in which the influence of technology may encourage the factual reply to client consultation (i.e. the diagnosis and cure prescription that is representative of the MYCIN-type Expert Systems), without taking the wider considerations of the problem situation into account (e.g. the problems the illness would cause in a social context and the disruptions to the client's life). The point made in this example is summarized by Vickers in his Presidential address to the ISGSR in 1978, when he warned his audience of the way in which technology was being used for design purposes.

'The danger of creating a world to fit our present understanding of digital computers, is, I think, real and serious'

With the increasing use of technological design aids, such as CASE tools and automated KE techniques, this warning seems to be even more apposite today. It is not suggested that technological design aids have no role whatsoever in processes such as KE, but that it is of utmost importance that the models upon which they are based and the view they perpetuate should be clearly understood and their limitations recognized.

Vickers' example also highlights the way in which much of human expertise is practised within a specific 'context' which may be unique to each consultation, a point which has been central to the work of Collins et al. (1985). Cohn (1985) has criticized expert systems for their inability to present their advice within such a meaningful and dynamic context. In certain domains, it may be acceptable to make decisions and give advice separate from the wider context of the problem domain (e.g. in domains where the problem variables are relatively well-established, such as in fault diagnosis of circuit boards). In other domains, such as in medical diagnosis as illustrated by Vickers' anecdote, the context of the problem not only directs the advice given but also may change the nature of the problem situation itself. For example, if Vickers had been intending to use teleconferencing facilities to conduct his business abroad, he would not have been advised to cancel his work-plans on account of his illness. Expertise then, can be seen to draw upon the context, or circumstances, of the problem in order to direct its application in each new situation and to help assign appropriate and timely meaning to the advice it offers (Stowell & West, 1988).

## A SUBJECTIVE VIEW OF EXPERTISE

In the research reported here a view of 'expertise' has been sought which reflects its arguably subjective nature. The stance adopted from which to undertake this investigation has been that which Burrell & Morgan term the 'Interpretive paradigm' which is characterized by a nominalist, anti-positivist, voluntaristic and ideographic view of the 'social world' (1979, p. 28). The way in which the author's view of 'expertise' differs from that which can be recognized in the majority of KE approaches can be summarized in a passage by Burrell & Morgan who explain a fundamental difference between the objective and subjective poles which are representative of the functionalist and interpretivist paradigms. They suggest a distinction between the two to be that of:

'Whether . . . it is possible to identify and communicate the nature of knowledge as being hard, real and capable of being transmitted in tangible form, or whether knowledge is of a softer, more subjective, spiritual or even transcendental kind, based on experience and insight of a unique and essentially personal nature' (ibid. pp. 1–2).

This dilemma is one that involves a question of epistemology, that is, it deals with the need to recognize the stance that the investigator adopts in his inquiry. The former description of knowledge, the positivist stance, is that which is embodied in the 'rationalistic' tradition, the paradigm of 'science' (Winograd & Flores, 1986, pp. 16–17); the second description of knowledge, the anti-positivist stance, is advocated by those researchers whose work can be loosely categorized as 'interpretive', namely, work which adopts concepts and ideas drawing upon the phenomenological and hermeneutic traditions of social theory.

It is this second view of knowledge that is adopted here and which has led to the search for a useful way of viewing the acquisition and practise of expertise as experienced by the human expert. Similar views have been voiced by other researchers in the field of KE and in Information Systems in general. For example, Dreyfus criticized the 'scientific' approach towards artificial intelligence (AI), offering instead a phenomenological approach, a suggestion which was not well-received by the AI community (Dreyfus, 1965; see McCorduck, 1979, for a discussion of the reception Dreyfus' ideas received); Winograd & Flores have attacked the 'rationalistic' tradition of western science, maintaining its inappropriateness to computer system design and proposing the benefits of an approach based upon 'biology, hermeneutics and phenomenology' (1986, p. xii); Gregory has criticized attempts to develop expert systems from within the 'realist' tradition of western thinking, suggesting instead an 'idealist' approach to expert system development (1986). Likewise, Bell & Hardiman have advocated the adoption of a 'naturalistic' approach to knowledge engineering, a stance which can be placed within the interpretive paradigm (1989), and Gill (1990), has proposed a 'dialogical framework' as an approach to KE that draws upon ethnography, an approach also drawing upon concepts assigned by Burrell & Morgan to the interpretive paradigm.

However, in spite of the growing awareness of the potential benefits of what here are loosely described as 'interpretive' approaches, a difficulty seems to exist in the translation of theory into practice. A common result is that the approach remains a theoretical construct or else when an attempt is made to operationalize the ideas, the authors continue to develop and use 'functionalist' techniques. In the rest of this paper suggestions are made as to how a practical 'interpretive' approach to KE may be achieved.

## Vickers' notion of 'appreciation' and an 'appreciative system'

The author has investigated Vickers' notion of an 'appreciative system' (1965), which, it is suggested, shares the characteristics of the interpretive paradigm, as a possible and appropriate view of the way in which a human expert becomes an expert and practises 'expertise' (West, 1990).

Vickers described ‘appreciation’ as a process in which judgements are made by the appreciator about the perceived state of the system with both its internal and external relations. These judgements, which he calls ‘reality judgements’, may be based upon the state of the system past, present and future. A second aspect of ‘appreciation’ is judgement concerning the significance of the perceived ‘reality’ of the state to the appreciator. Vickers called these judgements ‘value judgements’. For Vickers, reality judgements and value judgements’ are inseparable components of appreciation. He explains:

'The relation between judgements of fact and of value is close and mutual; for facts are relevant only in relation to some judgement of value and judgements of value are operative only in relation to some configuration of fact' (1965, p. 40).

'Appreciation' then, is dependent upon the appreciator's perception of reality and the value that the appreciator assigns to this reality. (The description of reality and value judgements above also serves to emphasize the significance of 'context' to the notion of 'appreciation'.) 'Appreciation' therefore, does not deal with absolute rights and wrongs, only with situations which are more, or less, acceptable to the appreciator. Similarly, becoming an 'expert' is argued here to be a subjective and personal process.

Checkland & Casar have used Vickers' writings on 'appreciation' to develop a diagrammatic representation of an 'appreciative system' which is reproduced here to help illustrate the dynamic process of 'appreciation' as a description of 'the actual social process which characterizes human communication and action' (1986).

Checkland & Casar's model of 'appreciation' can also provide a useful illustration of the way in which a human expert gains 'factual' knowledge about a particular area of interest (e.g. through instruction and demonstration), and begins to build up a picture of the domain. By interacting with the domain the individual refines and develops this picture, gaining an increasing 'appreciation' of the domain, which in turn, aids and directs further interaction in the field of interest. This 'appreciation' may be developed upon a wide range of activities such as past experience, personal preferences, intuition, rules-of-thumb and what may be considered to be bias and even prejudice. Perhaps the most important contribution that Vickers' notion of appreciation has to offer to understanding expertise is a description of a continuous and largely unconscious process of learning. This learning-based activity contrasts with the models of expertise to be found in the KE literature (West, 1990; Slatter, 1990) which tend to present a rational schema for the acquisition and practice of 'expertise' (Gaines, 1987; Greenwell, 1988, pp. 15–23).

## The practical contribution of Vickers' 'appreciative system' to KE

The aim of the research currently being undertaken by the author reported here is to attempt the identification of possible key elements of a methodology to alleviate some of the problems associated with KE (Stowell & West, 1990, pp. 330–331; D. West, 1991). The research so far has helped highlight the importance of understanding the concept of 'expertise' in order to develop appropriate tools and techniques to aid the knowledge elicitor during KE. If, as suggested in this paper, we accept the association of the processes of 'appreciation' and gaining and practising 'expertise', can Vickers' ideas of an 'appreciative system' make any practical contribution to the process of KE? A possible way forward here may be the application of other systems ideas which can be said to operationalize Vickers' thinking about 'appreciation'.

Vickers' notion of an ‘appreciative system’ can be described as a meta-level model of the process of ‘inquiry’ as it is discussed by Churchman (1971). It offers an explanation of the way in which an individual (or group) can be said to take part in ‘social reality’, learn from this participation, which in turn directs their further participation as illustrated below:

This circular process is also reflected in the theory-practice cycle that is action research (Lewin, 1948; Trist & Bamforth, 1951; Susman & Evered, 1978), and in the way that Checkland illustrates the use of SSM as an inquiring methodology (1989). In addition this process is reflected in the hermeneutic circle (Dilthey, 1961), thus emphasizing the 'interpretive' nature of both Vickers' notion of an 'appreciative system' and SSM.

The space available here does not permit a detailed description of the relationship between Vickers' 'appreciative system' and SSM. However, Checkland summarizes this relationship when he describes SSM as a 'Singerian inquiring system whose mode of operation provides a formal means of initiating and consciously reflecting upon the social process of 'appreciation' (1981, p. 264). For these reasons, the intellectual traditions underlying Vickers' notion of an 'appreciative system' and Checkland's SSM are proposed as a framework for thinking about KE. In practice, elements of SSM, together with other systems ideas, were adopted as a potential KE approach which could address the subjective nature of human 'expertise'.

## THE APPLICATION OF KE AS AN INQUIRING SYSTEM

A study to explore the use of ideas developed during the research took place within a large UK business enterprise. The Personnel department of this business enterprise possessed an expert system which provided a useful environment within which to work. The problem domain identified for investigation was that of 'management training', an area that could be usefully incorporated into the existing expert system to give advice on career and personal development, training, and succession planning.

The elicitation process took place in three stages over a period of 10 months. This time delay was largely due to the pressures upon the experts' time, a common factor during expert system projects. Over all, 11 different experts were used from a wide range of departments, each one undertaking the same KE process. At the end of each elicitation session, the experts were asked to comment upon their experience of the KE approach used by means of a pre-prepared questionnaire. The comments from these completed questionnaires, together with the observations of the elicitor and the results of the exercise, have enabled a number of useful lessons to be learnt about the notion of subjective KE and the particular KE approach adopted.

In the interest of clarity, in the following description of the practical application of a subjective KE approach, the pronoun 'he' is used to refer to the 'expert' whilst 'she' is reserved for reference to the 'knowledge elicitor'.

## Stage I

At the first stage of the elicitation process each expert, interviewed separately, was requested to produce a 'systems map' of the elements he felt to be relevant to the central, broad problem of 'management training'. The elicitor took care to influence the process as little as possible, allowing the expert to express his thoughts about the domain with the only constraint being the 'bubbles' used in the map. After producing the map the expert was asked to explain the map to the elicitor which allowed the elicitor to ask questions and encourage the expert to explain, qualify and expand on his comments. During the discussion it was common for the expert to make alterations to his map, indicating the process of learning, teaching and clarifying that the approach supported. The pictorial approach was well-received by all the experts and the general comment was that whilst the approach restricted the information they gave to a relatively low level of detail, it encouraged a wide view of the domain. This was deemed to be an advantage at this early stage of domain analysis as a problem reported of many KE approaches is that they produce a narrow mass of highly detailed information which is difficult to structure (Welbank, 1987, p. 14). The experts felt the 'map' helped them focus their attention upon the domain, describing the exercise as a way of achieving a 'panoramic', or 'birds-eye' view of the domain, and were pleased at the simplicity of the task and the quick results (10–20 min on average for a 'good' map). The maps produced by the experts illustrated not only areas directly relevant to the domain but also indicated possible sub-systems and environmental influences. ## Stage II

A comparison by the elicitor of the ‘maps’ produced by the different experts highlighted ‘common areas’ of interest as well as areas particular to individual experts. In order to provide as wide a view of the domain as possible and to draw upon all comments provided, the elicitor developed a ‘composite’ map which included representations of all the different map elements provided by the experts. The composite map was further divided into three to represent areas that had been referred to by various experts and which, at the time, best represented the elicitor’s understanding of the domain after the first KE stage. These ‘new’ maps (referred to as ‘sub-maps’) were then taken back to each expert in turn and used as the basis for discussion with the elicitor explaining her understanding and the expert agreeing with or ‘correcting’ the view expressed. The maps were edited/re-drawn to reflect the new level of understanding reached by the elicitor and expert. Although this approach offers the potential to undertake group discussion, this tactic was decided against as it was felt that the more vociferous experts or those occupying the more senior posts may have dominated the session and hence the view of the domain elicited.

The discussion engendered from the maps were seen as a useful way of clarifying ideas and, if needed, provided the elicitor with a range of questions about the elements defined by the expert, as opposed to either, thinking 'on the spot', or devising questions before the session. The experts were then asked to describe each map element in more detail and in order to guide them in this task the elicitor used the CATWOE elements from SSM to initiate questions about each element (Smyth & Checkland, 1976). Although this process was led by the elicitor, the questions derived from the CATWOE elements were of sufficient abstraction to avoid any pre-conceived answers and forced the expert to view the element described as a process of activity, or 'system', which could be explored as a whole, rather than as a single point about the domain. The information acquired was used by the elicitor to construct a Root Definition (RD), for each 'map' element. The process of building RDs is difficult and time-consuming and so was undertaken by the elicitor away from the interview session. This allowed the elicitor to take time developing a RD which best seemed to capture her understanding of the expert's description of the domain. ## Stage III

A conceptual model (CM), was developed by the elicitor from each RD which was then used as an agenda for discussion with the expert (see Figure 6). Although the RD and associated CM were constructed by the elicitor, it is important to emphasize that they were developed from the elicitor's understanding of the expert's view of the domain. When used as an agenda, the elicitor was careful to explain that the CM was only a logical model of what needed to take place in order to achieve the process defined in the corresponding map element. The conceptual models proved to be of great use in that they provided a framework within which to investigate the different areas of concern but did not seem to impose their structure upon the elicitation process. They allowed the expert to compare his view of the domain with the elicitor's developing understanding of the domain which provided a valuable learning process for both expert and elicitor. The CMs were also used to build up a wider picture of the problem domain represented in the original map as the inputs and outputs to the different CMs indicated the relationship between the different map elements identified by the experts (i.e. the output of one CM became the input to another CM). Figure 6 illustrates the CM developed from the RD in Figure 5. The agenda for discussion was formed from the different elements of the CM and resulted in questions such as:

In what form do senior managers receive Directorate policies about management training for Unit heads?

Is there any mechanism in placing which enables you to monitor how well the policies are being implemented through training?

What effect do the constraints of the unit head's working environment have on training?

It was found that in many cases such questions were not necessary, as, when looking at the CM, the experts talked voluntarily about the different elements of the model, describing the way that the activity was either manifested in their approach to training or was unaddressed by their current approach.

An interesting result from the study was that not only 'functional' (or primary task) descriptions of the problem area were elicited. Descriptions of what several experts' referred to as 'heretical' issues were also identified, modelled conceptually, and their implications discussed. A further observation from the study was that managers who were responsible for their staff training often had different views of 'management training' to the Personnel staff who had previously been responsible for the development of the expert system.

The approach used seemed to encourage thorough domain analysis and definition, a process which has been argued to be lacking in current KE techniques (Stowell & West, 1990). Further iterations of the CATWOE-RD-CM-agenda cycle indicated that high-level detail, appropriate for development in a form suitable for an expert system knowledge-base could be obtained by use of the approach developed during this research. Of particular interest was the way in which the approach could be used to explore even those areas which are less easy to articulate (e.g. ideas pertaining to the 'culture' and sought-after 'image' of the host organization and how these influenced management training). In such cases it may not be necessary to convert this information into a rule-based format for inclusion within the expert system but it does provide an invaluable insight into the environment within which the domain of expertise exists and is practised.

The iteration between the different stages of the KE approach used and the feedback generated as a result of the different activities provided a means of inquiring and learning about the domain within a structured framework which did not in itself determine the results of the learning process. Nor was the opportunity to learn restricted to the elicitor: for example, as a consequence of the KE process one expert within Personnel revised his thinking about the problems of management training, resulting in him publishing his 'new' ideas and instituting further training considerations within the organization.

## SUMMARY

In this paper the view has been put forward that Vickers' notion of an 'appreciative system' offers a basis for a model of 'becoming expert' and 'practising expertise' that may be of benefit to the KE researcher and practitioner. From an exploration of this model it has been suggested that KE may be usefully thought of as an ‘inquiring system’, so as to redirect the emphasis placed on the process of extraction (as is traditionally the case with current KE techniques), towards that of inquiry. In this way, an increased understanding about the domain of expertise is encouraged within which the task of knowledge extraction and formalization may be more easily achieved.

A possible way of rendering operational a meta-level process of inquiry suitable for KE is through the application of systems ideas and, in particular, those ideas which relate to 'inquiring systems' (Churchman, 1971; Checkland, 1981). Finally, a brief outline of a systems-based approach to KE, developed as a means of operationalizing the idea of KE as an inquiring system, has been given and illustrated using examples taken from a KE study conducted within a large UK business organization. The concepts and practice reported here are now being explored further with specific reference to the use of hypermedia technology (Hopkins, 1991).

## Biography

Daune West is a Lecturer in the Department of Computing Science at Paisley College. Her PhD from Portsmouth Polytechnic was in Systems and Information Systems Development. Her main research interest lies in the use of 'systems' ideas in the design of information systems and, in particular, their use during the process of knowledge elicitation for the development of expert systems.
