# Evaluation of EIS from a managerial perspective $^{1}$

F. Bergeron and L. Raymond\*

Département systèmes d'information organisationnels, Faculté des sciences, de l'administration, Université Laval, Québec, and \*Departement d'administration et d'économique, Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières, Québec, Canada

Abstract. While executive information systems (EIS) constitute a rising trend in the use of information technology in organizations, much is still unknown in terms of the attributes an EIS should possess in order to increase the probability of successful implementation. A survey was conducted on 28 Canadian organizations, with 52 users giving their perceptions on the various attributes of EIS. Results indicate that the more important aspects relate to the quality of information and the benefits provided by EIS. Less important are the user-interface and technical capabilities. User satisfaction with the attributes of their present system was also measured. In this regard, managers were more content with the quality of information and the user-interface than with the benefits and the technical capabilities of their EIS. Overall, top-managers were less satisfied with the present system than lower-level managers.

Keywords: attributes, executive information systems, executive support systems, satisfaction.

## INTRODUCTION

Executive information systems (EIS) are among the most critical applications of the new information technology(Straub & Wetherbe, 1989). An increasing number of organizations are implementing this technology; indeed, more than 25% of the 500 largest US corporations have done so between 1984 and 1988 (Coffey, 1988). One of the first EIS reported in the literature is the one developed at the end of the 1970s by Lockheed-Georgia, a division of Lockheed Corporation (Houdeshel & Watson, 1987). In 1988, Rockart & De Long (1988) surveyed and studied 30 US organizations making use of an EIS. More than half of these systems were actual successes, others showed mixed results and some were complete failures. Although Rockart & De Long's study is certainly a major contribution to the field, much is still unknown in terms of the characteristics an EIS should possess in order to increase the chances of successful implementation and usage (Friend, 1986).

The first area of uncertainty is whether the object of EIS is to provide easy access to explicit details about the organization or to offer a richer insight into the key performance indicators (Preedy, 1990). The second area concerns the target of EIS in terms of whether it is the individual Chief Executive Officer (CEO), the top-management team or all managers. In this regard, Rockart (1990) notes that EIS has, in fact, evolved in many organizations from a system designed for one specific individual, to a group support system, and finally an organizational support system accessible to all managers and staff personnel. Another related concern is whether EIS needs to be integrated with other types of managerial support system including traditional DSS and expert systems (Turban & Watson, 1989). It is also noted that EIS technology can be implemented, not only by large firms but also by small businesses with limited resources (O'Shea, 1989). In the latter case, in-house development using fourth generation tools or acquisition of a cut-down version of a full-featured EIS product have been proposed.

The newness of the field is reflected in the various points-of-view concerning EIS uses and users. While some argue that EIS are simply high-performance computer-based systems designed to assist executives in dealing with massive amounts of data (Jones & King, 1989), others provide more information on their characteristics. Garelick (1987) suggested that an EIS should focus on providing relevant information for a non-technical user, preferably in an environment where reports, charts, text and data can be drawn from a very wide variety of internal and external sources. An EIS should also be tailored to user needs (Duffy, 1989). Indeed, it is important that these systems be designed with the executive group in mind and include access to internal and external information, selected specifically to suit the unique requirements of the user group. These systems may include electronic mail, text processing and other desktop tools such as calendars and reminder systems (Watson et al., 1991). Moreover, Rockart & De Long (1988) noted that an executive support system can be implemented at the corporate or divisional level.

As the field of EIS is evolving rapidly and it is not, as yet, clear where its boundaries lie, the definition of EIS retained for this research encompasses most of the elements found above. The definition of EIS proposed in this study is as follows:

An executive information system (EIS) is an information system supported by a mainframe or personal computer, used for various business functions on a current basis. The users are either the CEO or a member of the senior management team reporting directly to him or her. The system is designed specifically for them. It can support various communication, planning or control functions, and provide access to relevant information of a general nature. The executive information system can be implemented at the corporate or divisional level of the organization.

Given the preceding characteristics, this research aims at acquiring added knowledge about the attributes of EIS and their use. More specifically, an empirical study was designed to determine the present characteristics of EIS, as perceived by the executives who use these systems. Another objective was to identify areas of potential improvement by having users determine what for them would be the important attributes of an ideal EIS.

## Characteristics of EIS

The literature is replete with suggestions concerning the essential characteristics of EIS. These suggestions are usually drawn from case studies and personal experiences (e.g. Rinaldi & Jastrzembski, 1986; Houdeshel & Watson, 1987), and rarely on a more systematic study of EISs (e.g. Rockart & De Long, 1988). An inductive approach can be used to infer four essential EIS characteristics: quality of information, user-interface, benefits and technical capability (see Table 1).

The first characteristic relates to the quality of the information output of an EIS. This includes such criteria as timeliness (EDP Analyzer, 1987; Zimmerman, 1989)], validity, relevancy, flexibility, accuracy and completeness of the information (e.g., Rinaldi & Jastrzembski, 1986; Garelick, 1987; Main, 1989; Wilkinson, 1989).

The second characteristic pertains to the interface by which the user interacts with an EIS. In this regard, top executives have even more need for easy access and user-friendliness than other categories of users (Coffey, 1988). This would include characteristics such as minimal keyboard use (Goldstein, 1988). The interface must also provide for a short response time and secure and reliable access (Giordanella, 1989). In addition, the access to data should be adequately protected, the EIS should be accessible at home and provide for a hard-copy capability (EDP Analyzer, 1987; Jordan, 1988; Wallis, 1989).

Many characteristics refer to the tangible and intangible benefits provided by an EIS in terms of support for managerial activities. This includes support for top-management's planning, tracking, and communicating functions (Rockart & De Long, 1988; Duffy, 1989; Friend, 1989). It also relates to increases in productivity, quality of problem solving, decision-making, and the attainment of organizational objectives or competitive advantage (Madlin, 1986; Goldstein, 1988; Giordanella, 1989).

The fourth type of attribute is technical in nature. It refers to the tool characteristics. The EIS should provide detailed and hierarchical aggregation of information (drill-down), and allow for different formats of presentation of data such as graphics, histograms, textual, and a combination of both on the same report (Jordan, 1988; Moad, 1988; The Cooperative Newsletter, 1989; Pilot Executive Software, 1989). It should also generate exception reports when needed, highlight problematic areas (traffic lighting), support ad hoc analysis, and use historical as well as actual data to trace trends and forecasts (Owen, 1986; Rockart & De Lang, 1988; Welter, 1988). It usually makes a wide use of ratios and variances (Reid, 1988; Friend, 1989).

In summary, a relatively large number of characteristics have repeatedly been suggested by practitioners and researchers. However, no study has systematically assessed these attributes or the actual situation with regard to these attributes for in-use EIS. It is important to gather this information in order to obtain a better understanding of the field and to set priorities in terms of EIS improvements.

## Research questions

From the preceding arguments, the first research question pertains to the importance of EIS characteristics as perceived by users. EIS characteristics suggested as important in the reviewed literature have not been the object of empirical verification. The major exception to this assertion is Rockart & De Long's study which focused on EIS implementation. However, these researchers did not look at EIS design features, and additional knowledge as to what is really important to users is still needed if we are to improve the design of present and future executive systems.

The second research question regards the satisfaction of EIS users with the present attributes of their systems. The literature does not provide us with a clear picture of the actual characteristics of in-use EIS. One must differentiate executive wishes and vendor promises from reality. In other words, there could be either a demand for certain EIS attributes emanating from top-managers or an offering of various EIS features from vendors of commercial software. For instance, are users satisfied with the quality of information and benefits provided by present-day EIS? In addition to obtaining an evaluation of these systems, this will allow the identification of differences between existing EIS attributes and those deemed to be most important.

The third research question refers to the fact that EIS has changed from supporting only the chief-executive, to supporting systems available to, and used by, top and middle-management (Rockart, 1990). Differences can be expected, therefore, between top-managers and other managers concerning their satisfaction with EIS and the importance they attribute to various EIS features.

## Methodology

Questionnaires were mailed to 900 organizations listed in the directory of the Canadian Information Processing Society, asking them if they had implemented an EIS (using the definition given above); 153 questionnaires were returned giving a response rate of 17%. Of these, only 28 organizations, half from the public and half from the private sector, had implemented an EIS. From these organizations, 52 questionnaires were received from EIS users and used for data analysis (a sample user questionnaire is shown in Appendix 1). Descriptive data on responding organizations and users are presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4. Looking at Table 4, one can note that all EIS functions are not implemented. When functions other than the basic retrieval of preformatted information are made available, they are, in fact, used by managers. These include the re-formatting of information to tables and graphs, ad-hoc queries and report generation, and electronic mail.

As to the origin of the systems, 16 out of the total of 28 were developed in-house, while the others were commercial EIS products: Commander EIS by Comshare, Inc. (8), Command Center by Pilot Executive Software, Inc. (2), Executive Edge by Execucom Systems Corp. (1) and Media by Info Innov, Inc. (1). A product such as Commander EIS (Comshare, 1990) includes a variety of features such as an electronic briefing book (report or chart information), a reminder capability (electronic calendar), electronic mail, dynamic investigation (through free-form data exploration) and access to external data bases (newswires).

The questionnaire gathered data related to the organization, the information system department and the EIS. It aimed at measuring the executives' satisfaction with their EIS and the level of importance executives attribute to EIS characteristics. Both measures were based on the same list of EIS characteristics (Table 1) and each attribute was rated on a five-point Likert type scale. The anchors of the satisfaction scale varied from very unsatisfied (1) to very satisfied (5), while they varied from very unimportant (1) to very important (5) for the importance scale.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

As mentioned previously, the various EIS characteristics are subdivided into four types for analysis purposes. These are characteristics pertaining to the quality of information, the user-interface, the benefits and the technical capabilities of the system. For each respondent, a value was obtained for the importance of each type by averaging the importance of its component characteristics (Table 1). Another value was obtained in a similar fashion for the user's satisfaction with his actual EIS. Overall values for both importance and satisfaction were also obtained by averaging all 41 characteristics. A measure of the internal consistency of related scales, Cronbach's alpha, provided evidence for the reliability of the classification scheme proposed in this study, both for the importance and satisfaction scales, as shown in Table 5.

## Importance of EIS characteristics

The four types of EIS characteristics were ordered by decreasing importance on the basis of their mean values, as perceived by the sampled users. Paired-sample t-tests (two-tailed) were then used to determine if the respondents felt that certain types of EIS attributes are significantly more important than others. The results presented in Table 6 show that there is, in fact, a significant order of importance in the attributes that a successful EIS should possess. The most important attributes relate to the quality of the EIS's output information. Next come the benefits associated with the use of such a system. Finally, less important on an equal basis are the user-interface and the system's technical capabilities.

Analysing the answers to the first research question, these results can be interpreted as indicating that the respondents, top-level managers, view an EIS first and foremost as a system developed to satisfy their own specific information needs. From such a perspective, executives have unique requirements (Rockart, 1979; Bergeron & Bégin, 1988) especially in terms of timeliness (for decisions made under pressure), completeness (internal information across all functional areas plus external information), relevancy (to their critical success factors) and validity (due to the strategic nature of decisions). From such a perspective, success would primarily be achieved by an EIS if it provides the executive with better information inputs for his or her decision-making.

An EIS is next viewed in terms of the benefits it provides to users and to their organizations in support of executive work. This support should not only include functions such as planning, tracking, controlling and communicating, but also the unique roles played by top-managers (McLeod & Jones, 1986), i.e. as disturbance handlers (anticipating and resolving problem situations) and entrepreneurs (making strategic improvements for competitive advantage). Such support should result in individual and organizational gains, both in terms of efficiency (greater productivity of executives) and effectiveness (improved decision-making process and attainment of goals).

The other two types of EIS attributes are felt to be significantly less important by the respondents. This is not surprising in view of the fact that the user-interface and technical capabilities of the system are basically a means to an end, i.e. the satisfaction of top-management's unique information needs and the support of its specific functions and roles. Attributes of the interface, such as fast response times and user-friendliness, would not be valued in themselves but only insofar as they lead to better (e.g. more timely) information or allow the executive to be more productive in his work. The same can be said of an EIS's technical capabilities; for instance, 'traffic lighting' and 'drill-down' capabilities are oriented toward the executive's tracking function and disturbance handling role. These results thus indicate the presence of a hierarchy of user perspectives in evaluating EIS attributes, namely an informational perspective (system outputs) which is the most important, a functional perspective (system benefits), and a technical perspective (interface and system capabilities) which is the least important.

Results pertaining to the importance aspect of the third research question are presented in Table 6. No significant difference was found between top-managers and other managers with regard to the importance placed by them on various EIS characteristics. It would seem that both groups would have the same expectations regarding the attributes of an ideal EIS. These results confirm the emerging view of an ideal EIS as being able to support managers at all levels of the organization rather than only executives (Rockart, 1990).

## Satisfaction with EIS characteristics

In answer to the second research question, paired-sample t-tests were also employed to compare the respondents' evaluation of the actual characteristics of their EIS. Table 7 shows that the sampled executives were significantly more satisfied with the quality of information and user-interface attributes than with the benefits and technical capabilities of their systems. One must first note here the two pairings in terms of ends and means. It seems that users evaluating their present EIS do not separate the quality of system outputs, which is the desired end, from the interfacing process, i.e. from the means by which these outputs are obtained. In the same manner, they do not separate the functional or role support (benefits) presently provided by executive systems from the technical capabilities which allow for this support. Whereas it seems logical for users to consider system ends to be more important than means in the ideal case, it also seems realistic for them not to dissociate an end from its mean when expressing their satisfaction toward their present system.

The respondents were, in fact, significantly more satisfied with the quality of information (and the associated user-interface) than with the benefits (and the associated technical capabilities) provided by their present EIS. This is congruent with the type of analysis capabilities included in most executive systems to date, which are more oriented toward reporting and displaying rather than diagnosing and explaining, with data and relationships addressing a known present or past rather than an unknown future (Turban, 1990). Typical of commercially available and widely used systems of this nature would be a product such as Command Center. In this sense, most EISs deliver information rather than 'intelligence', lacking the decision support (DSS) and knowledge-based capabilities required to make them into true executive support systems (Rockart & De Long, 1988). Note however, that some products such as Executive Edge have added modelling and expert system (ES) capabilities, thus providing added potential benefits while serving as a basis for eventual EIS/DSS/ES integration.

As to the satisfaction aspect of the third research question, results presented in Table 7 indicate significant differences between top-managers and other managers. The former group were less satisfied overall with their present EIS, specifically with the benefits provided and the technical capabilities of their system. This could be explained by the specific needs of top-managers in terms of organizational impacts (e.g. attainment of organizational objectives, or of a competitive advantage), and by the specific managerial roles of these individuals which must be supported (e.g. anticipating problem situations, planning). Given their higher expectancy level in regard to EIS, one can understand why top-managers view present-day systems less satisfactorily. The same can be said of the technical capabilities required to provide expected benefits (e.g. access to aggregate information, forecasting, ad-hoc analysis). The technical limitations of present EIS implementations, be they commercially packaged or developed in-house, are thus felt more acutely by top executives than by other users. Overall, these results indicate that even though both groups rate the importance of EIS attributes at the same level in the ideal case, this is borne out differently in reality.

Finally, when looking at the mean values in Table 7 and comparing them to the corresponding value in Tables 6, one can see that there is a significant gap between the high importance accorded to EIS by the users and their mitigated satisfaction with these systems at present; this is true overall and for each type of EIS attribute (as confirmed by paired-sample t-tests, P<0.001 in all five cases). This is also true of the extent to which the importance and satisfaction scales are comparable. Nonetheless, given the present level of satisfaction, there is room for improvement in all aspects of the design and implementation of EIS. For instance, the quality of information does not seem to be as high as it should be, given the critical and strategic nature of executive decision-making. In this regard, experience has shown that the quality of an EIS's information output is strongly related to the quality of the underlying data architecture of the transaction processing systems from which this output is generated (Armstrong, 1991). Thus, in many cases the organizational data bases are not adequately integrated and maintained, the quality of EIS-output information suffers.

## CONCLUSION

This study has contributed to increasing our knowledge of EIS, by empirically and systematically indentifying the characteristics which executive users think such systems should possess in order to be implemented successfully. By order of importance, these attributes pertain to the quality of information, the benefits provided by the system, the user-interface and the system's technical capabilities. A second contribution of the same nature relates to the characteristics presently exhibited by executive systems in organizations. In this regard, executives were found to be more satisfied with the quality of information and user-interface than with the benefits and technical capabilities of their EIS.

More research needs to be done, however, particularly on the actual usage-behaviour of top-managers in relation to EIS, if we are to help these individuals and their organizations to achieve the full potential of this rapidly emerging technology. IS researchers should also attempt to ascertain if the important characteristics of EIS found in this study are mediated by situational contingencies and methods of implementation. Practitioners who are implementing EIS in their organizations and vendors who are building state-of-the-art systems should take into account the evaluations made by executives surveyed here. They should design their products to meet actual user expectations relative to information quality and specific benefits in addition to the more commercially attractive user-interface and technical capabilities.

This study aims to find out your opinion on various characteristics associated with Executive Information Systems (EIS). More precisely, we are interested in finding out your level of satisfaction with the EIS you are presently using, and your evaluation of the characteristics which an ideal EIS should possess. The results of this study will contribute to the evolution and development of systems designed specifically for top-management.

As an EIS user, your participation is essential to this research. We would thus greatly appreciate it, if you could fill out the enclosed short questionnaire. This questionnaire is in three parts. The first part allows us to obtain your perceptions regarding various characteristics of your present EIS, versus an ideal EIS. The second part asks for information on your position in the organization. Finally, the third part aims to measure your level of EIS usage.

This questionnaire can be answered by any individual who uses an EIS in the normal course of his work. For the purposes of the study, an EIS is defined as follows:

Information system supported by a mainframe or a personal computer, used for various business functions on a current basis. The users are either the CEO or a member of the senior management team reporting directly to him or her. The system is designed specifically for them. It can support various communication, planning or control functions, and provide access to relevant information of a general nature. The Executive Information System can be implemented at the corporate or divisional level of the organization.
