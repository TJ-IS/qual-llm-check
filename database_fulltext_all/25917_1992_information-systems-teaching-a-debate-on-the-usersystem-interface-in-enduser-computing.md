# Information systems teaching: a debate on the user-system interface in end-user computing

S. Smithson and R. Hirschheim\*

London School of Economics, London, England and \*University of Houston, Houston, Texas, USA

Abstract. Advances in educational technology have had little impact on the lecturing process, a process on which university-level information systems education depends. This paper argues for the use of 'playlets', in the form of scripted dialogues that present the material in a more interesting and easily interpreted fashion. An example of a playlet is given which argues for a broader perspective on the user-system interface. Experience of using such playlets is discussed and appropriate lessons drawn.

Keywords: end-user computing, information systems education, role-playing, user-system interface.

## INTRODUCTION

Over the last 5 years, there have been considerable advances in educational technology including hardware and software to support lecture presentations and various computer-aided learning techniques. However, the mainstay of university teaching remains the traditional lecture where one teacher talks, almost without interruption, for an hour or two whilst large numbers of students make copious notes. This is often not the most interesting way of learning; student participation and feedback are minimal and there is no question of tailoring the material or pace to match the needs of individual students. Few teachers have sufficient charisma or skill to produce stimulating lectures on a daily basis throughout a long term. Similarly, the responsiveness of students is likely to decline as the term progresses and the hours of lectures accumulate. On the other hand, it is highly cost-efficient in terms of the lecturer's time, both parties usually develop a certain tolerance and the process continues with little complaint and limited enthusiasm. Where teaching involves only small groups of students, lectures can be made more interactive but this is rarely successful with class sizes of more than 50.

Information technology can be used to improve the visual display of information during a lecture through the use of presentation software to create sequences of screen displays that Correspondence: Dr Steve Smithson, Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A, UK.

replace traditional overhead transparencies. Alternatively, the transparencies can be made more readable through the use of desktop publishing software. However, these are relatively cosmetic changes: improvements in technology have done little to change the basic nature of the lecturing process. Similar problems can be seen in academic conferences where a large, highly sophisticated audience is expected to absorb many hours of unabated lecturing.

Within the teaching of information systems, lectures are particularly important. The multidisciplinary nature of information systems means that teachers must integrate material from computer science, organizational behaviour, psychology and various other disciplines with limited support from textbooks. Furthermore, the practical aspects of the subject demand a 'real-world' orientation to teaching through the use of case studies and examples. This integration and orientation is normally achieved through the medium of lectures. These demands on lecturing become more marked in the later stages of university education: in the final year of a first degree and in taught Masters courses. Then, the material typically becomes more diverse and simultaneously closer to the practical application of information systems thinking in business.

Despite the reliance on lectures, there are a number of different theories of learning, each of which suggests particular teaching methods. Entwistle & Hounsell (1975) placed these theories and their associated methods along a continuum. At one end is the behaviourist position, which advocates the rigid control of content and method and the measurement of outcomes against predetermined learning objectives. Programmed learning and computer-assisted instruction are associated with this extreme. At the other extreme is the humanist position, which emphasizes exploration, freedom and self-expression and views evaluation as retrospective and subjective. Co-operative projects and leaderless discussion groups are typical of humanist teaching methods. In between these extremes can be found the developmental, cognitive and information processing theories of learning. Individual projects and tutor-led discussions typify the developmental school and some commentators (for example see Lee, 1987) argue that projects deserve to form the dominant components of the curriculum.

In an effort to move towards a more humanist position and thus overcome some of the inherent deficiencies of lecturing, we have developed a number of semi-theatrical playlets (or role-plays). These scripted dialogues are acted out by two of more teachers, with the objective of improving the presentation of information to large audiences. They usually take 20–40 minutes and, to avoid the problem of taking notes, students are given copies of the script after the session. We believe that they deliver the required information in a more interesting and stimulating manner. By acting out realistic situations, we can achieve the practical orientation demanded for information systems teaching, as well as breaking down the barriers between individual academic disciplines. The notion of story-telling should help students to interpret and understand the material within the proposed context.

We regularly use these dialogues in teaching Masters students and have also used them to present papers at two conferences, one of which has already been published (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1987). The playlet presented below was used to deliver a paper at a conference in Cornell University in 1989 (Smithson & Hirschheim, 1990). The main message behind this playlet is the need to take a broader view of the user-system interface and the difficulties involved in such an approach. The sub-plot concerns the role and ethics of information systems consultants.

Like most seasoned speakers, he still felt twinges of nervousness before giving a presentation but he knew, from his years of experience as a consultant, that if he did fluff his speech, or lose his train of argument, he could recover, and anyway the audience was unlikely to notice.

Joe's ego was unaffected by the fact that none of the notices was affixed at all straight; some climbed at 20–30 degrees, others followed a downward incline. Also, the sticky tape holding the notices precariously to the walls seemed to have been recycled a number of times. Joe fiddled with his overhead transparencies as the audience shuffled in; a straggling, chortling mixture of lonely middle-aged project managers, in their crumpled brown suits; young high-flying assistant consultants in serious blazers; a few assorted programmers in jeans; and some unidentifiable eccentrics. Meanwhile, Joe wondered how many questions from the floor his presentation would provoke; it looked bad if there were no questions, although the chairman could be relied upon to produce at least one feeble little query. Joe was a past master at dealing with questions: both the penetrating incisive ones, that if properly presented could demolish his argument, and the extremely silly variety that demonstrated that the questioner had either not been listening or had some deep psychological problem. The easiest type of question to deal with was the one where the questioner rambled on at great length, at a snail's pace, in a sort of mini-lecture. Joe's concise well-structured responses, often delivered with a terse one-line wisecrack in his now fashionable New York accent, economically saw off that sort of question.

At last, with the fun-run organized, Sid started to introduce Joe to the audience. As Joe looked out suavely at the upturned faces, he caught sight of a vaguely familiar profile that he could not quite put a name to. In the course of his work Joe met many, many people but this serious-looking man in his late thirties, who held Joe's attention did not seem like a customer or a colleague. Joe did not live in the area but had given a talk here a couple of years ago on his patent systems development methodology — SASADAMDAM (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1987). Perhaps . . .

Joe's musings were cut short by the realization that Sid had finished the introduction and Joe's presentation had begun.

## Joe:

There's nearly as many people here as when I last visited. I think I can promise you an interesting, thought-provoking, 45 minutes and I look forward to some really good discussion at the end. By the way, if something is not clear, I'm happy to take questions during the presentation, so please just interrupt me.

The topic that I am going to discuss is of great concern to all of us information systems professionals. That issue is the control of end-user computing. This is one of today's big issues; in fact I think I can truly say it is the biggest issue facing our great profession. It's an issue that if we can just grasp the nettle, then we can be assured of competitive advantage.

My talk is going to consist of three parts. Firstly, I want to outline the rationale for the control of end-user computing, in other words, why such control is necessary. Secondly, I am going to briefly summarize my methodology for end-user computing, called UDMUDAM, which stands for User-Developed All-embracing Managerial Unix-compatible Distributed Action Methodology.

(Muffled groans from the more experienced members of the audience.)

This is a systematic, strategic, computer-aided, CASE-based methodology, developed after many years of experience with the highly popular SASADAM-DAM tools. It provides an easy-to-follow, step-by-step guide to successful end-user computing. The well-documented techniques are sound in theory and proven in practice. There is an optional training package and regular updates. Anyway, more of that shortly. The third part of the talk will kinda draw out a few conclusions and place some recommendations on the table. That's what's on the menu this evening folks.

## (Joe's confident smile is not reflected in the stony faces of the audience.)

OK, let's start by talking a little bit about what's happening in end-user computing. In terms of numbers, a few years ago it was estimated that between 40 and 50 per cent of computing resources were tied up in end-user computing, and this figure was growing at between 50 and 90 per cent per annum — these figures are pretty impressive, as I'm sure you'll agree. Users can now purchase, develop and operate computer-based systems, independently of the IS department. The prices of microcomputers are low enough that departments can buy them from their existing budgets — even from petty cash. They are cheap, easy to use, and easy to install and, with the powerful, user-friendly packages that are available in virtually every shopping mall, the users can have a system up and running in no time. Many users are no longer frightened of computers; they've become familiar with keyboards and screens over the years — some users have got a heck of a lot of experience and knowledge.

Why do they want to develop their own systems? Well, as you all know, the software development backlog has been growing and users, not unreasonably, are not prepared to wait 2 years before we start their pet projects. They think that the system is going to be out-of-date before we finish the feasibility study — sometimes they may even be right.

## (Broad smiles can be seen on the faces of many of the audience.)

I'm sure end-user computing is the way of the future. We don't have to use up scarce skilled manpower developing noddy systems, the users are happy because the systems are being delivered quickly and cheaply, and there are no hassles over implementation.

But, leaving the users to their own devices, are we going to see flexible systems that provide our organizations with competitive advantage? Are we going to see effective systems delivered on time?

(Joe pauses long enough to build up to a climax, but not long enough for anybody to interrupt his flow by answering the rhetorical questions.)

No, we are not. Instead, unless we take some action now, we are going to see a hotch-potch of poorly engineered, incompatible, rickety, old systems that are going to require three times the existing number of maintenance programmers to even vaguely keep them on the road. And the blame for this chaos, the over-shoot in budgets will not be with the users; those wily old foxes will, sure as hell, lay the blame with the IS department.

The burning issue in end-user computing is not the technology — we have the hardware and software in place. No, the issue is one of control; IS professionals have to control the development of end-user systems. Man must talk to man; machine must talk to machine. We need integrated solutions for corporate systems and, in a tight cohesive organization, all systems are corporate systems.

With professionals at the helm, we can prevent the incompatible and avoid the unmaintainable. It is only professionals who have the skills in project management and procurement. We have expertise in techniques for testing and verification, and years of experience in the validation of data, the provision of back-up and recovery mechanisms, and the provision of audit trails. We have developed skills in analysis and design. The users may have the enthusiasm but they lack our professional knowledge.

OK, I'm sure you all agree that control is the key issue; then the next question is how to effect that control, what sort of mechanisms to use. Now, my methodology

Now Joe placed the man — this was Bill Knot-Bother who, on Joe's last visit to the Tropicana, 3 years ago, had argued doggedly with Joe over the need for a formal post-implementation evaluation. Bill: Whilst I agree that control is an important issue, you haven't once mentioned 'support'; yet surely this is our duty in end-user computing. Our mission should be to support the users.

Joe: Of course. I'm certain that we're all familiar with information centres and help desks. They're an accepted part of most modern IS departments. They are there to provide support for the users. But surely the main issue though, is one of control

Bill: Excuse me, but do you really consider that the few words of technical advice cynically offered by a handful of untrained, personable programmers is sufficient. These are mostly novice users, sitting, perhaps for the first time, in front of a strange technology that's going to change their whole work patterns. It's not like giving them a new phone, an updated staff manual, or a different coloured pen. Surely they need more support than that.

Joe: Of course you're right, it goes without saying that the information centre staff should be trained. And of course the users themselves should be given intensive computer based training courses, so as to bring them up to scratch. I have found that redundant university lecturers are often the cheapest and most effective training personnel to appoint.

(A few wry smiles emerge from the audience.)

Now let me move on to my methodology ...

Bill: Excuse me, but I don't think that training is enough.

(Joe, feeling that once again Bill will make his views known no matter what Joe tries, decides to go along with him and says nothing.)

Bill: We have to try to understand the interaction between users and computer-based information systems. These systems change the way that people work, they change the way that people communicate, they can change the way that an organization functions. Two-day training courses on particular packages are not enough. It needs more than just that; the interaction between users and machines is a complex process. We need to consider all the factors that are likely to affect the way that people use computers, in other words, the user-system interface. It is only then that we can plan how to effectively support end-users. We don't want a methodology that constrains our thoughts; at this stage we need to move the boundaries outwards and broaden our horizons.

Joe: Well, my horizons are pretty broad already, and my methodology does not constrain anything. I, as much as anyone else, understand the need for supporting end users and that is why I advocate information centres and help desks. I'm sure that your concerns are well catered for in my methodology.

Bill: I'm not so sure. Because of the sheer number of issues, plus their interconnectedness, we need to classify them, and so I look at these user-system interface issues through a five-level framework.

At the lowest level, the Physical Level, we are concerned with the user as a systems component, who interacts physically with the hardware. The main issues here concern the 'physical' efficiency, comfort, health and safety of the user.

Physical discomfort can reduce the productivity of the user, and health and safety failings may result in increased absenteeism, long-term disability and expensive litigation.

I'm sure that you must have read about the various health risks. Whilst the worries over the effects of electromagnetic radiation from VDU screens on pregnant women may not be warranted, there is clear evidence of muscular and visual problems due to poor equipment design, poor environmental conditions and inadequate concern with job design. Computer usage is associated with increased stress and there is some suggestion that it is associated with an increased risk of chronic illnesses like angina.

What does your methodology have to say about these crucial issues? And where does the information centre fit in?

These issues are naturally important; an organization doesn't want its employees becoming ill and being off work due to computer technology. But by the same token, you have to be realistic about today's competitive environment, and the issues that really matter to an organization such as market share, lowering costs, etc. The point is, these ergonomic issues are the concern of the computer manufacturers — they're the ones who will be the subject of law suits if the technology isn't safe.

## Bill:

I'm afraid the manufacturers are not responsible for the environmental conditions or aspects of job design.

At the physical level, we're not just concerned with health and safety, it's more than just that. As well as keyboard and screen design, for safer working, we should also be interested in alternative input devices, like mice or trackerballs; and voice input. Then there is the use of colour and graphics on screens; and windowing techniques. Users need knowledgeable support in the selection and configuration of safe and effective hardware.

## Joe:

(whose eyes and wrists still ached from typing up a sales report yesterday.) Well, I always recommend high resolution screens and mice. It goes without saying these types of I/O devices are much easier on the user. I thus always advocate user-friendly devices.

## Bill:

But, you see, it's more than just that. That's only the physical level. The user is more than merely a physical systems component. At the second level, the Communication Level, we can think about issues concerning user-system dialogue, where man is seen as a communicator, or dialogue partner.

## Joe:

Yes, I see. Actually I usually recommend menu-based systems for end-users. This allows them to interact more easily with the system. Users instinctively prefer a menu to choose from rather than having to remember some obscure command sequence.

You're getting the right idea. This level is concerned with different dialogue styles. Fortunately, we've moved on a lot since the days of impenetrable 1960s mainframe job control languages. Well, most of us have, except perhaps Unix buffs.

(More groans from the audience, but this time for Bill.)

Whilst full natural language remains out of reach, there are many options available: command languages, menus, form-filling interfaces, query-by-example, and so on. Each of these styles has a host of design questions surrounding them; for example menus: how should they be organized? how many choices per menu? how can you label each choice concisely, yet unambiguously? What about icons? are they gimmicks or godsends? are they informative or misleading? For example, the icon of a trash-can; can you recover data from the computer's trash-can in the same way that you do with a real one?

Researchers are discussing direct manipulation interfaces, where the users interact with objects, rather than a command language. Instead of being command (or verb) oriented, they are object (or noun) oriented. Other researchers are discussing adaptive interfaces, for different types of user, and intelligent interfaces, that utilize stored knowledge of the user, the task, the system, or the data.

I agree, I think that the selection of a dialogue style is very important. And this is something we as IS professionals can help with.

Good, but I think you'll find that it's more than just that. At this level, like the others, there are design issues involved. If you look in the literature, you'll find that many guidelines for screen layout and dialogue design have been proposed. These normally revolve around such notions as consistency, simplicity, adaptability, and visibility, which, taken at face value, appear to be almost beyond reproach, but do in fact contain hidden design dangers. An excessive concern with consistency of presentation can result in dull uniformity; simplicity may not be compatible with functionality (i.e. a large range of functions); and improved adaptability may result in excessive complexity. Finally, visibility of objects can lead to screen 'clutter'. These issues require an appreciation of what makes a good design and the availability of certain design skills to strike the appropriate balance.

Yes, you've probably got a point there. User-friendliness is certainly a key mechanism for improving communication and, as I have said before, I always advocate such systems. They are easier to use and make users more productive. And that translates into more efficient organizations. And that is really the key, isn't it? How to make people more productive.

Yes but, it's more than just that. So far I've just outlined two of the five levels. The third level, the Conceptual Level, is probably the most difficult to examine. Whilst, at the last (communication) level we were dealing with man as a communicator, there's much more to working with computers than just the syntax of message passing. At the conceptual level, we are concerned with man as a knowledge worker and the contents and meanings of the messages. This conceptual level is concerned with the users' tasks, goals and knowledge, and, in particular, the way that they are interpreted and represented by both the user and the system. If the system's information structures are somehow in tune with those of the user, then we have a working partnership.

Cognitive scientists know a lot about the workings of the lower levels of human information processing, (such as attention and perception) and there has been some discussion over people's different cognitive styles. However, at a higher level, little is known about the human processing of concepts and the semantic aspects of information. There's been a lot of recent interest in mental models, where these models are supposed to be our internal representations of a problem situation. But, do we really manipulate semantic nets in our heads? I don't think anybody knows or has the faintest clue how to find out. Also, communication is more than mere message passing, many researchers argue that we should view communication in terms of speech acts, where these are social actions, governed by particular rules. But, can we frame and formalize those rules?

Now, if you think about it, the user has one model of the world but the model that is built into the system is that of the systems developer, which is almost certainly not the same as the user's. Thus, we have a perfect recipe for systems failures.

But any systems developer worth his salt would clearly recognize this and would draw out from the user what his model was. In this way, the model in the system would reflect the user's model.

I don't think it is that easy. The developer might want to draw out the user's model but he may not be able to. You just can't ask someone 'what is your model' and expect it to emerge magically. Such world views are largely tacit and hence incapable of being articulated.

Another issue here concerns experimental systems that try to maintain a dynamic model of the user, based on the user's objectives and behaviour, so as to try to predict his/her next move, or offer advice to the user. But, it's not at all clear how to construct such models. I don't know what your methodology has to say about modelling, knowledge representation, and the semantics of information.

## Joe:

Well, my methodology includes a data dictionary that supports entity modelling and normalization, but I suppose this really isn't in the same league that you are talking about, and it's too complex for end-users to use anyway as it wasn't designed specifically for them. On the other hand, I'm not aware of any methodology that can deal with issues such as this. There aren't any, are there?

## Bill:

You see, there are large gaps here. Now, you could argue that as the conceptual level is so difficult to deal with, it's best left to academics, but even if we haven't got the solutions, we should be able to support the users through an understanding of the problems.

## Joe:

But, the user-system interface is more than just the user sitting alone at a PC. The user is not just an isolated individual, he/she is a member of one or more groups in the organization. And so we come to the fourth level, the Organizational Level.

We know, both from first hand and from the organizational sociologists, that group behaviour is very important. Now, the acceptance of a new system and the way that new system is operated are going to be strongly influenced by the norms and values of the user. Many of these norms and values originate in the work group.

User resistance to change can range from the physical sabotaging of a new system, as was the case of the US postal workers pouring honey and inserting paper clips in their data entry devices, to the simple non-use of a system, and to the subtle and covert, political manoeuvring. Resistance may appear at the first hint of a new system, making the development a running battle, or it may not appear until the system becomes operational, resulting in low productivity, low effectiveness, high labour turnover, disputes, absenteeism, psychological withdrawal and aggression.

Yes, we've all heard horror stories about user resistance. It can leave you dead in the water. I can remember one case ...

(interrupting) You see, the relationship between different groups is very much a political one, as through conflict and coalition they strive for increased status and a bigger share of the organization's resources — departmental budgets, equipment, staff, and territory; and individual authority, status, salary, and roles. The development of a new information system may be a signal these resources are up for grabs — hence the resistance. Moreover, the implementation of a new system may have a direct bearing on the ownership and control of information which has important repercussions for power.

You might have developed the most elegant, technically efficient system imaginable but, if the users do not accept it, then that system is a failure. Resistance to change is a complex piece of behaviour — can we predict it? Can we avoid it? What does your methodology have to say about these issues?

Well, resistance is always a sticky issue and one must be conscious of it at all times. I believe that it can be minimized if you do a good job explaining what the system will do for the users. To this end, I always advocate thoroughly professional presentations to the users, using 35mm colour slides, with coffee in the interval; otherwise I steer clear of organizational politics — it's not my problem.

We know very well that the interface between the user departments and the IS department has been fraught with problems over the years, mostly because the goals of users and developers are often very different. The culture of systems staff inculcates a belief in its own mission to rationalize organizations. Systems staff are fascinated with the technical aspects of information systems, while the users are normally only interested in the impact upon their work. The result tends to be a mismatch of interests and an incompatibility of goals. Developers seem to feel that while individuals in general need an environment which fosters autonomy and creativity, the people they develop systems for need to be controlled, lack initiative and motivation, and so on. This naturally leads to problems during user-systems staff interaction.

I'm not sure that is so much a problem now as it once was. Now that we have better tools to interact with the users, I think there is less an 'us vs. them' mentality. And good systems staff have always had good relations with the users anyway.

Bill: Perhaps, but I still find considerable misunderstandings and power struggles occurring between the two groups.

Another aspect of this power struggle is the potential conflict associated with end-user computing. Prior to end-user computing the role of the systems developers was fairly clear cut: they were the technical experts who had expertise in developing computer-based systems. Their power was due to the specific knowledge and expertise they possessed which the users did not have. With EUC, this knowledge and expertise begins to disappear, and with it, their perceived power over the users. Users no longer rely on systems staff for systems development. The IS department loses both status and power.

## Joe:

Are you implying that IS needs to have power over the users? That we use our technical expertise as a power resource? Well, I suppose to some extent that may be true, but I don't think we lose that much with EUC as we are still the 'experts' and moreover, it is up to us to control end-user computing anyway. That, you may remember, is precisely the point of my talk. We need to control the growth of EUC as it is only us who have an understanding of the true nature of computing — the need for standards, back-up and recovery, documentation, and such like.

You keep returning to your ‘control’ hobby-horse, which I think totally misses the mark. We need to better understand this user-system interface, and it is more than just the four levels I have already outlined. There is still the fifth and final level, the Societal Level. This level treats man as a member of society. His interaction with technology not only affects the way he works, what he works at, the quality of his work and such like, but also wider issues such as the overall quality of life, personal privacy, democracy, and so on. These wider issues are important as they have a direct bearing on the individual's overall ability to carry out organizational duties and live as an accepted member of society.

We have new possibilities in the way work is organized. One key issue here is remote work, involving working at home on a PC instead of travelling into the office. This promises to change dramatically not only the way individuals carry out their organizational duties, but also their interaction with colleagues. Whilst remote work reduces the need to travel and provides considerable flexibility in terms of where, when and how the work gets done, we have to face the potential consequences of social isolation and role ambiguity, which lead to considerable stress and strain.

Thus, organizational hierarchies are breaking down, more lateral relationships are being fostered, opportunistic social contacts are formed, both within and between organizations, and the interaction with the rest of the world changes.

(Joe wonders where Bill's analysis is leading as it is clearly a much broader view of EUC than Joe possesses. But rather than rejecting it out of hand, or trying to interrupt Bill's line of argument, he decides to say nothing and continue listening. What Bill says does seem to strike a somewhat consonant chord with Joe even though that isn't the way he generally thinks about EUC.)

Another work-related issue is that of the quality of working life; end-user computing can increase task variety and skill requirements, as well as improve job feedback, which may lead to higher perceived status and greater job satisfaction. On the other hand, that quality may also be negatively affected through the increased stress and strain, to which computer use generally appears to lead.

Another burning social issue is that of personal privacy; by which I mean that an individual should have some influence in determining how information about him/her is used or communicated to others. The individual should be assured of openness and fairness in relations with any organization that holds data about him; and be protected against unwelcome, unfair, improper or excessive collection or dissemination of that information. End-user computing threatens to lessen personal privacy as information about individuals can be held in many databases which are directly accessible. It becomes more difficult to control who accesses what information on what subjects. This is worrisome as it has strong implications for civil liberties, and more generally, social democracy.

There are various other social issues but the point is that information systems professionals should be able to support end-users through an understanding of these difficult problems. Like me, they don't go away.

Joe: No, I guess they don't . . . (looking around) . . . although it seems that the audience have.

## DISCUSSION

Apart from the above debate, between a rather traditionalist consultant and a somewhat more far-sighted project manager, there are numerous other debates or situations that lend themselves to this treatment. Potential topics for debate might include controversial subjects such as the reliance on untested software in the American Strategic Defence Initiative, the conflict between freedom of information and personal privacy, and the extent to which information technology has tended to improve or worsen the quality of working life. On the other hand, there are many relevant, more prosaic situations that can be illuminated by this approach; for example, the interaction between the systems analyst and user during requirements analysis, or a meeting between a project manager and the project's sponsor for a project that is running very late and over budget.

In addition to playlets acted out by faculty members, we have also used role-playing by students. A typical scenario might be a meeting to discuss the development of a new computerbased sales system. Students are selected to play the parts of senior managers (who are sponsoring the new system), the information systems department (who are implementing it), sales management (who will receive the new system into their department), and sales clerks (who will use the system). Whilst the participants can prepare their opening statements in advance, they do not know how the others will respond. They must therefore think 'on their feet' and interpret the situation, according to the role they have been allocated. Thus, if the exercise is enacted seriously (without excessive play-acting) students will 'be', say, senior managers for an hour and experience the pressures involved in sponsoring a new system. Apart from providing a sense of validity, based on the real-life situation being enacted, role-playing is an excellent way of providing students with an insight into viewpoints that might totally conflict with their previous opinions and values. Researchers in the field of the psychology of learning (e.g. Lawshe et al., 1959) have found that this cognitive dissonance can lead to basic changes in attitude. The related teaching technique of management games (Kibbee et al., 1961) also involves a simulation of a real-life situation. Student teams compete through decision making at both the operating and strategic level, based on their understanding of the case data. These games are typically complex, often involving non-trivial decisions ranging across the marketing, production and financial areas of a company. Such games have become a mainstay of many MBA courses (McKenney, 1962).

At a recent summer school (Social Citizenship in the Information Age, Brighton Polytechnic, 28 July – 2 August, 1991), each day's proceedings ended with a production of a Swiss play, The Court of Ethics. This piece of theatre involved a systems analyst answering charges of social irresponsibility before a jury, composed of the audience. This proved to be a particularly effective way of communicating controversial issues. Within the practice of information systems development, role-playing has been advocated as a way of predicting and evaluating the consequences of technological change (Kein & Hirschheim, 1983), while management games have been recommended as a way of choosing between alternative systems designs (Hawgood et al., 1972).

Considering the non-technical aspects of information systems, there are many human-centred issues and situations that can benefit from such a teaching method. However, a perennial problem of novel teaching methods is that of evaluation. This can be simply (critics might say 'superficially') dealt with by considering the response of the students. In our experience, the extent of their immediate participation and enthusiasm is clearly visible and discussions in subsequent weeks can show whether the knowledge has been retained. On the other hand, questionnaires could certainly be used for a more formal expression of student reaction to these teaching methods.

## CONCLUSION

The above playlet has been used to good effect on a number of occasions. It has always been well received and, from the comments of the audience, it has succeeded in getting the appropriate message across in a memorable way. Clearly, within university courses, such playlets should be used sparingly; academic study should not resemble a variety show. Our experience suggests that playlets can be used for certain topics, as an interesting supplement to traditional lectures. They are particularly suitable for practical material and for finely balanced issues where two opposing points of view have equal validity (see the playlet on evaluation in Hirschheim & Smithson, 1987). They can be used to present complex issues where the scenario helps to reduce that complexity and ease the interpretation and understanding. For example, a traditional lecture to deliver the above information can very easily turn into a presentation of endless lists of issues. Our experience suggests that such playlets do need to be scripted in advance in order to deliver the right information in a timely fashion. Few academics can successfully 'ad-lib' such situations. Also, care needs to be taken not to inject excessive humour, at the expense of the underlying message. The teachers concerned may feel that there is a danger that the playlet will collapse in a welter of embarrassment but, with careful preparation, we have never found this to be the case. Rather, the opposite has been seen, with improved student participation in small-group role-playing exercises later in the term. The technique has been successfully used at academic conferences and lends itself to management training and professional seminars.

Finally, it should be noted that we, the teachers, have gained additional insight into the material being presented. Transforming our ideas into these playlets, based on practical situations, has not always been easy. However, the problems encountered have facilitated our understanding of complex issues in a way that would have been hard to attain through lectures and journal articles, the traditional tools of academic discourse.
