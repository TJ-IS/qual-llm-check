# Viewpoint: A question of System

BH Straub and IO Angell

Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London, UK

All practitioners of information systems methodologies should harbour doubts about the validity of their actions. Whether we like it or not, personal history will colour and shade all observations and analyses. The very words used to communicate our analysis are prejudiced by experience, yet the vocabulary so often goes unquestioned in the drive to deliver 'answers'. Perhaps the lesson of a much older problem solver should be learned; the Oracle at Delphi had the motto 'Know Thyself' emblazoned on the walls! But knowing oneself is often a very painful experience, especially if the attempt at self-discovery is anything other than superficial or cosmetic. In our subject, which places so much emphasis on the importance of different Weltanschauungen, it is crucial that we accept views different from our own. Otherwise, our discourse will lose its relevance, as we finally submit to over-exposure of a self-created, task-orientated and ill-reflected jargon.

The Calvinist maxim, 'Are you willing to be damned for the greater glory of God' (Wiener, 1964, p. 6) is most appropriate here. 'There is bound to be deception in any approach to the system' (Churchman, 1968, p. 229), and therefore, to justify our Systems approach, we must be willing to deny its very basis, and ask the awkward questions! Of course by doing this, we run the risk of, not only intellectual self-indulgence, a charge often levelled at academics by the businessmen who, after all, employ the end product, but also the possibility of damnation and the despair of self-rejection of our most prized values.

Nevertheless, such questioning is totally justified. The difference between the notion of System and particular instances of systems is exceedingly opaque and nebulous; if indeed there is one. In this short paper, we will ask some awkward questions about the basis of scientific efforts that harness the notion of Systems to investigate problems. We will refer to these approaches as the scientific Systems paradigm. They are the result of a series of dialogues sparked off by the realization of a need for systems analysis with self-analysis. The questions raised are a synthesis of the reactions we have observed from people who, although outside the Systems arena, are those that have to live with its implications. The driving force behind this question is a suspicion that, perhaps, the development of the scientific Systems paradigm does not in itself yield a better understanding of our world. Having said this, we must acknowledge the place of the paradigm within the efforts to come to terms with today's problems in a seemingly new order (Duncan, 1971, p. 271; Laszlo, 1972, p.12), as well as its position in causing much of this change. But we do see it as just one method amongst others, and recognize it, not necessarily as an agent of change, but as dependent on the successful application by a human agent. We harbour suspicions that the emphasis on System as an intellectual construct (with an inherent benign potential) is too often camouflaging a forced precaution against failure.

It is most appropriate that we question System, a word which has its origin in Ancient Greek. The concept of Systems cannot, therefore, be claimed to be new, yet it is only since the Second World War that Systems have risen to prominence as a domain of scientific study in their own right. Some protagonists of this approach have left no stone unturned in demonstrating the ubiquity of systemic evidence. Others have suggested that the scientific Systems paradigm is 'a form of cross-disciplinary thought which made it possible for members of many disciplines to communicate with each other in a language which all could understand' (Mead, 1968, p. 2). It is proper therefore that we discuss this recent concept of systems, and contrast it with alternative opinions. The necessity of considering different Weltanschauungen of the artefact of a particular system is self evident, but what is often not perceived is the need to consider the variety of Weltanschauungen of the concept of systems itself.

General System Theory, as propounded by v.Bertalanffy (1971), and Cybernetics, as propounded by Wiener (1961), are prime developments in the early stages of the concept of a Systems paradigm. Both approaches consider the investigation of aggregations with a high degree of complexity, that display a certain level of organization. It is this combination of complexity with organization that is the phenomenological foundation of the Systems paradigm. The metaphysical justification is captured in the notion of 'wholeness' (Angyal, 1941, p. 243), or of wholes (Checkland, 1981, p. 14), rather than the whole.

However, these three concepts (complexity, organization and wholeness), the cornerstones of the Systems paradigm, are used in a combination which is often perceived as idiosyncratic, and so they are problematical to the users (as opposed to analysts and designers) of systems. Although it is arguable that these three concepts are vital for the understanding of Systems, there are possible differences in the understanding of their semantics with regard to particular systems. It is these semantic and conceptual differences that we see as underlying the need to question Systems.

The Systems paradigm has developed from scientific roots, and so naturally the semantics of its cornerstones are heavily influenced by the scientific paradigm. This is especially so, as the development of technology, particularly in the engineering and the physical sciences, has played such a major role in the careers of the early protagonists (Wiener, v.Bertalanffy, Turing, Ashby, Shannon, Weaver, etc.). It is hardly surprising that the development of the Systems paradigm, created to deal with 'organized complexity' (v.Bertalanffy, 1971, p. 33), is drenched with scientific notions.

Systems get subjected to a 'laboratory-like' treatment; the empirical input is used to build a representative functional model, that in turn produces output consistent with the empirical evidence. Feedback, feedforward, homeostasis, negentropy, communication and control occupy the systems analyst's conscious, and possibly unconscious, thought on the 'problem-situation' (Wilson, 1984, p. 4). The co-ordination of the various influences on the black box leads the analyst to deal with 'transfer functions' (Gerardin, 1968, p. 44) instead of the situation itself. The system can so easily become a dispirited, denaturalized, machine-like representation of 'reality'. Often, this step from the complex richness of reality to the trite, brittle poverty of the mechanistic system is not made explicit in the Systems paradigm. Recognizing this situation, Checkland tackles it by linking reality and the system with the use of multiple 'root-definitions' (Checkland, 1981, p. 167) and a form of prototyping and learning approach. However, within this approach there is an inherent danger of 'putting the cart before the horse', by attempting to humanize the notion of system instead of de-substantializing it in favour of humanity. It may be better not to impose unnecessary Systems practice; nonetheless his approach does have its merits. It is obviously better than the direct neglect of these issues, as apparent in many other authors (e.g. Miller, 1978; Klir, 1969), and certainly far in advance of the de-humanistic question of system with 'machine' (Ashby, 1952, p. 16). There is a real fear abroad, which cannot be ignored by Systems practitioners, that once this equation is made, then the machine-like system inevitably produces machine-like output, void of everything that makes reality real. Whatever Systems Thinkers say to the contrary, the reduction of the concept of System to a mere science of the machine (or mechanistic contraption) is a real possibility from the developments in the Systems paradigm. The issue is even more critical given the domination, implicit or explicit, of computers over the minds of so many developers of systems. The application of computers is imposing the system machine metaphor far beyond the limits of appropriateness. So the empirical cornerstones of complexity and organization may lead to the degeneration of a complex reality to the pale shadow of an elaborate machine. And we do not apologize for using the word 'reality'. We have difficulties with the wishful thinking of stipulating any clear distinction between 'intellectual construct' and 'reality'. To us, it smacks of a lukewarm excuse and an avoidance of the issue, by those who cannot accept the responsibility that they might be wrong. Only the dream of a perfect idea, valid in all cases, can force them to designate such precautions.

In contrast, the metaphysical cornerstone of Systems, wholeness, is concerned, not so much with the representation of a particular system but, with its justification. It must be understood that wholeness refers to the scientific Systems paradigm to an idealized notion of reality that transcends the limits of singular disciplines or phenomena. This is the justification for the claim of Systems being 'meta' in relation to other disciplines. Yet, the transcendental value of wholeness vanishes in the process of the systemic analysis in two ways. Firstly, creating a confusion of interconnections with elaborate layers of 'numerical complexity' replaces the concern for complex wholeness. Secondly, organized wholeness degenerates into a mechanistic wholeness of the 'bubbleware' of systems analysis methods. The wholeness of the system, therefore, hinges on the machine metaphor that works like reality and not, as it should, on the reality itself. The interrelatedness of these two degeneracies, perhaps, becomes understandable when we assess the metaphysical significance of wholeness.

The wholeness of the system 'must be intuitively seen and recognized' (v.Bertalanffy, 1971, p. 69) or, stated rather more boldly, it will be defined by the investigator (Checkland, 1981, p. 100). That type of wholeness cannot transcend the investigator. Yet, the scientific Systems paradigm is claimed to go beyond the analyst. In fact, its scientific origins suggests a superiority of method over analyst. The result of this irreconcilability is the confusion over the assertion of wholeness. This confusion is biased in favour of the assertion: 'In the state of wholeness, a disturbance of a system leads to the introduction of a new state of equilibrium' (v.Bertalanffy, 1971, p. 69). The nominal wholeness of the system becomes a matter of principle; an initial requirement of order, instead of a phenomenological discovery. The analyst's critical power of observation is directed at unveiling the system's laws of homeostasis. An unscientific selection of conceptual models, Weltanschauungen and root definitions serves to bring this about. The best that this line of reasoning might lead to is a 'logico-mathematical science of wholeness' (v. Bertalanffy, 1971, p. 94), but this may be seen as irrelevant with respect to the relationship to human reality, perhaps even a statement of futility.

Perhaps the understanding of systems is simply a reflection upon the preoccupation of man with machines. The powers that the machine has given mankind to transform his reality is translated into the promise that systems will do the same, albeit on a different level. However, the systems paradigm after recognizing the adverse effects of machines (pollution, alienation, etc.) inherent in the shortcomings of the singular disciplines fails to addres them. Yet, the apparent success in overcoming the restrictions of one-dimensional, reductionistic investigation by the use of multi-dimensional, holistic investigation may have merely increased the input of modelling, but not brought about a re-evaluation of the applied concepts themselves. Perhaps the Systems paradigm has, in just being a 'meta-discipline' (Checkland, 1981, p. 6), made a dilatory and somewhat marginal attempt at developing its own epistemology of Systems. The pitfall of the singular sciences to develop appropriate epistemologies is not overcome by assembling them according to a systemic principle. Just supplying a discursive framework for the application of the respective sciences may simply be inadequate, and definitely not reconcilable with the claims being made for the Systems paradigm.

To many information systems practitioners, a system is understood to be complete when it eludes any attempt at 'logico-mathematical' modelling and any attempt at a description as a single(!) wholeness (a direct result of the drive to deliver 'answers'). Such attempts will fail, because emergent properties and the feedback of that very system are not accountable by ossified ideas about its wholeness or its logico-mathematical structure. In this context, complexity should refer to a potential for understanding beyond systemic investigations, and organization should point to the investigator's acknowledgement of interrelating structures that represent this ill-understood complexity. Then finally, wholeness, if it must be used, is the insight into the impossibility of 'getting things right', in contrast to the often perceived struggle of 'getting the system working'.

So if we want to carry on a meaningful discourse about Systems, we must reflect upon these three fundamental concepts, and relate them to an on-going self-analysis concerning the process of analysis. We must accept that our involvement is not only professional but also personal. And we must remember that the system is not the result of the task at hand, it is its raison d'etre!
