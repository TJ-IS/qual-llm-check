# Hybrid managers — what do we know about them?

MJ Earl and DJ Skyrme\*

London Business School, London and \*Templeton College, Oxford, UK

Abstract. There is increasing interest in 'hybrid managers', a breed of managers who blend information management skills with general management skills. Such people are perceived as important in enabling organizations to exploit information systems effectively in today's competitive business environment.

Based on a literature survey this paper summarizes what we know about hybrid managers, as expressed by both 'expert opinion' and formal research studies. We conclude that little substantive research has been conducted into hybrid managers. Much has still to be learnt about their possible role, characteristics and competences; and how organizations should recruit and develop them. Because of the paucity of published material about 'hybrids' in the information systems context, we have drawn inferences from appropriate analogues in other functions — namely personnel, finance and R&D. Relevant studies in related areas of information systems, general management, careers and management development are also put into the 'hybrid' context.

Keywords: career, human resource, hybrid manager, IS management.

## INTRODUCTION

As the successful exploitation of information technology becomes an increasingly important ingredient for business success, organizations, the education sector and professional bodies have begun to consider what knowledge, skills and experience managers require in the 'information age'. A typical example is the British Computer Society (BCS) hybrid manager initiative, a task force of leading UK MIS managers, academics and consultants. Its initial report argues that hybrid managers are central in enabling organizations to improve business performance through the use of IT (Palmer & Ottley, 1990). It calls for the UK to develop, by 1995, 10 000 such managers

who can conceive and implement, in the line organizations, systems that will win global customers and beat global competitors, be it on time to market, cost, quality, service or any of the dimensions which are used to measure competitiveness.

Despite this somewhat grandiose language, the emergence of such a report is evidence of the seriousness being given to the notion of the concept of 'hybrid managers'.

Hybrid roles were first suggested for IS professionals by Keen (1986), but hybrid managers were seen by Earl (1989) as being likely to be in line. Thus Earl's definition is the most precise one available to date:

people with strong technical skills and adequate business knowledge, or vice versa . . . hybrids are people with technical skills able to work in user areas doing a line or functional job, but adept at developing and implementing IT application ideas.

Earl arrived at this tentative definition by extrapolating research evidence from studies of the use of IT in innovation (Lockett, 1987) and the pursuit of competitive advantage (Earl & Runge, 1987) carried out by the Oxford Institute of Information Management. Many successful IT innovations had been initiated and pioneered by user managers in the line who had not only substantial business experience but also sufficient IT experience and knowledge to allow them to recognize an IT opportunity, act upon it and ensure the resultant project succeeded. Often they had had to battle against substantial organizational and technical resistances. The evidence pointed more at hybrids being business managers with IT experience rather than vice versa. Experience often included time spent in the IS function but could also include significant prior involvement in application development projects. As discussed later, the British Computer Society incorporated a number of potential roles in their conceptualization of the hybrid manager and, perhaps not surprisingly, in view of their concern with the computing 'profession', tended to imply that computer specialists were obvious candidates for 'hybridization'. $^{1}$

As a result of topical interest and at the request of the BCS, the authors conducted a literature survey whose results are summarized in this paper. Our aim was to draw out key factors, not immediately apparent, that could be useful to organizations and managers wanting to develop hybrid managers. As at that time, there were few explicit references in the literature to 'hybrid managers', numerous electronic searches were made of terms which could be analogous and might yield useful results. It turned out that the most useful material broadly fell into the following categories:

(a) information systems management, especially project management,

(b) management of analogue functions, especially finance and R&D,

(c) studies of general management, including issues such as the generalists vs specialists debate, managerial roles and career development.

The implication and conclusions from these three bodies of literature all contribute to developing an understanding of hybrid managers as described in the rest of this paper.

## THE CASE FOR HYBRID MANAGERS

There appear to be several arguments supporting the need for hybrid managers. First, various surveys indicate that the majority of IT applications conferring strategic advantage emanate not from IS departments, nor strategic planning, but rather from the line (Henderson & Treacey, 1986; Butler Cox, 1987; Earl & Runge, 1987; Rockart, 1988). Previous research has shown that the search for strategic IT opportunities can be seen as an innovation problem (Earl et al., 1988) and that factors for success include the presence of champions and sponsors (Lockett, 1987; Beath & Ives, 1988; Earl et al., 1988). These individuals are noted to have some hybrid characteristics. Champions, in particular, must understand the possibilities and limitations of IT (Beath & Ives, 1988).

Secondly, a recurring theme in surveys and research of key IS issues is that of alignment of business, IT and organizational factors (Amorso et al., 1989; Grindley, 1990; Scott Morton, 1991). Achieving this in practice, it is suggested, requires appropriate management processes with involvement from people who can work in multifunctional, collaborative teams on business problems where IT may provide a solution.

Thirdly, Dixon & John (1989) cite a key challenge facing corporate managers in the 1990s as the need to create 'hybrid partnerships' to 'bridge the gap' between line and IS management, a theme elaborated by Rockart (1988) and Henderson (1989). Indeed, in their study of the evolution of IS functional leadership, Earl et al. (1986) showed how the strategic focus of IS activities and their management was evolving over time through various phases – delivery, re-orientation and re-organization. The re-orientation phase sees the first step towards a stronger business orientation, where very often someone who is predominantly a business manager steps into the IS leader position. In the re-organization phase the management focus becomes the interfaces and relationships between IS and the rest of the organization. Such relationships are traditionally poor, a problem characterized by lack of mutual understanding, poor communications, lack of common language — all factors which suggest the need for hybrids.

Another indication of the need for hybrid manager characteristics is the evolution of the IS leader role. A recent survey of 50 MIS managers in top US companies concluded that this role is changing fundamentally and that to be successful CIOs they need to become more business-oriented (Hartog & Rouse, 1987). It also highlighted the need for them to exhibit self-reliance, versatility and adaptability.

Finally, a current continuing concern is the failure rate of many systems development projects, either in terms of meeting user needs or of completion on time and within budget. Again, the literature suggests the need for skills to relate business needs to IT activities (Hochstrasser & Griffiths, 1990).

In addition to these arguments may be added a key conclusion from the 5-year Management in the 90s programme at MIT that 'IT, to be successful, must be harmonized with its organizational context' (Scott Morton, 1991). Their case examples of how to achieve this suggest the need for 'managers who understand the implication of IT for business strategy'.

All of these arguments suggest not only the increasing interdependence of business and IT, but also of managers within and without the IS function. Development of hybrid managers then can be seen as one means of facilitating this interdependence. This appears to represent the evolution of thinking on hybrid roles which can be traced through the empirical and theoretical work of Keen (1986), Lockett (1987) and Earl (1989).

Whether hybrid managers have one particular or dominant role or whether several managerial responsibilities in exploiting and implementing IT demand hybrid characteristics remains an empirical question. Normatively, the above arguments suggest the latter and suggest beneficial results. Although the business benefits of deploying hybrid managers have not been rigorously verified, various programmes cite evidence of success. Thus General Foods Bakeries claim a 50% reduction in application development time since hybridization activities started (Kerr, 1989), presumably due to efficiencies gained in understanding both business needs and technical solutions. Similarly, Esso UK claim that, since having hybrid managers involved in every project, 90% are now delivered on time and within budget compared with 60% previously (Meiklejohn, 1990).

## WHAT ARE 'HYBRID MANAGERS'?

In defining hybrid managers, Earl (1989) outlined different mixes of competences for different IS role demands in two domains — driving and doing (Table 1). Besides the hybrids defined earlier three other roles were proposed:

(a) leaders: executives in user areas who can drive the exploitation of IT in their business,

(b) impresarios: the few executive level managers in the IS function who can propel the organization into strategic consideration of IT and/or respond to user needs in a business-like manner,

(c) professionals: those on specialist career paths upon whom delivery of IT will often depend.

Although these proposed roles are clearly distinct, our survey reveals that in popular discourse the distinction between leaders, impresarios and hybrids is rarely made, the term 'hybrid' being used as all embracing. Indeed, difficulties have been reported in identifying particular people as hybrids (Mercer, 1990). We therefore use the term 'hybrid' here as a broad amalgam of these three role demands in the rest of this paper. Indeed, as indicated earlier, the initiative which begat this paper has tended to conflate the role. The cost of these amalgamations is that it becomes less clear what a hybrid manager does. A working description at this stage might be 'any manager who is equipped to enhance the integration of IT and the business at the level of the application, the IS function or the organization and does so'. Hybrid managers could be project champions, self-sufficient end-users, project leaders, systems managers, systems analysts, line and functional executives or IS executives. The hybrid manager is a concept, a capacity for a role, a management development goal; it is not a job title or a description and will not be found on an organization chart.

## Roles and responsibilities

While our search revealed no detailed studies of the hybrid role in an IS setting, it is possible to make realistic assumptions based on the broader contexts of integration and partnership between IS and the business, and of exploiting IT for strategic advantage. From a synthesis of several sources (Ives & Olson, 1981; Earl et al., 1986; Beath & Ives, 1988; Carter, 1988; Earl, 1989) key role descriptions emerge (Table 2).

A general management perspective would add some refinements to the fourth category including management of blurred boundaries (BIM, 1988), balancing short-term and long-term portfolio of activities (Kotler, 1982), ensuring co-operation from peer and staff groups (Katz, 1974) and developing networks of co-operative relationships to implement new agendas (Kanter, 1983).

Apart from understanding the technology, at some level, the hybrid management role is therefore seen to be very much concerned with enhancing business performance through IT and of developing organizational relationships.

A hybrid manager, almost by definition, has a blend of business and technical competences. Having suitable business knowledge is an important prerequisite, especially for hybrid managers coming from an IS background. This can pose particular problems for IS managers who have spent many years in technical careers, as pointed out by Keen (1988).

The degree of technical competence needed by hybrid managers is open to debate. On one side is the belief that inadequate exposure to, and experience of implementing, using or managing the introduction of IT will lead to failure to recognize application opportunities and to introduce them successfully (Lockett, 1987). Neither the potential capabilities of IT may be understood nor the challenges of implementation recognized. On the other hand, technology changes so fast that some argue that it is difficult for any specific or detailed knowledge to be useful for anything but a short time (Hartog & Rouse, 1987). Indeed, it may be preferable not to have detailed knowledge and not to be deterred by familiarization with the problems of IT implementation! Thus while hybrids may not have the depth of knowledge of specialists they should have sufficient experience to select and manage internal or external providers (Dearden, 1987).

Of all the competences, organizational-specific knowledge seems an important but often over-looked characteristic. Poor understanding of an organization's culture ('how we do things round here') and its internal processes seems likely to present insurmountable obstacles to incoming managers from external companies or specialists who have worked in an island of the organization. Koter (1982) pointed out that business and organizational knowledge are cumulative. Hence effective hybrid managers are likely to have remained in the same or related industry for many years, and according to Keen (1988) to have spent significant time (2–3 years) in each job. As MIS managers, apparently, stay with one employer for only 3 or 4 years (Nolan, 1986; Carlyle, 1988), this may partly explain why many MIS managers apparently have difficulty in becoming good hybrid managers. Early evidence from our current research indicates that successful hybrids appear to be 'company people'.

Finally, the most critical competence area seems to be general management skills (Butler Cox, 1986). Kotter's study (1982) on general management and Farriss' study (Farriss, 1988) on R&D leaders identified the important competences of particularly effective leaders. These included cognitive, interpersonal and communications skills. Similarly, in his study of financial managers, Sathe (1982) showed that those who were described as being 'involved' in the business exhibited strong personal commitment and energy as well as communications and interpersonal skills. The desirability of such 'soft' skills is reinforced in DP publications where communication skills are often cited as the single most important skill characteristic of successful IS managers.

This analysis suggests such a wide range of competences that a well-qualified hybrid manager appears an almost impossible dream. Thus Redditt comments that 'the notion that a single executive can straddle the business and technical worlds and effect an alignment between them is simplistic at best' (Carlyle, 1989). This argument may be supported by a study carried out by the training division of Aetna Insurance into the competences needed by a systems department (IS Analyzer, 1988). In 1986 they identified that 34 distinct skills were required, whereas by 1988 the figure was 91! Many of the extra skills needed were non-technical skills — interpersonal, business knowledge and time management skills.

## Personality profiles

It is now widely recognized that personality traits and behavioural characteristics have a large part to play in orienting and motivating people towards certain careers (Miller, 1985). Surveys of the profiles of DP professionals and managers, carried out by Couger and Zawacki in 1977 and again in 1987, show them to have the lowest social affiliation needs of all professional categories (Couger & Zawacki, 1980; Couger, 1988). Ferratt & Short (1988), though, disputed Couger and Zawacki's interpretation and suggested that the differences could be due to the conditioning of people's behaviours by their working environment.

In another study of personalities, Lyons (1985) used the Myers Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), based on Jung's theory of psychological types. In terms of the MBTI preferences, his results show that DP staff appear to be significantly more introvert and intuitive and somewhat more thinking and judging than the general population. Of the 16 Myers Briggs psychological 'types', the three most frequent in Lyon's samples of 12 000 DP professionals and managers are reproduced in Table 4.

All these types prefer working alone. This is in accordance with Couger and Zawacki's findings (1980). A particular sample of general managers surveyed by Vaccoro also indicated the introversion bias of the DP population relative to this group, whereas there were close similarities in terms of intuition and judgement (Vaccaro, 1989). This closeness is reflected when temperaments (particular combinations of MBTI preferences) of the two populations are compared.

Although a relatively consistent picture of DP personality profiles now exists, there is as yet no published data on the personality profiles and temperaments of hybrid managers (assuming they can be identified). If we hypothesize that we expect them to be catalysts or visionaries, the data suggest that the DP population is likely to be as good a source of hybrid managers as the general management population. In both cases, though, the overall percentage of the catalyst combination — who are presumably closer to our hybrid manager ideal because they possess good interpersonal skills — is small.

## DEVELOPING HYBRIDS

If we accept the need for hybrid managers and the competencies and characteristics required, then attention must be given to their selection, career and personal development. Some competences may be acquired by education and others as much by experiential development in organizations. We have therefore identified from diverse sources approaches which seem to work in practice and have also collated the needs expressed about education.

## Career planning

In the case of IT specialists, rather than consider traditional career paths such as programmer to analyst on to systems manager and then to DP manager, Keen (1988) proposes a perspective based on 'career trajectories' plotted on a two-dimensional business experience — IT experience grid (Fig. 1). He suggests that IS specialists face the stark choice of either maintaining technical currence or developing business and organization skills.

IS professionals run the risk of losing their technical edge while not becoming 'real' business people.

It would seem that IS professionals wishing to become hybrids should be encouraged to move laterally and gain a broad range of experience in the different dimensions. Hartog is among those who believe that IS workers should be moved into other functions within their first 4 or 5 years of work (Carlyle, 1989). This opens up the possibilities of not just one or two career paths but multiple trajectories. The main route into hybrid management seems to be through involvement in user projects (Carlyle, 1988; Kerr, 1989). This transition appears to happen more frequently in information-intensive industries that make extensive use of IT such as airlines and financial services. $^{1}$

However, most observers believe it is easier for experienced and senior business people rather than IT professionals to become hybrid managers and a number have successfully achieved this (Carlyle, 1989; Meiklejohn, 1989, 1990). Common mechanisms appear to be exposure to IT applications as a user (Kerr, 1989), increased interest in IT through exposure to IT possibilities (Jasper, 1986) and a prominent role in project management of new IT applications (Meiklejohn, 1985).

To date, there are few data on the career paths of hybrid managers, even from the comprehensive Career Development Surveys CDS-I and CDS-II of management career paths (Nicholson & West, 1988). However, general management literature expresses a concern that general managers only start emerging at the top of the organization having been funnelled for much of their career through a specialization route (Adair, 1983). Kotter's successful general managers had a career mostly in one industry and a long time with the current employer. Their first job had a good fit with their interests and values and they gained promotion in one or two functions only. The CDS surveys also suggest the importance of a good track record with a single organization; this reinforces the notion of 'organization people' as a likely source of hybrid managers.

Another perspective is offered by the literature on career stages (Dalton et al., 1977; Shein, 1978). In particular, Schein's career 'anchors' — their preferred work motivation and horizons — which become evident in mid-career may help individuals and organizations identify people likely to have hybrid manager characteristics. Of Schein's five anchors, general management appears to be the most relevant to hybrids. Only eight people in his small sample (44 MIT/Sloan School alumni) had these anchors (Table 6). It is possible, though we do not know, that people with creativity or technical anchors would also make effective hybrids.

The notions of trajectories and anchors combined with that of competences and personalities lends weight to the importance, for both organizations and individuals, of identifying hybrid manager potential and carrying out career planning. In an analysis of management potential in scientists, Gratton (1987) places great emphasis on individual aspirations. She cites three implications for career development of specialists: measurement of (management) potential is more important than current (technical) job performance, a dual career ladder should be created for those who wish to remain specialists, and a formal career development programme is required.

These lessons seem relevant for the development of hybrid managers, particularly for those with IT backgrounds. Following this argument, we can propose that organizations should clarify what competencies, psychological profiles and anchors they seek for hybrid managers, and search their work force base using techniques such as role and skills analysis, job simulations and psychometric testing.

## Higher education

Concerns about the depth of specialization in early career are now being reflected by graduate recruiters seeking IS staff. Computer Science courses are often considered too narrow and are criticized for not teaching interpersonal and communications skills (Hodges, 1988; Ridgway, 1988). Less specialized courses combining relevant subjects such as computer science and business studies are sometimes considered more appropriate for the potential hybrid (Ridgway, 1988). Also apparent is the increasing trend towards 'sandwich' degree courses, which give students one or two periods of industrial experience during their academic studies.

Attention is also being given to the suitability of existing current post-graduate degrees and diplomas for the development of hybrid managers. For example, MBA qualifications may offer a better route for the potential hybrid coming from an IS background or wishing to join the IS function. Thus, Pfizer is one of several companies who recruit MBAs in their initial intake into the IS development (Kerr, 1989). The expected value of such people in IS is that they can talk in business language and be pro-active with internal clients. There is also increased attention being paid to what the IS core curriculum of an MBA course should be, particularly for those who are not IS specialists.

Part-time MBAs are particularly favoured by the BCS 'hybrid' task force (Palmer & Ottley, 1990). However, their survey of existing MBA courses in the UK found that many had minimal information management content. IT is more often just an optional subject and is usually treated separately. Few courses weave the impacts and interactions of IT into other subjects (e.g. marketing, finance) which is the suggested need for hybrid manager development. These latter two concerns would seem especially important for non-IS professionals on MBA courses who wish to become hybrid managers.

However, there does seem to be a consensus on the need for provision of management education before a move into either a general management role (Constable & McCormick, 1987), whatever the departure point, or into a hybrid role (Gratton, 1987) from a technical specialist.

## Management development and training

Whatever the background and education of potential hybrid managers, their capabilities will need to be continually broadened. Three typical approaches for developing the IT awareness and skills of line managers have been identified. Many a hybrid manager's involvement in DP started as a user or involvement with user projects (Jasper, 1986; EDP Analyzer, 1987; Kerr, 1989). Participation in IT for selected line managers (Meiklejohn, 1985; Jasper, 1986) and certainly IT awareness and skills training would seem useful. Finally, events or locations where users and MIS people come together to discuss IT impact issues or see IT in action (Jasper, 1986; I/S Analyzer, 1988) have been another device employed in some organizations.

Likewise, developing the business and management competences of DP professionals may require a related set of approaches. In-house management training for technical staff is one option (Ball, 1988). Provision for IS staff to attend external education institutions, such as business schools, is another (Kalbacker, 1985).

Sending DP staff on external industry update sessions to keep them abreast of trends and developments within their industry (Carlyle, 1989) is common. Bolder experiments include induction of IS staff in business operations, such as sending IS people alongside sales people on sales calls or encouraging IS staff to visit 'real customers' (Carlyle, 1988, 1989). An increasingly frequent organizational design step is to appoint MIS people as 'consultants' or 'co-ordinators' to business units in order to develop their business skills.

Many of these proposals are conjectural and are based on firms experiments. Importantly, it would appear that a major challenge in converting IS staff into hybrid managers is stimulating their interest in management (EDP Analyzer, 1987). Their lack of motivation to manage may well be a reflection of their personality and career anchor.

Clearly, the potential level of competence acquired through any of these mechanisms varies enormously. A 2-day appreciation course is unlikely to convey in-depth knowledge. For such reasons, a commonly advocated vehicle for developing hybrids from both the IS and business routes is that of job rotation or secondment. Both Earl and Keen suggest that a token period of 6 months IT experience is inadequate and that 2–3 years is a minimum. No-one appears to dispute this. Two years is indeed a typical period (Kerr, 1989). The most successful business schemes appear to be those where the business units are encouraged to second their best people into MIS or to manage projects (Jasper, 1986; Ball, 1988). Also IT units are sending their managers out into the business. The biggest concern expressed about such secondments is that some people who have successfully made the transition do not want to return to their former department. This is not necessarily an unsatisfactory outcome.

To date there have been no significant studies into the effectiveness of these various mechanisms, although their proponents generally pronounce themselves satisfied. The variety of mechanisms in use does suggest the task of 'hybridization' is not trivial — whether of IT professionals or others.

## ORGANIZATIONAL CONTEXT

So far our attention has been very much on the hybrid manager as an individual, and the routes for development and career progression. Organizational context can also be expected to be an important influence on both selection of hybridization mechanism and eventual success.

## Organization success factors

Experience from other functions may be instructive for IS activities. In both R&D and finance functions, one of the success factors cited for the functional manager has been a board of management receptive and appreciative of their function (Rosenberg, 1982; Sathe, 1982; Roberts, 1988). For IS, several surveys cite the need for a corporate culture sympathetic to IS (BIM, 1988; Carlyle, 1988).

Unfortunately, these and other surveys show that appreciation of the strategic benefits of IT is generally low at senior organization levels. One way of addressing this problem was identified by Feeny et al. (1989b) in a study of organizations where high integration existed between IS and business. This involved placing an IS executive as a full member of the board or business unit management team and then arranging ongoing education for business managers into the capabilities and potential of IT.

The development of a Human Resource Management capability within MIS — looking at the whole gamut of careers, training and reward systems — is also considered important by some (Carlyle, 1988). Finally, the BCS points out the importance of organizational development in general (Palmer & Ottley, 1990). As hybrids are often concerned with innovation and change, a culture geared to ‘continuous learning’ would seem to be needed (Garrat, 1990). This echoes the British Institute of Management report on training and education requirements of UK managers in IT which found a ‘scarcity of training in middle and line management’ and recommends the ‘creation of a learning environment’ (BIM, 1988).

At the group level the composition of steering committees brings IS specialists and business specialists together in group situations (Alloway, 1976; Drury, 1985). Often project development methodologies are the mechanism which encourages IS-user communication. Interestingly, the notion of 'hybrid teams' may be as relevant as hybrid managers. Indeed there is an interesting parallel here with the Charter Initiative in the UK which prescribes a list of competences required by all managers (Day, 1988). In contrast, Cadbury's have adopted the team approach which recognizes that all managers do not need all competences but should provide complementary competences in an overall team (Skapinker, 1989).

Finally, at the individual level, more informal working arrangements could go a long way to giving specialists, whether in business or IS, a much deeper appreciation of the other's domain. These could include routine cross-departmental interactions, social juxtaposition at lunch-times and the like, coming together on education and training events, and avoiding creation of island communities.

## IMPLICATIONS

Although our current state of knowledge about hybrid managers is limited, there do appear to be significant implications for management, education and future research.

## Implications for management

Most of the implications for management appear to be in the area of human resource strategies — for example selection and recruitment, management development, career structures and planning, although there are wider organizational factors to consider. Together, these create a need for a strong human resource orientation, in managing the IS resources. However, in the UK, Sparrow has reported that few IS departments have a coherent HR strategy (Sparrow et al., 1989).

Our survey suggests that selection processes for people moving into hybrid management could usefully be extended to incorporate psychometric testing, aptitude and motivational testing, and assessment of management skills and potential. Development of hybrids also seems to require cross functional experience early in their careers. Another practice would be to insist that people must have had both IS and line experience before taking senior functional manager appointments.

Finally, there is evidence from interviews conducted by the BCS task force (Palmer & Ottley, 1990) that many organizations will need to change their own environment to be supportive of the development of hybrid managers. This is reinforced by the comments of Sathe (1982) for the financial function and Roberts (1988) for R&D.

Such changes include the creation of a more entrepreneurial climate, gaining acceptance for continual change, making functional boundaries more blurred, developing senior management awareness of IT and its implications for the business, and using several of the 'hybridization' mechanisms shown in Table 7 in combination.

For its part the BCS has introduced a hybrid manager career stream into its professional development scheme (PDS).

## Implications for education

Our survey also raises many questions about the scope and content of undergraduate and post-graduate courses. Along with other specialist courses, some computing courses may need to broaden out to include general management skills. Particularly required are some of the 'soft' skills, such as inter-personal skills, interviewing and communications. Increased attention might also be given to general business knowledge. For aspiring hybrids, this could be achieved through encouragement of more combined honours degree courses or industrial placements and projects.

Finally, management studies courses should perhaps recognize IT and information management as core topics, just as finance, marketing or production usually are. Moreover, linkages with IT should be emphasized throughout each of the other functional topics, so that, for example, the role of IT in marketing or organizational design is fully appreciated.

## Implications for future research

The BCS initiative has triggered some current research specifically on hybrid managers (e.g. Mercer, 1990), but few results have been reported to date. Many questions remain unanswered in other than a superficial way. The fundamental question is whether the concept of the hybrid manager is useful in building effective integration between IT and the business and, if so, what are the characteristics of the best hybrid managers? It then becomes important to understand what are the best practices in developing and using them and how their success depends on the organizational context. It will be necessary to know how hybrid management potential can be recognized and suitable people be motivated to become hybrids. Then we should ask how well prepared are human resource functions for creating a context for hybrid management?

Such queries call for research into career patterns, and personality profiles. In addition, there are many broader aspects of hybridization and the IS-line partnership that warrant further investigation, as several writers suggest that creating hybrid managers is not, by itself, sufficient to develop a meaningful partnership between IS and the business.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper has summarized the state of readily published material which is relevant to the development of hybrid managers. The main conclusions are as follows.

1 This is a relatively new field of research and practice in the management of IS resources.

2 'Hybrid managers' and 'hybridization' mechanisms have been reported by their advocates to result in significant improvements in terms of IS or business performance.

3 Valid experiences can be drawn from the specialist vs general manager debate and from analogues in other functions such as finance or R&D.

4 Organizations are not in general giving enough attention to human resource management either within the IS function or in managing IS resources generally.

5 The development of hybrid managers needs a careful consideration of career paths, management potential and continuous development.

6 It is unclear what proportion of either the IS or business population have the potential to be successful hybrid managers; psychometric testing may well be one way of rapidly assessing hybrid potential.

7 More of the newly emerging broadly based information management degrees and advanced diplomas are needed as is development of IT related modules in general management courses.

Furthermore, it is important that hybrid managers are considered within their organizational context. The organization needs to create an appropriate culture and use a range of 'hybridization' and other integrating mechanisms to improve the effectiveness of IT in business. In other words, to maximize the business benefits of IT, hybrid managers may be necessary, but they are certainly not sufficient.
