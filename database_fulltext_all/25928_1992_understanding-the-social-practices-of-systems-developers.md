# Understanding the social practices of systems developers

Brian P Bloomfield

Centre for Research on Organizations, Management and Technical Change, Manchester School of Management, UMIST, P.O. Box 88, Manchester M60 1QD, UK

Abstract. The aim of this paper is to explore some of the ways in which we think about the development of information systems by drawing upon arguments articulated within the philosophy of science and the sociology of technology. It argues that many of these approaches operate on the basis of a reified and sociologically restricted view of technology. Drawing upon recent work in the sociology of technology the paper proposes a more thorough-going analysis of the social practice of information systems development and discusses examples from knowledge engineering and requirements analysis to illustrate its argument.

Key words: knowledge engineering, requirements specification, sociology of technology, systems development.

## INTRODUCTION

In this paper, I propose to examine various ways in which information systems development in organizations is construed and offer a different perspective derived from the philosophy of science and the sociology of technology. My aim is to illuminate the implicit sociological assumptions inherent within what is usually regarded as the 'technical' side of information systems development, to reveal the social within the 'technical'. In particular, the focus will be on the social practices of systems developers, including those of knowledge engineers (e.g. data modelling and knowledge elicitation). My argument will be that, paradoxically, many, though not all, of the current socially oriented views of information systems (both in computer science and the literature on information systems) often operate with a sociologically restricted 'black-box' view of technology. $^{1}$ That is, they take technology as given and concentrate on the processes involved in adapting it to organizations—thus giving rise to a myriad of techniques, both technical and organizational, for the analysis, design, and implementation of information systems. As a result, there is a preoccupation with methodology: reviews of existing methodologies, typologies for describing different methodologies, and suggestions for new and improved ones, are common features of the literature. However, this methodological preoccupation really begs the issue of whether the poor development record for information systems merely lacks the right method or whether the problem lies elsewhere.

Indeed, it is interesting to note that in the philosophy of science Lakatos (1970) suggested that endless arguments about methodology were symptomatic of a 'degenerating' research programme; they merely served to insulate a failing programme from a radical re-evaluation and possible moves toward an alternative programme of research. I do not want to get bogged down in the debate about whether or not computing is 'really' a science, at least not here, but Lakatos' position should at least say something to those in the computing and information systems communities. Namely, is the search for better methodologies or more powerful techniques in danger of displacing or diverting attention away from other more pertinent issues at the very heart of the practices of systems development?

It is contended that if the development record of information systems is to improve then academic computer scientists and information systems professionals must go further than adopting a more informed approach towards organizations (Yourdon, 1976; Keen & Scott Morton, 1978; Loucopoulos & Lazell, 1987). More specifically, they must address the ways in which the work of developing and implementing an information system (of whatever form, e.g. a database management system, an expert system, or a financial ledger system) is a social practice involving inclusions, exclusions, and the exercise of power (Markus & Pfeffer, 1983; Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987; Bloomfield & Best, in press). Thus it is not just a matter of coming to terms with the social and political character of organizations or the subjective nature of user views, for example their inability to state information needs clearly, but requires systems developers to reflect on the subjective, social, and political aspects of their own practice. In contrast with the commonly held notion that information systems developers are technical people, I will argue in keeping with recent sociological work on scientists and engineers that systems developers can be regarded as heterogeneous engineers (Law, 1987) or even 'engineer-sociologists' (Callon, 1987). For example, in Callon's study of an electric vehicle project in France he asserts that not only did the engineers involved seek to determine the exact technical characteristics of the vehicles concerned but that they also sought to specify 'the social universe in which the vehicle would function' (Callon, 1987, 84). This sociological view of the work of engineers is part of a distinct approach to technology. As Law (1987, 115) puts it:

Let me, then, define technology as a family of methods for associating and channelling other entities and forces, both human and non-human. It is a method, one method, for the conduct of heterogeneous engineering, for the construction of a relatively stable system of related bits and pieces with emergent properties in a hostile or indifferent environment.

The question as to the nature or identity of other methods of heterogeneous engineering will not be pursued here; $^{1}$ what is important in this view is the light it can throw on information systems development. It is contended that systems developers seek to associate other human actors and non-human artifacts in the process of developing information systems. Thus rather than taking a narrow view of information technology, one which would restrict the concept to physical artifacts or programs, this broader perspective suggests that the technology of information systems includes hardware and software but also assorted computer engineers, systems analysts, programmers, operators, and users, etc., each of whom has a voice in the development or use of such systems. Therefore the achievement of stability of an artifact — hardware or software — is simultaneously a technical and a social accomplishment. $^{2}$

Of course it is now accepted in certain areas of the critical literature on information systems that the orthodox or narrow technical view of systems development represents but one paradigm or approach (for a review of some of the different 'paradigms' see Boland & Hirschheim, 1987; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). But while agreeing with much of this literature I feel that it suffers from two drawbacks. First, it seems to cut little ice with followers of computing orthodoxy: critical studies of information systems development have been in evidence for some time now but the production and publication of papers reinforcing the conventional view continues unabated. Thus the aim here is to challenge some of the assumptions of computing orthodoxy and in particular the dichotomy between what is regarded as technical and what is regarded as social (or non-technical). $^{3}$ This finds expression, for example, in the notion that the task of information systems development is to integrate the technical (e.g. physical devices such as hardware) with the social (e.g. a user organization) through the use of appropriate techniques. The challenge will not be attempted by arguing that adherents to the orthodoxy have ignored the social nature of organizations but rather that they have ignored the social nature of the 'technical' aspects of systems development practice and that what is often regarded as a purely technical matter (the hardware or the techniques for information requirements analysis) is actually a social one. This is the reason why I will invoke ideas from the sociology of science and technology in order to achieve my end; in contesting the very bedrock of the orthodox view — its understanding of the technical qua technical — by revealing the social within the technical, it is hoped that this will contribute towards an alternative strategy for informing the debate on the problems of systems development.

A second drawback is that the 'black-box' view of technology is also to be found in the critical literature. This has two implications. On the one hand, the sociologically restricted view of technology closes off any substantive understanding of how some of the concepts which underpin information systems originate, take root and are nurtured within computer science: by focusing on the social impact of computers on social structures there is silence as to the question of how computer science develops in relation to ideas about organizations, decision making, or management, and how it therefore already tacitly presupposes a view of social reality and organizations. And on the other hand, there is a parallel silence about the nature of the products of computer science within the social systems that are organizational information systems. Indeed, as Klein & Hirschheim (1987b) show, those who adopt a social ontology of information systems and see them as social as opposed to technical systems — for example, the contractual view (Ciborra, 1983) or the language action view (Goldkuhl & Lyytinen, 1982) — tend to focus on the impact of information systems development on organizational structures or processes.

To reiterate the point, the problem addressed in this paper is not that of how to ensure that information systems developers embrace the social aspects of applying computer science in organizations more fully. Rather, the task is that of unravelling the implicit (albeit underdeveloped) sociological dimension within the areas of computing and information systems and in particular the social practices already inherent in the work of information systems specialists.

To set the scene, in the next section I will refer to the idea of technological determinism because it is part of a way of thinking and speaking which tends to run through many accounts of technology; alternatively, it serves as a marker against which many have sought to craft alternative non-deterministic accounts. Then I will move on to consider some examples from within the broad consensus centred around the notion that the development and implementation of information systems requires that attention be paid to the social aspects of organizations. I will then turn to the philosophy of science and refer to the debate about internal and external accounts (or histories) of the development of scientific knowledge because this has parallels with the debate over technological determinism. Then, drawing upon recent developments in the sociology of technology I will endeavour to point the way toward an alternative sociology of the practices of information systems development. This will be applied to examples from the area of knowledge engineering and requirements specification. Furthermore, the discussion will also address the tendency toward the proliferation of techniques to alleviate the problems of system development — the 'fetish' with methodology — and relate these to the question of management control of the processes involved.

## Understanding IT — restricting the sociological imagination

Technological determinism, the notion that technology follows an inherent logic of development and, further, that changes in social organization can in part be explained in relation to the engine of technical change, has long been the target of attack for sociologists and philosophers but has never really disappeared (Winner, 1977; Marcuse, 1978, orig. 1941). For example, de Sola Pool (1987) offers some scope for social and political choice in the development of information technology (IT) but is still wedded to the primacy of technics: technology 'shapes the structure of the battle, but not every outcome'. As far as the orthodox view of information systems is concerned — what Klein & Hirschheim (1987b) refer to as the technical ontology — this represents a clear expression of the assumption of technological determinism:

An information system is a symbol processing system. It receives symbols, stores them, transforms them, transmits them, and prints alphabetic, numeric, and pictorial symbols. (Gessford, 1980, 3 cited in Klein & Hischheim, 1987b, 287).

For in construing information systems in purely technical terms it implies that the social aspects of computing are subordinate to the technical.

However, in opposition to the idea of technological determinism, IT is actually an area where it is not difficult to find arguments for the importance of the social dimension in the application of technology (for example, see Boland & Hirschheim, 1987; Finnegan et al., 1987; Zuboff, 1989). Thus it seems fairly widely accepted (e.g. in the psychology and sociology of organizations, management science, and increasingly in computer science) that information systems must be socially or organizationally adapted, that the question of implementation is just as important an issue as what is perceived to be the 'raw' or 'inherent' potential of a technology. For instance, as an exponent of the socio-technical approach Mumford talks of the necessity for users to participate in the design process in order to increase worker satisfaction and work efficiency (Mumford, 1980); while psychologists and social psychologists work on the design of user-interfaces, endeavouring to make information systems more 'user friendly'. Thus, there have been calls to tailor decision support systems (DSS) to the individual specifications and requirements of particular users. Following either a cognitivist information processing model (managerial decision making style is seen as a cognitive variable) or a personality model (e.g. individual decision making style can be related to Jungian psychological theory) the aim is to optimize the form and presentation of information in accordance with the needs and capacities of the end user (MacIntosh, 1985).

Perhaps one of the more sociologically enlightened positions regarding system development is advocated by Floyd (1988) who argues for a paradigm shift from what is described as the predominant 'product-oriented' perspective which is underpinned by a mechanistic worldview, to a 'process-oriented' view emphasizing the learning and communicational aspects of software engineering: 'the paradigm change does not mean rejecting technology, but finding ways to a more human oriented technology' (my emphasis).

But though the argument for a rigid technological determinism may no longer carry such pervasive force in the field of information systems we should still be on our guard. Indeed, it would be prudent to ask what price has been paid in the emergence and increasing acceptance of the current social or humanistic orientation toward IT. Here I propose to argue — perhaps, it might seem, rather perversely — that as far as IT in organizations is concerned, the 'victory' over technological determinism may have been rather more Pyrrhic than decided. That in a certain sense many of those concerned with social aspects of IT in organizations have unwittingly reinforced the potency of technological determinism.

In seeking to counter the view that information systems are the determined outcome of the logic of science and technology, there has been a tendency to restrict the scope of sociological investigation and at the same time reify computer science. For example, with the aim of developing an understanding of the organizational role or impact of information systems, the task of social science has effectively been reduced to that of revealing how the technology is tailored or adapted to the particular social and political context which prevails. While in no way wishing to detract from the importance attributed to this social dimension of technology, none the less there are problems with such a perspective.

The major difficulty with this position is that it has nothing, or virtually nothing to say about what philosophers of science would designate the internal development of the products of computer science; it treats these rather as a given, as black-boxes, and concentrates instead on the impact or implications of such products for social practices. $^{1}$ In other words, studies which embrace this particular conception of IT and organizations may tell us a lot about how technology is applied and made to fit the organization; or how it is used as a political resource (Kling, 1980), as a symbol and signal (Feldman & March, 1981), or as ritual (Robey & Markus, 1984), often yielding interesting social and organizational insights, but such studies do little to inform us about how this supposedly independent technology develops before it is applied. By implication, presumably this question is to be left to computer scientists and technologists to answer. Moreover, even Floyd's approach (Floyd, 1988), while explicitly recognizing that software developers actively construct reality through learning and communication with users, still appears tied to the notion of adopting technology to the social: her emphasis is on making technology fit the social — shifting attention from information products to information processes — and not on asking what technology is.

A theme I want to concentrate on in this paper is the whole notion of thinking about technology as something which is developed outside as it were before being brought into organizations to be applied (Layton, 1977). This notion reifies computer science because if we were to peer inside the implied black-box we would find not just hard facts and mathematical and logical formalisms but also social relations and practices (e.g. the representational practices such as flow charting and entity-relationship diagrams, etc.), the specific history and culture of computer science which give meaning and stability to those facts, formalisms and practices. Moreover, computer science has not developed in isolation from other disciplines but has been shot through with ideas about decision making, organizations, and management (e.g. the work of Herbert Simon).

Whatever the particular form in which questions about the social dimensions of IT are couched they seem to have a common thread running through them — namely, each posits the existence of a boundary between what is social and what is technical. Someone who argues that information systems must be contextualized to the specific organization where the systems are to be introduced must implicitly or explicitly operate on the basis of a particular view of the technology (Hirschheim, 1986). The fundamental issue to be aired in this regard is not so much the notion of adapting the technology to fit the social (or even its instrumental corollary — adapting the social or changing organizational culture to fit the technological) but rather the problematic nature of any attempt to differentiate between the technical and social in absolute terms. Such a boundary is neither 'read off the book of nature' nor is it imposed by technological artifacts; it represents a social convention or institution.

In everyday discourse it is of course common to refer to a notion of technology as if it were a thing-in-itself with specific technical properties — e.g. the idea that new information and communications technologies are making more and more information increasingly widespread; convenient though this might be, even such seemingly innocent statements are problematic. For example, the idea that we really have more information as a result of the IT 'revolution' and that by implication this is a public good is surely questionable. Unless we restrict ourselves to the communication engineer's notion of information (wherein the qualitative distinction between a speech from Hamlet and a leader from the tabloid press becomes lost) we could argue that what really matters is not more access to information but an increase in our ability to judge it and act upon it. The technology itself does not provide more information; it might facilitate the greater provision of more data but we decide and subscribe to social conventions concerning what exactly we will call information and by implication who shall be deemed to be an informed person. In short, I want to contend that behind any claim or account about technology we will find a network of social relations, inclusions and exclusions, choices and negotiations. These are themes tackled by sociologists of technology and will be taken up in the next section.

To summarize, it is apparent that in an attempt to resist notions of technological determinism, humanize information systems, or simply make them more organizationally acceptable, certain social approaches to IT in organizations can pave the way for a quasi-determinism which creeps in through the back door. In order to throw some further light on this issue I will now turn to the philosophy of science and examine a debate which has an interesting parallel with the arguments about technological determinism and offers insights into how arguments concerning information systems development in organizations can be better understood.

## THE SOCIOLOGY OF SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY

One of the interesting debates which raged not too long ago in the philosophy of science centred on the notion that the development of scientific knowledge could be differentiated into internal and external histories (Barnes, 1974; Bloor, 1976). External history is concerned with all the factors (social, economic, political, etc.) which tend to shape the location or pace of development of knowledge. In contrast, internal history is concerned with the content of knowledge itself and therefore its legitimacy. According to this view, the only circumstances in which a sociological account of the development of the content knowledge should be sought occurs, ironically, when that knowledge is deemed to be false (e.g. the case of Cyril Burt and the question concerning the hereditary aspects of intelligence, or phrenology, etc.) — thereby reducing the sociology of knowledge to the sociology of error (Bloor, 1976). Moreover, when internal histories were written they tended to be rational reconstructions (sometimes following Lakatosian prescriptions [Lakatos, 1971]). Thus, internal history, often referred to as the context of justification, was seen as the realm of scientists or those within the philosophy of science while external history — the context of discovery — was to be left to sociology and the conventional historical study of science.

To illustrate how these ideas might operate when applied to computing, let us take the case of the development of digital computers. $^{1}$ Put very simply, we might argue that these devices were developed against the background of global warfare when there was a desire on the part of the military to have the capacity to solve various complex mathematical equations in connection with the design and use of particular weapons systems, or with code-breaking. Thus, we might say that some of the problems facing the military during the Second World War provided the opportunity (location) and adequate resources (pace of development) for the emergence of digital computers. This form of account would be part of an external history. However, for an account of the internal history of digital computation we would have to turn to the disciplines of electrical engineering, cybernetics, communication engineering and mathematics, etc.: in other words we might say that the development of the theory and practice of computation was facilitated by the resources devoted to it because of the war effort but that the latter had no role in the internal content of that knowledge (cf. Redmond & Smith, 1980). Similarly, though the fact that the various scientists, engineers and mathematicians were members of professional organizations and institutions might form part of an external account of the development of digital computers, these sociological observations would be seen as having no bearing on the actual content or truth of the scientific and technical knowledge of computing.

In opposition to this view of scientific knowledge, the task of the sociology of science has been to cut through the artificial barriers between internal and external histories, to send sociologists and anthropologists into the laboratory and to turn the sociological spotlight on the very core of scientific theories and experiments. This move has two useful facets: first, it provides a sociological perspective on the development of the content of knowledge, and second, it focuses attention on how the boundary between science (or scientific knowledge) and the social is sustained in different contexts. Starting from the view long- ago articulated by philosophers such as Duehm and Quine — to the effect that all data are theory laden, that the results of scientific experiments are underdetermined by nature and that therefore there is a measure of interpretative flexibility in accounts of experiments — the sociology of science aims to offer an account of how scientists choose and negotiate between rival theoretical schemes. In other words, it offers a sociological view of epistemology (Bloor, 1976; Hollis & Lukes, 1982; Shapin, 1982; Collins, 1985). Thus, when scientists agree on the outcome of an experiment it is not because nature has forced their hands but, rather, because they maintain a socially shared interpretation, that is a social institution. To paraphrase Latour (1987), nature is not the cause of the settlement of scientific controversy; rather, nature, that is our shared understanding of reality, is given by the outcome of the settlement.

Recently, there have been moves to develop a sociology of technology which stem directly from the advances made by sociologists of science (Bijker et al., 1987). $^{2}$ In conventional views of technology there is a common split between the properties of physical objects (e.g. machines)

or processes (e.g. chemical engineering) and the sponsors or users of technologies; in other words between internal (technical) and external (social) history. To counter this, sociologists of technology draw a parallel between the interpretative flexibility associated with the outcome of scientific experiments and the corresponding flexibility in terms of how technological artifacts are understood and designed. There is always more than one way to design an artifact or a technological process and moreover these can mean radically different things to different social groups (which in turn gives rise to alternative contents). In addition, as indicated earlier, Law (1987) has proposed the notion of the heterogeneous engineer, scientists and engineers are seen as system builders, that is they construct networks of interwoven actors both human (individuals and institutions) and non-human (e.g. devices or artifacts). Just as engineers build artifacts they also have to build agreements with design teams and all the particular groups involved in a technological project.

Understanding what facts and machines are is the same task as understanding who the people are. If you describe the controlling elements that have been gathered together you will understand the groups which are controlled. Conversely, if you observe the new groups which are tied together, you will see how machines work and why facts are hard. (Latour, 1987, 140).

Latour (1987, 10) gives this idea its most radical formulation in an assertion about machines, their design and whether or not they work. In contrast to the conventional view that once a machine works people will be convinced, he urges that a machine will work when all the relevant people have been convinced. $^{1}$ For example, a new computer system does not simply work and convince people of the efficacy of its design; rather, it must be judged to pass all manner of tests along the way. When these hurdles are cleared, in other words when all the relevant people have been convinced, the machine works. Of course, when people are convinced and agree that a machine is working, this is then seen as a fact; the once debatable features assume the mantle of technical matters, they become stable and the technology becomes a black box; it appears as a technical artifact and all the social processes involved in its construction become hidden from view.

There are now a number of studies which have demonstrated the potency and potential fruitfulness of a sociological approach to technology: from missile guidance systems to electric vehicles, from urban electrification to the development of plastics (see MacKenzie & Wajcman, 1985; Bijker et al., 1987). Moreover, computing has also come under the spotlight of sociologists of technology. For instance, in relation to Artificial Intelligence, Woolgar (1985) has aired the idea of a sociology of machines; on the other hand, Collins (1986) has provided a sociological account of the celebrated test proposed by Alan Turing for deciding whether or not a computer is intelligent — the so-called Turing Test. Also, Fleck (1987) has given us an account of the development and establishment of AI as a scientific discipline; while Bloomfield (1987, 1989) has looked at the discourse, the hopes and beliefs of AI and computing.

And as for the notion of heterogeneous engineering, one example in connection with computing is to be found in Latour's (1987) reading of Kidder's (1981) account of the development of DEC's VAX range of computers. Kidder suggests that the internal organization of these machines could be seen to mirror the organizational structure of the parent company. Another case crying out for a similar interpretation is related by Cooley (1980, 42). This implies that the centralized control and structure of IBM mainframe operating systems in the 1970s echoed that of IBM itself. Moreover, it is noteworthy that these particular operating systems reflected a division of labour amongst software people and led to unnecessary constraints on programmers. In fact, this feature of computer development is well-known in certain circles of computer science and has been captured in the notion of 'Mealy's Law': 'The eventual structure of the system reflects the structure of the organization that builds the system' (Yourdon, 1976, 82). In terms of heterogeneous engineering this can be interpreted by the notion that the technical order of the system and the organizational order of the designers/systems developers mirror each other; engineering a system simultaneously involves 'engineering' individuals and groups, negotiating, persuading, and securing agreements with them; or again in Callon's term, engineers are engineer-sociologists.

It is a pity that the alternative epistemological and sociological reading of 'Mealy's Law' has not been available to computer scientists. Indeed, the conventional view of the 'law' is that a good system could be corrupted by poor social interaction within a design team; alternatively, good communication will allow a machine's potential to be fulfilled. In other words, the social can either get in the way of the technical or it can assist it — but only in terms of the pace of development toward a design which ultimately is technically determined.

## A SOCIOLOGY OF THE PRACTICES OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

So, how can the ideas under present discussion help us in an attempt to come to terms with technological determinism and understand the social practice of information systems development? The first point to make is that those interested in the development of information systems must follow the lessons of the sociology of science and technology, and seek to dissolve the 'technical' barriers which surround computer science and information systems. That which appears technical obscures its sociological content. Lest we be consigned forever to the task of writing 'second best' externalist accounts of the development of IT in organizations we must enter the internalist domain. Just as the boundary between internal and external histories of scientific knowledge are sustained by a social convention, so too is that between those aspects of an information system which are deemed to be technical (determined by technology) and those which are open to social influence or shaping during implementation. This of course is not to deny that a given technology has various constraints associated with it; rather, it sensitizes us to the idea that the very awareness of those constraints is not simply read from the book of nature as it were, but is context dependent. To take an obvious example, constraints due to compatibility problems between different hardware systems could be said to reflect divisions in the market rather than some form of natural division in the technical order of things.

Second, we can see that the tendency to locate the source of systems development failures within the realm of organizational politics or other socially rooted 'shortcomings' (e.g. lack of skills, or bad communication between systems analysts and users) is analogous to the urge to look for social explanations of erroneous knowledge. Hence, in addition to asking how organizations develop in the context of the use of IT — qua the social implications school — we must ask how IT develops both in connection with management science and in the context of the use of information systems in organizations. That is, we have to examine the extent to which developments in IT (even in their earliest conception) and in particular the practices of systems development might be already oriented toward particular types of organizational application (e.g. the desire to reduce uncertainty through the use of information in practices of prediction and control [Cooley, 1980]) and be predicated on particular choices and assumptions about the nature of information, decision making and organizations. In fact, Noble (1984) argues that the military and industrial application of cybernetics has been intimately interwoven within the development of computing, that the orientation toward automation and the enhancement of managerial control have served to constitute computing in much of its current manifestations (see also: Beniger, 1986; Edwards, 1988; Roszak, 1988).

Having paved the way for the framework adopted in this paper and drawn attention to other sociological research on different aspects of computing, I will now go on to consider two areas concerning the development and use of information systems in organizations. It should be stressed that my aim is not to provide a final account of the technologies or techniques under discussion but rather to stimulate debate and argument.

## The construction of expert systems

My first example concerns the applied area of Artificial Intelligence (AI) known as expert systems or IKBS (intelligent knowledge-based systems). In the course of constructing an expert system, an attempt is made to elicit the working knowledge or heuristics of a domain expert (e.g. a manager, a doctor, a social worker, a chess player) and then represent these within a knowledge base which is typically composed of formal logical rules (the process referred to as knowledge engineering). This is not the place to argue about the status of expert systems vis-à-vis human intelligence (Weizenbaum, 1976; Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1986) but instead let us try and get a sociological angle on this technology. Hitherto, much of the socially oriented discussion concerning the technology of expert systems has been centred on either the deskilling implications (Clement, 1988; Dawson, 1988) or the difficulties of getting experts (willing and unwilling) to articulate their knowledge in a form which can be formally represented in a computer program (Kidd & Welbank, 1984; Bloomfield, 1986; Collins, 1987).

To begin with, we can consider the reasons cited for the development and use of expert systems. Typically, there may exist a desire to archive rare or costly skills and thus make them more visible and therefore available to higher management within an organization. Or, there may be some notion of pushing back the 'bounds on rationality' of the decision maker by making informal satisficing heuristics more programmed or formal and thus, by implication, more effective. Both these examples are of course the other side of the coin to the control of the managerial labour process. And, needless to say, in certain circumstances the development of expert systems may be undertaken solely for the symbolic purpose of being seen to have competence in the latest or newest cutting-edge technologies. But whatever the external (social and economic) reasons underlying the construction of an expert system it is not difficult to show that sociological issues can be connected to significant internal considerations in respect of these technological artifacts. For instance, the motivation to build a particular expert system in respect of certain decision making tasks must be tied to or draw upon knowledge concerning the domains most appropriate for attempts at knowledge engineering. Stated in even stronger terms, we might draw an analogy with science and argue that knowledge engineers work within a paradigm containing an evolving set of exemplars (e.g. fault diagnosis, configuration of orders for computer systems, drug prescription, etc.) against which they must judge the suitability of particular proposed applications for the exploitation of expert systems techniques. The development of particular applications or artifacts is shaped by the received wisdom surrounding the exemplars, each potential site must be recognized or affirmed as similar to or different from those exemplars — in short, knowledge engineering is not a programmed task!

The technological process of knowledge engineering is based on particular assumptions about the nature of knowledge and decision making. Typically, it follows the work of Simon (1960) and presumes — either in theory or in practice — that decision making is equivalent to an heuristic search through a decision space undertaken by a socially isolated individual decision maker (the domain expert) whose knowledge (conscious or unconscious) consists of rules or heuristics (Boland, 1986). In other words, at the very core of what might appear to be a 'hard' technical discipline we have a model of expertise, of knowledge, and of decision making which conceals key assumptions about the nature of social reality. Surprisingly then, perhaps we should regard knowledge engineers as sociologists in disguise albeit ones who operate with a socially restricted view of human beings. This rather asocial view of knowledge and decision making is found in many texts on knowledge engineering and in those on AI more generally; it represents a shared understanding which sustains the very core and practice of work in the construction of an expert system.

On another point, the metaphor of heterogeneous engineering provides a further illumination of knowledge engineering: because in addition to engineering the knowledge of a domain expert, knowledge engineering involves social interaction. Thus as a consequence of being forced to think about rules, a domain expert may come to reconceptualize their own expertise. Whether they come to believe that their own expertise is governed by rules or not their understanding of themselves will be different. In fact this could be seen as crucial to the potential success of any particular knowledge engineering exercise. Put another way, knowledge engineers must engineer the domain expert as much as the rules which are derived from the knowledge elicitation process; their practice is not just 'technical' but also and at the same time social.

In terms of actual software, an expert system incorporates a knowledge base which contains the domain rules and an inference engine which is alleged to mimic the reasoning or problem solving strategy of human experts by utilizing those rules in conjunction with user-supplied items of information. The assumptions about knowledge and decision making which underpin knowledge engineering thus play a similarly crucial role in the construction and operation of expert systems. These assumptions do not just shape the application of expert systems technology in some externalist fashion but help to constitute the core of knowledge about expert systems and the internal content of specific applications, of actual items of expert systems software.

Another point worth making here is that, just as the development of expert systems could be viewed as an attempt to formalize and regulate what would otherwise remain implicit, so too we might argue that the evident search for better and better tools for knowledge engineering similarly represents the extension of rationalization to the process of software production. In other words, the development of better knowledge engineering tools may or may not facilitate the building of more powerful expert systems but it may also enable a greater degree of control over the practice of knowledge engineering.

Moreover, as regards my opening remarks about diverting attention away from more fundamental issues, we can see that indeed this has been the case with certain aspects of work in knowledge engineering. Thus the search for more powerful knowledge engineering techniques (such as programs for parsing and representing user dialogue) represents a 'technical' solution to what is construed as a 'non-technical' or social problem — for example, an expert's inability to articulate rules — while the assumption that users actually use rules remains unchallenged. Similarly, in the mid-1980s rule induction by machine (Michie, 1970) was seen by some as a means of alleviating the bottleneck due to user's limitations; the difficulties facing knowledge engineers were not seen as a reason for examining the assumptions about knowledge and expertise but became instead the excuse to look for even more powerful technologies which would yield rules in spite of the domain expert.

## Requirements specification

Requirements specification is that part of computer science which deals with the problems of how to ascertain and formally specify users' information 'needs' as well as the technical requirements of an information system within a given organization. It is a process which is fraught with difficulties and no end of techniques and methodologies have been put forward to alleviate them. Some of these take the form of technical tools — e.g. data dictionaries or program specification languages such as Structured English — or semi-technical procedures such as cognitive mapping (Montazemi & Conrath, 1986); while others are more clearly aimed at the human or social side of information systems project management. The overdevelopment of technical fixes for requirements specification may be connected to the view among certain systems specialists which sees users as perverse because they continually change their minds about what they want. According to this perspective, technical developments are seen as imperative to make up for the shortcomings of the social or the bounds on rationality of users.

Among the more social approaches to requirements analysis we find the work of Mumford (1980) who locates reasons for systems development failures in the lack of user-participation in the system design process. While on a different tack we can observe advocates of Checkland's soft systems methodology which attempts to cater for the differences in individual's worldviews in connection with the perception of problems and therefore the types of information system required to solve them (Checkland, 1981; Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald, 1982). Both the socio-technical and the soft systems approaches can be interpreted in terms of heterogeneous engineering. The former seeks to integrate the technical and the social by securing a consensus through user satisfaction. The latter requires the systems analyst (cast as a form of 'therapist') to inaugurate a learning system where different views and perspectives can be integrated. This social system can then decide on the definition of its problems and goals and an information system can then be designed to tackle them. In each case the social and the technical must be engineered or orchestrated in unison. While it is easy to describe these social approaches in terms of heterogeneous engineering the important point to convey is that they entail particular social practices and these have a bearing on their inherent limitations. For both the socio-technical and the soft systems approach ignore vital questions concerning the nature of power in organizations: for example, who is included and who is excluded on a project team, who is consulted and who is not; what about the differential distribution and access to resources, including knowledge and skills; and how do experts shape users' understanding of information systems? (For a fuller discussion of these points from various perspectives see: Markus & Pfeffer, 1983; Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987; Bloomfield & Best, in press).

Whatever the designation of the various attempts to improve requirements specification — that is, whether they are purportedly technical or organizational — each can be tied to specific social practices (and thus to implicit and explicit choices and exclusions) which bear upon the development of information systems. Thus the technical approaches to requirements specification are also examples of heterogeneous engineering because they presuppose and indeed create a certain form of user role; whatever is ceded to the domain of the technical is simultaneously lost to the social.

Another method used in this area of information systems is that of data modelling. This involves the use of special representational practices in an effort to build a model of a user organization; for example, the systems analyst attempts to represent different user perceptions of an organization within a series of diagrams (data flow diagrams) depicting relationships between data entities and information processes. The aim is first to represent user views, and second to arrive at a consensus by identifying and eliminating discrepancies and logical contradictions amongst these views before synthesizing them into one unified model. These representational practices are underpinned by the conviction that data modelling provides a neutral medium of communication between disparate views; it thereby begs, but does not raise, all manner of difficult problems about the nature of the world and of social reality. Some of the philosophical assumptions inherent without different approaches to data modelling are discussed by Klein & Hirschheim (1987a) and will not be reiterated here. Instead, let us consider the social aspects of the practice of data modelling.

In a sense, data modelling appears as a synthesis of the technical (the concepts of a data structure, entities and relationships) and the social (particular user views) but again we can see that the boundary between them is problematic. What is technical and what is social are not given a priori, these are both social achievements. This is brought into particularly sharp relief if one considers data modelling vis-à-vis power and the exercise of control over how organizational members construe the organization and where they perceive the boundary between the technical and the social. In addition to mapping out organizations, data models also create organizational reality by raising, framing and resolving conflicting viewpoints. This is to be found in both academic tests on data modelling and in the literature of practitioners. As for the former, Oxborrow (1986) states:

For example, the separate functions and departments in an organisation are interdependent — they are related to each other in various ways. Such relationships should be reflected in the data structure which underlies the organisation's data. This data structure is commonly referred to as a data model.

Here the assumption that organizations are equivalent to a set of functions and departments is taken as a given. While a document provided to project teams embarking on the development of information systems in the NHS argues:

A data model provides a picture of what we know about our world — in our case about the NHS. It ties down the meanings of the words and numbers we use for planning, managing and monitoring the NHS ( . . ) Data modelling provides a language which enables people to avoid misunderstandings and mistakes ( . . ) This will not only help two people who are looking at one subject, such as manpower, but also people who are looking at work in a healthy authority from different angles, such as medical and financial. A data model can help them to get at the underlying essence of things, however different these things may appear (NHS Information Management Group, 1987).

Thus we see that the methods of data modelling cannot be read as more technical strategies, heuristics, or communications devices for smoothing the path toward successful system implementation because they also function as a means of implicit and explicit social control concerning the legitimate view(s) of organizations and information systems. They appear as technically neutral but as far as organizations are concerned they are socially and politically charged. Without a data model of some form a system cannot be constructed, yet the formulation of this apparently technical object rests on a practice of social control; again that which is purported to be technical is in fact social.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper I have argued that a number of current socially oriented views within the literature on information systems adopt an 'impacts' approach to technology. That is, technology is seen to have an impact on social structures or organizations and this goes hand-in-hand with an implicit restriction of sociology in which IT is treated rather as a black-box in a quasi-deterministic fashion. Drawing insights from the philosophy and sociology of science as well as the sociology of technology I have argued for a more thorough-going sociological analysis of the development of information systems in which the received notion that posits a dichotomy between the technical and the social (what is internal and what is external) is to be challenged. To illustrate the potential of such an approach I have looked at examples from the development of information systems within organizations and shown how they have embedded within them various assumptions and views concerning the nature of decision making, knowledge and expertise, management and organizations. For instance, I have focused on the work of knowledge engineers and data modellers and argued that the 'technical' processes of knowledge engineering and requirements specification are also social practices, and that therefore the social relations between them, users and management, have an important role in the development of information systems. These examples have illustrated the problematic nature of attempting to distinguish the technical from the social in any straightforward and unreflexive way.

At the beginning I stated that there was an evident tendency toward a proliferation of methodologies within the area of information systems. The perspective adumbrated here would suggest that the root of the problem facing systems developers does not lie so much in the lack of the right method but, rather, sociologically impoverished views of organizations and management, and above all their own practices. The striving for better, more formal and explicit, methodologies has organizational implications. For example, there is the question of managerial control over systems development: the more that systems development work becomes formalized the more it becomes open to control by higher management and the more it may be automated — though this is not, of course, the same thing as saying that systems development will therefore be more successful.

In summary then, I have argued that computer science does not develop autonomous technologies which then pose opportunities, issues, or problems in terms of organizational implementation. Rather, computer science embodies ideas about management and organizations, knowledge and decision making, and builds these into specific techniques which are then employed within the social practices in organizations of systems developers. These people are heterogeneous engineers, simultaneously engineering or exercising control over people and artifacts. Given this we can suggest that if the development of information systems in organizations runs into difficulties it may not be because the so-called technical specialists have ignored vital social factors among users but that the assumptions about the social already inherent in their various approaches are flawed. Information systems have to be constructed with a sociologically adequate view of organizations but this requires an understanding of the 'sociology' already implicit within computer science as much as it begs a greater level of sophistication toward organizations. It beholds us to examine the social practices underpinning computer science techniques; to do so would allow us to move away from externalist views of information systems and offer a more substantial understanding of these systems within organizations where the a priori boundaries between internal and external, technical and social would be dissolved.
