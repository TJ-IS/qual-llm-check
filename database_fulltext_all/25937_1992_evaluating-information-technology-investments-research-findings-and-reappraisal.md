# Evaluating Information Technology investments: research findings and reappraisal

L. Willcocks

Templeton College, Kennington, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

Abstract. Investments in Information Technology are large and rising, but frequently the subject of disappointed expectations. Some explanations can be found in how they are evaluated and controlled. The paper reports findings from case study work and a 50 organization research project carried out in 1990–91. It first highlights 10 common problem areas that can be addressed. It then focuses on evaluation at the critical feasibility stage of projects and identifies from the research a number of ways in which evaluation practice can be improved. It finds a fall-off in evaluation at subsequent project stages, little attempt to link evaluation across the lifetime of systems, and a fragmented approach to learning from the evaluation experience for future investments. A number of modern alternatives to traditional financially based investment appraisal techniques that dominate the sample are then critically assessed. Tailored to specific organizational circumstances and projects these offer ways forward. Broader thinking, in terms of Information Systems (IS) investments, and a deeper understanding of IS as human activity systems lead on to multiple effectiveness criteria and related measures. However, major improvements may lie in allying more appropriate techniques and measures to developing evaluation as an organizational and social process, with the deeper organizational learning about IT this would entail.

Keywords: evaluation, information systems, information technology investment.

## INTRODUCTION

It is clear that, on any measure, Information Technology (IT) is big business in private and public sector organizations alike. By 1992, UK company expenditure on IT was exceeding £10 billion per year, equivalent to an average of over 1.2% of annual turnover. Public sector IT spending, excluding Ministry of Defence operational equipment, was over £2 billion per year, or 1% of total public expenditure. According to the Price Waterhouse Review 1991/2, the average annual IT budget in 1990 in companies with a data processing department of five or more staff was £3.5 million, an inflation adjusted rise of 20% over the previous year. A comparison with US companies, which tend to be further down the road in their IT use, may indicate where this is leading. Investment in IT equipment grew from \$55 billion to \$90 billion in the 1980s, an annual growth rate of nearly 15%. In the USA computer and telecommunications now amount to half of the annual capital expenditures of most large firms (Keen, 1991).

This size and continuing growth in IT investments, coupled with a recessionary climate and concerns over cost containment from early 1990, have served to place IT issues above the parapet in most organizations, perhaps irretrievably. Understandably, senior managers need to question the returns from such investments, and whether the IT route has been, and can be, a wise decision. This is reinforced in those organizations where IT investment has been a high risk, hidden cost process, often producing disappointed expectations. This is a difficult area about which to generalize, but research studies suggest that at least 20% of expenditure is wasted and between 30–40% of IT projects realize no net benefits, however measured (see, for example: Kearney, 1987; Eason, 1988; KPMG Peat Marwick, 1990). A more optimistic picture is suggested by a 1990 survey of over 400 companies (Kearney, 1990). However, this contained mixed messages. On the one hand some 17% of new systems did not bring the advantages expected of them, leaving 83% of projects producing benefits as expected or better. On the other, only 11% of respondent organizations were successful IT users according to the relatively modest objective criteria established by Kearney.

The reasons for failure to deliver on IT potential can be complex. Technical, human resource, environmental, organizational and management issues interrelate where explanations are sought. However, major barriers, identified by a range of studies, occur in how the IT investment is evaluated and controlled. For example, Wilson (1991), looking at over 100 companies, found that difficulties with measuring benefits formed the overall major barrier to the formulation and implementation of information systems strategy. Kearney (1990) found the major constraint on further investment in IT was the lack of credibility in how IT benefits were being justified; cost justification credibility formed the second major barrier. These relate back, again, to problems of measurement and evaluation. This is supported by other commentators, who also point to indifferent IT evaluation practice as a major problem area (for example, Hochstrasser & Griffiths, 1991; Strassman, 1990; Touche Ross/IAM, 1991; Willcocks, 1992).

This paper focuses on improving information systems (IS) evaluation practice. It will first highlight common problems encountered by research studies and cases, including the author's own, in appraising and monitoring the IS contribution to organizational performance. Findings from research carried out in 1990–92 on evaluation practice at the critical feasibility stage, and at subsequent stages, will then be reviewed. These results will then be used as a basis for assessing more recent approaches to evaluation, and for developing processes, methods and techniques for better practice. The importance of continuing the evaluation across the lifetime of IT-based systems will emerge as fundamental, but neglected. Above all, with IT development and usage, and evaluation practice itself, yet to be imbedded in the routine texture of how most organizations are managed, the paper will stress the need for evaluation to be set up as a learning process — to build up experience and reduce risk on future IT investments. One conclusion is that at the present stage of information management attained by many organizations, the process of evaluation may well be more important than the individual evaluation techniques adopted.

## EVALUATION: EMERGING PROBLEMS

When discussing evaluation in the context of IT and organizations, it is useful to make explicit the meanings assigned to the terminology employed. Taking a management perspective, evaluation is about establishing by quantitative and/or qualitative means the worth of IT to the organization. Evaluation brings into play notions of costs, benefits, risk and value. It also implies an organizational process by which these factors are assessed, whether formally or informally. To clarify further the definitional minefield, IT is taken to refer to the hardware, software and communications technologies — essentially equipment — and attendant techniques. From a management perspective IS is used here as a wider concept, referring to how designed information flows attempt to meet the defined information requirements of the organization. IS may be more, or less, IT-based. It should be pointed out that the author and others would see an IS as essentially a human activity system and part of social community, incorporating formal and informal elements (Hawgood & Land, 1988; Hirschheim & Smithson, 1988; Stone, 1991; Willcocks & Mason, 1993). Taking a social action perspective on IS leads to an increase in the categories of effectiveness and a multivalent approach to assessment. However, the research reported below uncovered little inclination to adopt a wider social action perspective, or move from a restricted assessment of what Lyttinen, Klein & Hirschheim (1991) have called 'the effectiveness of instrumental action' in its technology, language and organization contexts. As a related point, interviews conducted during the research reported here, revealed a marked tendency amongst respondents to refer to IT rather than IS evaluation. As a result the term IT is used frequently, where felt appropriate, throughout this paper. These issues will receive more attention below.

There are major problems in evaluation. Many managers and stakeholders believe that for competitive reasons their organizations must invest in IT. However, economically they frequently cannot find sufficient justification, and evaluation practice cannot provide enough underpinning, for making the investment. One thing all informed commentators agree on: there are no reliable measures for assessing the impact of IT. However, this does not mean that there are not a number of common problem areas that can be addressed. In addition to those mentioned above, the following were frequently found in the research study and in earlier case study work, and are supported by other researches in the field.

## a) Budgeting practice conceals full costs

Costs can be understated where size of IT budget — present and predicted — is used to evaluate an IT investment proposal. Recent studies show that up to 40% of relevant costs can be outside the traditional IT budget (Hodges, 1987; Keen, 1991; Moad, 1991; Price Waterhouse, 1990; Strassmann, 1990). In one large manufacturing company studied by the author, user department IT and training costs were in fact 29% of total costs, but were hidden in departmental, often non-IT related budgets. Clearly, the IT budget as a control mechanism may need to be reformulated to reflect full costs. IT costs are no longer equitable with the budget of the IT department.

## b) Understating human and organizational costs

As hardware and equipment costs get driven down, IT related human and organizational costs are rising. Strassman (1990) estimated these to be as much again over the lifetime of a system. Hochstrasser (1990) suggests that they can be as much as three to four times as high as hardware/equipment costs. Invariably, however, these costs are rarely fully budgeted for in IT investment proposals, and may partially explain the phenomenon of cost-creep that occurs over the course of most IT projects. In a retrospective analysis of an insurance company database application, the author and project manager analysed human and organizational costs three years after development began. These included such items as training, management and staff time, extra staff, staff turnover, losses in productivity and organizational restructuring during changeover. These were more than double equipment costs, and only 20% were included in the original feasibility assessment.

## c) Understating knock-on costs

There may be political reasons for understating costs, the main one being to gain support for, and acceptance of the project from senior managers. Furthermore, in a recessionary climate, a concern for cost-justification, quick returns and applying IT for cost reduction purposes, would seem to be paramount, as the Price Waterhouse 1991/2 Review shows. Again, this can pressure proponents of IT into understating costs. A major problem area is where knock-on costs are understated. Thus, maintenance costs may well be one to four times as much as systems development costs, and are not avoidable over the lifetime of a system (Laudon & Laudon, 1991; Swanson & Beath, 1989). An additional problem is incurred here. Development costs incur maintenance costs that then feed into the future IT budget. If IT is evaluated by size of spending, the picture will be one of rising investment. However, this will conceal that most of the expenditure will be on existing systems; it will not be focused on future systems possibly critical to the competitiveness or health of the organization.

## d) Overstating costs

Many projects fail to be delivered within time and budget (see, for example, Grindley, 1991; Keamey, 1990; KPMG Peat Marwick, 1990). However, in two projects researched by the author, it was clear that the project managers overstated costs at the feasibility stage with the express purpose of making sure they could deliver systems within time and budget. In one case a £500,000 home help organizer system was delivered to a social services department within the time allocated, in this case three years. In another, a CAD/CAM system was brought into a small manufacturing company within the £2.1 million budget, but two months outside deadline. In each case the project manager revealed political astuteness and gained a reputation for effectiveness.

## e) Using traditional finance-based evaluation techniques

A common evaluation practice identified in the 50 organization research study (see below) could be characterized as cost-benefit assessment described in monetary terms, allied with managerial judgement. The more traditional techniques of relating benefits to the costs of obtaining them are discussed by Coleman & Jamieson (1991), Earl (1989), Farbey et al. (1992) and Parker et al. (1988). The main methods include: return on investment (ROI); discounted cash flow/internal rate of return (DCF/IRR); net present value (NPV) and profitability index (PI); payback period; and present worth. Some limitations of these specific methods can be noted. ROI is widely used as a 'hurdle rate' against which the merits of different projects are assessed. Dangers occur where the method is not allied to discounting cash flow, for example failure to account for variations in timing and risk. For DCF/IRR Coleman & Jamieson (1991) note that it finds favour because of its relative simplicity, but fails to take account of the relative scale of projects and the time loading mentioned in the Net Present Value method. For NPV/PI, much depends on the discount rate adopted. If used alone, normally the project with the highest NPV will adopt. PI is used less for IT projects. PI involves dividing estimated cash flows by the initial capital investment, but, as discussed above, IT projects rarely incur most costs at their beginning. The payback method was frequently used in respondent organizations because it determines the amount of time required for the cumulative cash inflow to equal the initial investment. Managers find attractive the question: how long will the project take to pay for itself? However, Parker et al. (1988) note technical deficiencies with this method.

There are more sophisticated methods for developing a cost-benefit analysis. These include probability of attainment, decision models, break-even analysis, structural models and the hedonic wage model (Sassone, 1988). A number of composite methods have also been developed. However, all the methods reviewed in this section need to be used with some care:

1) In themselves they largely exclude the significant problem of risk. Coleman & Jamieson (1991) convincingly make the case for more widespread use of risk analysis, based round formal techniques like measures of variance, sensitivity analysis and simulation/modelling techniques.

2) Based on monetary value they can exclude costs and benefits that may be difficult to quantify, but could be critical to the evaluation. For example, some of the best returns may well be from IT projects that deliver competitive advantages, improved customer service, higher product quality, innovation and more timely information. One manufacturing company installed a flexible manufacturing system costing \$18 million. The return of the project was only 10% per annum, even taking into account significant labour savings over a 20 year period. The project was appraised as unprofitable against typical hurdle rates of 15% with expectations of payback within five years. However, it offered significant productivity and performance benefits, as well as flexibility. Kaplan (1986) makes the point that the major benefits from computer integrated manufacturing — such as quality, flexibility, responsiveness, functional integration — fall very much in the intangible class.

3) From the above points, it is clear that discount rates may be difficult to arrive at for IT projects. Also, hurdle rates set for non-IT capital investment may need to be lowered for IT with the capability of impacting on competitive advantage, creating new businesses or that represents significant innovation with unclear payoffs. This applies especially to application projects like CIM (electronic mail networks) and to infrastructure investment. These will tend to have a longer useful life than non-IT projects and deserve different treatment in feasibility evaluation. Unfortunately ROI measures can create minimum hurdle rates that shield managers from understanding the differences between types of IT projects, and between IT- and non-IT projects.

4) In his case study work Norton (1986) concludes that, in respect of IT, the more traditional cost-benefit analysis and ROI approaches: are microeconomic, encouraging low risk investments with small returns; are the result of a manufacturing economy that treats labour as an expense; and, the analysis tends to be static and short term.

## f) Inappropriate measures

It is now widely accepted that many widely used measures do not assist the process of establishing how IT adds net value to an organization. Strassman (1990) establishes convincingly that the size of IT spending bears no relationship to subsequent IT effectiveness. Furthermore, IT expenditure as a percentage of turnover, then compared to an industry average, or what a competitor is spending, will do little to focus investment on where IT adds value to the organization (Willcocks, 1992). Internal systems efficiency measures do not relate performance to added value for the organization (Butler Cox Foundation, 1990). The more appropriate measures relate IT spending not to revenue, but to profitability. However, even here the more traditional measures of profit, for example return on assets or return on sales, would tend to discourage the making of IT investments, particularly where the payoffs are likely to be long-term. Keen (1991) suggests that tracking IT progress over time is most meaningful in relation to organizational revenue or profit per employee. This is because IT rarely reduces cost; rather, its main value more often is in changing the cost structure of the organization so that it can increase sales or output without increasing personnel.

The IT impact may also be traced by establishing non-financial measures around key business processes where IT is now being heavily used, for example, ratio of seats occupied to seats available in an airline. Given the unreliability of single measures it may be wise to use several. Butler Cox (1990) suggest that IT expenditure can be usefully controlled and evaluated if measured against revenue, IT spending per employee, business volume (measured non-financially), and key business process measures. Another area that can be usefully evaluated is levels of customer/user satisfaction with what is delivered (Rands, 1991; Remenyi & Money, 1991).

## g) Neglecting 'Intangible' benefits

There are a whole raft of such benefits, some quite critical to operation and future competitiveness and direction, that are regularly omitted from feasibility proposals. This is often on the grounds that they cannot be financially quantified, are not cost justifiable, not certain and/or cannot produce obvious short-term paybacks.

To some extent this caution is understandable. Thus, at the Trustee Savings Bank the Banking Division contains some 1,600 branches. A very large six year strategic Network Redesign project is under way to automate branch administration and sales support systems. According to its Director, a rigorous business case had to demonstrate early payback; therefore, the first two years focused on automation of administration tasks. In fact, subsequent phases produced major benefit opportunities excluded from the original business case, notably increased office space worth £15 million, reduction in headcount, and redesigned branch layout to give 75% of the space to customers and new revenue generating opportunities.

Nevertheless, failure to assess intangible benefits may well check potentially profitable proposals. Some, possibly the major, potential benefits from IT are not measurable using traditional financially-based evaluation techniques. These include: improved customer service, development of systems architecture, greater job satisfaction, higher product quality, improved internal/external communications and management information, gain of competitive advantage, cost avoidance, avoiding competitive disadvantage, and improved supplier relationships. Techniques for their evaluation will be discussed further on.

Another problem in assessing intangible benefits is where evaluation procedures lead to understating costs as detailed in sections a) to c). Lower than anticipated costs can result in a less rigorous search for benefits to match against those costs. Thus, bad practice reinforces itself. One consequence could be the pursuit of very limited objectives relative to what IT could do for the organization.

## h) Not fully investigating risk

There are two major areas of risk that are frequently downplayed in evaluating IT proposals. First, there is the risk of additional costs where implementation may be less than smooth. As one example additional implementation time can add heavy cost burdens. Much of the speed in implementation between 1987–92 in the £2 billion Department of Social Security Operational Strategy can be explained in terms of managerial perception of the otherwise inhibiting costs of delay (Margetts & Willcocks, 1991). The risk of disruption to the organization and to customers, indeed loss of customers, may also not be fully assessed.

The second area involves the risk exposure of the organization in terms of security and what happens when, for example, a complex network management system is no longer functioning. A networked organization like a bank or an airline, and its booking system, need very high reliability and back-up systems. When systems are not working, neither is the business. Managing such risk can incur substantial costs, and only some of the risk can be offset through insurance.

## i) Failure to devote evaluation time and effort to a major capital asset

Many organizations have a number of limitations in their IT evaluation practice. These will be detailed below. This remains surprising in the light of the substantial investment IT now represents in most organizations. Keen (1991) is useful here in suggesting the need to create an IT balance sheet. Senior management rarely know how much capital is tied up in IT resources. In one bank he investigated, management were aware of the \$200 million annual IT expenditure, and the \$120 million value of centrally managed computers. However, when personal computers, departmental telecommunications, small items, and the cost of creating the software and data currently in use were added together, the bank possessed an IT asset worth \$2 billion. Keen's point is a good one. Failure to appreciate the size of this investment leads to IT being undermanaged, a lack of serious attention given to IT investment evaluation and control, and also a lack of concern for discovering ways of utilizing this IT asset base to its full potential.

## j) Failure to take into account time-scale of likely benefits

The search for quick payoffs from IT is understandable. However, even fairly straightforward office systems automation, for example bringing in word processors, can have delayed cost savings. As a matter of experience, the full impact of process innovation — aimed at efficiency and cost savings, that is, doing existing things better — can take over two years to materialize, and would still be difficult to isolate in traditional financial statements like cash flow, and profit and loss statements. As Keen (1991) suggests, we are still learning about the likely time-frame for assessing the impact of IT investments. In fact he suggests that infrastructure investments — for example, those in telecommunications networks, shared database management resources, network management utilities — may be long-term, critical, but difficult to evaluate. They need to be evaluated separately and funded by top or corporate divisional management as a long-range capital investment in line with corporate policy requirements.

The issue of the time-scale of benefits is illustrated by the case of a US manufacturing company (Willcocks, 1989). Here, one casualty of the erosion of market share was the IT budget. The plan to migrate existing applications to a common manufacturing, engineering and purchasing database was deferred. However, sales people were to get laptop computers because competitor salesforces were seen to make good use of these for rapid order entry. The problem rested in the evaluation approach adopted. Traditional cost-benefit analysis proved the need for a freeze on IT spending. Only when the long-term 1987–92 implications of IT spending on core activities were taken into account, did it emerge that without such expenditure the very survival of the firm was jeopardized. As Strassman (1990) establishes, there is no real way of knowing what IT can do for the business, and the risks of not using, as compared with using IT, always have to be significant factors in the subsequent investment appraisal. In the case in question, a useful way of evaluating IT plans was to establish the value-added by comparing the business plan with changes to IT costs over five years, against the business plan without changes to IT costs.

## k) Failure to create a strategic climate in which IT investment can be related to organizational direction

The organizational investment climate has a key bearing on how investment is organized and conducted, and what priorities are assigned to different IT investment proposals. According to Butler Cox (1990), the investment climate is affected by: the financial health and market position of the organization; industry sector pressures; the organizational business strategy and direction; and the management and decision-making culture. As an example of the second, 1989–90 research by Datasolve shows IT investment priorities in the retail sector focusing mainly on achieving more timely information; in financial services, around better quality service to customers; and in manufacturing, on more complete information for decision-making. As to decision-making culture, senior management attitudes to risk can range from conservative to innovative, their decision-making styles from directive to consensus-driven. As one example, conservative consensus-driven management would tend to take a relatively slow, incremental approach, with large scale IT investments being unlikely.

The third factor cited above — the relationship of IT investment to information needs and organizational strategy and direction — can be critical. The case of an advertising agency researched by the author is instructive. In the mid-1980s the agency installed accounting and market forecasting systems at a cost of nearly £100,000. There was no real evaluation of the worth of the IT to the business. It was installed largely because one director had seen similar systems running at a competitor. Its existing systems had been perfectly adequate and the market forecasting system ended up being used just to impress clients. At the same time as the system was being installed, the agency sacked over 35 staff and asked its managers not to spend more than £200 a week on expenses. The company was taken over in 1986. Clearly there had been no integrated plan on the business, human resource, organizational and IT fronts. This passed on into its IT evaluation practice. In the end the IT amplifier effect may well have operated. IT was not used to address the core, or indeed any, of the needs of the business. A bad situation was made correspondingly worse by the application of IT (Willcocks & Mason, 1993).

In the respondent organizations discussed below, strategic misalignments of a less extreme nature were common rather than atypical. Research by others both in the USA and UK suggests that this is a generalizable finding (see, for example, Willcocks & Mason, 1987; Walton, 1989; Ernst & Young, 1990; Scott Morton, 1991). One result is that IT evaluation practice tends to become separated from business needs and plans on the one hand, and from organizational realities that can influence IT implementation and subsequent effectiveness on the other. Both need to be included in IT evaluation, and indeed are in the more comprehensive evaluation methods, notably the Information Economics approach (see below). As will emerge, this discussion of common problems is by no means exhaustive of the problems faced.

## EVALUATING FEASIBILITY: FINDINGS

How do organizations go about IT feasibility evaluation and what pointers for improved practice can be gained from the accumulated evidence? The general picture is not an encouraging one. Organizations have found it increasingly difficult to justify the costs surrounding the purchase, development and use of IT (Hochstrasser & Griffiths, 1991; PA Consulting Group, 1990; Price Waterhouse, 1989). The value of IT/IS investments is more often justified by faith alone, or perhaps what adds up to the same thing, by understating costs and using mainly notional figures for benefit realization (Peat Marwick, 1989). A whole string of second-order costs are often missed out of the evaluation (Hochstrasser & Griffiths, 1991; National Audit Office, 1987, 1989). Hochstrasser & Griffiths found only 16% of their sample relying on rigorous methods to calculate the benefits of investment in IT. Over a quarter of managers did not know (presumably because it was not measured) whether or not IT was producing better or worse returns than other investments. In a survey of 90 managers, Vowler (1990) found 66% admitting that their organizations were poor at measuring the IT contribution to the business. All this is put into sober perspective when set against the Kearney (1990) finding that successful users are twice as likely to set formal targets for return on investment, and to measure the performance of new IT systems against them, as unsuccessful users.

To gain insight into evaluation practice, a survey was carried out in a three month period in late 1990. Fifty organizations responded, drawn from a cross section of small, medium and large private and public sector manufacturing and services. There were 12 financial service companies, eight in manufacturing, six in information technology, four in central government, with the rest spread through retail and wholesale, education, broadcasting, oil, telecommunications, publishing, transport, accountancy and consultancy, computer manufacturing, betting and postal services. Preliminary findings on evaluation at the feasibility stage appear in Willcocks & Lester (1991). This section represents a summary of selected findings following completion of a follow-up interview programme throughout 1991 and early 1992. The individuals who were selected for and completed the 1990 survey questionnaire were all of manager status or above. They were identified as organizationally responsible for and/or knowledgeable about IS evaluation methods and outcomes. In the case of 32 respondents, further in-depth information was gained from follow-up interviews which were up to one to two hours in length. Subsequently some triangulation of IT, senior and user manager viewpoints was achieved by extending the interview programme to 30 minute structure discussions with two other managers in each of the 32 organizations.

In the sample, all organizations completed evaluation at the feasibility stage, although there was a fall-off in the extent to which evaluation was carried out at the later stages of Development, Implementation, Post-implementation and Routine Operations (see Figure 1 for a summary). This means that considerable weight falls on getting the feasibility evaluation right. Some 14% were 'very satisfied' (the results from the evaluation reach a high standard), and 72% 'satisfied' (the organization can use the results with a degree of satisfaction and reliability, though improvements would be welcomed) with the evaluation methods and process. However, these perceptions need to be qualified by the fact that only 8% of organizations measured the impact of the evaluation, that is, could tell us whether the IT investment subsequently achieved a higher or lower return than other non-IT investments. Additionally, there emerged a range of inadequacies in evaluation practice at the feasibility stage of projects. The most common are shown in Figure 2.

Organizations revealed a variety of practice on evaluation at the feasibility stage of projects. Their experience suggests that the need for flexible, regularly reviewed approaches to evaluating intended IT investments. This arises from each organization having its own IS objectives and strategies, which themselves may change over time. Most organizations surveyed used cost-benefit at the core of their evaluation, often of a traditional type. However, it does not follow that this is the most appropriate, or sufficient, way to evaluate IT projects.

The literature shows that the cost-benefit analysis used by organizations is outdated and fails to assess the real worth of IT. Cane (1987) found that organizations had not changed their analysis techniques even though their systems were becoming more sophisticated. Goodwin (1984) shows that if quality is not assessed, projects which could give a better product or competitive advantage are likely to be passed over. The case for enhanced cost-benefit analysis is regularly made. Malitoris (1990) is typical in suggesting that measurement techniques must match the task. Where efficiency and effectiveness objectives are pursued with IT, these are more easily measured using financial appraisal methods, but the rigour applied will vary with the likely size of perceived net benefit. Where inputs and benefits are increasingly intangible but sizeable, as is common when pursuing innovation and competitiveness objectives, then the information economics approach of Parker et al. (1988) becomes more appropriate. These approaches will receive attention in the next section.

However, the sample evidence and interviews indicate that few organizations adopt these techniques, or a planned contingency approach in practice. Thus, 56% of organizations used the same evaluation method for all projects, regardless of the different objectives for and different types of benefit that might be expected from the investment. Of this 56% only 16% reviewed the method on a regular basis as at the end of 1990. Furthermore, in the period 1991–2, interviews in 32 organizations revealed a marked move toward using hurdle rates and shorter time-scales for payback from IT investments — that is, an entrenchment in the use of traditional finance-based evaluation approaches. This invariably reflected concerns over cost containment in a recessionary climate. However, respondents in 10 organizations admitted that it meant missed opportunities for investing in systems that they could see as being of significant business value in two to three years time, and indeed for gaining competitive edge on other organizations not making those investments either. Senior managers increasingly talk of, and are urged toward, the strategic use of IT. This means doing new things, gaining a competitive edge, and becoming more effective, rather than using IT merely to automate routine operations, do existing things better, and perhaps reduce the headcount. However, only 16% of organizations use over 4 criteria on which to base their evaluation. 62% use cost-benefit as their predominant criteria in the evaluation process. 96% use cost-benefit together with one or several of: competitive advantage; service to public; quality of product; job enhancement. Other criteria used by three or less organizations included: improved management information; user requirements and timescale; legal requirements; strategic importance, and organizational requirement and necessity. The survey evidence here suggests that organizations may be missing IS opportunities, but also taking on large risks, through utilizing narrow evaluation approaches that do not clarify and assess less tangible inputs and benefits. There is also little evidence of a concern for assessing risk in any formal manner. However, the need to see and evaluate risks and 'soft' hidden costs would seem to be essential, given the history of IT investments as a 'high risk, hidden cost' process.

Further findings indicate room for improved practice. A sizeable minority of organizations (44%) do not include the user department in the evaluation process at the feasibility stage. This cuts off a vital source of information and critique on the degree to which an IT proposal is organizationally feasible and will deliver on user requirements. Only a small minority of organizations accept IT proposals from a wide variety of groups and individuals. Despite the large literature emphasizing consultation with the workforce as a source of ideas, know-how and as part of the process of reducing resistance to change, only 36% of organizations consult users about evaluation at the feasibility stage, while only 18% consult unions. While the majority of organizations (80%) evaluate IT investments against organizational objectives, only 22% act strategically in considering objectives from the bottom to the top; that is, evaluating the value of IT projects against all of organization, departmental individual management, and end-user objectives. This again could have consequences for the effectiveness and usability of the resulting systems, and the levels of resistance experienced. In one case cited by Hochstrasser & Griffiths (1991) IT professionals installed an integrated Office Automation system with over 100 functions. Later evaluation found users accessing up to only twelve of these. Clearly, earlier evaluation embracing user needs would have stemmed the cost of this overfunctionality. Finally, most organizations endorsed the need to assess the competitive edge implied by an IT project. However, somewhat inconsistently, only 4% considered customer objectives in the evaluation process at the feasibility stage. This finding is interesting in relationship to our analysis that the majority of IT investment in the respondent organizations was directed at achieving internal efficiencies. It may well be, as the Hochstrasser & Griffiths (1991) survey evidence also suggests, that the nature of the evaluation techniques (but also from our own study the evaluation process adopted) had influential roles to play in this outcome.

## FROM DEVELOPMENT TO ROUTINE OPERATIONS

This paper has focused primarily on the front-end of evaluation practice. However, we know too little about IT, how it can be managed and its impacts, and the evaluation techniques at our disposal are too inadequately developed for evaluation to be conceived as anything other than a learning process. In pursuing research on evaluation beyond the feasibility stage of projects, the study found evaluation variously carried on through four main additional stages (see Fig. 1). Respondent organizations supported the notion of the evaluation learning cycle, with evaluation at each stage feeding into the next to establish a learning spiral across time — useful for controlling a specific project, but also for building organizational know-how on IT and its management. The full research findings are detailed elsewhere (see Willcocks & Lester, 1993). However, some of the limitations in evaluation techniques and processes discovered are worth commenting on here.

There were only weak linkages between evaluations carried out at different stages. As one example, 80% of organizations had experienced abandoning projects at the development stage due to negative evaluation. The major reasons given were changing organizational or user needs and/or 'gone over budget'. When the data were reassembled, abandonment clearly related to lack of attention given to these areas in the feasibility stage evaluation. Furthermore, all organizations citing abandonment of projects because they were 'over budget' depended heavily on cost-benefit in the earlier feasibility evaluation, thus probably understating development and second-order costs. Discussions in follow-up interviews with respondents showed clearly that organizations rarely applied their development stage evaluation, and indeed their experiences at subsequent stages, to improving feasibility evaluation techniques and processes.

Key stakeholders were often excluded from the evaluation process. For example, only 9% of organizations included the user departments/users in development evaluation. At the implementation stage, 31% do not include user departments, 52% exclude the IT department, and only 6% consult trade unions. There seemed to be a marked fall-off in attention given to, and the results of, evaluation across later stages. At the implementation stage, 84% do carry out evaluation, but over a third of these use the evaluation only to learn about any narrow changes required during the implementation. 20% of respondents do not carry out evaluation at the post-implementation stage, some claiming that there was little point in doing so (see Figure 1). Of the 56% who learn from their mistakes at this stage, 25% do so from 'informal evaluation'. At the routine operations stage one organization used only 'system reliability' as the criterion for evaluation on a continuous basis. On the whole, the other organizations used several criteria, as shown in Figure 3. However, 17% used 'system availability' alone, while a further 15% used only 'system availability and capabilities' as their evaluation criteria. The significant finding is that at the routine operations stage, only 20% use in their evaluation criteria all of: systems capability, systems availability, organizational needs and department needs.

These would appear to be the most necessary criteria to use and it is surprising to find so many organizations using such a limited number of criteria. One also needs to point out that the bases for evaluation adopted at this stage differ from other stages, with few indications in the research sample either in the survey or in follow-up interviews, of relationships being established between the methods and outcomes of evaluation at the other, and at the routine operations stage. Additionally, it is interesting to note that 42% of the organizations reported on IT performance in routine operations monthly or even less frequently. In such a volatile area, and given the fact that many organizations now require systems to be reliable at all times, a large minority of the sample would seem to be under-reporting IT performance. Swanson & Beath (1989) suggest that evaluation of routine operations should be extensive and the evaluation should be related to the strategic needs of the organization. There is little sign of this occurring in the surveyed organizations.

These points suggest a number of guidelines on how evaluation practice can be improved beyond the feasibility stage. At a minimum these include linking evaluation across stages and time. This enables 'islands of evaluation' to become integrated and mutually informative, while building into the overall evaluation process possibilities for continuous improvement. It is evident that the people who participated or were represented in the evaluation process at all stages could easily be widened to include more or all of the parties affected, including trade unions. This was highlighted in the implementation stage where the 6% who were very satisfied had included all affected parties in the evaluation. The criteria used during the feasibility stage also proved to have an influence on whether the projects would be abandoned at the development stage. The organizations who used cost-benefit analysis allied to criteria based on 'service to customer' or 'service to user' tended to correlate with more successful development of projects.

Some organizations claimed to want to learn from the implementation and post implementation stages for future projects and yet their lack of flexibility did nothing to promote the idea. The number of organizations that stated that they did not learn from their mistakes was even less encouraging for the future of IT evaluation processes in the organizations sampled. However, 83% used direct comparison with the feasibility study at the post-implementation stage, which to some extent endorsed the view of an evaluation cycle. In contrast to this these same organizations also used additional criteria in the post-implementation assessment and failed to update their feasibility evaluation accordingly.

The relative neglect given to assessing the actual against the posited impact of IT, and the fall-off in interest in evaluation at later stages, mean that the effectiveness of feasibility evaluation becomes difficult to assess and difficult to improve. The concept of learning would seem central to evaluation practice but tends to be applied in a fragmented way. Moreover, the increasing clamour for adequate evaluation techniques is necessary, but may reveal a 'quick-fix' orientation to the problem. Furthermore, it can shift attention from what may be a more difficult, but in the long term more value-added area, which is getting the process right.

In practice, managements appear to need to make more explicit what they are trying to achieve through IT/IS evaluation. Ways of doing this are reviewed in the next section. Activating an integrated learning process across the lifetimes of systems would seem to be a necessary central objective. The subsequent route to effective evaluation may well be an uphill climb on an experimentally based evaluation cycle. However, if this sounds difficult, it would seem to be better than the current practice of many organizations of either standing still in their evaluation process, or even regressing through developing over time, from a history of indifferent evaluation practice, a lack of credibility about and belief in the efficacy of evaluation of IS investments in the specific organization.

## BEYOND COST-BENEFIT: MODERN APPROACHES APPRAISED

How far are the prescriptions detailed so far in this paper supported by the more recent advances to be found in the management oriented literature? Much work has been done to break free from the limitations of applying to IT investments the more traditional forms of capital investment appraisal. The major concerns seem to be to relate evaluation techniques to the type of IT project, and to develop techniques that relate the IT investment to business/organization value. A further development is in more sophisticated ways of including risk assessment in the evaluation procedures for IT investment. Again, it is noticeable how most work is being done in the area of feasibility evaluation techniques. There is growing concern for assessing the quality of service from the IT department, sub-contractors or facilities management companies. However, as at 1992, beyond using user satisfaction surveys and similar techniques, there seems little inclination to conceptualize then assess information technologies in a systematic way as being components of information systems that comprise and are made up of the outcomes of social and human activity. Some of the main developments will now be reviewed.

## MATCHING OBJECTIVES, PROJECTS AND TECHNIQUES

One useful way forward on IT evaluation is to match techniques to objectives and types of projects. A starting point is to allow business strategy and purpose to define the category of IT investment. Butler Cox (1990) suggest five main purposes:

1) surviving and functioning as a business

2) improving business performance by cost reduction/increasing sales

3) achieving a competitive leap

4) enabling the benefits of other IT investments to be realized

5) being prepared to compete effectively in the future.

The matching IT investments can then be categorized, respectively, as:

1) Mandatory Investments, for example, accounting systems to permit reporting within the organization; regulatory requirements demanding VAT recording systems; competitive pressure making a system obligatory e.g., electronic-point-of-sale (EPOS) among large retail outlets.

2) Investments to improve performance, for example, Allied Dunbar and several UK insurance companies have introduced laptop computers for sales people, partly with the aim of increasing sales.

3) Competitive edge investments, for example, the SABRE airline reservation system at American Airlines, and Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account system in the mid-1980s.
4) Infrastructure Investments. These are important to make because they give organizations several more degrees of freedom for manoeuvre in the future.

5) Research investments. In our sample we found a bank and three companies in the computer industry waiving normal capital investment criteria on some IT projects, citing their research and learning value. The amounts were small and referred to CASE tools in one case, and expert systems in the others.

Hochstrasser (1990) provides additional detail when suggesting that different business purposes will produce a classification of eight types of project. These are Infrastructure (as above); Cost Replacement (e.g., automating manual activities); Economy of Scale (e.g., EDI allows increased volume of data); Economy of Scope (e.g., a relational database performing an extended range of tasks); Customer Support; Quality Support; Information Sharing and Manipulation (e.g., DSS and MIS); and New Technology (e.g., smart cards and home banking have been late 1980s innovations in some organizations). There seems to be no shortage of such classifications now available (for a review see Willcocks, 1990). One of the more simple but useful is the sixfold classification shown in Figure 4. Once assessed against, and accepted as aligned with required business purpose, a specific IT investment can be classified, then fitted on to the Cost-Benefit map (Figure 4 is meant to be suggestive only). This will assist in identifying where the evaluation emphasis should fall. For example, an 'Efficiency' project could be adequately assessed utilizing traditional financial investment appraisal approaches; a different emphasis will be required in the method chosen to assess a 'Competitive Edge' project. Figure 5 is one view (with no great claims of accuracy) of the possible spread of appropriateness of some of the evaluation methods now available.

At the root of the evaluation methods available he finds seven types of benefit justification. In fact the first type involves little formal justification at all — that is, in the case of a 'must-do' project. An example is a clothing manufacturer required by British Home Stores to adopt Electronic Data Interchange as a condition of business. But should such investments be made at minimum

The decision might be based merely on senior management judgement or 'hunch'. A logic justification would sharpen the business case by arguing how and why these benefits would materialize. A direction justification would specify observable measures, e.g., more purchases, more shoppers. A size justification would give a quantitative estimate of these changes. A value justification would weigh the relative merits of different benefits — e.g., are more shoppers desirable even if each buys less. A money justification would seek to express such benefits in terms of profit and/or revenue. ## Return on management

Strassman (1990) has done much iconoclastic work in the attempt to modernize IT investment evaluation. He concludes that:

'Many methods for giving advice about computers have one thing in common. They serve as a vehicle to facilitate proposals for additional funding . . . the current techniques ultimately reflect their origins in a technology push from the experts, vendors, consultants, instead of a "strategy" pull from the profit centre managers.'

He has produced the very interesting concept of Return on Management (ROM). This receives a detailed description and critical assessment elsewhere (see Willcocks & Lester, 1993). ROM is a measure of performance based on the added value to an organization provided by management. Strassmann's assumption here is that in the modern organization, information costs are the costs of managing the enterprise. If ROM is calculated before then after IT is applied to an organization, then the IT contribution to the business, so difficult to isolate using more traditional measures, can be assessed. ROM is calculated in several stages. First, using the financial results of the organization, the total value-added is established. This is the difference between net revenues and payments to external suppliers. The contribution of capital is then separated from that of labour. Operating costs are then deducted from labour value-added to leave management value-added. ROM is management value-added divided by the costs of management. There are some problems with how this figure is arrived at, and whether it really represents what IT has contributed to business performance. For example, there are difficulties in distinguishing between operational and management information. Perhaps ROM is merely a measure in some cases, and a fairly indirect one, of how effectively management information is used. A more serious criticism lies with the usability of the approach and its attractiveness to practising managers. This may be reflected in its lack of use, at least in the UK, as identified in different surveys (Butler Cox, 1990; Coleman & Jamieson, 1991; Willcocks & Lester, 1991).

## SESAME

IBM have developed SESAME to provide a more flexible approach to cost/benefit analysis. Here the costs and benefits of an IT-based system are compared against an equivalent manual system. Butler Cox (1990) note that it is essentially a method based on establishing the cost of clerical substitution, although it does incorporate the capacity for more sophisticated calculations. This method bases much of the assessment on user opinion. This may involve users more in the process of assessment, but user evaluation may not be in itself a sufficient benchmark of IT effectiveness. Lincoln & Shorrock (1990) also suggest that the flexibility of SESAME is highly dependent on the analyst who will need considerable experience of dealing with complex, unstructured issues. A limitation is that it seeks to compare the cost-benefits of an IT system with those of performing the tasks manually. This may be a cumbersome and less than meaningful comparison to make in many already computerized operations. However, the IT projects to which it has been applied generally reveal good results, according to its proponents (Lincoln & Shorock, 1990).

## From cost-benefit to value

A particularly ambitious attempt to deal with many of the problems in IT evaluation — both at the level of methodology and of process — is represented in the Information Economics approach (Parker et al., 1988). They build on the critique of traditional approaches. Costs have been taken from the IT department who often use chargeback systems, or taken from departmental costings. Benefits are seen differently according to the department carrying out the calculation. Monetary gains may, or may not, be easy to evaluate, but matters like staff satisfaction and competitive advantage are not. One way around this, of course, is to leave them out. At worst, traditional modes of analysis ignore IT as an organizational asset, and all that entails. Another problem occurs. Too frequently a Gresham law applies: technology management problems drive out business perspective and awareness. Furthermore, justification processes often fail to take account, or are unable to quantify new risks associated with IT. Information Economics emphasizes the necessity of dealing with these problems on the way to defining the value of information to an organization.

Parker et al. look beyond benefit to value. Benefit is a 'discrete economic effect'. Value is seen as a broader concept based on the effect IT investment has on the business performance of the enterprise. How value is arrived at is shown in Figure 6. The first stage is building on traditional cost-benefit analysis with four additional techniques for establishing an enhanced Return on Investment Calculation. These are:

a) Value linking. This assesses IT costs which create additional benefits to other departments through ripple, knock-on effects.

b) Value acceleration. This assesses additional benefits in the form of reduced time-scales for operations.

c) Value restructuring. Techniques are used here to measure the benefit of restructuring a department, jobs or personnel usage as a result of introducing IT. This technique is particularly helpful where the relationship to performance is obscure or not established. Research and Development, Legal and Personnel are examples of departments where they may be usefully applied.

d) Innovation valuation. This considers the value of gaining and sustaining a competitive advantage, while calculating the risks or cost of being a pioneer and of the project failing.

Information Economics then enhances the cost-benefit analysis still further through Business Domain and Technology Domain assessments. Thus, on the benefits/value side Parker et al.'s valuable suggestions may be summarized as expansion to include six classes (see Figure 6).

These involve the enhanced view of ROI established above. Strategic Match — that is, assessing the degree to which the proposed project corresponds to established corporate goals; Competitive Advantage — that is, assessing the degree to which the proposed project provides an advantage in the market place; Management Information — assessing the contribution toward management need for information on core activities; Competitive Response — assessing the degree of corporate risk associated with not undertaking the project; and Strategic IS Architecture — measuring the degree to which the proposed project fits into the overall information systems direction. As an example of what happens when such factors and business domain assessment are neglected in the evaluation, Parker et al. point to the case of a large US truck leasing company. Here they found that on a 'hard' ROI analysis IT projects on preventative maintenance, route scheduling and dispatching went top of the list. When a business domain assessment was carried out by line managers, a Customer/Sales profile system was evaluated as having the largest potential effect on business performance. An important infrastructure project — a Database 2 conversion/installation — also scored highly where previously it was scored bottom of eight project options. Clearly the evaluation technique and process can have a significant business impact where economic resources are finite and prioritization and drop-decisions become inevitable.

Except for Strategic IS Architecture, all the above form part of the Business Domain assessment. When the cost/risk side of traditional analyses is expanded, the Business Domain is also required to assess Organizational Risk. That is, looking at how equipped the organization is to implement the project in terms of personnel, skills and experience. A Technology Domain assessment will then embrace three additional classes of risk and uncertainty. IS infrastructure Risk measures the degree to which the entire IS organization needs, and is prepared to support, the project. Definitional Uncertainty assesses the degree to which the requirements and/or specifications on the project are known. Incidentally, research into more than 130 UK organizations shows this to be a primary barrier to the effective delivery of IT (Willcocks, 1991a, b). Also assessed are the complexity of the area and the probability of non-routine changes. Technical Uncertainty evaluates the dependence of a project on new or untried technologies.

Parker et al. have provided an impressive array of concepts and techniques for assessing the business value of proposed IT investments. Their concern for fitting IT evaluation into a corporate planning process, and for bringing both business managers and IS professionals into the assessment process is also very welcome. Some of the critics of Information Economics suggest that it may be over-mechanistic if applied to all projects. It can be time-consuming and may lack credibility with senior management, particularly given the subjective basis of much of the scoring. The latter problem is also inherent in the process of arriving at the weighting of the importance to assign to the different factors before scoring begins. Additionally, there are statistical problems with the suggested scoring methods. For example, a scoring range of 1–5 may do little to differentiate between the ROI of two different projects. Moreover, even if a project scores five on one risk e.g., Organizational Risk, and in practice this risk may sink the project, the overall assessment by Information Economics may cancel out the impact of this score and show the IT investment to be a reasonable one. Clearly much depends on careful interpretation of the results, and much of the value for decision-makers and stakeholders may well come from the raised awareness of issues from undergoing the process of evaluation rather than from its statistical outcome. Another problem area may lie in the truncated assessment of organizational risk. Here, for example, there is no explicit assessment of the likelihood of a project to engender resistance to change because of, say, its job reduction or work restructuring implications. This may be compounded by the focus on bringing user managers, but one suspects not lower level users, into the assessment process.

Much of the criticism, however, ignores how adaptable the basic Information Economics framework can be to particular organizational circumstances and needs. Certainly this has been a finding in trials in organizations as varied as the British Airports Authority, a Central Government Department, and a major food retailer. In the latter case the author and Ong (1991) investigated a three phase Branch Stock management system. Some of the findings are instructive. Managers suggested including the measurement of risk associated with interfacing systems and the difficulties in gaining user acceptance of the project. In practice, few of the managers could calculate the enhanced ROI because of the large amount of data required, and, in a large organization, its spread across different locations. Some felt the evaluation was time-dependent; different results could be expected at different times. The assessment of risk needed to be expanded to include not only technical and project risk, but also the risk/impact of failure to an organization of its size. In a highly competitive industry, any unfavourable venture can have serious knock-on impacts and most firms tend to be risk-conscious, even risk-averse. Such findings tend to reinforce the view that Information Economics provides one of the more comprehensive approaches to assessing the potential value to the organization of its IT investments, but that it needs to be tailored, developed, in some cases extended, to meet evaluation needs in different organizations. Even so Information Economics remains a major contribution to advancing modern evaluation practice.

## CONCLUSION

The high expenditure on IT, growing usage that goes to the core of organizational functioning, together with disappointed expectations about its impact have all served to raise the profile of how IT investments can be evaluated. From the arguments and evidence in this paper it is not only an under-developed, but also an under-managed area which organizations can increasingly ill-afford to neglect. There are well-established traps that can now be avoided. Organizations need to shape the context in which effective evaluation practice can be conducted. Traditional techniques in themselves cannot be relied upon to assess the types of technologies and how they are increasingly being applied in organizational settings. A range of modern techniques can be tailored and applied. Broader thinking, in terms of Information Systems investments, and a deeper understanding of IS as human activity systems lead on to multiple effectiveness criteria and related measures. However, techniques can only complement, not substitute for, developing evaluation as a social and organizational process, and the deeper organizational learning about IT that entails. Past evaluation practice has been geared to asking questions about the price of IT. Increasingly it produces less than useful answers. The future challenge is to move to the problem of value of IT to the organization, and build techniques and processes than can go some way towards answering the resulting questions.
