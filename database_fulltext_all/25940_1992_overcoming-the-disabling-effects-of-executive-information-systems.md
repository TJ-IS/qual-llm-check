# Overcoming the disabling effects of executive information systems

S. Lilley

Department of Accounting and Business Method, University of Edinburgh, 50 George Square, Edinburgh EH8 9JY, UK

Abstract. Computer support for the executive has always been controversial, although the recent expansion in the Executive Information Systems (EIS) market suggests that this picture may be changing. In this paper, the implications of the upward expansion of computer use in organizations are considered. At present, most EIS applications seem to be informed by the same objectivist orientations that earlier Management Information Systems (MIS) embodied. It is suggested that the use of such systems may disable executive creativity, and the paper concludes with the consideration of an alternative framework for executive computer support.

Keywords: executive information systems (EIS), organizational learning.

## INTRODUCTION

There has been much controversy concerning the issue of computer use by senior managers (Rockart & De Long, 1988; Mintzberg, 1973). However, recent press reports suggest that the Executive Information Systems (EIS) sector is one of the fastest growing computer markets in Europe. The computer research company Romtec claim that in the four years up to December 1989, the market in the UK, France, and West Germany grew by 273%, and in the last year alone by 72% (Financial Times, May 15, 1990). According to Rockart & De Long (1988), the use of IT to support executives makes good sense. Despite the complex, unpredictable nature of executive work, there are logical applications of IT that can effectively support executive tasks. Hardware which is faster and possesses expanded capabilities is being coupled with 'user-friendly' software specifically designed for executive use. Often, software is available that can access external information about the environment of the organization through the use of news services and innovations such as Value Added Networks (VANS). Rockart & De Long (1988) also suggest that the increasing computer literacy of top management is a significant factor in the increasing prevalence of EIS. This may result from a personal desire to understand the new technology, from a need to know how subordinates are using it, from a sense of the strategic importance of IT for the firm, or from the promotion of middle managers who relied heavily on IT to do their previous job. Whatever the reason, it seems as if sales of EIS are on the increase, and computers in the boardroom may be here to stay. However, the picture is not all rosy — according to De Long's figures (reported in Computing, July 18, 1991), 50% of mature EIS sites have failed to derive significant benefits.

In this paper it is suggested that EIS implementations informed by the objectivist orientation of traditional Management Information Systems (MIS) may only partially support the executive function, and such systems may, in fact, disable the essential creative elements of executive thought that are required to deal with multiple strategic issues in a complex and changing environment. The systems in use in an organization may have quite profound effects on their users, through their influence on the social construction of the organizational and environmental realities that are created and confronted through participation in the organization.

This is not to suggest that executives who use EIS are abandoning the sources of information that they have traditionally used in their decision making, such as talking and listening to other individuals both within and outside of the organization in which they work (Mintzberg, 1973). In addition, I do not wish to create the impression that the introduction of an EIS into the boardroom will immediately and inevitably lead to impoverished executive creativity. Clearly, the extent to which this occurs will depend largely upon the way in which the system is employed by its users. However, I do believe that uncritical acceptance of the potential benefits of EIS, without consideration of the potential costs of such systems, may lead to increasing rigidity and repetition in executive thought and perhaps, consequently, organizational stagnation. Moreover, these problems are likely to increase as executives come to rely more and more upon such systems as their main source of organizational information.

## THE TWO ASPECTS OF ORGANIZATIONAL ORIENTATION

Argyris & Schon (1978) suggest that organizations can learn in two ways, or at two levels. In 'single loop learning', organizations attempt to fulfil pre-determined objectives, and monitor performance in relation to those objectives. 'Double loop learning' requires that the organizational objectives, and the assumptions underlying them, be evaluated themselves in relation to changes perceived in the environment. There are similarities here with the two processes that make up the human inquiring system described by Singer (1925):

'One is the process of defending the status quo, the existing "paradigm" of enquiry, with its established methods, data and theory. The other is the process of attacking the status quo, proposing radical but forceful paradigms, questioning the quality of the status quo.'

Most organizational behaviour involves the following rules rather than the direct calculation of consequences (March & Simon, 1958). Routine activities may be calculatively maintained, but the knowledge and rules of calculation employed will be those of the society of the calculator. These calculations will be the social actions of an individual who accepts a given system of routines (Barnes, 1988).

'Conformity in the common use of one version of experience results from tendencies to routinization causally sustained by shared interests in communication and intelligibility and opposed by no significant countervailing interests.'

Organizations have standard operating procedures which lead to appropriate rather than optimal behaviour (March, 1981). Organizational participants pursue those allocations, strategies and technologies that have led to criterion success in the past, and tend to avoid those that led to failure (Cyert & March, 1963). This all seems relatively unproblematic for continuing success. However, organizational participants also develop competencies. They improve performance in activities often undertaken, and lose competence at things they do infrequently (Herriott et al., 1985). Within such a framework, it has been shown that organizations can easily become fixated on sub-optimal allocations, strategies, and technologies (Herriott et al., 1985). For example, rewarding the sales team with the highest average performance also reinforces the 'bad' behaviour of the poorer performing salesmen in the team.

Max Weber (1978) suggests that:

'...bureaucratisation ... offer[s] the best chance of putting into practice the principle of division of labour in administration according to purely objective criteria.'

That is, once the objectives of the organization have been specified by double-loop learning, the achievement of those objectives is most effectively accomplished through bureaucratization and routine. However:

'... the bureaucratic machine may, and in fact does, also in its turn create definite obstacles to the possibility of carrying out its task in a manner adapted to the individual case.' (Weber, 1978).

Double-loop learning is required to prevent fixation on sub-optimal activities and to allow the recognition and exploitation of new and different opportunities. It is not suggested here that such a role is to be fulfilled exclusively in the domain of the executive function. However, in hierarchical organizations one would expect a significant proportion of the double-loop learning that occurs to be concentrated in the higher echelons of the organization. Someone must set the objectives in the first place:

'Thus, at the top of a bureaucratic organisation, there is necessarily an element which is at least not purely bureaucratic.' (Weber, 1947)

'The capitalist entrepreneur is, in our society, the only type who has been able to maintain at least relative immunity from subjection to the control of rational bureaucratic knowledge.' (Weber, 1947)

Executives and managers, rather than capitalist entrepreneurs, direct modern organizations. However, if these individuals are to question organizational objectives, and the assumptions underlying them, in order to better direct the organization in its complex and changing environment, they too require some 'immunity from subjection to the control of rational bureaucratic knowledge'. The extension of computer support to the executive function may be eroding that immunity.

## MANAGEMENT INFORMATION SYSTEMS AND AUTOMATION OF SINGLE-LOOP LEARNING

Traditional MIS thinking reflects a rationalistic objectivist philosophy (Stowell, 1991) akin to that underlying Webers' bureaucratization. This is in part probably a reflection of the roots of the discipline in the automation of 'well understood' business activities. A problem is identified, the information required to solve it specified, and an information system is built. However, the environmental contingencies that led to the emergence of the problem are inclined to change, and there are no guarantees that the problem was adequately perceived in the first place. The issue here is not merely that the world is incompletely or inaccurately perceived [although this is important (Slovic et al., 1977; Nisbett & Ross, 1980], but:

' . . . also that actions taken as a result of beliefs about the environment do, in fact, construct the environment, as, for example, in self-fulfilling prophecies and the construction of limits through avoidance of them. (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Weick, 1977, 1979)' (March, 1988, p. 177)

Relating to such an environment (which is seldom, if ever, recognized as having been so constructed) is in part at least, relating to one's own previous actions and reactions reflected through the environment via the information system. Robb (1988) suggests that:

'The prescription, of the structure and contents of the organization's information flows, itself constrains, and probably prohibits, the construction of any understanding beyond that of the taken-for-granted model of the world.

Thus, there develops a mutually causal relationship between the view of the world that gave rise to the information system and that view of the world favoured by system use. The information that is most available is that information consistent with and hence reinforcing of, the model of the environment—organization complex that shaped the system. Alternative viewpoints and courses of action become fewer and further between as information systems develop and their users come to rely on them more and more as their main sources of apparently unequivocal information.

Single-loop learning is institutionalized in traditional MIS. The assumptions underlying one view of organizational reality are embodied in the system which provides, for example, 'variance and exception reports' highlighting critical deviations from target performance. Managers are thus able to zoom in on potential problems and correct them to keep the organization on its pre-prescribed course, as envisaged by prior assumptions. However, the success of this enterprise may result in impoverished creativity in the system users. This institutionalization of single-loop learning seems to be equally important in currently available EIS. As Martin & Clarke (1990) note:

'A vital feature of the EIS is an executive summary of the organization's key operating data, based on an analysis of the organization's key performance indicators, or critical success factors (Rockart, 1979) . . . Data is colour-coded so that the eye is quickly drawn to exceptional items, thus alerting the manager to either notable performance or potential problems.'

Systems supporting 'objective' views of organizational reality are useful, and probably even essential, for the support of everyday operations conducted and managed in the operational functions of the organization. However, in functions devoted to strategy formation where the concern is mainly with interpreting and reinterpreting the organizational situation, and in effecting change in operational behaviour, the acceptance and reinforcement of a consistent and coherent view may disable innovative change and adaptation. Executive flair in the latter activities may be being stunted by an extension of MIS beyond its natural domain.

As briefly noted above, many elements of EIS do not seem to reflect any significant change from the objectivist orientations of MIS. According to Computing (Classe 1991), '... the essential EIS elements are trend analysis, exception reporting, and a "drill-down" capability'. 'Drill-down' involves the ability to descend layers of information to target relevant facts. The important point here is that the executive often receives largely the same view of organizational reality that informed the creation of the MIS in use.

Many of the tools that some EIS provide seem to be those that support the monitoring and regulatory functions of the single loop orientation. We have already considered the role of exception reporting in facilitating this approach. Moreover, the drill-down facilities described above will serve only to make the execution of this function more effective so long as the information that the executive drills down into is the same as that presented in the traditional MIS that many EIS sit above. The ability to drill down does present opportunities for critical reflection on the part of the executive user, but these opportunities will undoubtedly be limited if the direction of his or her drilling is largely determined by a conventional view of the organizational structure, reflected in the data structures of the system. Trend analyses may also be used merely to ascertain whether the organization is drifting towards or away from its pre-prescribed development path. However, the potential for alternative and more creative uses for this facility does seem to be present. The extent to which this opportunity is grasped will largely depend upon the orientation of the user.

Providing the information necessary for keeping the organization on a pre-defined trajectory is certainly not an insignificant achievement.

'But the more difficult and more important task for information engineering involves the design of a system for an imprecise and changing decision structure. The relevant question is: How do you construct an account when you do not know when that account is going to be used, or by whom, or for what purpose, or in what context.' (March, 1988)

How can we support the double loop organizational orientation that executives require? It would seem that the last thing they require is an upward extension of the MIS.

## THE TECHNOLOGY OF FOOLISHNESS

March (1988) has considered the requirements of a mechanism aiding the development of interesting goals. He suggests that some strategy is needed for suspending rational imperatives toward consistency in order to allow the discovery or creation of novel ideas about organizational purpose.

'Contemporary theories of decision-making and the technology of reason . . . [have] . . . reinforced the uncritical acceptance of a static interpretation of human goals.' (March, 1988)

I think that March uses 'technology' in this context for dramatic effect, but the terminology employed seems to echo the description of the deleterious consequences of an over reliance on an objectivist MIS that were presented above. According to March, 'playfulness' is what is required to rectify the problem. 'Playfulness is the deliberate, temporary relaxation of rules in order to explore the possibilities of alternative rules'. Play and reason are thus seen by March as functional complements, although he suggests that over emphasis on rational consistency has rendered them behavioural competitors, with play the loser in the battle for attention.

March proposes a 'technology of foolishness' to enable the playful aspect of decision making to re-assert itself. Within the organizational setting he suggests that individuals may be made more playful through encouragement of '... the attitudes and skills of inconsistency'. Moreover, he also suggests that organizational structures and procedures can be made more playful '... by permitting (and insisting on) some temporary relief from control, coordination, and communication'. These prescriptions do not seem to form an easily usable 'technology'. Thus, in the next section an attempt is made to describe a potential method for providing computer assistance for this orientation, and thus facilitate the double-loop learning aspect of the executive function.

## THE 'EXTERORIZATION' METHOD AND DOUBLE-LOOP LEARNING

The requirements of the creative orientation for the reformulation of organizational purpose, could be seen to be similar to the requirements of expository writing. Expository writing is a systematic account, or explanation, of a subject. It concerns the type of expression employed in academic journals. The task of expository writing:

'... involves a possibly disorganized subjective representation which must be translated, and eventually accommodated, to the world of ideas (Popper's World Three). There are two separate demands in such a task: saying what one thinks, and saying it so that it connects to what already exists.' (Green & Wason, 1982)

Creativity in the organizational context involves the production of novel views of organizational purpose and their integration with, and moderation of, the perceived realities of organizational activities. Ideally, double-loop learning should identify opportunities that the organization is well placed to exploit because of its present assets. In March's terms, 'play' is only the temporary suspension of our standard view of 'reason'. We should not ignore the organization that will be subject to any re-orientation. Rather, we should consider both the present organization and the suggested new views or organizational reality, in the same way that someone playing 'Scrabble' considers both the state of the board, and the letters in her/his hand (Latour, 1988). This orientation involves '... saying what one thinks, and saying it so that it connects to what already exists' (Green & Wason, 1982).

Green & Wason (1982) discuss the importance of the affective elements of expository writing that have frequently been ignored in cognitivist accounts. They suggest that the critical work demanded by expository writing may inhibit the production of creative texts. We can see similarities here with the critical analysis to which new strategy ideas are subjected in the organizational setting. In order to preserve creativity in writing, Green & Wason assume that the writer must regard him/herself with openness and trust.

'By openness we mean a freedom of access to our own thoughts and feelings, and by trust we mean the toleration of this aspect of ourselves on paper. Openness relates to the process of generating ideas, and trust to the process of translating them into words. These attitudes control the cognitive component.' (Green & Wason, 1982)

The last sentence of the above quote reveals the extent to which these attitudes are considered important. Lack of openness and trust stifles the creative component of writing before it gets expressed. Creative cognitive activity is killed by distrust of the value of our contribution — we become close-minded and accepting, rather than open-minded and challenging (Singer, 1925). Comparisons with acceptance and questioning of the 'given' wisdom of the accepted organizational reality are self evident.

So how does one both say what one thinks, and say it so that it connects to what already exists? Two different skills are involved — saying something, and saying it so well that others can understand it. The exteriorization method, independently formulated by Elbow (1973), and Wason (1970), assumes that these two demands are incompatible and cannot be met simultaneously. In the executive function, one is required to say something novel, and integrate it with the present organizational reality to produce competitive advantage. In both expository writing and executive creativity, the second part of the task involves consideration of the consistency and coherency of what has been said, along with its interpretation by others. The encroachment of the second part of this task in the first part, may prevent the production of anything novel in the first place. Thus, the solution proposed in the exteriorization method is the seriation of the two distinct tasks that are required.

In applying this method of expository writing, the first draft involves an uncritical 'exteriorization' of initial thoughts about a subject. Some conscious controls will inevitably interfere, but the deliberate absence of critical analysis and the temporary toleration of disorder and contradiction should enable greater creativity. Whatever is written is taken to be acceptable, grammar and style are disregarded. Thus trust is encouraged, and openness supported as the writer attempts to suspend judgement to prevent premature filtering or negating of ideas that may be internally incompatible, or incompatible with the existing world of ideas. In the second draft the material produced is reordered and changes made to assimilate the novel with what already exists. Further trust is encouraged by the existence of something novel that can be moulded, shaped, and integrated with the accepted wisdom on the topic under consideration.

'The writer is set free to believe and set free to doubt — but not at the same time. Spontaneity and criticism, the two elements of creative work, are seriated in a manner which has been found to occur naturally, both in problem-solving (Tweney et al., 1980) and in the attempts of young children to explore the physical world (Karmiloff-Smith & Inhelder, 1974/5).' (Green & Wason, 1982)

Green & Wason point out that the critical factor in the initial exteriorization phase is the belief that writing in a less restricted manner is acceptable. Such a belief is thought to lead to '... products which are both more original and more effective because the method both frees cognitive capacity, and encourages the development of openness and trust.'

## EXTERIORIZING CREATIVITY IN THE EXECUTIVE FUNCTION

So what are the implications of the exteriorization method for executive double-loop learning? The attitudes of openness and trust seem to be equally useful in this situation. Suspension of critique and analysis in the first stages of idea generation may facilitate the production of creative views of organizational purpose. The use of EIS in this stage would seem to be particularly detrimental to the creativity required. What is suggested instead, is that computer support should be provided for the second stage of the process, the 'doubting phase' (Elbow, 1973).

The social setting in which executives produce potential strategies should be free of formal constraints and encourage spontaneity, trust, and openness, with critical analysis and other constraining restrictions on thought temporarily suspended. These proposed changes in orientation are primarily dependent on the ability of executives to establish and sustain a temporary belief in the validity of inconsistent and 'wacky' ideas. Such an approach is probably the antithesis of the traditional safe stewardship role that is normally fulfilled by the executive function of large organizations. Although one can envisage how the over-use of information systems during the early stages of strategy formulation can further exacerbate the problems associated with routinization, prescriptions for improving the creativity of executives are much harder to provide. Presumably a great deal of original thinking will need to be devoted to this issue before one can expect the potential for greater executive originality to arise.

The attitudes of executives have a large part to play in some of the disappointing results so far derived from EIS implementations. The single-loop thinking embedded in some current EIS implementations will do little to constrain the most creative executives, and even the most imaginative systems will be unlikely to fulfil their promise in the hands of an executive for whom rule following is second nature. The latter need a change in attitude, rather than a change in the technology available to them. Some executives may initially need to use the EIS as a tool to keep the organization on track in order to familiarize themselves with its basic facilities, before taking the plunge to go on to experiment with it and use the system in more creative ways. They may need to understand their new toy through using it to support their familiar activities, before they can employ it to try out radical new ideas.

## SYSTEMS SUPPORT FOR THE DOUBTING PHASE

Robb (1988) has addressed the problem of decision support for those involved in dealing with novel organizational problems. He suggests that recent technological developments may provide a way forward. It is proposed here that the technologies described by Robb may provide the type of support the doubting phase of the exteriorization method requires. Robb (1988) points out that the use of intelligent front-ends as inquiring systems, interfacing users with data sources, potentially opens all available data to inquiry. Moreover, the use of natural languages may enable even the most unskilled users to design and structure their own inquiries, regardless of the particular logical structure employed to organize the transaction processing underlying the data required.

The products of the first stage of the exteriorization method may be tentatively formulated into new views of the organization and its purpose, which can be tested against the available data on the organization in novel ways. The potential of the new ideas so generated could be quickly assessed, without the pre-prescription of organizational data flows embodied in traditional systems serving to make such a view practically unattainable. Executives may also be able to employ the systems to try out 'stupid' things, ideas that they would not dare ask their research staff to undertake for fear of them producing an enormous tome on the reasons why the request was unreasonable. Indeed many current EIS implementations do seem to offer some limited opportunities to ask 'stupid' questions, and this freedom from dependence on the often staid and condescending views of the organization's information systems department may, in itself, be a major breakthrough.

The ability to experiment with different organizational realities becomes a possibility when the strictures of the present organization are completely ignored in the creative phase, and when they can be treated flexibly in the critical assessment phase.

Many EIS are built upon a relational database, and many executives do use them in a flexible way. However, many systems are not providing this potential and one must assume that many executives are not using this potential when it is provided — 50% of mature EIS are failing to deliver significant benefits. These opportunities need not necessarily be restricted to executives.

There may be advantages to the generation and rapid testing of novel orientations at all levels of the organization.

Some of the currently available EIS do incorporate modelling (or, 'what if?') facilities, and these systems may thus offer some support for the doubting phase. Such systems may be considered in some ways to be hybrids of traditional MIS and the somewhat later development of Decision Support Systems (DSS). However, as Martin & Clarke (1990) note:

'This apparent similarity with DSS will need careful examination in the context of the degree of structure and uncertainty in senior managerial problem solving. Traditional DSS deal with relatively well-structured problems with quantifiable uncertainty. But senior managers deal with strategic issues involving relatively unstructured and ill-defined problems. So the modelling facilities in EIS may not add significantly to the benefits for top managerial interactive use.'

The use of systems incorporating intelligent front-ends as inquiring systems interfacing users, directly with data sources, should alleviate the problems of lack of structure in the type of 'what if' modelling that is required at the executive level.

Other advances in information and communication technologies (such as increased power and storage capacity), provide the potential for relatively cheap information distribution, not just for executives, but throughout the organization (Perez, 1985). The cost of the collection of information by organizations has not been reduced by anything like the same extent, and consequently the type and amount of information that is actually collected is still, and will remain, a constraining factor. However, organizations have at their fingertips the possibility of providing, at a small direct economic cost, extremely wide access to their databases and informational resources. It is therefore questions of the organizational, and indirect economic costs and benefits of such an open policy that organizations must address. Are organizations ready and able to tolerate novel views of their purposes from executives, let alone from the lower echelons of the organizational hierarchy?

## CONCLUSION

The article from Computing cited earlier appeared under the heading: 'EIS: More than just an executive toy?' The systems described in the article seem to be much less than executive toys. They present opportunities for executives to tighten their hold on operational control and ensure that the organization stays firmly on its given path. But with the notable exception of the external information provided, they may present fewer opportunities for reassessment of the adequacy of the present organizational reality and purpose for dealing with the exigencies of the changing world that the organization confronts. Rather, such systems seem destined to extend the job of unquestioning regulation right through to the top of the organization. If such systems have a role in organizations, it is surely not at the very top of the organizational hierarchy where their disabling of creativity may have the greatest costs. A recent press article suggests that EIS may be finding a home outside the boardroom.

'Executive Information Systems . . . are proving a misnomer. Instead of appearing in the offices of top executives, they are finding a home on the desks of middle managers eager to analyze what is happening within the company.' (Financial Times, March 1, 1990)

It is encouraging that executives may be avoiding the disablement described in this paper. However, the deleterious effects of objectivist systems are likely to impair the creativity of middle managers, who are often required to implement the strategic changes formulated at the executive level. Many EIS as they stand at the moment seem to be simply glorified MIS, presenting similar problems to executives as the latter presented to junior and middle managers. The aim still seems to be to guess in advance the informational requirements of the user, and to construct a system that will provide them.

What this paper has attempted to advocate as an alternative is the use of a more flexible device that is capable of generating one-off novel reports quickly, that are informed by creative views of organizational reality produced by the sort of 'brainstorming' that occurs in the first stage of the exteriorization method. The creative aspect of the direction of organizations should not be snuffed out by an over reliance on devices informed and designed around a static view of organizational objectives and reality. The pre-prescribed and taken-for-granted data structures embodied in traditional information systems, may virtually serve to guarantee the maintenance of a sub-optimal and predictable organization, despite the myriad of changing opportunities that may be available. Taken-for-granted assumptions must be questioned.

Intelligent front-ends accessing relational databases provide opportunities for the testing of new frames of reference by the enlightened executive user. The conditions for the generation of novel views may be provided by the application of the exteriorization method of creative production to the executive function, although the practicalities of introducing and supporting such an approach may present significant difficulties. What seems to be lacking is not only the corporate will to make available access of the type described but also, and perhaps more importantly, the encouragement of a playful attitude towards information. The serendipity of information (March & Sevon, 1984) is there, what is required for its exploitation is a temporary dismissal of its conventional utility.

## Biography

Simon Lilley is in the process of completing a PhD under the auspices of the Programme on Information and Communication Technologies (PICT) at the University of Edinburgh. He studied for his first degree, in Psychology, at University College, London. His doctoral research focuses on the impact of integrated computer based information systems on the refining sector of the oil industry. Other interests include a more general concern with the social shaping of technology, accounting information systems, and the role played by computerized information systems in shifting patterns of organizational control.
