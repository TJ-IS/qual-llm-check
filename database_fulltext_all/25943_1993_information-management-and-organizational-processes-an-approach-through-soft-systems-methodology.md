# Information management and organizational processes: an approach through soft systems methodology

P. Checkland and S. Holwell

University of Lancaster, UK

Abstract. This paper explores an approach to understanding information provision in organizations which is built around soft systems methodology (SSM). It also, from recent research in both industry and the National Health Service, derives a sense-making framework for work of this kind. A view of the fundamental nature of information systems is presented. This is compatible with SSM, and uses of SSM in information-focused studies are illustrated. These cover the rethinking of the Manufacturing Function in the Shell Group and a number of studies in the NHS concerned with the information implications of current changes. Out of this work, in order to make sense of disparate experiences, a framework is derived which captures three necessary domains (conceptual, organizational, technological) whose interaction defines an organization's response to its information provision.

Key words: information, information system concepts, soft systems methodology.

## INTRODUCTION

The first true printed book in Europe, the Gutenberg Bible, appeared in 1455, and by 1501 a prescient Pope was suggesting to bishops that the control of printing might be the key to preserving the purity of the faith (Roberts, 1980). Thus was the importance of recorded information in changing Western culture quickly recognized. Nowadays, it is a general view that the creation and management of its information resource is an important issue for society, and for all organizations if they are to survive through times of turbulent change.

This paper examines an approach to managing information provision in organizations which is based upon soft systems methodology (SSM).

Over the last 20 years SSM has been developed as a general approach to tackling the messy problem situations with which managers of all kinds and at all levels have to cope. SSM grew out of failed attempts to apply the methodology of systems engineering in such situations.

Its essential nature is that it is an iterative system of logical and cultural inquiry in which the real-world situation is examined through cultural analysis and through comparisons between (parts of) the situation and models of purposeful activity deemed relevant to it. The models are, in general, neither accounts of real-world action nor Utopian designs; they are merely notional purposeful 'holons' (Checkland, 1988a), epistemological devices which help to structure a debate. The object of the debate is to learn a way to changes which represent accommodations between different interests and views, accommodations which emerge as both feasible and desirable. Figure 1 shows the general shape of SSM and Fig. 2 shows a model of the kind used in SSM; this one is derived from the mission statement of the furnishing company IKEA, concerned with obtaining and marketing home furnishings of good design and function at prices so low that a majority of people can afford to buy them. Such models help to structure the debate focused on possible changes which would bring about improvements. Or they may be the basis of coherent discussion of information requirements. Figure 2 could initiate and structure discussion of IKEA's basic information requirements as defined by the mission statement.

Throughout the development of SSM, in fact, (Checkland, 1972, 1981; Wilson, 1984; Checkland & Scholes, 1990) attention has frequently been given to problems of information provision in organizations, that being a common feature of many problem situations—see, for example, Checkland & Griffin (1970), Wilson (1984) and Checkland (1988). The approach is in fact now much used in work aimed at providing appropriate information strategy and information systems within an organization—see, for example, Avison & Wood-Harper (1990), Central Computer and Telecommunication Agency (1989), Patching (1990) and Schafer (1988).

In 1988, giving the 2nd Annual Rank Xerox Lecture, Checkland set out a view of information system development which derives from the experience of developing SSM. That view will be summarized here; then more recent work is described which illustrates and extends it, work which shows SSM in action as a means of creating and continually updating information provision in an organization.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS: A SYSTEMS VIEW

A systems-thinking-based view of the nature of information systems and hence of how to create them may be summarized as follows (see Checkland & Scholes, 1990, for a more detailed exposition of this argument).

1 There are myriad facts about the world which can be stated neutrally—such as the fact that the authors were brought up in Birmingham, England and Canberra, Australia, and that they are members of Lancaster University.

2 Usually, however, data do not keep their virginity: we accept them, and in doing so interpret them, in a context of meaning (Holwell, 1989; Jackson, 1989). So we may usefully use a different word, 'information', for the product of the act of interpreting data in a particular context at a particular time. Thus:

'The sales figures for the new product in its first month' are an item of data, but will not be seen neutrally by the Sales Manager, the Sales Representative, the Production Planner or the person responsible for the advertising campaign.' (Checkland & Scholes, 1990, p. 54.)

3 What are usually referred to as 'information systems' are in fact not that: they are data manipulation systems. A true information system would consist of data manipulation (which machines can be set up to do) together with meaning attribution, which is a uniquely human act.

4 The reason for creating such systems is to support purposeful action in the world; hence the definition of what information systems an organization needs must start with the world views which make the organizational activity meaningful. This may be done via the use of SSM, using the debate to learn a way to activity systems which are generally agreed to be relevant and which require information support.

5 The information support relevant to purposeful activity may be defined by analysing SSM activity models, asking what information flows would be necessary to support the structured set of activities in the model in their context. Thus, if we are taking seriously the mission statement of the IKEA Company, we could use the model of Fig. 2 to define the crucial information flows needed to pursue that mission. From these information flows the necessary data structures and the way they need to be manipulated can in principle be worked out. System design can then follow.

6 Thus, in logic, the sequence of considerations leading to working information systems will be as follows:

(a) meanings attributed to the world by the people concerned;

(b) purposeful activity, purposeful in the light of those meanings;

(c) information flows which are relevant to the activities; and

(d) data structures and ways in which they could be manipulated to yield appropriate categories of information.

This is the sequence which logically should be followed if appropriate information systems are to be coherently defined, designed and implemented.

We may now illustrate these ideas in action in recent work in industry and in the National Health Service.

## SSM IN ACTION IN CREATING INFORMATION SYSTEMS: INDUSTRY

Checkland & Scholes (1990) describe the reorientation of the Manufacturing Function (MF) in the Shell Group. The head of the MF committed himself to a process of exploration, rather than imposing his own ideas, and the project was carried out using SSM in workshops and seminars which eventually involved virtually all of the 600 members of this head office function. The project was carried out initially in a series of 2-day workshops with about 15 participants, in which many models were built which were thought relevant to various versions of the possible role of MF in Shell. Comparison of these models with the participants' knowledge of the real world of MF led to new choices of relevant system and eventually to a new concept of MF as an internal service business, following service business concepts expounded by Normann (1984). One participant who had been at several workshops eventually summarized the corporate learning which had occurred, in a way which most people recognized, when he said:

'Although we could not have articulated it at the start, we now see that we were thinking of MF as being concerned with the creation and preservation of skill pools. The new concept is to see MF as operating a definable service business, at several levels over several time scales, oriented to enabling our customers to meet their customers' needs more effectively.'

A new organization structure for MF appropriate to the new concept was developed and implemented, and a new project evolved of creating the appropriate information systems to support the now reoriented MF. The new structure entailed a dozen sections each with a defined group of 'customers'—who might be, for example, people running Shell plants in south-east Asia, or (for the section concerned with formulating manufacturing strategy) the main board of the Shell Group.

Following the implementation of the new structure and processes in MF, work on information systems began with another workshop which brought together the heads of the new sections. At the workshop SSM-style activity models like that in Fig. 2 were built, models which were relevant to their perceived role in providing a particular kind of service to their defined group of 'customers' within the Shell Group. These models were used to define both the information categories required to perform the activities in the models and those generated by performing them. These information categories were assembled in a matrix which linked activities on one axis with information categories (whether required inputs or generated outputs) on the other. A combined matrix for the whole of MF then provided a basis for MF's information strategy, and work began on information system design.

No one concerned with the study imagined either that the new structure and processes of MF, together with its new information systems, represented a permanent solution to the question of MF's role in the Shell Group: the real world is not stable enough for that! But the SSM-based process used to achieve the reorientation of MF provides a mechanism which, if evoked periodically, will ensure that the activities of MF and its attendant information strategy continue to evolve coherently.

This experience was one in which the concern was the conceptualization of MF as a distinct organization within the Shell Group, followed by the definition of required information systems. The sequence of 2-day workshops saw the expression of many different views on the role of MF, with about 25 different activity models being built and debated in half a dozen workshops. The workshop process can be seen as that of finding an accommodation between the various conflicting views, leading to the new conceptualization of MF as an internal service business. Once that product of the accommodation process had been reached, then it was possible to shift attention from the activities of MF to the supporting information systems. It is worth remarking that if the work had addressed the information questions too early in the process, the study would undoubtedly have led to the information systems needed by the 'skill pool' concept of MF—the concept which was superceded and rethought in the workshop debates.

## SSM IN ACTION IN CREATING INFORMATION SYSTEMS: THE NATIONAL HEALTH SERVICE

The British National Health Service (NHS) remains a tax-funded service free at the point of delivery, but under the current changes imposed by Government the way it is managed is changing significantly in ways which have not been tested in pilot schemes or thoroughly thought through. So the NHS, the UK's largest employer, with almost a million staff, is in a state of some turmoil. It is an arena much in need of ways of conceptualizing the change process it is undergoing, and in the last few years has provided fertile ground for the use of SSM.

Structural change is not new to the NHS. In 1983 the report of Sir Roy Griffiths, accepted by the then Secretary of State, led to the introduction of general managers at several layers in the organizational hierarchy, these being 'charged with the general-management function and overall responsibility for management's performance' (Griffiths, 1983, p. 5). Sir Roy's concerns are revealed in a striking sentence from his report:

'... if Florence Nightingale were carrying her lamp through the corridors of the NHS today she would almost certainly be searching for the people in charge' (p. 12).

However, only a few years after this change had created the role of District General Managers, to whom Unit General Managers reported (a Unit being, typically, an acute hospital or a group concerned with community services) the most recent changes have transformed completely the relationship between District and Unit managers. The latter are now 'providers' of health care services which are bought by Districts (or consortia of Districts) as 'purchasers'. Negotiated contracts, rather than reporting responsibility, will now link Districts and Units, and, in addition, Districts as purchasers are not required to purchase from their local hospitals as providers. It is hoped that this 'internal market' will lead to greater efficiency. In parallel, other initiatives have overlapped and interacted with the major shift to negotiated contracts between purchasers and providers. These include: the introduction of 'medical audit' procedures to ensure the good quality of services provided; the introduction of 'resource management' (aimed at involving clinicians in the costing of services provided and matching them to financial resources); and the setting up of 'clinical directorates' with budgets, this bringing clinicians into managerial roles in hospitals. Finally, some general practitioners will also choose to be 'budget holders', thus becoming purchasers of health care for their patients from either local or more distant hospitals.

All of these changes require information provision way beyond anything previously in existence in the NHS.

Work in a number of hospitals in recent years has convinced us that in terms of organization structure, they are best thought of as sets of skill pools, this reflecting the strong emphasis in the NHS on the professional background and allegiance of individuals. But of course any 'episode of care' in which a patient receives appropriate treatment is likely to require a contribution from many skill pools: surgeons, doctors, nurses, radiologists, pharmacists, physiotherapists, etc, etc. And moreover, under the conditions of the purchaser-provider relationship, information on the disparate contributions to each and every episode of care needs to be assembled, that information including detailed costings for the different contributions. Logically, that is a prerequisite for the negotiation of realistic contracts with purchasing Districts or fund-holding general practitioners. Actual information provision in hospitals has been extremely rudimentary in comparison with what is now required, and the situation is not helped by thinking which resorts too quickly to consideration of existing, packaged, off-the-shelf computer systems. That is to start at the wrong end of the sequence in which meanings and purposes lead to action which requires information support such as computer systems might provide.

In addressing these problems we have found SSM useful in providing, via its models of purposeful activity, a means of introducing coherent discussion of purposes, activities, structures and information needs. Once necessary information has been defined, from agreed accounts of desirable activity, sensible discussion of the information technology (IT) relevant to such information can begin. Many putative 'information strategies' written in the NHS, in fact, have been essentially IT strategies based on unexamined, taken-as-given, purposeful activity. A compilation of 38 such strategies submitted by their purchasers and providers to a Region of the NHS in 1991 pays no attention to conceptualizing either the activity which the information systems will serve, or the structures for carrying out the activity (North-West Region Regional Health Authority, 1991).

In terms of the analysis in the first section of this paper, the current information-provision problems in the NHS can be stated in the following way. Government has imposed a new way of conceptualizing the provision of health care; the 'meanings' have been changed by the imposition of the purchaser-provider relationship. Hence what is needed is examination of the purposeful activity necessarily implied by that relationship, and its embodiment in a particular organization structure, enabling needed information to be defined and IT to be selected coherently. Recent studies have illustrated the complexity of that problem.

As part of studies in hospitals which have included Huddersfield Royal Infirmary, Kidderminster General Hospital, Lancaster Royal Infirmary and Stepping Hill Hospital, Stockport, it has been found useful to build SSM-type models derived from the purchaser-provider relationship and to use them to examine information needed in a particular organizational structure.

These studies were carried out in a variety of circumstances. One was an appraisal of the incremental process by means of which an acute hospital created a Clinical Information System which had the strong support of participating clinical consultants since it enabled them to treat their stream of patients as a research object, leading to ongoing learning about diagnoses, treatments and outcomes. Others were in hospitals which were being funded by their Regional Authorities to develop information systems under the 'resource management' initiative aimed at improved patient care through the better management of a finite resource. Here we were normally working with project teams, offering modelling and facilitating support. Our aim was to inculcate a particular way of thinking about such problems in the hospitals in question and to develop the approach further.

Given the imposed roles of 'purchaser' and 'provider', and the requirement that they link through negotiation of contracts for services, it was found very useful to express the two roles as activity models of the kind used in SSM. Figures 3 and 4 show respectively models of 'purchaser' and 'provider' in the context of the NHS. These are holons which express the purposeful carrying out of the two roles. Any actual NHS District or Unit will have to find some way of embedding the logic of such activity within its own structures in a way which is locally congenial. Both models are at a low resolution level, picturing the broad context of purchasing and providing, but can be expanded to higher resolution levels. In work in acute units, for example, it was found useful to expand activity 11 of Fig. 4 ('Provide services') into a model with 19 activities, and then to expand again some of the activities in that model, at which point the activities were at the level which describes what most people think of as 'hospital activity'—for example, 'Request patient to attend', 'Carry out medical examination', 'Inform GP of outcome and discharge', etc.

Activity modelling of this kind can now provide a basis for discussion of locally appropriate organization structures for carrying out the provider role. Many hospitals are now introducing new structures as part of the current changes in the NHS. A common solution is the introduction of 'clinical directorates' through which budgets for specialties are managed, often with doctors in management roles. Conceptualizations of the kind in Figs 3 and 4 enable the relation between such structures and the hospital activities to be coherently discussed.

Similarly, the models offer a coherent basis for defining information-related activities. Such activities are relevant to both contracting to provide a particular service and to the delivery of that service, enabling it to be planned, delivered, monitored and improved. Figure 5 illustrates some information-related activities relevant to the provider model of Fig. 4. They indicate the kind of costing systems needed to aggregate the contributions to any particular 'episode of patient care', each of which will inevitably involve many of the hospitals' professionals—doctors, nurses, radiologists, physiotherapists, caterers, etc.

It is at this stage that discussion of IT becomes relevant and appropriate. With both well-defined purposes and possible organization structures, it is now realistic to ask questions concerning possible architectures of computer-based systems which might provide the data manipulation required by the organization, given those structures and purposes. If discussions start from consideration of IT (and especially if vendor-driven!) it is unlikely that the path to effective enactment of the purchaser and provider roles will be smooth.

## DISCUSSION

The recent experiences in both industry and the health service upon which this paper is based all illustrate the fact that the nature of an organization's purpose, its structure, the desired information systems and the technology which 'delivers' the information systems, need to be thought about together. Some way of achieving that is required. In work of the kind described here, the authors were using SSM in relation to all of these aspects except that of technology choice. The initiator of one of the studies upon which this paper is based, who was project manager of a 'resource management' initiative in an acute hospital, said that he sought 'the big picture' to which could be related all the hospital's development of 'provider' activity carried out through clinical directorates with IT support. SSM provided the means of building and updating that picture in models of purposeful activity.

Such models express only the contingent structure of the subactivities which together constitute a purposeful whole. They are thus not committed to any particular organization structure. Rather they allow intelligent discussion of such structure, the chosen form of which needs to be politically feasible in the local culture while embodying the logic of the purposeful activity as expressed in a model agreed to be relevant. Equally, as argued above, the models provide an unambiguous base for the definition of information support relevant to the intended activity.

However, SSM is here only one way of doing something which has to be done in some form or other (whether consciously or not!) in all work aimed at the provision of managed information systems in organizations. We have found it useful, in making sense of experiences like those upon which this paper is based, to form a picture of this wider framework. It applies to all work on information provision, from the level of transaction processing to that of formulating strategic information systems and/or information strategy itself (Galliers & Somogyi, 1987; Earl, 1989; Ward et al., 1990), though it is likely that at the transaction-processing level issues of purposes and organization structure will be less complex than they are in work at the strategy level.

The general problem can be stated in the following terms. How can an organization achieve appropriate managed technology-based information systems in an appropriate structure for carrying out the purposeful activity of that organization according to a coherent concept of its role and nature (that concept normally being an accommodation of conflicting views and interests rather than a complete consensus)?

Solving that problem entails conceptually linking these three domains, as shown in the conceptualization-organization-technology (COT) framework of Fig. 6. The technology domain T and the organization domain O, are concerned, respectively, with: the real-world achievement of managed information support for the organizations' activities via appropriate IT; and an organization structure appropriate both to the organization's perceived mission and the nature of the IT available to it. Domains T and O are connected by the third, meta-level domain, C.

This domain is different in kind from the other two. It is the domain of organized explicit thinking about the O and the T and their linking through the concept of information. It is the conceptualization domain in which coherent thinking about organizational nature, about structure, and about information support is carried out. Though domain C is more abstract than O and T, it is the crucial domain if a satisfactory combination of organizational purposes, activity, structure and information support is to be achieved. It is the domain which is usually not addressed directly in the IS literature. And in practice it is the neglect of C as an explicit activity which makes for the confusion in so many situations in which IT is being introduced into organizations. Neglect of C as a conscious, organized, explicit activity is what leads to a premature leap from general statements of purpose to specific discussion of particular IT 'solutions'. The argument here is that the domains C, O and T are linked, define and redefine each other in a learning process, and that in this process C is prime.

It is of course the case that the COT model of Fig. 6 is an ideal type used to make sense of real-world experience, rather than a prescription. We have found it useful in building and updating 'the big picture' in work on information systems.

We have also found using SSM to be one way of carrying out the process of domain C. Galliers (1991) gives another example derived from SSM in his scenario-based approach to planning strategic information systems, his concern being to address problems of linking information systems planning to business planning. He uses SSM-type activity models to express alternative scenarios for the business future, followed by exploration of their different information requirements.

A virtue of SSM in this context is that as a process it is applicable not only to developing information systems at any level, from tactical to strategic, but also to information systems planning. Of course it is best used flexibly, in a manner closer to what Checkland & Scholes (1990) term 'Mode 2' (rather than the 'enact-the-seven-stages' Mode 1 use of SSM through which most users initially come to the methodology).

Finally we may emphasize that some version of C has to be enacted, even if those doing the work are not conscious of doing so. In much work in this area in the past, the use of such devices as critical success factors, existing organization mission statements, or the outcome of SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats) can be seen as seizing upon ready-made conceptualizations in order to furnish some version of C. Our experience so far is that it is better to work with fundamentals (such as purposeful activity models) which can be built in ways uniquely relevant to specific situations in organizations.

In conclusion it is hardly necessary to add that the use of the COT framework in this field is not, or should not be, a once-and-for-all occurrence. It is the nature of the framework that it helps to make sense of dynamic situations. Use should be continual, as organizations and their environments change.

## Biography

Peter Checkland is Professor of Systems at the University of Lancaster. He entered university teaching after 15 years as a manager in ICI, where, latterly; he was manager of a 100-strong research group. At Lancaster he undertook a research project to ascertain whether or not the approach of systems engineering could be applied to ill-structured management problems. Out of that research came the development of soft systems methodology, now used and taught in many countries. In 1991 he was awarded an Honorary DSc by City University.

Sue Holwell is a graduate in commerce and computing. She has 12 years experience as an IS manager with the Australian Government Construction Service department, from soldering to strategic planning, and is currently on extended leave to complete research at the University of Lancaster on information systems in organizations.
