# Strategy, structure and information management\*

R. Espejo

Aston Business School, Birmingham B4 7ET, UK

Abstract This paper offers a framework to relate strategy, structure and information systems in an organization. Current developments in information systems, particularly in the executive information systems (EIS) area, make apparent that the emphasis of design is in the ergonomy and flexibility of the reporting, and not in their alignment with effective management processes. Partially to overcome this shortcoming the paper offers criteria to improve management processes. Such criteria are provided by the viable system model (VSM). The emphasis is on the structural improvement of strategic processes. This leads to the discussion of a class of information systems aimed at balancing the longer-term, strategic and the shorter-term, operational needs of the organization. Methodological and practical aspects for the implementation of such systems are discussed with reference to a management tool—CYBERSYN—which illustrates one way of linking information systems to management processes.

Keywords: complexity, management processes, organization structure, performance measurement, viable system model.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this paper is to offer a conceptual and practical framework to relate strategy, structure and information systems in an organization. Current developments in information systems, particularly in the executive information systems (EIS) area, make apparent that the emphasis of design is in the ergonomy and flexibility of the reporting, and not in their alignment with effective management processes. While in the short term some managers may find these developments attractive, in the longer term they may find them overwhelming, intrusive and not well focused on the most pressing aspects of the tasks for which they are responsible.

To overcome the above shortcoming it is necessary to have criteria to improve management processes. This is a prerequisite in order to make possible the effective use of information systems in an organization. In this paper such criteria are provided by the viable system model (VSM). This is a model of the organization structure of any viable system, or, in other terms, a model of the necessary and sufficient functions and relationships between them to allow the organization to operate effectively as a whole.

In section 2 of this paper aspects of this model are discussed, in particular those related to strategic processes; this discussion should help us to see some of the links between strategy, structure and information systems. Section 3 offers a system of management aimed at overcoming some of the perceived shortcomings in the current situation. This system is based on a performance measurement system which balances the strategic and operational needs of the organization. Finally, methodological and practical aspects for the implementation of this system are discussed with reference to a management tool—CYBERSYN. This tool helps to illustrate one way of linking information systems to management processes.

## 2 MANAGEMENT PROCESSES AND INFORMATION SYSTEMS

The VSM has been discussed extensively in numerous publications (Beer, 1979, 1981, 1985; Espejo & Harnden, 1989) and therefore in this context the idea is to make use of the model, rather than to explain it in full.

In an earlier publication (Espejo, 1989a) the process of policy formulation was explained from a structural point of view. The outcome of this explanation was the 'mechanism for adaptation' (see Fig. 1). This mechanism defines structural criteria to enable an effective policy process and its basic implications are as follows.

## (a) Functional requirements

In any organization, we may detect three different functions concerned with the formulation of its policies. These are the policy, intelligence and control functions. They may take any structural form, from the simplest case where all of them are performed by one person to the more complex arrangement where many people and several units are involved in performing these functions. However, in any effective organization policy, intelligence and control exist in one form or another.

The policy function is that which gives closure to the organization. It is the function that ascribes purpose to the organization and defines its strategic intent. It provides, explicitly or implicitly, answers to questions such as; what is the organization all about? What is its mission? Which are its business areas? Which values does it stand for? Which responses, among those open to the organization to cope with opportunities and threats, are consistent with its identity and intent?

The policy function is normative; it defines policies and directions, based on vision (or lack of vision), values, preferences, intentions and the like. It is an expression of the interests and values of the stakeholders in the organization. This function shapes, in one form or another, the identity of the organization but, by definition, is not involved in its internal processes. Hence, structurally, it is a low complexity function. It is this structural characteristic of the policy function that necessitates the intelligence and control functions in the organization.

The intelligence function is defined by whatever capacity is available in an organization to create opportunities in the environment and to respond to external challenges and threats. Its focus is the 'outside and then' (Beer, 1979). The intelligence function, by means of whatever capacity it may have, creates over time an external environment to the organization. This environment may or may not be consistent with organizational policies. However, if the communications between the intelligence and policy functions are adequate then the likelihood is that intelligence will become more sensitive to policy directions at the same time that it will be in a better position to influence their shaping and definition.

The control function is defined by whatever capacity is available to support (i.e. manage) the creation and use of the organization's capabilities. It creates the space for people to develop the organization's resources. Its focus is the 'inside and now'. Whether these developments are along the lines defined by the policy function depends on the adequacy and quality of their communications. However, in one form or another the control function has an influence on and is influenced by the global organizational policies. An effective control function creates the conditions for people to implement the organizational policies to the best of their abilities. This implies capacity for allocating resources, managing the development of the organization's capabilities, and above all, creating the conditions for people to coordinate their actions.

Structurally, an implication of the above definitions of the intelligence and control functions is that they must be much more complex than the policy function itself. Their complexity is constituted by whatever capacity is there in the organization to create an 'outside and then' (i.e. an external environment in the future), and an 'inside and now' (i.e. an internal environment today). These functions are constituted by the large number of individual actions that, consciously or not, are coupling the corporate level of the organization to its external and internal environments. On the other hand, they are not constituted by activities, however sophisticated they might be, which are not transformed into relationships with either of these environments.

## (b) Criteria of effectiveness: relationships among policy, intelligence and control

Having established that structurally the intelligence and control functions must be far more complex than the policy function, the next issue is to work out the relationships among these three functions in order to increase the chances of an effective policy process. If either intelligence or control communicates directly with policy, without submitting its views to the checks and balances of the other function, the policy function will have to comment on proposals for which it does not have enough structural functional capacity to assess their merits. Though the proposals may be acceptable on political grounds, they may be damaging for the long-term viability of the organization. To increase the effectiveness of the policy process it is necessary to reduce the chances of this being the case. For this purpose it is necessary that both functions check their views with reference to each other, before they inform the policy function. In fact, in the context of the VSM, it is the responsibility of this latter function to create the conversational framework for the other two functions to check their views.

However, to have balanced conversations, the complexities that these two functions generate vis-à-vis each other should be equivalent. Otherwise, most likely, the quality of the organization's policies will suffer. They may be overinfluenced either by a short-term, inward-looking perspective, or by a long-term, outward-looking perspective. In the former case policy may be short-sighted, in the latter, weakly rooted in the capabilities of the organization. Of course, the quality of these debates will also affect the nature of the policy issues emerging from within the organization. The more balanced these debates are, that is, the better the counterbalance of the intelligence and control function works, the more likely it is that the policy function will be either considering robust issues, which have survived a stringent vetoing process within the organization, or will benefit by the structural support of functions performing a balanced assessment of its own concerns.

The mechanism for adaptation offers the structural framework to discuss strategic processes. This is not so much concerned with the content of the debates as with their structural requirements. If structurally there is no balance in the complexities of, and adequate communications between, control and intelligence, no EIS will overcome the fact that policy-makers are more likely to decide weak policies. However good the EIS might be, the related management processes (i.e. the policy conversations) will not be supporting the production of robust policy issues. In this scenario the EIS is irrelevant; it may produce more and more sophisticated reports for weaker and weaker policies. If the definition of strategies, directions or policies is the outcome of an emphasis in one policy dimension to the detriment of the other, the interpretation of whatever 'information' is provided or made accessible to managers will only reinforce the malfunctioning of the organization.

Furthermore, if the operational control mechanisms (i.e. the mechanisms for monitoring-control, see Espejo, 1989a) do not support the effective control of the organization's internal activities, no information system, however sophisticated it might be in its design and implementation, will produce valid management information. The control function will lack the necessary capacity to appreciate the meaning of this information, thus rendering the systems ineffective. If this is the case, even if the communications between the control and intelligence are good, the contribution of the control function to the policy debates is likely to be weak or misleading.

However, there is one further structural consideration. The mechanism for adaptation is not only relevant to the corporate level but to all levels with autonomy in the organization. To have a viable system 'the complexity of organizational tasks makes necessary autonomous systems within autonomous systems' (see Fig. 2 and Espejo, 1989a). This is not a choice but a necessity. Unfortunately, most of the time this de facto autonomy is achieved at a high cost, often as an outcome of conflicts, disloyalties, frustrations and ignorance, and not, as it should, as an outcome of cooperation, mutual respect and effective communications. However, to have an effective organization autonomous systems will emerge within autonomous systems, and for each of these systems there will be a mechanism for adaptation.

If in the implementation of information systems organizations do not take into account these structural and processual considerations then, even if these systems are aligned with management processes, the implementation will be ineffective; the information systems will be making more efficient ineffective management processes!

The conclusion is that without adequate structural arrangements, however sophisticated the information systems might be, it is unlikely that organizational conversations (i.e. management processes) will produce effective policies and strategies. The EIS will be only as good as the management processes it supports. In fact these management processes are the most powerful 'information system' in use; the EIS is only the tip of the iceberg.

The argument developed in this section goes far beyond the argument that information must produce knowledge and that knowledge must be combined with a strategic management style to be effective (Tang, 1991). Tang argues that the best information—such as that provided by a good EIS—is useless in the hands of a short-sighted manager; equally, without information, the most strategically oriented manager is operating only on the basis of good intentions. The argument of this section implies that, to a large degree, the individual styles of managers, whether strategic or otherwise, are secondary to the quality of the organizational conversations underpinning their decisions.

## 3 INFORMATION SYSTEMS AS A SYSTEM OF MANAGEMENT: FROM VISION TO ACTION

The implication of more effective structures is people in the organization with more flexibility and capacity to participate in organizational processes, that is, people operating in a context which provides better communication channels and more appropriate checks and balances. This is a context which supports the distribution of information needs throughout the organization. The more effective the organization is, the more evenly distributed are these information needs (Espejo, 1990). In other words, the traditional structural aberration, where senior managers are overloaded with information while people at lower levels are underloaded, is replaced by a structure which irons out these unbalances. This transformation can be greatly facilitated by the design and implementation of effective information systems. These are information systems supporting management processes which allow all organizational members to participate creatively in the organization.

All organizational members should have the opportunity to participate, within the scope of their autonomy, in conversations about future possibilities and current actions. It makes no sense to leave all the responsibility of creating the organization's future in the hands of senior management alone. The structure should allow everyone to have visions in the domains of their competence, and to transform these visions into plans and these plans into everyday actions; inventing, creating the future, including the identity of their tasks, is the right of everyone in an organization. For them planning should be neither forecasting nor guessing what is going to happen so that the organization can plan a response, it should be responsible conversations to construct today the organization's future. Today's conversations are a means of feeding forward a desirable future.

The above views will be seen as impractical for as long as organizations continue to measure results in the way they do today.

It is extraordinary that for the last 500 years, since Luca Pacioli invented double-entry book-keeping, the same system of performance measurement has been in place. The key assumptions about an organization made by double-entry book-keeping and accountancy have remained basically unchanged. The major concern still is to measure results with a historic perspective, rather than with a view to the future. This emphasis on past information assumes organizations operating in relatively stable environments; it can be assumed realistically that the future will be an extension of the past. However, as everyone knows this is not a realistic assumption in today's world. Even if accountancy is used with a view in the future, as when enterprises rely on discounting to assess whether possible investments are likely to be profitable or not, the accounting procedures simply lack requisite variety to distinguish between (normative) desirable or undesirable futures.

At a deeper structural level, sticking to traditional accountancy systems, as the main tool for measurement, is likely to be responsible for the reproduction of inadequate management processes in the organization. This fact is likely to bias the structural balance between intelligence and control towards the latter. This may be the case even if there is an understanding that the situation should be otherwise.

This discussion is making apparent the interrelated nature of management processes and information systems. There is no doubt that in a changing world, in which the constitution of the 'outside and then' requires increased attention in order to survive, it is necessary to have information systems that measure not only the short-term results of people's activities, but also take into account their abilities to transform their long-term expectations and possibilities into reality.

The emphasis upon profits should change to an emphasis upon viability. This is more than an emphasis in long-term economic viability; more importantly, it is an emphasis on those environmental factors, like values, attitudes and concerns, which may influence the acceptability or otherwise of particular enterprises in the long term. Criteria of viability should be a pre-control for profits (Schwaninger, 1990). And, in fact, the criteria of viability offered by the VSM support everyone in the above propositions. Current measurement systems, and the related information systems, tend to obscure not only the need for a balanced 'outside and then' and 'inside and now' but also the need for a distribution of autonomy throughout the organization. Concepts like profit centres may ameliorate, but not overcome, the problem; firstly, their focus is still in the short term, and secondly, they seldom go to 'small' enough tasks in the structure (i.e. lower structural levels in the organization). These arguments suggest the need for an 'accounting system' that highlights autonomy and brings forth conversations about possibilities and actions at all structural levels.

One such measurement system is based on the concepts of actuality, capability and potentiality (Beer, 1981), when they are understood as organizational concepts closely intertwined with the VSM. What follows is an explanation of this measurement system.

The three values, actuality ('Is'), capability ('Could be') and potentiality ('Ought to be') of a chosen variable permit us to define three indices; these are the indices of achievement, latency and performance (see Fig. 3). If the definition of potentialities and the measurement of capabilities and actualities are made for all the autonomous activities, at all structural levels, in an organization (Fig. 2) then they constitute a comprehensive system of measurement.

The question is, which are the activities that must be autonomous in an organization? The answer depends on the intent, vision and strategy of the organization. Autonomous activities are defined by answering the question: which are the activities in which the organization must excel—be world champions—if it is going to be viable as a whole? As technology pushes down the competitive edge into smaller and smaller activities, larger organizations need to adjust their answers to the above question. If, with reference to any specific activity, however small it might be, the answer is that the organization expects/needs to make it viable, either today or in the future, then it is not enough to work out a competitive edge for it today, it is also necessary to endow it with a viable organization structure.

Of course, the creation of an effective enterprise depends on the quality of the organizational conversations producing the above answers. If the structure underlying these conversations is good, that is, if it is the structure implied by the arguments of autonomy and the mechanism for adaptation, then the likelihood is that these answers will be more appropriate and have more chances of succeeding. In this case, the conversations will involve, at the appropriate level and with the appropriate checks and balances, most people in the organization, thus increasing their commitment to the outcomes.

The underlying value of the system of measurement based on actualities, capabilities, potentialities and the unfolding of complexity is that it offers a means of balancing between short- and long-term criteria of performance throughout the organization. For all autonomous activities, at all structural levels, achievement refers to current results, latency refers to 'realistic' commitments about necessary developments to actualize the mission of the organization, and performance offers the balance between the two.

Moreover, these concepts provide a platform to relate structure and strategy.

Potentiality—what the organization ought to be doing—is only meaningful as a result of discussions about possibilities. It is the policy function that provides a normative framework to the organizational activities; it ascribes purposes to the organization. The more clear is the normative framework the more likely it is that effective organizational debates, cross-checking the intended 'outside and then' with the 'inside and now', will take place; this framework helps to work out the boundaries of both environments. In this context the more likely it is that meaningful, and useful, definitions of potentialities will emerge. These potentialities provide direction to develop the organizational resources. It is the concern of the intelligence and control functions to work out investment plans and development programmes to realize the latent resources of the organization. That is, it is the conversations between these two functions that will transform vision and intent into plans for action.

Capability—what the organization can do with the resources currently at its disposal—requires adequate control mechanisms for its successful measurement. These mechanisms should allow managers to know about the available resources as well as to monitor the allocation and use of operational resources. Otherwise, the definition of capabilities becomes an arbitrary definition of targets. Knowledge about capabilities is necessary to define the scope of improvements in the organization. It is the responsibility of the organization's control function to achieve the best use of the available resources, that is to move actualities closer and closer to capabilities.

Actuality—what the organization is doing today—is measured for all those variables perceived by management to be essential for the control of organizational activities. The perception as to which variables are essential will depend on the structure; the more the 'outside and then' is represented in the organization at all structural levels, the more the essential variables will evolve from operational to strategic variables. From the traditional accountancy-based variables, like income, expenditure, revenues and costs, the variables will evolve towards including also those measuring the organization's competitive position, market share, ability to solve customers' problems and the like. As more attention is given to strategic variables the more clear will become the potentialities associated with the more operational variables.

It is the logic of complexity unfolding offered by the VSM that provides the power to the above measurement system. The definition of actualities, capabilities and potentialities is not only the concern of the organization as a whole, it is also of all those autonomous activities producing the identity of the organization, that is, of all those activities in which the organization must excel if it is going to remain competitive.

This concept suggests that appropriate indices of achievement, latency and performance must be defined and measured at all levels in the organization and therefore that potentialities, however difficult their definition might appear at lower structural levels, are meaningful and valid at all levels and not only at higher levels as traditionally believed. In other words, the 'outside and then' is the responsibility of all structural levels. In this devolved form, the measurement system becomes a means to enhance the activities of people throughout the organization. Everyone is concerned with the short, medium and long term. From the point of view of organizational development this is a system to generate the organization's complexity and with that to support its long-term viability.

In the short term, these measurements will depend upon the existing information systems in the organization; however, in the long term, they should be the driving force to support the development of the information flows required for viability. In practice the variables to measure are the essential variables that managers perceive as necessary to manage the organizational tasks. These essential variables are the driving forces for the development of new information systems. This view seems to be consistent with well-established methodologies for information systems development like critical success factors (Rockart, 1979). However, the emphasis on structural viability and on conversations for possibilities and action is something that has not been fully appreciated so far. Information systems have to be much more than reporting systems, they have to be management support systems.

## 4 CYBERSYN: A MANAGEMENT SUPPORT SYSTEM

Implementing the ideas discussed in section 3 requires appropriate methods and tools. Current EIS, with their graphic and reporting capabilities, are not adequate. Some of their features for exception reporting, drilling down into the data, accessing external databases and communications are not consistent with effective managerial processes. The emphasis in drilling down into detailed data ignores the need for autonomy at all levels, as it bypasses necessary conversational processes between structural levels in the organization. For management information systems to be effective they must be aligned with an effective organization structure and must help to focus the attention of managers in conversations relevant to the tasks for which they are responsible. These are the criteria underlying the management support system called CYBERSYN (Syncho, 1990).

Implementing CYBERSYN requires first a study of the organization structure in a diagnostic mode and second a process towards improving this structure in all those areas where change is desirable and feasible. It is only then that the definition of indices of performance for individual managers and their connection with data-processing systems is meaningful.

Studying the organization structure in a diagnostic mode can be done with the support of the VSM (Espejo, 1989b). The outcome of this study is diagnostic points, or areas where communication mechanisms and information flows in the organization are inadequate or require improvements. This is a highly technical study based on interviews and the analysis of organizational data. However, it is in the management of the process to improve these mechanisms and flows where the difficulties of aligning information systems with an effective organization structure become apparent. This issue will not be discussed further in this paper. Schuhmann (1990) has discussed it with reference to a concrete application of CYBERSYN in a large German company.

It is the definition of indices of performance that is discussed here in some depth. Technical aspects for the definition of these indices have been discussed before in several publications (Espejo & Watt, 1979; Espejo, 1992; Syncho, 1990). Therefore, it is their significance as a system of measurement that is highlighted in this discussion. Figure 4 shows an interpretation of these indices from a structural viewpoint. This discussion is inspired by Beer's (1979) discussion of measurement. The following points emerge from Fig. 4:

(a) The policy function defines criteria of performance (C1) to measure (M1) the results of the interactions between management (policy, intelligence and control) and the organization's operations (the operational capabilities of the whole organization). These criteria of performance are defined in the form of potentialities which are consistent with the normative framework (i.e. vision, mission and identity ascribed to the organization) of the policy function. Changes, and expectation of changes, in the organizational capabilities, as a result of resources allocation and policy implementation, are measured and reported to corporate management. If they are to follow the execution of their policies then they need to concern themselves with actual and planned changes in the indices of latency (i.e. in the ratios between capabilities and potentialities). They need not be involved in the interactions between management and the operations at lower structural levels. It is enough to establish that the organization is realizing its potentialities. It is natural to expect that all kind of problems will emerge in this process, not the least adjustments in the potentialities themselves as a result of new conditions. This system of measurement depends on two aspects, the quality of the organizational conversations supporting the definition of potentialities and the capacity of the control function in establishing changes in the organizational capabilities. For as long as the policy function monitors the conversations between intelligence and control as explained in section 2, and the control function monitors effectively the operations—both structural and processual requirements—the measurement system will be meaningful and useful.

Figure 4. Structural interpretation of indices.

(b) Corporate management establishes criteria of performance (C2) to assess the results (M2) of the interactions between the organization and its environment. These criteria of performance are the capabilities of the variables implied by the managers' critical success factors. The measurements made are achievements, that is ratios between actualities and capabilities, for all these variables. Corporate management—the control function in particular—does not need to be involved in the details of the interactions between the operations and their environments; that is the concern of the operations themselves; however, it needs to know the extent to which the organizational capabilities are being realized by the operations. Capabilities define the direction and space for improvements. But they are not targets. Achievement measures the degree to which the environment (e.g. the market) is supporting the realization of the organizational capabilities. For as long as the control function has a realistic assessment of the organizational capabilities and is able to measure actualities in real time the measurement system will be enough to support the effective management of the operations.

It should be noted that there is "corporate management" in all autonomous operations, at all structural levels in the organization. In other words, corporate management is not exclusive to the highest structural level. Therefore, the same measurement system applies to all levels in the organization.

As for the technical solutions for data-processing and reporting, a number of alternative approaches may be possible. However, most EIS are implemented without an understanding of the implied management of complexity. This shortcoming most likely underlies the facts that their usage is limited and that 'In a number of organizations there is a move to expand the range of performance indicators used (in the EIS), usually away from the standard financial measures' (Fitzgerald, 1992). Perhaps CYBERFILTER (Syncho, 1990) is one of the few packages that offers an explicit link between management processes and the design of management information systems.

As a management system, CYBERFILTER depends on the management processes responsible for the definitions of potentialities and the assessments of capabilities. As an information system it is based on the following criteria.

(a) Reports are aggregated at the appropriate level relevant to the concerned managers. Only by exception managers receive reports about unresolved problems at lower structural levels. The indices relevant to managers depend on the structural level at which they operate and their functional responsibilities. If the managers are general managers, responsible for autonomous operations, the indices relevant to them are likely to be those related to the discretion exercised by them at that structural level in the organization. If the managers are functional, the resolution of their indices will be defined by the autonomous operations served by the functions they manage.

(b) Information is filtered in such a way that only significant changes or discrepancies are reported to managers. Reporting in CYBERFILTER is based on the detection of either incipient instability in the behaviour of an index or significant discrepancies between actualities and the planned improvements towards capability.

(c) Reports are as close to real time as practically possible. In other words, it makes no sense to wait until the next periodic report to alert a manager that a significant change has occurred or is likely to occur. The actuality of an index is formed as soon as the necessary data are available. Anticipatory reports are made available whenever possible, whether in the form of short-term forecasting or in the form of information to anticipate possible problems in the development of the capabilities of the organization. Managers are alerted about events that may hinder anticipated changes in the future.

## 5 CONCLUSION

This paper has explored, albeit briefly, the links between structure, strategy and information systems. Discussions were focused on structural criteria for the definition of effective strategies and policies and also on the need for new measurement systems to take into account the complexity of an organization.

This paper advocates structural adjustments in order to improve organizational and informational processes. This position is not the same as advocating a full restructuring of the organization before implementing information systems. While there is a clear link between structure and information systems it does not help to think in one before the other. Improvements in processes can be achieved by adjustments in relationships and changes in the allocation of resources. These structural adjustments, with no formal implications in the organization chart, may have significant effects in the use of information systems. Of course, this view does not deny that in some cases a fundamental restructuring may be necessary.

To be of practical relevance the concepts and models used in this paper do not assume consensus or good will from the organizational participants. Whether policy-makers agree or disagree about desirable normative frameworks for the organization, or ascribe different purposes to the organization, they depend on the quality of the internal conversations to support their policies. It is perfectly possible that a whole range of different conversations will be necessary to take care of disagreements, but policy-makers will still depend on using organizational resources to the best of their abilities to have 'in-formed' policies. Therefore, several mechanisms for adaptation may be working in parallel in a situation, serving different interests and concerns, implying several VSMs for the same organization. If the conflicts become dysfunctional the organization may have to pay for this lack of cohesion; however, either in the extreme of unity or of total lack of consensus complexity will still be underlying any view about the situation.

Moreover, the concepts and models used in this paper do not imply objective information. What is information or not depends on the purposes of the people concerned. However, once a particular purpose has been recognized by an individual, there are some structural considerations as to how to make it more likely that that individual will receive relevant information. This paper has provided these structural considerations; it has made no assumptions as to the purposes of the individuals.

CYBERSYN/CYBERFILTER was explained as a concrete example of an information system that is coherent with structure and strategy. However, CYBERSYN is more a paradigm than a technology. Not only its current realization will be improved as research and technology offer more effective means to translate the general ideas of this paper into systems, but more significantly its implementation will be more hopeful once managers accept a range of new, and sometimes counterintuitive, management concepts.

The VSM is a logical construct that has emerged as a tool to manage complexity. As such it offers a way of thinking about organizations and an approach for action. This approach may be in contradiction with established practices, and in some cases at odds with deeply held views. These facts do not invalidate the model; they may just make apparent that there is no reason to believe that our intuitive handling of complexity is the best way to do it.

At a more detailed level, research is necessary to develop further the ideas of measurement explained in this paper. At the core of the system is the definition of potentialities. While for this definition the global structural criteria may be clear, their practical implementation needs further investigation. Perhaps one of the most interesting developments in this direction is Beer's current work in organizational tensegrity (Beer, 1990). Also, from the point of view of methods and tools, the definition of potentialities may be supported by some of the group decision support systems currently in offer (e.g. Eden, 1989). Their concern with facilitating the process of defining strategic options for the organization are in line with the concept of potentiality.

However, in the end the concepts of potentiality, capability and actuality will be useful only if they coordinate the actions of people at all structural levels. If this does not happen then the measurement system will have no roots in the organization and the value of systems like CYBERFILTER may not be recognized.

Also, any new measurement system will have to be developed on top of existing data-processing systems. The problem will be to develop the necessary interfaces between them and the new ideas of measurement. The need for a shift from profitability to viability is already evident in organizations. This shift is expressed by an increased concern for environmental issues and social responsibility. However, there is still a limited appreciation about the structural implications of this shift, in particular about the need for viability at all structural levels. The irony is that while the acceptance of new, more flexible, management (information) systems may depend on this change in appreciation, the dissatisfaction with current systems appears to be rooted in the tacit recognition that they are unable to cope with the flexibility required by an increasingly complex world.

## Biography

Raul Espejo has an engineering degree from the Catholic University of Chile and a doctorate from Aston University. He worked for the National Development Corporation of Chile first as a systems engineer and later as project leader of the Cybernetic Project for the organization and management of the industrial sector of the country. Thereafter, he moved to Europe, undertaking research projects in the UK and with the International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (IIASA) in Laxenburg, Austria. Since 1977 he has been at the Aston Business School in Birmingham, where he is currently a senior lecturer and head of the operations research and systems analysis group. He has published extensively in books and journals, and is co-author of the book Organization for Programme Management and co-editor of the book The Viable System Model. He is an international consultant in the application of managerial cybernetics to all kinds of enterprises, both in the private and public sectors. Additionally, he has designed CYBER-FILTER, a management support system, and created VIPLAN, a software tool to support the diagnosis and design of organizational structures.
