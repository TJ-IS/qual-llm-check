# Reading the organization: metaphors and information management

G. Walsham

Judge Institute of Management Studies, University of Cambridge, Mill Lane, Cambridge CB2 1RX, UK

'In all aspects of life, not just in politics or love, we define our reality in terms of metaphors and then proceed to act on the basis of the metaphors. We draw inferences, set goals, make commitments, and execute plans, all on the basis of how we in part structure our experience, consciously and unconsciously, by means of metaphor' (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980).

Abstract. The purpose of this paper is to examine the use of selected metaphors as a way of reading organizations, and to relate this to elements of the practice of information management. A brief outline of the philosophical basis of the metaphorical approach is given. This is followed by a detailed discussion of the metaphors of organizations as cultures and political systems. These metaphors are then related to a specific issue in information management, namely the development and implementation of information systems strategy. Finally, some conclusions are drawn on the value of a metaphorical approach for information management.

Keywords: metaphor, information management, IS strategy, culture, politics.

## INTRODUCTION

The importance of organizational issues with respect to computer-based information systems (IS) is well recognized by practitioners. The top five key issues identified by Brancheau & Wetherbe (1987), from the results of a survey of IS executives in the USA, were strategic planning, competitive advantage, organizational learning, IS's role and contribution, and the alignment of the IS function in the organization. It is notable that all of these have a strong organizational emphasis rather than a technical emphasis. Organizational issues have also become a major focus for IS researchers in recent years, and IS journals contain many papers dealing with such issues. It is worth noting, however, that much of this literature reflects a rational-economic interpretation of organizational processes, and uses a positivist epistemology which is based on the view that the world exhibits objective cause-effect relationships which can be discovered, at least partially, by structured observation.

Many writers in the social sciences in general, and in information systems in particular, have criticized positivism as limited in its approach and practical value (see, for example, Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1989). An alternative style of research on information systems, which forms the basis for this paper, is interpretivism. Interpretive methods of research start from the position that our knowledge of reality, including the domain of human action, is a social construction by human actors. Thus there is no objective reality which can be discovered by researchers and the results replicated by others, in contrast to the assumptions of positivist science. Our theories concerning reality are viewed as ways of making sense of the world and shared meanings are a form of intersubjectivity rather than objectivity. Interpretivism is thus an alternative epistemological position, concerned with approaches to the understanding of reality and asserting that all such knowledge is necessarily a social construction.

A body of work on information systems which is centrally based on the interpretive approach is that by Boland (1979, 1985). Boland uses phenomenology and hermeneutics as the philosophical bases for his research. Phenomenology arose originally from the work of Husserl (1931), who was concerned to understand the phenomena which he believed to be the totality of what we can know. Phenomena were regarded by Husserl as the essence of our experience in the world, to be grasped through intuition rather than verified empirically. Hermeneutics began as the study of the translation and interpretation of sacred texts, but is concerned now with the interpretation of any verbal or written textual material. Hermeneutics can be thought of as a key strand of phenomenology, since the interpretation of texts is an important part of the search for meaning and the essence of experience. Boland draws specifically on the work of Gadamer (1975), who argued that language is fundamental to our being-in-the-world, and that every reading or hearing of a text constitutes a hermeneutic act of giving meaning to it through interpretation.

The theoretical ideas outlined above have direct relevance to information systems. Boland (1985) notes that the use, design and study of information systems are best understood as a hermeneutic process. In using an information system, the available output is a text which must be read and interpreted by people other than its author. In designing an information system, the designer reads the organization and its intended users as a text in order to make an interpretation that will provide the basis for a systems design. In studying information systems, social scientists read the interaction during systems design and use in order to interpret the significance and potential meanings they hold.

A powerful and interesting approach to the hermeneutical 'reading' of organizational processes, which is the focus for this paper, is provided by the use of metaphor. Metaphors can be thought of, at one level, as ways of livening up speech and writing. For example, when we say that 'the take-over bid was a pitched battle', we conjure up a vivid image of armed and deliberate conflict, using the pointed metaphor of physical conflict between armies for the social and psychological conflict of opposing sides in an attempted take-over. However, metaphors are more than a decorative device, and the widely cited book by Morgan (1986) is based on viewing organizations through a process of metaphorical enquiry. Morgan considers that the use of a metaphor implies a way of thinking and of seeing that pervade how we understand our world. He notes that many of our taken-for-granted ideas about organizations are metaphorical, even though we may not recognize them as such.

With respect to the specific area of computer-based IS, Walsham (1991) related the eight 'images of organizations' discussed by Morgan to the IS literature. It was argued that current literature and practice in IS are strongly linked to the machine and organism metaphors, that these provide a rather limited and constraining view of organizations, and that benefit could be gained by the use of alternative metaphors. Two metaphors are selected for further attention below, namely those of organizations as cultures and organizations as political systems; the 'lenses' provided by these metaphors are then used to address the specific topic of the development and implementation of IS strategy. These metaphors were chosen since they offer interesting perspectives on IS in organizations in general, and IS strategy in particular. The final conclusions section of the paper draws on the earlier analysis to discuss the usefulness of metaphorical approaches in information management.

## ORGANIZATIONS AS CULTURES

This section develops the cultural metaphor of organizations and discusses its relevance to the study of computer-based IS. A summary of some key elements of the cultural metaphor is given in Table 1.

A valuable paper by Smircich (1983) pointed out that the culture concept has been borrowed from anthropology, where there is no consensus on its meaning. In this paper, the primary view taken will be that of culture as a system of shared symbols and meanings related to a strand of anthropology (Geertz, 1973) which emphasizes the need to interpret symbolic action. When this perspective is applied to organizational analysis, culture is conceived of as a pattern of symbolic discourse. It thus needs interpreting, reading or deciphering. The research agenda is to document the creation and maintenance of organization through symbolic action.

Morgan (1986) addressed the metaphor of organizations as culture largely from this symbolic perspective. He considered that, in talking about culture, we are really talking about a process of reality construction that allows people to see and understand particular events, actions and utterances in distinctive ways. Culture should not be viewed as a set of variables that societies or organizations possess, but as an active living phenomenon through which people create and recreate the worlds in which they live. In trying to understand the process of "reality construction", Morgan draws on the work of Weick (1969) on the enactment process. Weick emphasized the selectivity of our view of reality and the subjective way in which we draw meaning from previous lived experiences and thus shape our attitudes and actions and our interpretations of current activity.

Although computer-based IS have a key role in the processes of enactment and reality construction in contemporary organizations, for example in providing a particular view of the reality of past actions and performance, the cultural metaphor in its symbolic form has received relatively little attention in the IS literature. A notable early exception was the article by Feldman & March (1981) which viewed the use of information in organizations as embedded in social norms that make it highly symbolic. Thus, for example, the requesting of information is not simply a basis for taking action, but is symbolic of competence and social virtue in Western civilization where the concept of intelligent choice is a central ideology. The work of Boland discussed earlier is in the same tradition, where IS are viewed as an environment of symbols within which a sense-making process takes place.

The metaphor of organizations as culture is developed below, by discussing two further aspects. Firstly, culture should not be viewed as a monolithic concept within an organization, and the metaphor is extended by describing approaches to the understanding of subcultures and their interaction. Secondly, a major issue regarding the concept of culture is whether and how it can be used in the management of organizations. This issue is discussed in its relationship to the prescriptive management literature on organizational culture.

## Subcultures and multiple meanings

The term subculture is a broad description of a phenomenon with which we are all familiar, namely the existence of subgroups within a broader social unit who share sets of meanings sufficiently to create and maintain their distinctive character within the unit as a whole. In an organizational context, this definition of a subculture raises a whole range of theoretical and practical issues concerning how such subcultures are created and maintained, the interaction at the boundaries between subcultures, and the management of subcultures within the context of the organization as a whole. Some work which has addressed these issues is now briefly described.

Young (1989) criticizes much of the literature on organizational culture as suggesting either a view of an organization as a single culture which ignores the existence of subgroups with different interests, or as a collection of sectional groups with different interests who basically strive for their own gain. This latter perspective recognizes the existence of subgroups, but Young points out that it often assumes that events have a single interpretation which then becomes a point of dispute between the subgroups dependent on how they assess their gain regarding the issue under consideration. This approach ignores the extent to which events and social relationships are capable of being invested with multiple meanings by their participative actors. Young illustrates the above ideas using a case study of different groups of machinists in a manufacturing company, and puts forward the view that the tension between fragmentation, implied by distinctive subcultures, and unity, necessitated by the need for cooperative action, constitutes the organizational culture as a whole.

An earlier theoretical paper by Morgan (1981) developed the idea of a schismatic metaphor of organization which relates to the conclusion above. The schismatic metaphor assumes a fundamental tendency within organizations towards disintegration as a result of endogenously generated change. This tendency to schism is considered to arise from the striving of groups or subcultures for functional autonomy on the one hand, and from the feedback that can arise from the interaction between subgroups resulting in a point being reached at which relationships can no longer be sustained. Management can adopt various counterstrategies against these tendencies for subculture autonomy and intergroup conflict. Viewed in these terms, organizations appear as networks of strains and tensions generated through the adoption of strategies and counterstrategies concerned with the development and constraint of the autonomy of system elements.

With respect to computer-based IS, there is little work in the current literature which specifically addresses the issue of subcultures, and the few exceptions (see, for example, Kendall et al. (1987)) normally contain no analysis of the process of the maintenance and change of subcultures. There is, however, a significant literature on the political interaction and conflict between different interest groups in the context of IS design and development. This literature will be discussed later, but it is worth noting here that such work normally takes the interest groups as 'given', with little reference to how they are maintained and changed over time, and with no account of the multiple interpretation of the same events. In other words, such literature takes a political stance, with the cultural metaphor being largely absent.

## Management and culture

The concept of culture has received considerable attention in the prescriptive management literature; for example, the popular book by Peters & Waterman (1982) described eight desirable attributes which were possessed by 'successful' companies. The book's findings are controversial since, apart from questions as to whether the measures of success were too narrowly chosen or adequately characterized, some of the companies have been noticeably unsuccessful since the publication of the book, even using narrow financial criteria. Nevertheless, the book can be considered of value in popularizing the issue of culture in a management context, in contrast to a narrow focus on such aspects as finance or technology. A considerable amount of work has followed concerned with managing culture. For example, in the context of IS, Schein (1988) describes hypotheses on how to use IT to 'produce' innovative cultures within organizations.

The discussion of culture presented in this section should make us wary of simple prescriptions, and in particular to be sceptical regarding the ability to manage culture in any direct sense. Organizational culture and related concepts such as subculture are problematic in definitional terms, and embody subtle, complex and rather nebulous characteristics. Culture cannot be managed through a set of levers which are pulled to 'bring people into line'. Turner (1986) makes a similar point when he disagrees with the principles of the 'pop culture magicians', who sell the belief that corporate culture can be controlled, changed and manipulated from the top down in ways which meet managerial needs for cost-effectiveness and productivity gains.

On a more positive note, what can usefully be said about management and organizational culture of relevance to IS? Morgan (1986) argues that managers can influence the evolution of culture by being aware of the symbolic consequences of their actions. For example, a manager of a systems design process should talk and act as if different perspectives on the final design are acceptable and indeed desirable, should be aware of the symbol systems of others, and should view the role of the systems analyst as trying to create a system which balances the tension between the desire for autonomy of work groups and the need for coordinated action. Members of a design team should avoid an approach which sees an information system as imposing a new way of working on other groups, and 'resistance' as a phenomenon that needs to be 'overcome'.

## ORGANIZATIONS AS POLITICAL SYSTEMS

The metaphor of organizations as political systems is developed in this section, and some key elements are summarized in Table 1. The political metaphor, according to Morgan (1986), encourages us to see organizations as loose networks of people with divergent interests who gather together for the sake of expediency. A crucial concept for the political metaphor is power, which is the medium through which conflicts of interest are resolved. Despite the common usage which is made of the word power, it remains a problematic concept, and it is worth briefly examining some theoretical views. Giddens (1984) emphasizes that power should not necessarily be viewed negatively, but is inherent in all purposeful action. He considers that power is the capacity to achieve outcomes and is not, as such, an obstacle to freedom or emancipation, although he notes that it would be foolish to ignore its constraining properties. A second influential writer on power is Foucault (1976). He emphasizes the local nature of the exercise of power and the way in which such local elements can combine to form a wider chain or system of power, which is supported by institutional elements of the state.

The implication for research and practice in organizations is that power is a pervasive and continuous element of all human action, that it has subtle local properties, and that local actions are linked in a complex way to more general networks and institutional frameworks. The study of the exercise of power and the taking of political action should certainly be valuable in contexts where the issues are important and where individual interests diverge. This leads naturally to computer-based IS, which are implicated in major changes in work and work roles, on issues such as the centralization of control, on the relationships between different groups in organizations, and on aspects such as surveillance and individual freedom of action. Divergent interests and perspectives are common to all these areas, and IS in contemporary organizations are arguably one of the key areas for political action.

The importance of power and political action has received some attention in the IS literature. For example, an early article by Keen (1981) pointed out the importance of the politics of organizational change related to computer-based IS. A classic case study by Markus (1983) described power and political action over an extended period of several years in the introduction of a financial IS which had significant effects on both divisional and central accounting functions and was the source of major conflict between them. Kling & Iacono (1984) described the post-implementation politics related to a material requirements planning system and discussed how key actors used the language of efficiency to help push the IS development in a direction which increased their own capacities for control in the organization.

It is relevant to question why the research literature in this area is not more extensive, bearing in mind the importance of the topic; a related point is that the prescriptive literature aimed at practising managers frequently makes little or no mention of political and power elements. A full analysis of this phenomenon cannot be attempted here but one contributory factor can be suggested. It has been noted earlier that the concept of rational choice is a central ideology in Western society, and that managers demonstrate their adherence to this, and thus their social virtue, by being seen to behave in the 'rational' interest; power and the taking of political action are ever present in the world in which they live, but such things are not to be spoken about, or particularly written about, too openly. Managers legitimize their formal authority over the rest of the work-force and their own subordinates by being seen to behave 'rationally' and, by implication, in society's self-interest in the longer term; legitimacy is undermined by an emphasis on political action and the divergent interests of different individuals and groups. This brief discussion of the legitimacy of management action leads on to the more general question of the relationship between morality and political action, and the specific issue of the desirable balance between employee autonomy and management control. These questions are now examined briefly in the subsections below, with particular emphasis on their relevance to computer-based IS.

## Morality and political action

Morgan (1986) looks at the darker side of the spectrum of political action when he considers the metaphor of organizations as instruments of domination whereby 'certain people acquire and sustain a commanding influence over others, often through subtle processes of socialisation and belief'. Morgan notes that domination can be exercised in a variety of ways including the exploitation of employees within organizations, institutionalized discrimination in societies, and the abuse of power through the medium of the international economy.

Information is an important aspect of the dominance metaphor. Cooper (1986) notes that information, by excluding certain alternatives, facilitates a form of social power which involves the forcible transformation of undecidability into decidability. An illustration of this with respect to computer-based IS is the case of accounting systems, which provide 'information' on the past performance of the business, current operating conditions and future projections. They are used as a basis for setting targets, for monitoring performance, and for identifying and correcting deviances. However, accounting systems are only one way of looking at the organization, which institutionalizes certain boundaries and ascribes a privileged position to particular numeric data. This is a concept of practical relevance; for example, an undue emphasis on the narrow perception of accounting systems diverts attention away from other organizational perspectives such as the cultural aspects discussed earlier. Thus accounting systems mediate the dominance of financial information and, if they are taken as the primary or sole way of viewing organizations, this may be detrimental in human terms for the individuals who work within them, and for the societies within which they live.

A related concern with respect to computer-based IS is the theme of surveillance. Zuboff (1988) coined the term 'informate' to describe the way in which such systems produce new information which makes activities, events and objects, and thus people, more visible. The positive side of this capability is the additional insights on the workings of the organization which arise from the informing process. The negative side concerns the potentiality for increased surveillance and domination which the visibility of human work makes possible. Zuboff, following Foucault (1979), refers to the architectural plan for a 'panopticon' conceived by Bentham in the 18th century (Harrison 1983) and 'consisting of a twelve-sided polygon formed in iron and sheathed in glass in order to create the effect of what Bentham called "universal transparency". Foucault saw the panopticon as a metaphor for modern society, and Zuboff applies this to the concept of an 'information panopticon' whereby IS may have the potential to make human behaviour in organizations much more transparent and subject to tighter central control. Zuboff does not, however, take a technologically determinist position, and argues that the degree to which we move towards such a vision of centralized dominance through IS is a matter for social choice.

## Autonomy and control

It has been argued in this section that power is intrinsic to all human activity, that computer-based IS are a crucial area for the exercise of power in many contemporary organizations, and that the use of power and the taking of political action in the IS domain has important moral implications. This subsection addresses the question of how to translate such ideas into management action, and it is argued that a key issue is the balance between management control and individual or group autonomy within an organization.

Feldman (1989) considers that much of the management literature is misguided in seeing control and autonomy as alternatives, whereas they should be viewed as inseparable and interrelated aspects of management action. In this view, autonomous work activity in organizations is a meaningless concept unless it is related to a context of management control; otherwise, the question 'autonomous from what?' has no answer. Feldman illustrates these ideas with a case study where the major management control activity was financial, which had the effect of limiting available resources. Within these resource constraints, the autonomous behaviour of groups in terms of work activities was generally given free rein, resulting in destructive levels of autonomy in terms of effective coordination of organizational action as a whole.

The above article addressed the topic of the management of innovation, but the conclusions are highly relevant to the management of the design, development and use of IS. For example, Kling & Iacono (1984) discuss the way in which computer-based IS provide an increased potential for social control, whereby control is exercised by many actors in a complex fabric rather than simply through vertical management control or through lateral work relationships. Participation in a larger institutionalized world of computer use defines work activities, and it subjects users, regardless of their position in the hierarchy, to tighter social controls. Kling & Iacono do not see heightened control as necessarily negative, for example in terms of degradation of jobs. Thus the control framework provided by a computer-based system can be associated with considerable autonomy within particular work groups, dependent on the way in which the subtle balance between control and autonomy is managed at all organizational levels and both within and between all organizational groups.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS STRATEGY

The two images of organization developed in the previous sections involve distinctly different concepts, as summarized in Table 1, but they are in fact inextricably interlinked. For example, the use of language in discourse involves a sense-making process as implied by the cultural metaphor, but the same language use is also an exercise of power in commanding the listener's attention along the dimensions controlled by the speaker. Thus the perspective on metaphors taken here is pluralist, linked to the view that a blending of different metaphors has a potential for synergy. Lakoff & Johnson (1980) describe the use of two metaphors in this way as a permissible mixed metaphor, where the metaphors are coherent when taken together although not necessarily consistent with each other.

The framework provided by this approach will now be illustrated by considering a specific topic in information management, namely the development and implementation of information systems strategy. A brief critique of the literature in this area is given below together with a view on the nature of the strategic process arising from a cultural and political perspective. This is followed by some specific proposals on the management of the strategy process which arise from the theoretical framework.

## Nature of the strategy process

The strategic planning of information systems was noted earlier as a key management issue. Much of the literature which seeks to advise management in this area takes a formal-rational view of organizations and sees the strategy formulation process as a series of logical steps. Such approaches can have value in specific cases, but provide a very limited reflection of organizational reality. The formal-rational view of strategy in organizations has been widely criticized in the general management literature. For example, Mintzberg (1978) coined the term 'strategy formation' to reflect the emergent nature of the strategy process, where realized strategy is viewed as a pattern in a stream of decisions which may well be radically different from the strategy which is formulated through a conscious process prior to decision-making. Quinn (1980) argued that successful strategic managers practise 'logical incrementalism', which is described as a jointly analytical and behavioural process in which they 'proceed flexibly and experimentally from broad concepts to specific commitments, making the latter concrete as late as possible'.

Earl (1989) provides a useful summary of much of the literature on strategy with respect to information technology and information systems. He distinguishes between the development or formulation of IS strategy, and management approaches to its implementation which he terms IM (information management) strategy. He gives a wide-ranging description of frameworks and contingency approaches in each of these areas. Many of these techniques are based on a formal-rational view of organizations, although Earl himself does discuss more diverse views, for example by emphasizing the existence of different stakeholder perspectives, and the general spirit of the book is largely consistent with Quinn's views on logical incrementalism.

What is noticeable about Earl's book, from the theoretical perspective taken in this article, is its relative lack of reference and serious discussion of culture and politics. In the final chapter (Earl, 1989, pp. 211–212) managers are advised to 'create a hi-tech culture' and 'adopt loose-tight management'. The spirit of these remarks falls centrally within the belief, criticized earlier in this article, that culture can be controlled and manipulated from the top down. With respect to politics, there is a similar absence of in-depth discussion, although 'political skills' are briefly noted as necessary for some categories of management personnel (p. 206). Yet politics are central to IS and IM strategy in many organizations. For example, Waema & Walsham (1990a,b) describe the importance of political actions and the exercise of power in the development and implementation of IS strategy in a UK building society and in the formation of IS strategy in a developing country bank.

So what different view of the strategic process arises by taking a cultural/political perspective on organizations? A summary statement is that the formation of IS strategy can be conceived of as a process of learning, with leadership provided by management, but involving significant dialogue and interaction between subcultures and related political activity. Implementation of IS strategy is linked to this since it requires political acceptability and cultural change in the style and content of work and the conception of their work role by different groups. This broad view of the IS strategy process is general in nature and some ways in which it translates into more specific prescriptions for action are now outlined.

## Managing the strategy process

The formation of IS strategy should not be thought of as a senior management prerogative since, even apart from moral issues concerning employee involvement, an understanding of subculture conceptions is an essential element of the management of the strategy process. The interaction between subcultures during IS strategy formation is a learning process on the part of all parties which can be facilitated or hindered by symbolic action on the part of managers. Examples of supportive symbolic action include verbal and written legitimization of the right for multiple perspectives to exist, and the recognition of the creative potential of tensions between different subcultures.

A further area for symbolic action concerns management's responsibility in the exercise of power on moral issues such as the level of employee surveillance. More generally, the balance between autonomy and control implied by a new information system should be a conscious design element, and one that can be debated by employees who will be affected. The view that new IS are valuable solely in order to tighten management control is unacceptable and undesirable, both on ethical grounds and in terms of organizational effectiveness. People who are subjected to a regime of tight organizational control are unlikely to respond by being vigorous and creative in their work.

With respect to structured and formal-rational approaches to the formulation of IS strategy, it would be too extreme a view to argue that they are valueless. Nevertheless, such approaches should be viewed with caution, and should be seen at best as aids to the more subtle management issues referred to above. Of course, IS strategy not only involves the management of the internal organization which has largely been the focus here, but also vision concerning the positioning of the organization and its products with respect to the external market. However, despite the enormous proliferation of aids to this external assessment process which have been put forward during the last decade, strategic vision and its linking with IS appear, in the view of this author, to be an art rather than a science, and the 'aids' to this process appear rather simplistic and unlikely to provide much help in developing strategic vision.

The above discussion has addressed the formation of IS strategy, but the implementation of strategy should be thought of as deeply related to the same issues rather than as a separate stage. Effective leadership during strategy formation is likely to generate a learning and change process which leads to commitment during system implementation. Nevertheless, even with the right conditions, the learning process will continue after the technical implementation of an information system, since results concerning use can never be fully predicted during system design. The Managing Director of the Trent Building Society (Waema & Walsham, 1990a), who pioneered and managed the development of highly successful systems during the 1980s, expressed a preference for 'half-baked systems' which, after being used in practice, could be subsequently changed to accommodate practical organizational realities which were not anticipated during initial design. This can be thought of as 'organizational prototyping' of IS, as an extension into the organizational domain of more conventional prototyping in such areas as screen design.

Thus the implementation of a particular information system and, more generally, the implementation of IS strategy is a continuous learning process, and every formal information system which is introduced is shadowed by the informal assessments concerning its usefulness and impact on themselves which users and others affected by the system will inevitably make.

These informal assessments create the conditions and suggest strategies for further modifications to the system, and are a contribution to the ongoing IS strategy formation process, which should involve employees at all levels.

## CONCLUSIONS

It has been proposed in this paper that the use of metaphors as a way of reading an organization has value for the theory and practice of information management. The approach derives from an interpretivist philosophy which sees knowledge as a social construction, in contrast to the certainties of positivism. A key issue with respect to the metaphorical approach is to ask the question whether, since knowledge is regarded as a social construction, all metaphors are equally valid. The response of this author is a clear 'No'. Lakoff & Johnson (1980) point out that our understanding of human affairs and the world in general is given in terms of conceptual systems that are grounded in our successful functioning in our environments. Thus we may interpret things differently, but our different systems of interpretation enable us to function in the world, implying some degree of shared understanding and commonality in our conceptual schemes.

If all metaphors are not equally valid, what are the epistemological grounds for choosing particular metaphors over others? Tsoukas (1991) raises this question when he classifies the work of Morgan (1986) in the category of 'metaphors as a way of thinking' and argues that, within such an approach, there has been almost no concern with justifying the use of particular metaphors. Tsoukas goes on to say that this use of metaphor implies a private, intuitive approach of trial-and-error occurring in a single mind, which does not allow metaphors to be publicly compared, evaluated and improved. A response which can be made to this criticism is that, although the use by an individual author of a particular metaphorical approach derives no doubt from his or her personal experience and insight, the testing of the value of these insights to others is then carried out by exposing the approach through verbal and written discourse to enable broader judgements to be made. Metaphors can be compared, evaluated and improved by this form of public testing; the result is not the generation of 'best' metaphors, which is a retreat to the naiveties of positivism, but the creation of intersubjectively tested metaphorical approaches, considered of value to a broader group than a single individual.

The above discussion can be specifically illustrated by the metaphors used in this paper. The mixed cultural/political metaphor was deliberately chosen since the author had found that it yielded valuable personal insight on organizational processes in general, and IS strategy formation in particular. This paper can be viewed as a contribution to the intersubjective debate, referred to above, concerning the value which others find in this cultural/political perspective. With respect to IS strategy in particular, it was argued that much of the existing literature in this area is rather simplistic, and that the new perspective enables a more interesting and insightful conceptualization of the strategy formation process. It is up to others to judge the validity of this claim on the basis of their own knowledge and experience. Further evaluation and improvement can be carried out by applying the approach to other real situations. In addition, the perspective suggested in the paper could be used to explore other information management issues such as the evaluation of information systems, and the process of system design and development.

The two images of organization used in this paper have been widely cited in the general organizational literature for a long time, and it can be argued that one of the main benefits of a metaphorical approach arises from the use of fresh and original metaphors as a creative device. Indeed, it could be considered that the cultural and political perspectives on organizations are so well known and understood that they are effectively models of the real world, rather than metaphors which draw from other domains. In response to this latter point, the contention in this paper has been that culture and politics are elusive terms, which derive from metaphorical comparisons with anthropological research on human societies and studies on systems of government, power and intergroup conflict. We do not 'understand' these phenomena in any total sense, and our insights on them are partly metaphorical. With respect to freshness of insight, the cultural/political perspective has not been widely used in the study of information systems strategy, and this was the rationale for its use in this paper.

Even the most insightful of metaphors are 'ways-of-not-seeing' as well as 'ways-of-seeing' and the continuous attempt to develop fresh images of organization should clearly be encouraged. Morgan (1986) offers a range of alternative metaphors such as those of organizations as brains which underpin the cybernetic work of Beer (1981) and others. Mangham & Overington (1987) suggest the metaphor of organizations as theatre, which 'permits a humanistic, artistic and creatively playful approach to organizations' and contrasts, in their view, with the 'heavyhanded models of systematic rationality'. The value of this latter approach, and indeed any metaphorical construct, should be subjected to the process of debate and analysis discussed earlier, and can be regarded as an interpretivist 'hypothesis' which needs to be intersubjectively tested.

Metaphors thus offer an approach to the generation of 'interesting and useful debates', which can be regarded as the interpretivist equivalent of the positivist communication of knowledge. The value for IS researchers is in the generation of new ways of investigating and describing phenomena in order to produce new knowledge and insights. The value for practitioners is that increased understanding and insight arising from metaphorical approaches should be valuable when intervening actively in a real situation. Alternative organizational metaphors offer a contrast to the view of organizations as machines or organisms which have been dominant in the literature. The cultural/political perspective taken in this paper stresses the existence of subcultures, multiple perspectives and, in some instances, political conflict; this view implies that the management of organizations is problematic, and suggests a stark contrast to the benign image of managers acting rationally in the interests of all employees and the broader social community.

## Biography

Geoff Walsham is a Lecturer in the Engineering Department at the University of Cambridge and a member of the Judge Institute of Management Studies. His early training was in mathematics, and he first worked with computers as an operational research analyst in the late 1960s. His earlier research and publications were in the areas of computer modelling and telecommunications, but his work over the last several years has centred on the social and organizational aspects of computer-based information systems. His specific research interests include IS strategy and implementation, and the use of information technology in the developing countries.
