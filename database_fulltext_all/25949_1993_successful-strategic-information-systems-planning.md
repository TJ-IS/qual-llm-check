# Successful strategic information systems planning

Anne D O'Connor

Department of Computing, Faculty of Science and Technology, University of Western Sydney (Nepean), PO Box 10, Kingswood, New South Wales 2747, Australia

Abstract. Strategic information systems planning (SISP) is the process of ensuring alignment between business plans and objectives and information systems plans and objectives and/or the process of identifying information systems which will provide the organization with a competitive edge. This paper distinguishes SISP from both tactical and operational information systems planning, discusses the reasons for management concern with SISP, summarizes problems which may be encountered with the planning process and resultant plant implementation and then focuses on the issue of SISP success. Specifically, success is considered not only in terms of how it may be achieved but also in terms of how it can be defined (i.e. what constitutes SISP success) and how SISP success can be measured. Specific research questions addressing the measurement of SISP success are identified.

Keywords: information systems planning, planning, SISP, strategic information systems planning, strategic planning.

## INTRODUCTION

While, strictly speaking, it is difficult to provide a single comprehensive definition of information systems planning, in general terms, it can be described as the process of establishing information systems goals to support business goals, the identification of information systems and associated resources necessary to support the achievement of these goals, the establishment of priorities, schedules and budgets and the management of specific development efforts. Although there is indeed a wealth of literature on the topic of information systems planning (ISP) there are few recent survey articles in the area and those which are available (while implicitly referring to strategic information systems planning) fail to define explicitly what level of information systems planning they are dealing with and, when considering what constitutes successful information systems planning, fail to consider the issue of 'success' as distinct from success guidelines or key success factors. This paper aims to focus on this issue specifically in relation to information systems planning at a strategic level—i.e. strategic information systems planning (SISP). The first section of the discussion is devoted to defining precisely what constitutes SISP as distinct from information systems planning at other levels. The remainder of the paper then provides an overview of SISP concepts before focusing on the important topic of what constitutes successful SISP and how can success in this area be measured. Potential definitions of SISP success are proposed and future research questions are raised.

## WHAT IS STRATEGIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS PLANNING?

ISP can take place at three levels—the strategic, the tactical and the operational (McLean & Soden, 1977).

## Strategic information system planning

SISP is the process of ensuring alignment between business plans and objectives and information systems plan and objectives and/or the process of identifying information systems which will provide the organization with a competitive edge (Lederer & Sethi, 1988). It typically addresses a longer time horizon than either tactical or operational planning although the exact time-frame is dependent upon the volatility (i.e. frequency of change) of the organization and its environment. SISP has, as its focus, effectiveness and efficiency (Galliers, 1988)—aiming at establishing the direction of development rather than identifying specific development projects. At the strategic planning level, the involvement of both information systems management and corporate management is necessary. The information systems personnel bring analytical, informational and technical skills to the planning process while corporate management bring business knowledge. This mix is also good for 'political' reasons 'since you need to promote dialogue, mutual respect and cordiality between DP personnel and the rest of the company' (Page-Jones, 1985, p. 30).

## Tactical information systems planning

Tactical planning focuses on prioritizing and scheduling development efforts and establishing action plans for development and performance measures to be used during operational planning. Generally speaking, information systems planning at this level covers a much broader spectrum of projects than SISP. Projects considered during tactical planning may have originated from strategic planning efforts, user requests, routine maintenance efforts or mandates from external organizational sources. Generally, the entire portfolio of applications is categorized first according to their source before being prioritized. Issues such as software and hardware migration, disaster recovery, information systems personnel training, capacity planning and general security will also be considered at this level (Johnson, 1984). During tactical planning a 'mix' of planning participants will again be found. This mix will depend upon the objective of the tactical planning exercise. For the purposes of prioritization and project approval, for example, committees (often called steering committees) consisting of user, information systems and management representatives will typically be constituted. When issues such as training, capacity planning, disaster recovery and security are being considered, however, the involvement of technical information systems staff will be most critical. For the purpose of identifying additional applications in the category of 'ongoing maintenance' or 'operational necessity' the participation of users at lower organizational levels will be required.

## Operational information systems planning

Information systems planning at the operational level involves the development of specific detailed plans for each project. It entails the selection and approval of projects to be commenced within the next planning year (generally 12 months) and the actual planning, monitoring and control of specific systems development efforts. Planning groups at this planning level more typically comprise information systems personnel. User/management representatives will, however, be required to participate in system development, review deliverables, prototypes, etc. Table 1 provides a summary of the major differences between the three planning levels. ## THE IMPORTANCE OF SISP

In recent years, the scope of SISP has widened considerably (Galliers, 1986a). Not only are the more 'traditional' information systems areas (transaction processing and office automation) considered in the planning efforts but planning now extends into the areas of data communications, end-user computing, data distribution and even factory automation (Sullivan, 1988). It is no longer concerned solely with identifying and prioritizing development efforts but it is also concerned with considering the 'organizational implications of alternative information systems strategies and implications for corporate strategy of advances in information technology' (Galliers, 1986a, p. 51). competitive advantage has also widened the scope of planning and placed added emphasis on its importance.

Studies conducted in the UK, America, Australia and even the Republic of China (Ball & Harris, 1982; Earl, 1983; Dickson et al., 1984; Herbert & Hartog, 1986; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987; Watson, 1989; Harrison & Fam, 1990) reveal that SISP (and related issues) have been key management concerns since at least the early 1980s. The reasons for this concern are many and varied. For example, it is suggested that some of the major driving forces behind the SISP push are:

— the relative growth in information systems budgets compared to other functions;

— the key role information technology can play in obtaining competitive advantage;

— the fact that information systems can be used to support strategic decision-making;

— a desire on the part of senior management to become involved in information systems decision-making;

— rapidly changing technology;

— scarcity of corporate resources;

— the trend to database design and integrated systems;

— a disillusionment with the quality, timeliness and accuracy of delivered systems;

— unmaintainability of delivered systems;

— lack of standards leading to piecemeal development and resultant system incompatibility;

— data redundancy;

— lack of system/data integrity and, finally

— cost and time overruns (Tozer, 1986; Galliers, 1987a; Cash et al., 1988; Connor, 1988).

It is believed (or rather, hoped) that SISP will address these concerns and provide benefits of both a tangible and intangible nature. Specifically, SISP may:

— provide a basis for performance assessment. It is not, after all, possible to measure achievement unless there is something to measure it against (Jackson, 1986);

— provide financial benefits to the organization. For example, one multinational organization was able to experience savings in excess of \$6 million as a direct result of SISP (Jackson, 1986) while an AT Keamey study (Lederer & Mendelow, 1986) identified that companies with well-integrated SISP/business plans outperformed companies which had no such plans by a factor of 6 to 1;

— aid communication between users, information systems personnel and top management (Tozer, 1988; Earl, 1990);

— provide a sound base for project selection and prioritisation (Tozer, 1988);

— raise the awareness of information systems potential throughout the organization and also increase information systems personnel awareness of the business (Tozer, 1988); and

— ensure projects are targeted at and aligned with business needs (Tozer, 1988; Earl, 1990).

In general terms, SISP permits the organization to exercise a favourable influence over future events, focuses attention on problem or weak areas of business operation, assists in obtaining organizational commitment to information systems activities (particularly when the appropriate top management participants are involved) and assists with resource control, ensuring that funds are channelled into activities most relevant to the achievement of goals at all organizational levels.

## SISP PROBLEMS

While recognizing the potential of SISP, however, many organizations either fail to undertake planning or planning, when undertaken, fails. Why is this so?

SISP problems fall into two broad categories (McLean & Soden, 1977)—those associated with undertaking the process and those associated with implementing the resultant plans. These problems must be carefully considered before effective guidelines for success can be established and before a definition of success and associated measures can be established.

## Problems associated with the process

(1) There is often difficulty associated with both formulating and communicating organizational objectives to the SISP team and hence difficulty in ensuring alignment between business and information systems plans (McFarlan, 1971; Galliers, 1986a; Lederer and Mendelow, 1986, 1987).

(2) Inadequate computer support for planning methodologies that typically require the production of voluminous documentation and inadequate documentation of the methodologies themselves (Lederer & Sethi, 1988, 1989).

(3) Too much dependence on the ‘quality’ of the team leader and planning participants (Lederer & Sethi, 1989).

(4) The time and cost involved in performing the SISP study is often considered excessive especially when one recognizes that it generally involves the most ‘expensive’ personnel (i.e. top management). Long and drawn-out planning processes also result in management loss of interest in and commitment to the plan (Galliers, 1986a; Lederer & Sethi, 1988).

(5) Selection of an appropriate planning methodology is often difficult since, while there are many competing methodologies, they are not directly equivalent. Different methodologies are applicable in different settings (Bowman et al., 1983; Sullivan, 1985; Pyburn, 1983).

## Problems associated with plan implementation

(1) Planning is inaccurately viewed as a one-off process rather than as an ongoing, continuous exercise (Galliers, 1987b). As a result, SISP become outdated and inappropriate to the changing business environment.

(2) Difficulty is often experienced in securing top management commitment for plan implementation (McLean & Soden, 1977; Head, 1982; Galliers, 1986a; Lederer & Sethi, 1988, 1989). Top management either fails to 'understand the plan or lacks confidence in the IS departments ability to carry it out' (Lederer & Sethi, 1989, p. 60).

(3) Many plans fail to consider the personnel and training requirements needed to support plan implementation (Lederer & Sethi, 1988).

(4) Plans fail to include an analysis of associated benefits and costs, even at a cursory level. Since priorities are often established and approval granted on the basis of such an analysis, implementation of plans may be hindered by their omission (Lederer & Sethi, 1988).

(5) SISP groups fail to consider the political side of planning—the relative power of participants, hidden agendas and power-plays (Bowman et al., 1983; Galliers, 1986a).

## THE MEANING OF 'SUCCESSFUL' SISP

## The current state of research

To date, the literature in the area of successful SISP has focused, most decidedly, on key success (or 'unsuccess' (Earl, 1990)) factors and guidelines for successful information systems planning (McLean & Soden, 1977; Martino, 1983; Pyburn, 1983; Sullivan, 1985; Galliers, 1986a, 1987a, b, 1990). Researchers have focused on factors contributing to a successful planning process and factors leading to successful plan implementation (a summary is found in the Appendix) but have failed to define SISP success or even discuss how such success can be determined or measured. Table 2 summarizes the extent of current research in the area of SISP success and clearly identifies the emphasis on key success factors and successful planning guidelines. While adoption of the guidelines may increase the chances for an organization of planning success the mere fact that these guidelines have been adopted does not mean that SISP is successful. The guidelines and success factors are a means to an end not the end itself—factors critical to the success of SISP are not to be confused with SISP success itself! To use a simplistic illustration, one may state that to be a successful Olympic athlete 10 hours of training per day is required. However, one is not automatically a successful Olympic athlete if this guideline or critical success factor is adopted. The measure of success in this case would be not the number of hours of training but rather some specified objective such as world-ranking, medal tally, Olympic selection, etc.

Galliers (1986a, 1987b, 1988) is one of the few researchers to consider how planning success may be measured. He identified, as a result of questionnaire research, that success may be measured differently by different organizational members. Specifically, non-IS management tended to perceive that a SISP is successful if it leads to 'improved and more efficient IS and improved user involvement in, and satisfaction with the information systems planning and development process' (Galliers, 1986a, p. 6). Information systems planners, on the other hand, tended to perceive the process as successful if it 'leads to a clearer view of overall corporate information systems requirements leading to agreed priorities' (Galliers, 1986a, p. 6). It is interesting to note that in this study the non-IS participants defined success in implementation terms, while the IS participants defined it in relation to the planning exercise itself. Just as SISP problems and success guidelines can be broken into two categories, it appears that the definition and measures of SISP success can be developed from two perspectives. Specifically, one may consider success in terms of the planning exercise itself (which results in the development of a strategic information systems plan) or ultimately in terms of plan implementation. It must be agreed, however, that such a differentiation is somewhat artificial since, after all, the planning exercise can hardly be considered a success if the plans are not approved and implemented!

Pyburn (1983) also considered measures of planning success. Unlike the Galliers studies, however, these measures were not identified by the research participants but rather specified by the researcher as a measurement instrument. Senior executives in the study were asked to rate SISP success on the basis of:

— the degree to which information systems addressed critical business needs;

— the degree to which the information systems organization was well managed;

— the extent to which the strategic IS plan made clear the role of IS, the direction of IS and the resources required (Pyburn, 1983, p.7).

While the basis for success measurement was at least given attention in this study the question must be asked—how were these measures identified and how can they be objectively applied? How can one, for example, measure the degree to which information systems address critical business needs and the degree to which the information systems organization is well managed? As well as identifying measures of success the ability to apply these measures objectively must not be overlooked.

Finally, Earl (1990), while failing to consider success measures, did consider measures of SISP failure ('unsuccess'). Specifically, in Earl's study SISP was deemed to be unsuccessful if plans were not fully implemented, if there was a lack of top management acceptance of plans and if the process of planning was too time-consuming.

## Future research directions

In the wealth of literature covering the topic of SISP and in the specific part of that literature which considers issues of SISP success there are very few researchers who have considered SISP success measures. Clearly, future research needs to be directed initially at establishing how organizations determine whether their SISP exercise has been successful or not. Since different planning participants may view success differently (Galliers, 1986a), it is likely that success will be measured in different ways not only by different organizational members but also by different organizations and even by the same organization at different points in time. Intuitively one may also assume that success can be defined at varying levels of complexity. As already indicated, a planning exercise may be considered ‘successful’ if a plan is developed; if the plan is implemented; if there is a clear interrelationship between business plans and objectives and IS plans and objectives; if opportunities for competitive advantage have been identified (and ultimately realized) or if any other planning objective has been achieved. Such planning objectives may take several forms. Table 3, for example, identifies a variety of SISP objectives, the achievement of which may well constitute success. Other measures may be based on outcomes such as improved communication between users, management and IS personnel, an increased awareness of the role of IS throughout the organization and conversely increased awareness of the business by IS professionals, improved system compatibilities, increased user satisfaction with implemented systems, etc. In order to provide more than an 'intuitive' definition of successful SISP, however, research in this area is needed.

Research to determine if correlations exist across industry sectors or across organizations is also required. Specifically, appropriate metrics for the measurement of planning success need to be established and the following types of questions need to be considered:

(1) How do organizations define and measure the success of SISP?

(2) Do measures of SISP success vary:

(a) within an organization from one planning effort to another,

(b) from organization to organization;

(c) across industry sectors;

(d) from country to country?

(3) On what bases do definitions and measures of SISP success vary? For example, do measures vary:

(a) with organizational size and/or complexity;

(b) with the amount of money allocated to IS/IT activities;

(c) on the basis of the 'strategic importance' of information systems;

(d) on the basis of the SISP approach adopted?

## CONCLUSION

Strategic information systems planning is an episodic process conducted by information systems management and corporate management personnel aimed primarily at facilitating alignment between business plans and objectives and information systems plans and objectives and/or identifying information systems development efforts which may provide the organization with competitive opportunities. Subgoals of the process may be to improve user involvement and understanding of information systems issues, improve information systems personnel involvement in and understanding of business issues, improve the adaptability, quality, timeliness and maintainability of information systems, improve coordination between development efforts and improve software management and resource allocation. It is believed that SISP can assist an organization achieve these goals and it is for this reason that SISP is commonly identified as a key management concern. While recognizing its potential, however, many SISP efforts fail or are never undertaken. The reasons for such failure are associated both with the planning process itself and implementation of the plans. Specifically, problems are encountered with identifying and communicating business objectives, selecting between and using the planning methodologies available, obtaining the involvement of the appropriate business and information systems personnel, obtaining top management commitment to (and approval of) plan implementation and ensuring the ongoing nature of the planning process. While a successful planning exercise is clearly dependent upon the ability of the organization to overcome these problems, one must look further when considering the issue of success. For example, a successful planning exercise is also dependent upon careful analysis of the organizational context within which planning is to take place. The organizational context influences planning factors such as who participates, the amount of time and money allocated and the focus. In organizations where information systems serve only a support role (as opposed to a strategic role) or where users and information systems personnel are relatively unfamiliar with the technology, strategic planning requires less attention with more focus on planning at an operational level. However, where information systems are of strategic value or in technologically more mature organizations, greater emphasis needs to be given to SISP which needs to be more formalized and involve higher management levels. While much attention has been given in the literature to identifying these success guidelines, little research into exactly what constitutes SISP success appears to have been conducted. What is, therefore, required are studies in this area. Specifically, research should aim to establish how success is defined and measured, whether measures vary within an organization from one planning effort to another, whether measures differ depending upon the objectives of the exercise (i.e. alignment vs impact), whether they differ from organization to organization, across industry sectors and/or from country to country. Such research will enable us to address not only how we achieve success but exactly what it is.

Different organizational contexts influence who participates, the amount of time and money allocated and the focus of the process—i.e. impact or alignment. Her current research interests include strategic information systems planning and the application of soft systems methodology within Australian organizations.

Where, for example, organizations are critically dependent upon IT for organizational success, in organizations at the later stage of organizational growth and maturity (Nolan, 1979; Earl, 1983, 1989; McFarlan & McKenney, 1983; Bhabuta, 1988; Galliers & Sutherland, 1991), where corporate culture supports formal corporate planning and as organizations become larger and more complex, SISP needs to be extensive and more formal, closely linked to corporate planning and focused on management of the data resource and identification of applications to provide a competitive advantage and facilitate linkage between information systems and business strategies. On the other hand, where IT plays more of a 'support' role (McFarlan & McKenney, 1983), where organizations are at an early stage of growth and maturation (Nolan, 1979; Earl, 1983, 1989; McFarlan & McKenney, 1983; Bhabuta, 1988; Galliers & Sutherland, 1991) and where management style is less formal, lower levels of investment in IT and planning are appropriate—

SISP is less formal and has an internal focus with emphasis on management of the computer.

A successful planning exercise is also dependent upon the ability of the organization to overcome the various SISP problems identified previously (Galliers, 1987a). Since one of the major problems involves the communication of organizational objectives to the SISP team and therefore ensuring alignment between business plans and information systems plans, techniques to facilitate alignment will help to ensure success. The inclusion of business personnel in the SISP process (Martino, 1983; Galliers, 1986a) and the active participation by information systems management in business planning (McLean & Soden, 1977; McFarlan & McKenney, 1983; Lederer & Mendelow, 1987) may facilitate such alignment. It is not sufficient to try to communicate objectives to information systems management after the business planning process has been conducted, rather, it is ideal to include information systems management in business planning. Clearly, a necessary precursor to successful SISP is the existence of business plans or, at minimum, the existence of business objectives.

The obtaining of management involvement in and commitment to planning (and ultimately plan implementation) is another factor which will help to ensure success (Earl, 1990). Such commitment may be obtained by the presence of a senior executive 'champion' and by ensuring the process is kept short and on schedule (Galliers, 1986a). Management often expects results quickly and hence loses interest in and confidence with exercises which appear to 'drag on' (Lederer & Sethi, 1988). Time overruns during planning also reduce management's confidence in the ability of information systems personnel to implement plans once established.

Ensuring the right planning participants is another important aspect of SISP. Ideally, the team should incorporate information systems professionals with business, communication, technical and analytical expertise and business professionals with both business and information systems skills.

Finally, the selection of a planning approach and, ultimately, a planning methodology suitable to the organizational environment (Davis, 1982; Pyburn, 1983; Sullivan, 1985; Earl, 1987, 1990) and ensuring due consideration is given to the 'political' nature of planning (Galliers, 1986a) may again aid SISP efforts. Ignoring the contingency nature of SISP and ignoring organizational politics can be the downfall of an information systems planning exercise.

## Guidelines for plan implementation

In terms of ensuring successful implementation of the resultant strategic plans, the following guidelines may be considered useful:

(1) Since management typically approves and prioritizes development efforts on the basis of identified benefits and costs, these should be included in the plan (Lederer & Mendelow, 1988). The plan should also be documented in terms that management can understand and formal presentation of the plan to management may also facilitate approval for implementation.

(2) Consideration should be given to personnel and training requirements (Lederer & Mendelow, 1988). Frequently, projects identified during planning require new or enhanced skills if implementation is to be successful. The attainment of these skills therefore needs to be carefully considered during planning. Recommending the development of systems doomed to failure because of inadequate skill or personnel levels will only serve to reduce managements confidence in information systems activities.

(3) Planning should not be viewed as a one-off process. Plans must be revised as organizational changes occur if they are to provide the benefits previously discussed. The establishment of a 'comparative systems intelligence activity' (Head, 1982) may also form part of the review process. The objective of this activity is to monitor the capabilities and future plans of competitors.

(4) Provision should be made within the plan for taking small steps rapidly with each small step contributing towards a complete, integrated system (Head, 1982).
