# Measures of success for lone-user management support systems

P. N. Finlay

Loughborough University Business School, Loughborough University of Technology, Loughborough, Leicester LE11 3TU, UK

Abstract. Management support sytems (MSS) are systems that involve the use of information technologies to support management: lone-user MSS are such systems used by managers acting alone, whether the systems have been developed for an individual's exclusive use or for more than one user. Information systems research has been hampered by the lack of widely agreed measures of success. There are two main reasons for this. First, there has been a lack of rigour in the presentation of research findings, particularly in the definition of the types of system that have been investigated. This is particularly the case with MSS. Second, there is often no direct way of determining the effect of an MSS on the goals of an organization in the midst of many other concurrent factors. A classification of lone-user MSS is first given. The many, often indirect, measures of success that have been suggested are then charted. These are then incorporated into a hierarchical framework of organizational goals and intra-MSS measures. Finally, appropriate measures of success to use with the two major classes of MSS are suggested.

Keywords: decision support systems, group support systems, management information systems, management support systems, measures of success, success.

## INTRODUCTION

Keen (1987) defines the 'mission' of information systems research as 'to study the effective design, delivery, use and impact of information technologies in organizations and society'. By implication, there is a need to measure information systems effectiveness.

For operating level systems such as those for order-processing and stock control, it is generally possible to determine both the costs incurred in their implementation and many of the major benefits that accrue from their introduction into an organization in terms of cost savings. The same is true of Management Science studies that are in the nature of projects (see for example the list of finalists for the Edelman Prize, 1990). In such cases, traditional cost-benefit analysis may be an appropriate tool. However, even in simple cases involving the provision of management information, the benefits of having more up-to-date or more precise information are less tangible and much less easy to evaluate. As information systems are developed to support more complex managerial situations the benefits become more intangible and measures of success become more difficult to justify and establish.

Scott Morton (1984) defines management support systems (MSS) as systems that involve 'the use of information technologies to support management'. They include such systems as management information systems and decision support systems.

Research evidence of the difficulty in assessing the benefits of an information system on organizational effectiveness comes from Wagner (1981). He quotes a study in which around 80% of the decision support systems' users could not quantify the benefits of their system. However, although quantification may be difficult, subjective feelings of success in terms of user satisfaction are not so: very early on in the introduction of information systems, Garrity (1963) found that there was little difficulty in characterizing user satisfaction.

The difficulty in determining the influence of information systems on organizational effectiveness and the ease with which users can state whether they are satisfied with an information system suggests that user satisfaction is likely to play a large part in the determination of information system success. Whilst user satisfaction has the great merit of making paramount the feelings of the user management, it does not take into account the views of higher level managers and other stakeholders who would like to know whether organizational efficiency or effectiveness have been improved by the use of the system. Tomlinson (1984) writes 'Client satisfaction is clearly essential for success, but client satisfaction may not be enough. The change must be for the better.' User satisfaction also does not consider that a 'better' system might have been made available, and there is no direct comparison with any 'standard' or 'ideal'.

As well as the problem of relating information systems to organizational effectiveness, the establishment of agreed measures of success has been hampered by a lack of rigour in the presentation of research findings. Recently, the author has been researching the factors for success in building and implementing MSS. What soon became apparent in the research was that no single classification of MSS was being adhered to in the literature and that it was often difficult, and sometimes impossible, to identify unambiguously the type of MSS being considered. Since different types of MSS are developed to support different organizational objectives, it would not appear, a priori, that the same measures of success would be useful measures for all forms of MSS. It is thus imperative when attempting to measure MSS effectiveness that a classification of MSS be established. For this reason, one section of the paper is concerned with establishing such a classification.

This paper is concerned with what are here termed 'lone-user MSS'. The term lone-user is preferred to 'individual MSS' for systems used by individuals working alone. This is because in design and implementation there is a great deal of difference between systems that are built for the exclusive use by one manager and those that may be used individually but have been created for use by many.

This paper is concerned with the success of MSS and the measures associated with success. It is not concerned with what might be termed 'measures of failure'—with the 'downside' to the use of MSS. For example, a well-established financial MSS will tend to lead to one particular and ossified view of an organization: MSS developed to aid strategic planning can well narrow down the options considered thereafter.

The paper is organized as follows. A simple classification of lone-user MSS into two types is first provided. The measures of success that have been proposed are then reviewed. Further consideration is then given to these success measures: in particular, through the structuring of the measures into a hierarchy of organizational goals and the incorporation of the associated concept of validity into this hierarchy. The proposed measures of success are fitted into the hierarchy developed previously, their appropriateness discussed and the most appropriate identified. In the final section the implications of the proposals are discussed.

## A CATEGORIZATION OF MANAGEMENT SUPPORT SYSTEMS

Data can be considered to be those stimuli from outside the system that pass the rules embedded in the interface between the system and its surroundings. For example, parts of a computerized order entry system might be constructed so that only numerical values are accepted: non-numeric stimuli would be rejected at the interface.

In a business context, information may be defined as data that are perceived by an employee to be of actual or potential use to him/her directly in his/her job, and that is presented in a manner which permits that use. Zachary (1986) considers that information refers to data that have been interpreted by the decision-maker in the context of some knowledge about the decision situation. For example, an information system for sales management might be designed to provide monthly summaries of aggregated sales for the preceding month and nothing at a finer detail.

Intelligence is a higher form of knowledge than is information. Murray (1979) rather felicitously defines intelligence as 'the outcome of the meshing and reconciliation of a set of information carrying inferences'. Note that this usage differs somewhat from that of Simon (1977): it is used in a manner similar to Simon's strategic management information. This process of information interpretation possesses what Checkland (1981) calls an emergent property: the outputs are of a different class to the inputs; constructs are created from concepts.

The contrast between information and intelligence provides the major classificatory feature of MSS—between systems that process data to provide information, and systems that help with the interpretation of information. Both support management activities so both are MSS.

Since the output from data processing systems of interest to managers is management information, these systems are termed management information systems (MIS). Davis & Olson (1985) define an MIS as 'an organizational subsystem for providing information to support the operation, management and decision-making functions in the organization'. This is in essence the definition to be used in this paper. Following the usage of Finlay & Forghani (1987), systems used for information interpretation, and which provide intelligence, will be termed management intelligence systems (MINTS).

It is recognized that most writers in the field have not used the terms MSS and MINTS, preferring to use the terms MIS and decision support systems (DSS). The term MSS is preferred as the major generic term since it eschews such restricting words as information and decision. The rationale for using the term MINTS rather than DSS is that MIS are also supporting decisions and to use both the terms MIS and DSS would suggest otherwise.

One well-known classification of MSS is that of Alter (1977). His major categories of what he terms DSS are defined by the degree to which the output of a system directly determines the decision. The major split made by Alter is between data-oriented and model-oriented systems, following the traditional split between computer science and management science. This paper follows that categorization in so far as data-oriented systems are almost always simply providing information (and are thus MIS), whereas in Alter's terms model-oriented systems are providing intelligence (and are thus MINTS).

A comparative view of the emphases within MIS and MINTS has been developed by several authors (for example, Bidgoli, 1989 and Finlay, 1992). The features of these comparisons that are particularly significant for the subsequent discussion are that MIS tend to be restricted to using past data, concerned with structured tasks, their use tends to be mandatory and they provide information in a context-free setting. (In particular, extrapolatory forms of MSS are considered MIS.) On the other hand, MINTS are concerned with supporting altogether more complex situations: they are concerned with the future, with unstructured tasks, enhancing intelligence and judgement through insightful learning and are used in a context-dependent setting.

## PREVIOUSLY SUGGESTED MEASURES OF SUCCESS FOR MSS

Three strands can be discerned in the development of measures of success for MSS. The simple types of MSS being developed in the 1960s and early 1970s enabled single measures of success to be postulated. As MSS were developed to support more complex situations, more complex measures of success were seen to be needed and were derived. Later, process considerations were seen to be important.

## Single measures of success

It has been argued by Garrity (1963) and by Powers & Dickson (1973) that in the case of commercial organizations, both organizational efficiency and effectiveness should ultimately show in the profitability, and that enhanced profitability is the most appropriate measure of the success of an MSS.

However, given the difficulties in determining the nexus between MSS and organizational efficiency and effectiveness, several indirect criteria of success other than profitability have been suggested. Both Garrity (1963) and Swanson (1974) have proposed widespread use as the measure of success. Garrity (1963) also proposed that the application of an MSS to a major problem in an organization be used as a measure of success.

Improvement in the quality of decisions has been proposed by Carlson (1967). An allied measure of success is that of level of performance, as proposed by Lucas (1975).

Gallagher (1974) attempted to quantify the benefits of MSS by conducting a survey within a firm and obtained a perceptual measure of the monetary value of existing reports by asking the users how much they would be willing to pay for each report. In his review article, Srinivasan (1985) additionally identifies incremental performance in decision-making effectiveness as a measure of success. Raymond (1985) considers information accuracy, timeliness and the relevance of the contents of reports as measures of success.

The measures of success given above are all concerned primarily with organizational impacts, seek to be quantitative and are product oriented. Others have looked more widely. User satisfaction has been proposed as a criterion of success by many authors (see Kim, 1989 for a listing). In his article, Kim (1989) has summarized the results of much of the work that has been published dealing with user satisfaction for lone-user MIS. Kim's (1989) review has led him to identify three interacting perspectives of user satisfaction: attitudes toward MIS, MIS effectiveness, and information quality.

The conventional commercially used measures of success such as return on investment (Rol) and return on assets (RoA) may be used for investments in information technology. Additionally, Strassmann (1990) indicates that information technology-to-expense ratio is the most frequently used comparative index for assessing computer operations

Recently, Strassmann (1990) has publicized the idea of return on management as the key to measuring organizational performance and, by extension, the most appropriate measure for MIS success. He defines return on management as the ratio of management value added to management costs, with management added-value being the residual after every contributor to an organization's inputs has been paid.

## Multiple success criteria

Although he was addressing the question of success in group DSS, it is valuable at this point to quote from Eden (1991) as the sentiments apply equally well to lone-user MSS:

'By their very nature GDSS are, and will be, complex systems of computer hardware, computer software, procedures, environments and facilitation in a mix of proportions. Ashby's law of requisite variety (Ashby (1964)) suggests that a complex system will be needed to support a complex situation. The criteria of success for such systems will inevitably be equally complex.'

Single measures of success would thus appear unlikely to be satisfactory for anything but the simplest forms of MSS and this leads to a search for more complex measures. However, it should be noted that Baroudi & Orlikowski (1988) quote Ginzberg to the effect that a single item overall user satisfaction measure provided better convergent and discriminant validity than a multiple item measure. Perhaps most measures of MSS success are highly correlated and hence one general measure can be as powerful as a complex instrument.

Several studies have defined systems success in terms of satisfying a list of success factors (see Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988 and Kim, 1989). In all cases weightings and scores are given to the factors and these values are combined mathematically to produce an overall systems success score. Alloway & Quillard (1981) identified 26 success factors, Bailey & Pearson (1983) identified 39, Welsch (1981) identified 40, and Schultz & Slevin (1975) identified 57 factors. Some researchers have simply given a list of factors, whilst others have used a 'two-level' approach. In this, the first level consists of dimensions of success and the second of the success factors contributing to each dimension.

Keen (1981) has produced a list of 12 benefits frequently cited by users of DSS. Examples are 'Increase in the number of alternatives examined', 'Better understanding of the business', 'Control' and 'Time savings'. It can be seen from this list that Keen was using the term 'DSS' very widely, seemingly to cover all MSS.

Sanders (1984) devised a questionnaire consisting of 13 statements for measuring the lone-user's perception of DSS success. These can be grouped into those factors that relate to the user's perceived overall satisfaction with the system (for example, 'I have become dependent on DSS in performing my job', and 'All in all I think that DSS is an important system for this organization') and those that indicate improved decision-making (for example, 'DSS has improved the quality of decisions I make in this organization', and 'As a result of DSS, the speed at which I analyse decisions has increased').

Webby & O'Connor (1990) report on their experiments on the value of DSS. The measures of success they used were performance, profit stability, confidence, alternatives considered, time taken and predictive accuracy.

## Process considerations

Some studies have extended the definition of information system success to include factors other than those relating directly to the finished (or 'signed-off') product. For example, Swanson (1974) included user satisfaction with the procedures required to obtain reports: Miller & Doyle's (1984) measures of system success include some that address the quality of the organizational support for developing and maintaining the system.

In general, however, there has been little research on the success of MSS where the process of supporting decision-making is as important or more important than the products developed. (This is especially so where the development of the decision aid is an evolutionary one.) In some ways this would be looking at the success of the providers of management support over a long period in the organization's life: that is, at the success of a management science group, information centre and the like.

Forghani (1989) has identified seven categories of individual in an organization who can have a considerable impact on the success of a MINTS. His view is that as one moves away from MIS towards MINTS the emphasis in MSS development shifts from a product orientation to one of establishing and maintaining good relations with people in the organization.

Forghani was predominantly concerned with spreadsheet and multicriteria decision systems. His emphasis in successful MSS development and implementation is on the interactions between the people involved with an MSS, particularly that between the designer and the manager for whom the system is being built. He views MSS as products but not as finished products; i.e. they are developed and extended as the need arises. The strength and quality of the relations (termed intangible relations) between all significant participants in the design, implementation and use of an MSS is how Forghani measures success.

## Organizational perspectives

It is not appropriate to consider the use of lone-user MSS without considering their use in organizational decision-making. McCartt & Rohrbaugh (1989) have developed the competing values approach (CVA) to determine the success of group MSS and their approach has applicability also to the use of lone-user MSS. The CVA was developed from Quinn & Rohrbaugh's (1983) work on the effectiveness criteria used by organizational theorists to evaluate the performance of organizations. In its application to group MSS, four perspectives emerged, which were called the empirical, rational, political and consensual. The empirical perspective emphasizes the importance of information in the decision process and stresses the need for full accountability. The rational perspective favours logic over empiricism and tends towards methods that can efficiently assist decision-makers with their reasoning. The political perspective values adaptability and flexibility and is concerned with solutions that maintain or enhance the standing of the decision-makers. The consensual perspective expects full participation and prizes collective agreement on mutually satisfactory solutions.

Two criteria are associated with each perspective, one of which provides a standard for the nature of the process (i.e. data-based, goal-centred, adaptable and participatory) and one which assesses the respective ends achieved by the decision-making process, NOT by the decision—i.e. accountability (the provision of supporting evidence and documentation), efficiency, legitimacy (the acceptability of the decision to absent stakeholders) and supportability (the acceptability of the decision to all parties to the decision).

## FURTHER CONSIDERATION ABOUT THE MEASURES OF SUCCESS

## Hierarchy of measures of success

It is now generally recognized (see for example Earl, 1989) that MSS should support the goals of the organization in which they are to be used. By extension, the measures of success of MSS must be sought in terms of the help they provide in achieving these goals. Organizational goals form a hierarchy: indeed, such a hierarchy is implicit in the foregoing discussion. Garrity (1963) and Powers & Dickson (1973) propose enhanced profitability as the most appropriate measure of the success of an MSS: Carlson (1967) proposes quality of decisions, others suggest user satisfaction. These are measures associated with different levels in the organizational hierarchy.

Enhanced profitability is likely to be only one dimension of corporate success. Sanders (1984) has adapted earlier work to provide further measures: productivity, growth, innovation, adaption to change, contribution and survival. To these might be added market share, cash generation and technical excellence. The particular measures used by an organization will be contextually determined. For commercial organizations the ultimate measure of success is likely to be increased competitive advantage: for not-for-profit organizations it may be maintaining adequate relations with all its stakeholders.

This lower portion is derived from the interactions of attitude, use and information quality as proposed by Kim (1989). The use of a lone-user MSS should enhance the information and intelligence of the individual manager. However, these enhancements are associated with the initial stages of decision-making—with information gathering and with analysis. To be usefully employed these enhancements must be incorporated into a wider decision-making context, taking into account factors not addressed in the use of lone-user MSS. These factors are of two sorts. First there are those aspects of a decision situation not included within the MSS—perhaps the knock-on effects of one option on other decision areas for example. Second, there is the need to involve others in the organization in the decision: very significantly, the outputs from the individual MSS will often be employed in a group context, as managers discuss problem situations with their work colleagues. Thus group decision-making effectiveness and efficiency will be affected by the informational and intelligence outputs resulting from the use of lone-user MSS. It is these two wider decision-making considerations that lead to the lower portion being linked to the upper portion of Fig. 2—the linking of the intra-MSS measures with the organizational context.

## Validity

The implication of the discussion so far is that it is possible to identify the changes wrought by the introduction of an MSS into an organization: at the higher levels through such measures as increased market share, at the lower levels by such measures as enhanced information quality. For some types of MSS, particularly those used at strategic levels where the decision-making is complex, ill-structured and non-recurring, it is appropriate additionally to address the question of success in a different manner, and in doing so develop another dimension to success. Here, the output measures need to be augmented by 'internal' measures through a validity of the MSS itself. Whilst the measures of success already discussed provide the basis for a form of validation (since they measure the appropriateness of the use of MSS) the validity considered here is of the form and structure of the MSS itself.

Three broad types of validity are generally recognized: construct, content and criterion validity (American Psychological Association, 1974). As argued by Kerlinger (1973), the significant point about construct validity that makes it different from other types of validity is its preoccupation with theory. The requirement to establish construct validity is first to examine critically the theory that lies behind the design of the MSS and second, to compare the implementation of the MSS with what theory suggests. For example, in multicriteria decision-making, it is generally recognized that the use of pairwise comparisons produces more reliable and reproducible rankings of options than does simply ranking all options in one list. Thus, if it were required to rank a set of options, an MSS that offered pairwise comparisons to help achieve this would he considered more valid than one that did not.

Content validity refers to the completeness and the appropriateness of the content of an MSS. Checks need to be made that the domain of the study includes sufficient ingredients of the real-world situation under investigation.

Criterion validity is concerned with the match between information generated by the system and that generated by an alternative procedure itself accepted as valid (Kirk & Miller 1986).

## INTERIM SUMMARY AND SUBSEQUENT ARGUMENTATION

MIS and MINTS were contrasted in terms of five characteristics. It is these differentiating characteristics that will suggest the most appropriate measures of success to be associated with different types of MSS.

The measures of success identified through a search of the literature have been listed. Five organizational dimensions were ranked in a hierarchy and linked to five intra-MSS measures, including intelligence quality and validity. Following Gray et al. (1990) these will henceforth be termed metavariables.

The remainder of the paper is concerned with developing an appropriate set of measures for gauging MSS success. The broad contribution to be expected from MIS and MINTS associated with each metavariable will be discussed and appropriate measures of success will be identified. Finally, the appropriateness of these measures will be discussed and the most appropriate selected to form the proposed instrument to use when evaluating MSS success.

## APPROPRIATE MEASURES OF SUCCESS

Whilst all forms of MSS should ultimately contribute to the highest organizational goals, they would do so indirectly. Given the difficulty of determining the link between the use of an MSS and organizational change, it is more appropriate to look for success in their influence on lower organizational measures, starting with organizational effectiveness and efficiency. Given the linking difficulty, only first order contributions will be considered.

## Organizational measures

## Organizational effectiveness

Effectiveness may be defined as doing the right thing (Drucker, 1974), organizational effectiveness with doing the right things for the organization as a whole. MIS provide information about the past and present. On the other hand, MINTS are future oriented, providing intelligence and option-generating features and are used for planning. This suggests that MIS will have a second-order effect on organizational effectiveness (through providing information for decision-making) whilst MINTS should make a more significant impact. Thus it would not be appropriate to seek success for MIS in terms of enhancements to organizational effectiveness. However, it would be appropriate to do so for MINTS.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered suitable dimensions of organizational effectiveness are: adaption to change/response to the 'new', 'faster response to unexpected situations; cash generation; cost-benefit trade-off; growth; innovation; IT-to-expenses ratio; market share; profitability; profit stability; RoA; Rol; return on management; and technical excellence.

The IT-to-expenses ratio and return on management as described by Strassmann (1990) are measures to be applied to the whole of an organization's investment in IT, rather than to individual MSS. Thus they are not appropriate. Cost–benefit trade-off is also not an appropriate measure of success because its use demands that a monetary value be placed on information and on other, even less tangible outputs, and this is well nigh impossible to do in practice. Since assets, as these are usually defined by accountants, are becoming an ever-smaller proportion of the cost of MSS, RoA is also a poor measure of MSS effectiveness.

Profit stability is a rather subtle measure; profitability is less so, is a more understandable measure and is preferred. Faster response to unexpected situations seems so similar to response to the new that there is no need for both to be used.

Whilst it is recognized that the significance of many of the measures other than profitability and response to the new will be determined by the context in which the organization finds itself, all are indicated as (potentially) appropriate measures of success for MINTS.

## Organizational efficiency

Efficiency may be defined as doing the thing right (Drucker, 1974). Since MIS are concerned with providing information about the past, particularly about the operations of the organization itself for control purposes, it would be appropriate to seek success for an MIS in terms of increased organizational efficiency. Given that the definition of MINTS used in this paper excludes those MSS that are extrapolatory in nature, there would be a less significant contribution expected of MINTS.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate dimensions of organizational efficiency are: communication, contribution, control, cost–benefit trade-off, cost savings, efficiency, productivity and time savings.

For the reasons previously given, cost-benefit trade-off is not an appropriate measure. Contribution can be ignored since in efficiency terms it is tantamount to cost savings (with enhanced profitability taken as a measure of organizational effectiveness).

MIS would be expected to contribute significantly to all measures. MINTS would be expected to contribute to cost savings, productivity and time savings.

## Decision-making effectiveness

Decision-making effectiveness is concerned with the respective ends achieved by the decision-making process, rather than by the decision per se. It is task-oriented and is concerned with the substantive rationality of the decision and with a commitment to action the decision.

MIS help change decision-making effectiveness through the provision of better information: MINTS seek to enhance decision-making effectiveness by providing managers with insights and understanding and allowing options to be explored. This suggests that both should make a considerable impact on decision-making effectiveness, but with MINTS making a stronger one.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate dimensions of decision-making effectiveness are: accountability, better decisions, commitment to action, emotional commitment, increased confidence, incremental decision-making effectiveness, more intelligent decisions, legitimacy, political commitment, predictive accuracy, quality of decisions, substantive rationality and supportability.

Better decisions, more intelligent decisions, quality of decisions and incremental decision-making effectiveness are all equivalent to decision-making effectiveness and consequently will not be used.

All these measures apply to MINTS. Measures appropriate to MIS are accountability, legitimacy and supportability.

## Decision-making efficiency

Decision-making efficiency is concerned with the resources used during the decision-making process and with whether the process of decision-making (the means rather than the ends) can be defended. It is gauged by the procedural rationality underlying the decision-making sessions.

The efficiency of decision-making would be enhanced through the provision of better and more timely information and improved process. Thus it would be expected that MIS in general would contribute significantly to this organizational goal; MINTS rather less.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate dimensions of decision-making efficiency are: the use of data, efficiency, focus on key issues, goal-centred, procedural rationality, and the time taken for decision-making.

Focus on key issues and goal-centred are tantamount to the same and the former term will be used. Efficiency is the same as decision-making efficiency and will not be used.

## Intra-MSS measures

## Intelligence quality

Intelligence is the result of the meshing together of information and thus the major contribution to intelligence quality would be expected to be from MINTS. Previously identified measures that may be considered appropriate to judge whether a MINTS has helped in this are: the ability to carry out ad hoc analyses, the number of alternatives examined, depth of analysis, emotional commitment, extending competences, generation of ideas, new insights and learning, political commitment, and better understanding of the business.

## Information quality

Information quality is a primary user measure of success for MIS. With MINTS it becomes less applicable as their outputs are primarily managerial insights and learning.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate dimensions of information quality are: information accuracy/reliability, content of reports, format, timeliness, information value, value of reports.

A measure for the information scope of the system is also needed: for example, whether the system is restricted to providing information on intra-organizational matters alone, or whether it also provides information from the environment. Value of reports is so close a concept to value of information that it will not be used.

## Attitude to MSS

Attitude to MSS is an overall, subjective measure of user satisfaction. It encompasses employee welfare and the relationships between other parties involved with the MSS.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate dimensions of attitude to MSS are: employee welfare, intangible relationships, tangible relationships, satisfaction with procedures for getting reports, satisfaction with the procedures for getting systems enhancements and maintenance.

All MSS would be expected to contribute to, or at least not detract from, employee welfare. For MIS, tangible relationships cover the procedures for obtaining reports and for maintaining the system. For MINTS, intangible relationships cover the personal relations between all significant parties.

## Use of MSS

Since use of a MINTS tends to be voluntary, system use is a valid measure of success: this is not so for MIS where use tends to be mandatory.

Previously identified measures of success that may be considered appropriate are: application to a major problem area, repeat use and utilization rate. Whether a manager voluntarily decides to use MINTS repeatedly must be the major measure of success to employ.

## Validity

The specific requirements for construct, content and criterion validity depend very much on the type of MSS and the context in which these systems are used. A detailed practical validity framework has been devised by Finlay & Wilson (1992a), and a contingent validation methodology is currently under development (Finlay & Wilson, 1992b). Specifically, ease of use is considered one dimension of validity.

## SUMMARY AND IMPLICATIONS

Most of the published work on measures of success has put the emphasis on the success of a product. This emphasis is inappropriate where the MSS is primarily concerned with the process of decision-making, and where the longer-term considerations associated with an evolving MSS are significant. Here, establishing and maintaining relationships are important.

It would be preferable to have a suitable measure of success associated with goals at the highest level in the organization, but practicalities mean that reliance must often be placed on indirect measures. A hierarchy of organizational and MSS goals has been developed and measures of success for each level in the hierarchy have been listed. MSS have been broadly classified into MIS and MINTS and appropriate measures for each of these types have been suggested. These suggestions have been listed in appendix 2.

It is recognized that the nature of the measurement process must be related to the purpose for which the measurement is to be used. If the purpose is to compare the investment in an MSS with other investments then organizational measures would be sought. If the purpose is to discover whether an MSS meets user needs then intra-MSS measures would be more appropriate.

No single measure of success is really appropriate and none has found full acceptance. With MIS, for which use is often mandatory, attitude to MSS would probably be the best single intra-MSS measure of success if one were needed. For MINTS, where use tends to be discretionary, repeat use of the system would probably be the most appropriate single measure of intra-MSS success.

One of the principal reasons for attempting to obtain a widely accepted set of measures of success is to help create a substantive framework for research: without such measures progress in understanding the factors of importance in the development of MSS will be inhibited. The set of measures of success appropriate to the MSS under investigation (as itemized in appendix 2) would be incorporated as dependent variables to augment research frameworks.

A second important reason is to offer a more appropriate and therefore useful set of measures to the practitioner, particularly for auditing systems effectiveness in supporting the achievement of organizational goals. Success would first be sought in terms of the highest organizational levels; failing that, less direct measures would be used appropriate to the type of MSS under examination. One procedure would be for practitioners and other interested parties to agree the weighting to be attached to each hierarchical level within their own organization, and then to estimate the strength of the influence that an MSS would be expected to have on the measures at these levels. This would provide, albeit crudely, a ranking of MSS projects and some measure of MSS effectiveness. Figure 2 and appendix 2 provide the framework for further research in this direction.

Several well-known instruments to measure intra-MSS characteristics (especially user satisfaction) have been developed and validated (for example, those of Bailey & Pearson, 1983, Ives et al., 1983 and Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988). These are primarily aimed at MIS. Appendix 2 and its associated argumentation provide the framework for augmenting these instruments as anticipated by Baroudi & Orlikowski (1988).

A third reason for seeking a widely accepted set of measures of success is pedagogic: a generally agreed set of measures of success would aid the teaching of the whole area of information systems, since such a set would provide the focus for discussing MSS design and implementation strategies.
