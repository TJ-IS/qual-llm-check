# Information technology in support of individual decision-making

J. R. G. Wood and A. T. Wood-Harper

Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

Abstract. The use of computers in organizations has often been justified by reference to the 'improved decision-making' which will result from the use of new technology. Rarely, if ever, is any precise measurement given as to how such improvements will be judged. Phrases such as 'better information leads to better decisions' and 'what managers require is more information' dominate the literature on management information systems (MIS). Alistair Preston argues that this alignment with top management allowed MIS to legitimize and elevate itself as both a serious practice and an academic discipline.

The two dominant themes in the relationship between information technology (IT) and managerial decision-making have been the use of Herbert Simon's work on decision-making and Anthony's pyramidal structure of types of application system, the latter model leading to a powerful categorization of types of computer-based information systems (i.e. transaction processing, management information systems, decision support systems, executive information systems). The models of decision-making employed at each level of this hierarchy are those characterized by Simon as 'objective rationality' and 'bounded rationality' and these models encourage a view of information as representing some objective reality which can be captured, stored and processed within some form of technology. Dick Boland has argued forcibly that Simon's work has been crucial to the current level of understanding of information and technology within organizations.

Less concern has been given, however, to the role which IT itself plays in reinforcing this rationalistic view of information and decision-making. As Mitroff has pointed out, to represent (or model) a problem is to conduct an inquiry into its nature. Likewise, Dick Boland suggests that information technologies are themselves social inventions that are malleable and shaped during use but that such sets limits and opens possibilities that shape the users as well. Hence, information technologies both incorporate and give rise to changes in our vocabularies for knowing ourselves and our institutions. It will not be enough, therefore, for us merely to change our existing models of managerial decision-making, it will also be necessary to design new technologies which will reflect and encourage the use of such models.

In this paper we shall use the framework provided by Mitroff to try to consider a number of different paradigms of inquiring system and to see what technological forms would need to be developed in order that we may provide technological support for individual decision-making based on each paradigm.

Key words: decision making, decision support, information systems, information technology, problem solving.

## INTRODUCTION

The impact of information technology upon managerial decision-making has had a somewhat chequered history, ranging from excessive enthusiasm for the potential of the computer to outright condemnation of the whole field of management information systems. In this paper we shall seek to explore some of the core assumptions which underpin the relationship between technology and the process of managerial decision-making. Our aim is to provide a new basis for the design of future 'decision support systems' and in so doing to set out the characteristics of such systems and to assess the extent to which current technological trends provide support for this new paradigm of design.

It is now widely accepted that computer-based information systems exist in order to support some form of purposeful human activity rather than as ends in themselves. It follows, therefore, that the design and implementation of these systems presupposes some understanding of such activity (Gorry & Scott Morton, 1971; Wilson, 1990). Although this understanding is now being sought from a wide variety of sources such a proliferation of viewpoints is a relatively recent phenomenon and still tends to be the domain of academics rather than practitioners.

Although much of the work in information technology (IT) and information systems (IS) is either implicitly or explicitly linked to the provision of information for managerial decision-making the most coherent treatment of this area has been provided within the field of what are termed decision support systems (DSS). The paper therefore begins by looking at the way in which this field has emerged out of the general concern with management information systems (MIS) and at some of the historical legacy of this development. We then look at the characteristics and components of DSS in order to distinguish such systems from other forms of computer application. Next, by using the critique of MIS set out by Mason & Mitroff (1973), the paper seeks to question the current design methodology underpinning much of the present work in DSS. We then highlight two of the changes taking place in technological development which may facilitate the emergence of a new design paradigm for DSS, before going on to discuss some of the desirable features of such a paradigm. This is not meant to be an exhaustive treatment of appropriate technological change, but rather to give some examples of the ways in which such developments will need to occur in order to support both epistemological and ontological change in the area of DSS. This paper should be seen, therefore, primarily as a contribution to the discussion on the desirability or otherwise of such epistemological and ontological change.

## BACKGROUND

The literature on IT and managerial decision-making has always been dominated by the work of two authors, Robert Anthony and Herbert Simon. In his book Planning and Control Systems: A Framework for Analysis, Anthony set out a classification scheme for types of managerial activity in which he described three categories, arguing that these represented activities sufficiently different in kind as to warrant the development of different types of system (Anthony, 1965). The three categories were those of Strategic planning, Management control and Operational control.

Although Anthony, together with subsequent writers who have adopted the so-called Anthony's Triangle model, admitted that the boundaries of the three categories were unclear this has not prevented the classification from being used extensively as a means of determining the necessary characteristics of information at each level of the model and as a way of identifying the technological support provided (Gorry & Scott Morton, 1971). For example, operational control systems are seen as requiring largely internal, well-defined, narrow and detailed historical information which must be highly accurate. Strategic planning, on the other hand, is seen as depending upon external, wide, aggregated information about the future which is required infrequently and need not be very accurate. Such a view of information 'requirements' led many people to define the categories of organizational activity in terms of their technological characteristics in such a way that Operational control became the domain of transaction processing (TP) systems, Management control that of MIS, and Strategic planning that of DSS and more recently executive information systems (EIS). The chronology of system development throughout the 1960s, 1970s and 1980s seemed to support this 'layered' view of information systems within organizations. Finally, the evolution of system development methods also appeared to follow this categorization as the life cycle methods used for TP systems, were followed by structured methodologies aimed at MIS development and then by the 4GL/prototyping approaches for DSS and EIS.

The second major strand of thought which influenced the role of IT in managerial decision-making was Herbert Simon's work on human problem-solving activity. In The New Science of Management Decision Simon distinguished between 'programmed' and 'nonprogrammed' decisions (Simon, 1960), and other writers have subsequently drawn a similar dichotomy using the notion of 'structure'. There is a clear belief in these classifications that the properties described are somehow 'possessed' by problem situations to greater or lesser degrees. Gorry & Scott Morton, for example, go on to talk about 'semi-structured' decision situations where one or more of Simon's three phases of decision-making is itself 'unstructured'. Worse still is the assertion that there are certain types of fully 'structured' problem situation (such as the traditional economic order quantity (EOQ) problem) which are well understood and amenable to a programmed (i.e. computer-based) solution. Following on from this, and incorporating Anthony's ideas, TP systems (e.g. Payroll) become 'structured' decision-making situations open to programmable solution, or as Gorry & Scott Morton would have it, 'straightforward data handling with no decisions'. Similarly, Sprague, in attempting to provide a definition of DSS that linked such a system to its contribution to decision-making, concludes that this would result in the term being applied to 'all (systems) but transaction processing' (Sprague, 1986).

The net effect of these two powerful stereotypes (one about managerial activity and the other about the nature of problem-solving) has been to create an image of the use of IT which is still pervasive throughout the industry. Even the advent of new 'technologies' such as office automation and expert systems has not served to alter this image; rather the use of the new 'technologies' has been dictated by the set of views represented by the Anthony/Simon tradition (see for example the idea of the rationalistic tradition put forward by Winograd & Flores, 1986).

## THE CHARACTERISTICS OF DSS

The impact of the Anthony/Simon tradition on the field of decision support systems (DSS) can be clearly seen in the literature. Alter and Keen, for example, attempted to provide definitions of the domain of DSS by reference to their characteristics (Alter, 1980; Keen, 1981). Observed characteristics of such systems which result from this work are, according to Sprague:

(1) that they tend to be aimed at the less well-structured, underspecified problems that upper level managers typically face;

(2) that they attempt to combine the use of models or analytic techniques with traditional data access and retrieval functions;

(3) that they specifically focus on features which make them easy to use by non-computer people in an interactive mode;

(4) that they emphasize flexibility and adaptability to accommodate changes in the environment and the decision-making approach of the user.

According to Sprague a DSS is 'a class of information system that draws on transaction processing systems and interacts with the other parts of the overall information system to support the decision-making activities of managers and other knowledge workers in the organizations'.

DSS are seen as being composed of the following three components (see Fig. 1):

(1) data management subsystem;

(2) model management subsystem; and

(3) dialogue management subsystem.

The data management subsystem is seen, in turn, as being made up of a database, a database management system (DBMS), a data dictionary and a query facility. In a similar fashion, the model management subsystem contains a model base, a model base management system, a model directory and a modelling command processor. Finally, the dialogue management subsystem provides for dialogue generation and management through the use of appropriate software.

DSS have been defined largely in contrast to what are seen as 'traditional' MIS but the differences between the two seem to be more apparent than real. Turban (1988), for example, lists what he terms an 'ideal set' of characteristics and capabilities for DSS, viz.:

(1) provision of support for decision-makers mainly in semistructured and unstructured situations by bringing together human judgement and computerized information;

(2) support for various managerial levels;

(3) support for both individual and group decision-making;

(4) support for both interdependent and/or sequential decisions;

(5) support for all phases of decision-making;

(6) support for a variety of decision-making processes and styles;

(7) they must be adaptive over time;

(8) they must be easy to use;

(9) they aim to improve the effectiveness of decision-making rather than its efficiency;

(10) the decision-maker has complete control over all steps of the decision-making process in solving a problem;

(11) they lead to learning; and

(12) they should be easy to construct.

Grouping these together we can see that the distinguishing features of DSS are seen to lie in:

(1) the mode of DSS development (e.g. adaptability, iteration, ease of construction);

(2) the relationship with the decision-maker (e.g. ease of use, support rather than replacement, decision-making style, differing managerial levels, individuals and groups); and

(3) the representation of the decision-making process (e.g. concern with semistructured and unstructured situations, interdependent and sequential decisions, all phases of the decision-making process and different varieties of this process, effectiveness rather than efficiency).

The first two categories apply equally well to other forms of information system development, including MIS. Although work in DSS may have focused attention on the importance of these issues, primarily through their association with PCs, such characteristics do not really distinguish DSS from other forms of information system. What seems to mark off DSS from other applications is the explicit technological support provided for the process of managerial decision-making. To understand both the potential success and the risk of failure in such systems, therefore, it is necessary to reveal both the assumptions concerning the process and those inherent in the technology.

To summarize, the view of DSS which has emerged from the theoretical and practical work on the last three decades can be described as follows.

(1) The manager acts as a rational decision-maker following Simon's three stages of problem-solving (Intelligence, Design and Choice).

(2) The role of technology within DSS is usually seen as operating at three different levels:

(a) the specific DSS application itself;

(b) technology to generate a specific DSS; and

(c) DSS tools which can be used for the development of both of the above.

(The term technology is used here to describe particular combinations of hardware and software.)

## ASSUMPTIONS ABOUT THE NATURE OF COMPUTER-BASED INFORMATION SYSTEMS

Mason & Mitroff, whilst also making use of Anthony's classification of managerial activity, suggested that there were a number of key variables which could determine the characteristics of an MIS (Mason & Mitroff, 1973). These variables were defined as being Psychological type, Problem type, Types of evidence generators and guarantors, Organizational context and modes of presentation. Mason & Mitroff saw Anthony's model as indicating the kinds of problems which organizations typically choose to solve (our italics). In this way, organizational structure becomes a reflection of the levels of problems the organization considers it important to solve. According to Wilensky, however, information problems or 'pathologies' are a reflection of the organizational structure (Wilensky, 1967). Thus, as Mason & Mitroff point out, the structure of an organization and its information system are in reality just 'two sides of the same coin'.

The majority of computer applications have been aimed at the operational level of the business, assuming operational level ‘problems’ (e.g. throughput, speed, bottlenecks) and attempting to meet operational level criteria (e.g. cost, labour reductions). In this way, according to Mason & Mitroff, such computer-based systems have reinforced those organizational structures designed to optimize the performance of the operational system. Other writers (Beer, 1975) have repeatedly pointed to the danger of replicating existing (and inappropriate) organizational structures and procedures within computer-based systems. Mason & Mitroff also point out that such computer-based information systems may be in direct conflict with the kind of structures necessary to promote future organizational growth and survival.

Mason & Mitroff also draw on Ackoff's distinction between structured and unstructured decision problems but at the same time they introduce Rittel's concept of wicked problems which, according to Rittel, are not well defined and for which there is no criterion which would determine whether a solution is correct or false and for which there is no rule which would tell the designer when to stop his search for a better solution. It is worth reflecting here upon Rittel's description of the difficulties involved in defining a wicked problem.

'Every formulation of the problem is already made in view of some particular solution principle. If the idea of the solution is elaborated or even changed during the design process, new aspects become relevant and new kinds of information will lead to different questions about what is the case in the particular situation and about what is desired or acceptable. Since nobody can anticipate all conceivable design possibilities before design starts, nobody can list all potentially relevant data in a complete, well-defining problem formulation.' (Rittel, 1971).

This paragraph should be emblazoned on the wall of every software development department in the country. Despite both theoretical and practical evidence to the contrary many writers on software engineering and formal methods still persist in the claim that we should seek to pursue complete, correct and unambiguous specifications for the IS which we build. Even those people who accept the difficulties involved in requirements analysis and determination still see such difficulties as being constraints on our ability to define such requirements rather than as calling into question the very nature of the deterministic approach being adopted in their definition (Davis, 1982; Valusek & Fryback, 1985). This viewpoint also ignores completely the 'shaping' element of the technology itself. As Dick Boland has pointed out information technologies are themselves social inventions which may be malleable and shaped during use but in turn such use sets limits and opens possibilities that shape the users as well. Hence Boland argues, ITs both incorporate and give rise to changes in our vocabularies for knowing ourselves and our institutions (Boland, 1991). The thesis of our paper, therefore, is that it will not be enough for us to change our existing models of managerial decision-making or of organizational structure, it will also be necessary to design new technologies which will reflect and encourage the use of such models.

Mason & Mitroff suggested two principles that might guide subsequent research into MIS:

'Managers need information that is geared to THEIR psychology NOT to that of designers.'

'Managers need a method of generating evidence that is geared to THEIR problems NOT those of designers.'

It is our contention that the majority of work in MIS and subsequently in DSS has been in direct contradiction to these two principles. For example, Mason & Mitroff offer a categorization of psychological types based on the Jungian typology which is characterized by four major modes or psychological functions, 'Sensation' and 'Intuition' (as ways of perceiving the objects of the world) and 'Thinking' and 'Feeling' (as ways of evaluating the objects of perception). It is clear that the majority of work on computer-based information systems has fallen into the category of 'Thinking/Sensation' hence stressing the symbolic nature of information together with its empirical form (e.g. raw data, hard facts, numbers). Again, the attraction of such a view to MIS and DSS designers is obvious given the characteristics of the technology they are typically working with. Mason & Mitroff hence come to the view that,

'What is information for one type will definitely not be information for another. Thus, as designers of MIS, our job is not to get (or force) all types to conform to one, but to give each type the kind of information he is psychologically attuned to and will use most effectively.'

Finally, Mason & Mitroff assert that managers will place more reliance on information which has been produced by one method of generating evidence than on that produced by another since, what they call, the guarantees provided by such an inquiring system will be that much greater. They go on therefore to suggest that an important factor in MIS design is the type of evidence generating system used and hence the type of guarantees that lie behind it.

## TYPES OF INQUIRING SYSTEM

Mason and Mitroff follow Churchman in distinguishing five archetypal inquiring systems which provide for a different representation of a problem by using different 'building blocks' (Churchman, 1971). They further suggest that each inquiring system produces a different kind of information about a given problem and that each has a different kind of guarantor for 'insuring the validity of the final information content'. Finally, Mason & Mitroff conclude that whilst any inquiring system can be used to generate evidence for any problem it does not follow that each such system is equally valid or appropriate as a way of representing all kinds of problems. The five approaches described are the Lockean IS, the Leibnitzian IS, the Kantian IS, the Hegelian IS and the Singerian–Churchmanian IS.

Lockean IS are seen as archetypal consensual systems which are built on an empirical, inductive representation of the problem. Elementary empirical judgements based on raw data and observations, for example, are constructed into increasingly expanding and general sets of 'facts'. The guarantor of these kinds of systems is the function of human agreement, that is the consensus of a group of human 'experts' that a particular empirical generalization is held to be 'objective' or 'true'. Thus the information content of a Lockean IS is held to be equivalent to its empirical content.

Leibnitzian IS are the archetype of formal, symbolic systems since for any kind of problem they will build a formal representation of it. Elementary primitive 'truths' are constructed into expanding and general propositional truths. The guarantor here ranges from a precise specification of some proof for a derived theorem or proposition to internal consistency, completeness or comprehensiveness. Thus the information content of a Leibnitzian IS is held to be equivalent to its symbolic content.

Kantian IS are the archetype of multi-model, synthetic systems since for any problem they will build at least two alternate representations of it. These representations will be partly Lockean and partly Leibnitzian in nature, hence providing a strong relationship between scientific theory and data. In this way Kantian IS also attempt to provide a number of explicit views of the problem. The guarantor here is the degree of fit between the underlying theory and the data which have been collected under the presumption of that theory. The final information content is a function of both theory and experiment.

Hegelian IS are the archetype of conflictual, synthetic systems since for any problem they will build at least two, completely antithetical, representations of it. Two opposing Leibnitzian models are used to expose the contrary underlying assumptions regarding the theoretical nature of the problem and both these models are then applied to the same set of Lockean data. The same data can be used here because data are not information since the latter results from the interpretation of data. The guarantor here lies in the conflict generated since it is presumed that such conflict will expose the assumptions underlying the expert's point of view. It is argued that these assumptions are often masked because of the apparent agreement of the experts.

Finally, Singerian–Churchmanian IS are seen as substantially more complicated than all the other archetypes and indeed as a possible means of studying all other forms of IS. Singerian–Churchmanian IS involve a process of continual learning and adaptation through feedback, which in turn are achieved by changing wicked problems into structured ones, and structured problems into wicked ones.

## DSS: THE CURRENT FOUNDATION OF DESIGN

Following Mason & Mitroff it can be seen that almost all the work on DSS has been based on the Lockean/Leibnitzian form of inquiry and that there has been little interest in approaches founded on the other three viewpoints. Thus we can characterize the present concern of DSS as being with:

(1) well-bounded structured problems;

(2) empirical data and their content;

(3) objectivity, and truth based on consensus;

(4) formal, symbolic representations; and

(5) decision analysis based on bounded rationality.

This set of assumptions about the nature and use of DSS have been reinforced by a technology which:

(1) demands the early and 'correct' specification of the problem to be solved;

(2) has limited capacity to store and retrieve the required data;

(3) has little or no concern with the semantic content of stored data;

(4) has few notations for representing objects of concern within the problem domain; and

(5) provides for a narrow, variety-poor and one-way form of communication with the decision-maker.

Much more recently than Mason & Mitroff, but in similar vein, Winograd & Flores have put forward what they believe to be the set of assumptions underlying much of the current thinking about computers and their uses (Winograd & Flores, 1986). They argue that this thinking has been shaped by what they call the rationalistic tradition, which is reflected in an orientation which typifies problem-solving as a series of steps, viz.:

(1) characterize the situation in terms of identifiable objects with well-defined properties;

(2) find general rules that apply to situations in terms of those objects and properties; and

(3) apply the rules logically to the situation of concern, drawing conclusions about what should be done.

There is an obvious correspondence between these steps and Simon's phases of the decision-making process, 'Intelligence', 'Design' and 'Choice'. Winograd & Flores go on to argue that this rationalistic orientation not only underlies all branches of science but has come to be thought of as 'the very paradigm of what it means to think and be intelligent'. Such a way of thinking has certainly dominated the literature and the practice of information system development. From the early work at the RAND Institute and Bell Laboratories through to present-day approaches to information engineering and CASE (computer-aided software engineering) there has been an unquestioning acceptance of the rationalistic orientation as the basis of the approaches taken to design. Only recently have alternative paradigms (notably those suggested by the rise of 'prototyping' and 'object orientation') begun to surface in the literature but they have, as yet, had little impact on the world of development. Using the critiques provided by Mason & Mitroff and Winograd & Flores we now wish to investigate how the design of DSS could proceed in the future and how recent technological advances may facilitate a change in the paradigm upon which such design would take place.

## COMPONENT ENVIRONMENTS AND OBJECT ORIENTATION

Previous approaches to information system design have been termed 'product' oriented in that they regarded software as being a product in its own right consisting of a set of programs together with related defining texts (Cox, 1990; Floyd, 1987). Thus the usage context of the software product was seen as being fixed and well understood, so allowing the requirements for the product to be determined in advance of design and implementation. Such approaches tend to place their emphasis on the efficient use of the hardware and often require working practices to adapt accordingly. So-called 'process'-oriented approaches, on the other hand, view software development as being carried out in a world of ever-changing human needs and emphasize the software 'product' as being necessarily tied in to the activities of human communication and learning. This orientation does not require that functionality is known in advance but rather that it emerges from the interleaving processes of development and use (Floyd, 1987). The development process can thus be conceptualized as a series of 'cycles' with the output from each cycle (i.e. the interim software 'product') being constantly evaluated in the context of the appropriate purposeful human activities. In this way 'users' can meaningfully intervene in order to adapt the functionality of the software product to their particular needs/requirements.

A number of leading writers on software development have suggested recently that the 'paradigm shift' in design described above will be brought about through the use of object-oriented methods and technologies. Brad Cox, for example, has advocated 'a software industrial revolution based on reusable and interchangeable parts that will alter the software universe as surely as the industrial revolution changed manufacturing' (Cox, 1990). This 'revolution' will be achieved by changing the emphasis of how software is viewed from the processes used to build 'objects' to the objects themselves. Thus it becomes more sensible to talk of systems being assembled, or even grown, from reusable components rather than being built from first principles. Such a change, it is argued, would also provide a means by which end-users rather than analysts or programmers would be the driving force behind system development.

A major problem, however, with object oriented computing is that the quality of the final 'system' will depend upon how well-designed the objects are and thus upon the methods used to select and define such 'objects' (Graham, 1991). The way in which a user and/or designer will conceptualize an object will be clearly determined by his/her intention (or Weltanschauung (Checkland, 1981). The initial problem for any user in this kind of situation will be finding out that a particular component exists and then developing an understanding of what it does. It has also been suggested that to be effective, any retrieval structure for these components should match the cognitive structure of the user. Thus the user interface of such a system will be crucial since it will largely determine the user's understanding of the behaviour of the system.

In seeking to understand how the interaction between an individual user and a DSS can be better handled it may be useful to consider the distinction between the conceptual (system) model and the user's mental model (Morris, 1991). The latter will be a personalized understanding of the system model based on the individual's set of previous experiences, knowledge, etc. The system model, on the other hand, represents some abstract account of how the system works and how this is conveyed to the user, as well as the basis upon which the system was designed. This interrelationship between design, behaviour and understanding is largely neglected by academics and practitioners alike.

We may further suggest a dichotomy in the way in which conceptual models may be formulated based on the notions of emulation and innovation. Whereas emulation emphasizes the familiar use of the user's knowledge through analogies and metaphors of an existing system, the innovation approach exploits the representational possibilities of the new tool and emphasizes new ways of thinking and doing. It can then be argued that these two approaches need not necessarily represent a dichotomy but could be combined in such a way that the selection of an analogy or metaphor does not inhibit the assimilation of new concepts (Morris, 1991). In this way both the user's model of the system and the conceptual model contained within it can come together through some adaptive process of achieving best 'fit' (Alexander, 1964).

It is also widely assumed in this kind of approach that the importation of application-specific knowledge into the development environment will always be creative. Such writers discuss notions such as extensibility, where users are not only able to automate current work procedures but may also incorporate new ones which will improve their environment. This approach may well conflict, however, with the need for coordination and control in systems development, given that there is an absence of a reliable and consistent model of the system being developed. Valusek & Fryback, for example, have pointed to the fact that users are often not sufficiently conscious of their wants and needs to be able to articulate them without the kind of assistance normally provided by systems analysts (Valusek & Fryback, 1985). It follows from this that any assumption that users can clearly articulate their own requirements in such a way as to allow effective design activity to take place will be highly questionable. This then is a key issue for proponents of the component approach.

To summarize, component-based software environments will support the development of software components which will provide generic building blocks of much finer granularity than those provided by existing application software packages. It is intended that these building blocks will be capable of self-assembly by users in order to provide software solutions to their information processing needs. Thus the development process will become informal, although not necessarily abnormal. This will result as a consequence of the user-driven nature of the process since in the component-world users not only initiate activity, as they do in most development processes, they also set the agenda for the project because of their continuous involvement in it.

## EVENT-BASED MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS

DSS have always been shown as having a data management subsystem at their core (see Fig. 1) and the literature always seems to assume that this would be some form of relational data-base. With the advent of 'expert systems' technology over the last few years it has become fashionable to talk about 'knowledge-based management systems' (KBMS) replacing conventional databased management systems (DBMS) in the future. The DSS community has been quick to realize the similarities between much of its work and that carried out in the artificial intelligence and expert system worlds. Turban, for example, talks of integrating the two approaches (DSS and ES) because 'they complement each other, creating a powerful, integrated, computer-based system that can considerably improve managerial decision-making' (Turban, 1988). It is suggested that AI can enhance traditional DSS in the following areas:

(1) by providing an intelligent interface through the use of natural language processors and graphics;

(2) by using inference engine mechanisms to improve the model base; and

(3) by using knowledge representation techniques to improve the data/knowledge subsystem.

However, as Doukidis points out, 'the technology-based approach of such management support systems have to be integrated with the human activity systems they serve' (Doukidis, 1989). It is still too often the case that research in these areas is driven by the technological imperative rather than by a concern with the real problems which organizations face. Waters, in complete contrast, starts from the day-to-day issues confronting a large commercial organization and argues that much of the theoretical work carried out in DBMS, whilst of considerable interest in its own right, has not been able to 'solve Citibank's MIS problems in practice' (Waters, 1989). He introduces the notion of an event-based management system (EBMS) as being a 'significant step towards achieving comprehensive knowledge-bases'. An event base is seen as containing 'a history of time-ordered significant events of the creation, change and suspension of entity, attribute and relationship occurrences and of their definitions'. The main thrust of Waters' argument is that current database technologies provide an inadequate treatment of time, particularly given the ways in which things like business 'rules', logic, definitions and relationships all change over time with respect to the stored data values. If we wish to use DSS (or indeed KBMSS) in order to undertake both historical analysis and future speculation then it is imperative that such systems are able to store and retain not only the life-cycles of entity, attribute and relationship occurrences but also those for their definitions, the logic (both processes and rules) as well as events (both real and imaginary). Low cost, mass storage technology will soon make the storing of 'all data over time' a real possibility and, as Waters says, attention will then focus on whether we can store 'all knowledge over time'.

## DSS: A NEW FOUNDATION FOR DESIGN

Despite its initial claims to the contrary it would appear that the focus of DSS for individual decision-making has been largely within the domain of 'structured' problems. The present logical structure of a DSS demands that this be so since the very construction of such a system requires the 'problem' to be defined in advance. How can we know what formal models are relevant and what data to collect and store, unless we have already determined the form of the problem and, more crucially, what constitutes the nature of a 'solution' to such a problem. Although workers in the DSS field implicitly recognize that following this line of thinking would inevitably constrain them to dealing with that class of problem which Ackoff labelled as difficulty, their reaction to this has been to talk about semi-structured problems, that is where one or more of Simon's phases of decision-making is seen to be uncertain in some way. What is not clearly explained is at what stage a problem situation becomes 'semi-structured' instead of being 'ill-structured'.

If we take ill-structured problem situations to be those labelled by Ackoff as messes (or in Rittel's terminology wicked) then clearly at the outset of the intervention one does not know what to model since it is not clear at this stage what the problem is, what, if anything, would constitute a solution, what the requirements are, and not even, perhaps, what questions to ask. In conventional system development these are the areas which the analyst, in conjunction with the 'users', would be expected to uncover during the life cycle of investigation, analysis and system design. Although, as was shown earlier, DSS development has emphasized issues such as the need for rapid iteration and the use of prototyping for both requirements analysis and design it does not eschew the idea that there is an organizational 'problem' domain within which the implementation of a DSS will take place in order to be used by a decision-maker to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the domain.

Humphreys, however, has suggested that senior decision-makers rather than seeking to constrain a problem situation in such a way that some form of prescribed solution can be determined, in fact use their discretion in order to 'act on the structure of that part of the social world of the organization within which they propose to implement the course of action they have chosen to handle the problem, with the aim of influencing the implementation process in promotion of the desired outcome' (Humphreys, 1989). Humphreys goes on to argue that such action requires three conditions to be met:

(1) the ability to explore the organizational impact of the planned changes;

(2) that the planned organizational changes be within the limits of discretion appropriate to the decision-maker; and

(3) that the process of change should be coherent and should cause the minimum of disruption.

These conditions emphasize the need to relate the given decision-making scenario to the particular Weltanschauung of an individual stakeholder who will explore such a scenario according to their appropriate level of resolution (Beer, 1975) or small world (Toda, 1976). Thus we cannot conceive of a single representation of the problem to be solved, or indeed a single representation of what would constitute a solution to the problem. Rather we seek multiple representations, both consensual and conflicting, which advance the alternative options open to the decision-maker.

The conventional view of DSS, along with all other forms of CBIS (computer-based information system), has been to focus attention on WHAT is to be modelled and HOW to model it. This has led, in turn, to two important consequences for the whole field of DSS. First, the separation of the 'modeller' from the 'decision-maker' and consequently of the model of the problem from the problem itself. This separation is now beginning to have serious consequences in industry (Zuboff, 1988) where individuals understand only how the MODEL works but no longer understand the situation which the model supposedly represents (e.g. recent experiences in the Stock Exchange). This divorce between form maker and form user (Alexander, 1964) has also contributed to the reification of the notion of problem (Preston, 1991). The belief that an outside expert or modeller can provide a formal description of the problem under consideration stems directly from the belief that such problems exist as 'objective realities' to be investigated, analysed and represented by means of existing formalisms.

Many writers in the field of information systems are beginning to question the implicit ontological and epistemological assumptions which underlie much of the thinking about this area, particularly with respect to the methodological implications of providing such a critique. With a few notable exceptions (Winograd & Flores, 1986) such criticisms have not yet provided a clear formulation of how interpretative decision support systems will look or how such systems will be developed. What we have tried to do in this paper is to set out the basis for rethinking the role that IT can play in support of the individual decision-maker and to suggest how technological change will need to proceed alongside concern with providing new accounts of managerial activity.

## A VISION OF DSS FOR INTERPRETATION

Douglas Engelbart has suggested that there have been five categories of developments in our cultural evolution which have augmented our human capabilities, these being language, artifacts, methodology, skills and knowledge and attitudes and beliefs (Engelbart, 1982). He further argues that elements within these different categories have an interactive effect on each other's evolution, and in particular that innovations in the Artifacts category almost inevitably produce changes in the categories concerned with 'culture'. Such changes will almost certainly be vital if the full potential of the innovations in the artifacts category are to be exploited.

What we have tried to show in this paper is how the artifacts of information technology have both had an effect on, as well as having been affected by, the cultural/organizational issues concerned with the processes of managerial decision-making. As Engelbart points out, it is the interaction between the categories of development which is ultimately crucial in deciding whether or not innovations in artifacts benefit or hinder human progress.

It is being increasingly recognized that the most important part of the problem-solving process lies in considering how we formulate or conceptualize the notion of 'problem' at the outset. Decision-making is not so much concerned with the rational search of a bounded set of possibilities for the optimum solution as it is to open up new sets of possibilities within which the decision-maker can act. Such action necessarily involves far more than the simple application of habitual means of problem solving (Winograd & Flores, 1986) or the selection of an alternative strategy from a predetermined list of logical possibilities. It is all too often the case that this form of approach suffers from what Winograd & Flores refer to as an 'exclusionary bias' which will lead to whole sets of possibilities never being considered or summarily dismissed from consideration.

Elsewhere Tony Gunton has proposed that 'the real long-term value of end-user computing lies in the accelerated learning on the part of the user about his or her job, about the discovery of innovative new approaches to tasks that can actually transform the nature of the job, and finally about the new opportunities and limits of the technology itself' (Gunton, 1988). It is precisely this sense of 'learning' about both the 'problem' and the technology by which this problem is brought into being which we are seeking to advocate here as the future role for decision support technology. In order for this to come about we shall need a view of such technology which will incorporate, amongst other things, the following features:

(1) a focus on the formulation of the 'problem' rather than merely providing an 'objective' description of the problem;

(2) it will allow the decision-maker to explore the problem context in terms of constraints, likely impact, leverage, etc;

(3) it will provide for ‘conversations about possibilities’ through the analysis of past patterns of action, and speculation about potential future actions; and

(4) it will be evolutionary and emergent in nature.
