# Linking soft systems methodology with data-focused information systems development

P. J. Lewis

Department of Systems and Information Management, The Management School, Lancaster University, Bailrigg, Lancaster LA1 4YX, UK

Abstract. Where the soft systems methodology (SSM) is used in the development of organizational information systems a clear division exists between the use of SSM to identify what information systems are required and conventional development activities in which it is decided how those information systems will be supplied. Discussion of how SSM might be more closely linked to conventional information systems development methodologies has been concentrated upon process-focused approaches to information systems development. This has been partly due to a perceived mismatch between the underlying philosophies of SSM and the alternative data-focused development methodologies. This paper argues that this perception may be mistaken; not only do the existing forms of data analysis have a large though unacknowledged subjective component but the SSM concept of appreciation may provide a model of human sense-making that the data-focused approaches currently lack and from which they may benefit. The idea of appreciation also allows that an alternative, interpretative form of data analysis might be used within SSM. It is therefore the conclusion of this paper that some closer integration of SSM with data-focused approaches to information systems development is theoretically feasible and may be practically desirable. A number of possible advantages of such integration are described.

Key words: data analysis, information systems development, systems thinking.

## INTRODUCTION

Although the soft systems methodology (SSM) already has a role in the development of information systems this has been principally as a precursor to conventional information systems development (ISD) activities organized within the framework of the systems development cycle; for example, in approaches such as Multiview (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990) or that of Wilson (1990) SSM is used only to convert a messy, unstructured situation into a structured one within which the need for new or amended information systems may be evident.

Such uses of soft systems thinking have been described as a 'grafting approach' (Miles, 1988) but this is, at least at present, a somewhat misleading description. The grafting of one plant onto the root-stock of another results in the two plants soon growing together into a seamless new whole; presently, however, when soft systems thinking precedes IS development there occurs an abrupt and distinct division between the two sets of activities. The conclusions of the SSM analysis, perhaps that particular new information systems are required, act only as a signal to initiate the conventional IS development process and the 'problem' becomes the creation of those information systems. From that point forward SSM is laid aside in favour of different methodologies, those of the IS professional. These make no use of the root definitions, conceptual models or any other artefacts produced during the SSM analysis; neither is any of the SSM thinking, which has provided the reason for believing that new information systems are required, recorded in manual or CASE documentation. This division is, we believe, unnecessary and certainly undesirable; the acceptance of SSM as a valuable and integral part of IS work requires a more seamless integration of SSM analysis with conventional ISD activities.

## INTEGRATION WITH PROCESS-FOCUSED APPROACHES

There has been debate (Benyon & Skidmore, 1987; Mingers, 1988; Prior, 1990; Doyle & Wood, 1991; Sawyer, 1991) as to whether or not links might be made by transforming the activity models of SSM into data flow diagrams. The difficulties of achieving such a transformation, however, appear great. The apparent similarities between the two types of model are superficial. The meaning of the arrows in the two types of diagram are completely different, the shifts in perspective involved in moving between resolution levels during SSM conceptual modelling and DFD decomposition are not of the same type, and the 'logicalization' process in DFD construction leads to conceptualizations of a different kind to those represented by SSM's activity models. Almost the only real similarities between the two types of model are that the central components of both are labelled using verbs.

Furthermore, the creation of such links, while being a significant theoretical development, would be of less practical importance now than it might have been in the past, when the structured analysis and design approaches (Yourdon & Constantine, 1975; Gane & Sarson, 1977; De Marco, 1978) were at their height of popularity. In subsequent ISD methodologies the importance of DFDs as a tool of the IS professional has been lessened. In methodologies such as SSADM (Cutts, 1987; Downs et al., 1992; Ashworth & Goodland, 1990; Eva, 1992) the creation of DFDs is only one of a set of equally important techniques.

With the growing popularity of object-oriented development methods the role of DFDs may be reduced still further. Some hybrid object-oriented analysis (OOA) approaches employ DFDs for an initial identification of objects but this is already recognized as undesirable and unlikely to continue. It has been suggested (Henderson-Sellers & Constantine, 1991) that while DFDs may still play a role in future objected-oriented development methodologies this may be a somewhat different role; they may be used at the later detailed design stage of individual classes rather than during exploratory analysis, or may be drawn only to please management and the users.

## LINKING SSM TO DATA-FOCUSED APPROACHES

There has been little discussion of whether a closer integration of SSM with the data-focused approaches might be possible. This may be because of apparent differences in the philosophies of the approaches and how they seek to investigate problematic situations.

SSM accepts subjectivity as inherent in all human affairs, and encourages exploration of the different perceptions of the problem situation that may result from different sets of values, beliefs and expectations. Those differences are explored rigorously through the formal definition and modelling of purposeful systems deemed relevant to the situation and by making explicit declarations of the 'Weltanschauungen' which lead to these being regarded as 'relevant'. From this stand-point there can be no neutral, objectively 'true' description of a problem situation, nor of a relevant data structure, but only a problem definition or relevant data structure as seen by an observer through a particular framework of values, beliefs and expectations. This cognitive framework will be continually constructed and re-constructed as the analyst, users and other parties negotiate the meaning and definition of parts of the situation.

Data-focused approaches to ISD are, in contrast, widely assumed to be 'neutral' or value-free. This arises from the claim that data analysis merely uncovers an independently existing, objectively true, structure of the data in a problem domain, recording this structure through some form of conceptual data model. Whether this model is a relational model (Codd, 1970), entity-relationship model (Chen, 1976), RM/T model (Codd, 1979) or object-oriented (Schlaer & Mellor, 1988; Coad & Yourdon, 1990; Rumbaugh et al., 1991) it is usually presented as descriptive of what Cutts (1987) describes as the generic underlying structure of the organization's data. Combined with an ontological use of the concept of 'system' this leads to representations of data analysis as an un-complex, albeit sometimes complicated, process concerned with the creation of models that reflect and are rooted in the real world.

It would appear that to bring together these two approaches would require a radical, possibly unacceptable, shift from the phenomenology of SSM to the objectivism of the data-focused approaches. However, a closer examination of the data-focused approaches, specifically concerning the extent to which they act as an organized means of rational enquiry, provides some grounds for not accepting the objectivism of data analysis too readily.

Much work has gone into examining the intellectual basis of systems thinking and it is relatively easy to represent, and to explain the differences between, the various forms of systems thinking in the terms of Fig. 1. It is more difficult to interpret data-focused approaches in the same way.

There is little difficulty in identifying the methodology, methods and techniques of the data-focused approaches for these are widely described in standard texts and are well defined; indeed, refinement of these has been the concern of most of the published literature.

Identifying the set of concepts used by the data-focused approaches is also reasonably easy to do, and we can see how the set of concepts used has grown over the years; for example, concepts such as 'file' and 'record' pre-date the computer, concepts of 'entity' and 'relationship' have become widely known since their introduction in the late 1960s and recent interest in object-oriented methods has led to such concepts as 'inheritance' and 'service' becoming added to the set of concepts used in the data-focused approaches.

The ethics of data-focused development may be assumed to be similar to those of IS development as a whole, though these are certainly not clearly defined. We can even find that the data-focused approaches have a small, though influential, mythology of their own. This includes useful, orienting, though ultimately untestable, assertions such as that 'data are a resource'.

When, though, we turn to considering the ontological and epistemological assumptions of the data-focused approaches we meet with problems, for there has been remarkably little discussion of these; nor has much attention been given to understanding those approaches through social science typologies such as that of Burrell & Morgan (1979). Hirschheim & Klein (1989), however, emphasize the important effect that the different assumptions made during systems development may have upon its results.

The evidence that is available suggests that data analysis follows an objectivist paradigm, characterized by ontological assumptions that are realist and epistemological assumptions that are positivist.

Although the presuppositions of the data-focused approaches are rarely made explicit in the subject literature many of the en passant statements made, and attempted definitions of such core concepts as 'entity', suggest the acceptance of the existence of a singular, objective reality and hint towards a positivist orientation. Certainly the few attempts to examine the philosophical assumptions of the data-focused approaches that have been made also suggest that they embody a substantially different philosophy to that of SSM. For example:

Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald (1982) argue that data analysis is based upon a science paradigm (rather than a systems paradigm) on the basis that its objectives are analysis rather than problem-solving and that its unstated assumptions are essentially reductionist. This taxonomic classification accords well with the general perception of data analysis as one of the more rigorous and precise techniques of ISD; whereas techniques such as data flow diagramming may be intuitively attractive and have proved to be practically useful, the claim that various forms of data model are based upon the axioms of mathematical set theory (a claim challenged by Kent 1978) has imbued them with greater academic respectability.

Klein & Hirschheim (1987) identify the most familiar and widely used forms of data-focused approach such as entity modelling as following an objectivist paradigm, characterized by ontological assumptions that are realist and epistemological assumptions that are positivist. Within the objectivist paradigm the nature of the problem domain is taken as given; it is assumed that 'real world' data structures exist, independent of the data analyst and that causal relationships within these may be identified and investigated. The alternative subjectivist paradigm holds that, whether or not there exists an objective 'real world', this cannot ever be directly cognized by human beings but only ever interpreted through the socially created and transmitted concepts and preconceptions of the observer. The essential difference between these two extreme positions, with respect to data analysis, is described thus:

The difference is whether one believes that a data model "reflects" reality or consists of subjective meanings and thereby constructs reality. The implication for subjectivism is that the immediate social milieu and society at large pre-order what subjectively is experienced as reality . . .

From this point of view a data model is correct if it is consistent with the perception of the Universe of Discourse as constructed by institutional programming, sedimentation and tradition. In contrast, the objectivist view holds that language neutrally depicts reality which is the same for all regardless of culture and individual perception.' (p. 9)

Klein and Hirschheim conclude that alternative, subjectivist approaches deserve further attention but have not developed to a sufficiently practical level.

Wand (1989) is a rare example of a discussion of the philosophy of object-oriented modelling. Few would argue with Wand's suggestion that much of the confusion surrounding the object-oriented approaches is a result of its roots in programming and that many of the core concepts are coloured by implementation considerations. The formal model of objects that is developed though is a conservative one, incorporating many assumptions of longer established data modelling practice. The ontology described is realist in that 'object' is used as a label for things of the real world, where:

'The world is made of objects. Objects describe concrete beings, that is, specific entities rather than types or classes' (p. 550).

'Objects are viewed here as describing real things' (p. 552)

... objects gain their importance because they reflect a "natural" view of the world we are modeling (abstracting) in our software. This being the case, if we view programs as models of some reality, the use of objects amounts to adopting programming and database constructs that are direct mapping of real concepts.' (p. 538)

livari (1991) reports the results of a paradigmatic analysis of contemporary 'schools' of ISD based upon the content of two exemplar standard texts for each school. He concludes that the ontology of data-focused approaches includes viewing information systems as primarily technical systems that manipulate and disseminate data/information, data as descriptive facts, and a formal-rational view of organizations. The epistemology of the data-focused approaches is found to be positivist.

If data analysis must necessarily be undertaken within an objectivist paradigm, then this presents considerable obstacles to any integration with the declared phenomenology of SSM. We wish to argue, however, that the case against closer integration is not proven.

First, data analysis, as currently performed within conventional systems development, is beset by a number of intellectual confusions and practical difficulties that lead us to believe that it contains a substantial subjective content.

Second, data analysis is not presently justified in terms of any model of human sense-making, but the model of human sense-making employed by SSM provides a very good basis for understanding the nature of data analysis and explains much of present practice. Understanding data analysis in such a way allows for the possibility of an alternative, interpretative, form of data analysis that is philosophically compatible with SSM whilst still providing a starting point for subsequent thinking about the content and structure of data storage mechanisms.

## SOME PROBLEMS WITH CONVENTIONAL DATA ANALYSIS

A number of anomalies and difficulties arise if we assume that data analysis is an objectivist approach. These are not insurmountable in practice, but that they arise at all, and that the means by which they are overcome are incommensurate with ontological assumptions that are realist, indicate fundamental inconsistencies within the view of data analysis as a neutral investigation of real world data structures.

Three major problems are discussed below. Without the bounds of this paper we must commend to the reader's own attention more technical issues such as the subjective categorization of the problem domain, the problems of identity and how identity is conserved through time, the reification of relationships and the difficulties of polymorphism and roles.

## Requirements of changed role of data analysis

Data analysis techniques were originally used late in the development process and concentrated upon how to best physically store data. By the stage in development at which they were employed the level of definition of 'the problem' was such that any lack of clarity concerning essential philosophy or any imprecision concerning the concepts used could be accommodated. The data analyst already knew what data items were to be used by the new information system, and the meaning of those data items was already defined. Thus we find that the development approaches of the 1960s such as ARDI (Hartman et al., 1968), while being extremely detailed prescriptions for development, need give no consideration to data other than how they would be used.

The development of more sophisticated data storage technologies allowed a separation of discussion of physical design issues and the way in which stored data would be used from discussion of a conceptual data model, which could be translated into a physical design at a later stage. We should note that these models are ‘conceptual’ or ‘logical’ only in so much as they are free of physical design considerations; this is a different kind of conceptuality to that found in the models of SSM.

Conceptual data models allowed a de-coupling of data analysis and date store design permitting data analysis techniques to be employed earlier in the conventional development project life cycle. Recently, they have begun to be allowed a role during the earliest stage of ISD work; Olle et al. (1991), for example, allow a role for data analysis in both information systems planning and business analysis. Use of data analysis techniques in these earliest stages of development implies that they are being used in a different way and for a different purpose. Whereas previously they were being used to determine the best means of storing data relevant to a well-defined problem domain they are now being used to investigate and define the problem domain itself. This changing role gives rise to a number of new problems.

When data analysis is used at the earliest stages of systems development the intent is to produce an extremely generalized data model for the problem domain as a whole; such data models are sometimes called 'enterprise models'. The value of such models lies in that they describe data requirements at a high level of abstraction, independently of any considerations of how the model might be reflected in physical data storage mechanisms or what use individual applications may make of the data stored within those mechanisms; they therefore provide a basis for planning that is relatively stable and robust with respect to change.

But the general data model will eventually be translated into data storage mechanisms that will be used by a very large number of different users, and employed for many different purposes. This makes it essential that any semantic ambiguities are resolved during its creation. Kent (1978) points out that where data are employed within a single context, such as being used within a single application, then semantic ambiguities are manageable; human beings have the ability to resolve differences in meaning in a largely automatic and unconscious way through understanding the context of use. But when the same data storage mechanism serves many different users and many different applications then any ambiguities must be identified and resolved. Using data analysis at the early stages of development therefore demands far greater consideration of the process by which meaning attribution takes place, of the system of enquiry that is being used to investigate the problem domain, and of the constraints imposed by its underlying philosophy and the concepts that it employs.

Furthermore, as we move towards the early stages of the development process, the analysis encompasses fewer defined technical problems and many more social and political issues. Analysis moves from addressing questions of how to store data efficiently inside the computer to questions of what it is that the organization wishes to achieve and how a balance may be achieved between the interests of different involved parties. Here a data model may easily become infused with political meanings and serve to legitimize a particular view of the organization. If data analysis is to be used at such a time then one would expect that its use would be justified in terms of a particular view of the nature of social organizations and how they may be validly investigated. It is surprising then that so few attempts have been made within the data analysis literature to justify the processes undertaken in terms of the nature of social reality and how human beings make sense of their world, or to relate them to any sociological paradigm. The literature of data analysis literature has been seriously impoverished by this omission, and it has led to some very practical problems.

## The problem of the identification of entities or objects

The lack of any clear declaration of the models of social reality and human sense-making within which the process of data analysis may be understood leads to certain methodological difficulties. For example, how may the data analyst identify 'relevant' entities within a problem domain? If one assumes the world to be composed of entities then how does one decide which of the infinitely large set available should be included in the conceptual data model? Directives to identify the things that are of relevance and importance to the organization only pose further problems: How does one identify what is or is not 'relevant'? What do we mean by 'relevant'? To whom are they relevant and in what context?

This problem reveals itself in the literature only obliquely, by what is not discussed. Few of the texts advocating data-focused techniques discuss how the initial identification of entities or objects may be carried out, and rather suggest that there is no problem. We find statements such that ' . . . the world being modelled is made of objects . . . objects are just there for the picking!' (Meyer, 1988, p. 51) or that 'Identifying objects is pretty easy to do' (Schlaer & Mellor, 1988, p. 15) difficult to reconcile with our observation of students and others learning to employ the techniques of data analysis. We have repeatedly found the greatest difficulty encountered by inexperienced data analysts to lie in initially identifying a set of 'relevant' entities or objects.

Those texts which do address the problem of entity or object identification provide only generalized suggestions for how to identify the entities and objects relevant to a problem domain. Most of these suggestions, while practically useful, are drawn from the 'craft knowledge' of practitioners, rather than being derived from any consistent theoretical framework. They include talking to users, examining what the current information systems store data about, underlining the nouns in written problem statements and relying on intuition and previous experience. Guided by such strategies the data analyst is then expected to make the choice of relevant entities or objects through an unacknowledged and unexplained subjective process.

## Status of entity or object as ontological or epistemological device

A further consequence of the ill-defined philosophy of data analysis is a lack of clarity concerning the status of entities or objects as either ontological or epistemological devices.

The original use of data analysis in the later stages of development made it relatively straightforward to identify the 'things' about which data were required to be held. At this time entities were presented as generalized descriptions of real world 'things', and authors such as Martin (1975) explicitly describe an entity as something which exists in the real world; entity occurrences were merely particular identifiable instances of the same real world 'thing'. Chen's (1976) widely quoted paper shares this ontological flavour in that the entity-relationship model is introduced from the perspective that

'The entity-relationship model adopts the more natural view that the real world consists of entities and relationships.' (p. 9)

As use of data analysis has shifted to earlier stages of development where 'the problem' is less well defined, one would have hoped that proponents of data analysis would be clearer as to exactly what they meant by terms such as 'entity', and to declare whether they are used as ontological labels for parts of the real world or as epistemological devices through which the nature of the real world may be explored. Unfortunately, the lack of any declared or shared model of human sense-making would seem to have precluded any such declarations. Instead authors have continued to rely on pragmatic, 'common-sense' definitions. Date (1990), for example, neatly avoids commitment to whether entities are ontological or epistemological devices by introducing several levels of qualification into his discussion of semantic modelling:

For example, we might agree that the world is made up of entities (even though it is impossible to state with any precision exactly what an entity is and what it is not; despite this lack of precision, the entity concept does seem to be useful in talking about the world, at least from an intuitive perspective).

We might go further and agree that entities can usefully be grouped into entity types . . .

We might go still further and agree that every entity has a property that serves to identify that entity. We might go further again and agree that any entity can be related to other entities by means of relationships.' (p. 581)

Many authors merely add to Chen's (1976) rather loose definition of an entity, as 'a "thing" which can be distinctly identified', the requirement that knowledge of that thing is important to the information system or of interest to the organization. Maciaszek (1990), for example, defines an entity as:

'... an object which can be distinctly identified and is important to the information system of the organization' (p. 31)

while Downs et al. (1992) describe entities within SSADM as:

... things of interest to the organization. They may physically exist, such as Borrowers. They may be transient such as Loans or even represent aspirations, such as Reservations. Unfortunately there is no simple or precise definitions of an entity. In practice, if it is something that you want to store information about, it is likely to be an entity.' (p. 123)

The recent interest in object-oriented analysis has led to re-interpretations of the nature of data analysis and at least some recognition that a data model might be an epistemological device, a coherent means of investigating the problem domain rather than being a description of the real world. Schlaer & Mellor (1988), for example, talk of a data model being:

'... a thinking tool used to aid in the formalization of knowledge. It helps us work out how we want to think about a problem: the terms we need to define, the assumptions we make in selecting those terms, and the consistency of our definitions and assumptions.' (p. 7)

Odell (1992), commenting not upon the use of data models but their subject, emphasizes that:

'Object-oriented (O-O) analysis should not model reality—rather it should model the way reality is understood by people. The understanding and knowledge of people is the essential component in developing systems.' (p. 45)

Since it is with just such interpretations of socially constructed realities that SSM concerns itself there may now be a greater scope for forging links between SSM and data-focused approaches to ISD than has hitherto been recognized.

Unfortunately, the literature concerning object-oriented analysis already shows signs of inheriting the lack of conceptual clarity of its predecessors. The comparison of different object-oriented analysis approaches made by de Champeaux & Faure (1992) compares a number of different approaches to object-oriented analysis, but the terms of comparison are limited to discussion of technical details such as whether the influence of re-use (desirable from an implementation perspective) affects the definition of classes, the diagramming conventions used and whether or not the different approaches allow multiple inheritance; more fundamental questions of ontology and epistemology are, significantly, not considered at all.

In much of the object-oriented literature the concept of 'object' is described, in that objects have identity, state, behaviour and properties and can be characterized by the set of operations that can be performed on it or by it and its possible states. However, it is not explained in terms of any meta-level model of human sense-making, so that what an object is or what an object model represents remains somewhat ambiguous. Lee & Carver (1991), for example, point out that there is some debate as to whether or not an entity is an object and vice-versa, though following Booch (1986), they define an object ontologically as

... an entity that exists in the real world and has a state whose behaviour is explained by the operations that it performs or is performed by.' (pp. 36–37).

Where object orientation is applied at the implementation level of database design and programming then, as before with previous data analysis approaches, the problem domain is so well defined that this may not be too serious. To define objects as '... a named representation of a real-world entity' (McFadden & Hoffer, 1991, p. 189) or state that 'Any real-world entity is an object, with which is associated a system-wide unique identifier' (Kim, 1990, p. 13) is unlikely, at these stages, to distort the system outcomes unduly. More serious consequences result if, during problem definition, we assume that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the objects within the computer or data model and parts of external reality; unfortunately, the likelihood of such assumptions being made for object models seems to be greater than for other forms of data model, and we cannot but agree that:

'The confusion between objects in the system and objects in the world, and the lack of an intermediate conceptual model, are cause for concern. The idea that object-oriented systems are a 'natural' representation of the world is a seductive but dangerous over-simplification. In reality, the fact that the object model seems so close to reality makes it far easier to misuse it than other modelling techniques which do not purport to represent the world so directly.' (McGinnes, 1992, p. 13)

Whether working with object-oriented ideas or more established forms of data modelling, the danger that arises from not being clear whether core concepts such as 'entity' or 'object' are epistemological or ontological devices is that one is forced to fall back upon 'common-sense', example led definitions that may be inconsistent. Such inconsistency exists in Rishe's (1988) definition of an abstract object as

'... a non-value object in the real world. An abstract object can be, for example, a tangible item (such as a person, a table, a country), or an event (such as an offering of a course by an instructor), or an idea (such as a course).' (p. 5)

The reader might well question whether 'a country' is a tangible item in the real world or merely an idea shared by many individuals; consider, for example, the aspirations of many

Middle Eastern and East European citizens for regional independence. Did there exist a real world thing called Slovenia in December 1991 when some inhabitants of Yugoslavia had declared its independent existence? Did it exist in January 1992 when the European Community (but not the United Nations or the government of Yugoslavia) recognized its existence? The answers, of course, depend on how we define 'a country', and this is socially and politically decided; it is not an absolute characteristic of something existing in an independent 'real world'.

## THE SUBJECTIVE CONTENT OF CONVENTIONAL DATA ANALYSIS

The critical examination of the underlying philosophy of data-focused approaches is clearly an area worthy of much greater attention and further research. In this paper we are content to observe that present practice of data analysis includes a large element of subjectivity. This subjectivity exists whether or not the philosophy of the data-focused approaches uses the concept 'entity' (or 'object') to be an ontological device.

If entities are taken to have real world existence then the analyst must be choosing, from the infinitely large number of entities that exist, and might be included in a conceptual data model, only some entities as being 'relevant' and suitable for inclusion. Over 20 years' worth of literature provides no suggestion that this is done other than subjectively, as a choice of the analyst. This means that however well researched and rigorous are the techniques for manipulation and refinement of the conceptual data model, they ultimately rely upon a subjective and interpretative identification of entities or objects. Consequently, the process of creating such a model is not value-free and the resulting data model is not 'neutral'; it will be affected very much by the values, beliefs and expectations of those creating it.

If real world existence is not assumed then conceptual data models are epistemological devices and entities or objects are, by definition, subjectively created by the analyst to understand the world. In either case, the conceptual data model cannot be described as a model of the real world but only as an interpretation of reality.

## AN ALTERNATIVE ACCOUNT OF THE DATA ANALYSIS PROCESS

The lack of attention given to the philosophical rationale for data-focused approaches and the existence of a largely un-admitted subjective element within it provides some grounds for doubting that data analysis need necessarily be located in the objectivist paradigm, and need differ in philosophy from SSM quite so radically as has hitherto been assumed. Perhaps more interestingly, the model of human sense-making employed by SSM is one by which the activity of data analysis can be usefully explained.

The model of how human beings make sense of the reality that surrounds them which is used by SSM is that described by Checkland & Casar (1986). This is based upon the concept of 'appreciation' provided by the work of Vickers (Vickers, 1965, 1968, 1970, 1983, 1984). An important consequence of the appreciative model of human sense-making is that no organization or individual can ever 'know' the 'real' situation directly but can only interpret the situation through a particular set of standards, norms and values. These are described as settings of the appreciative system of the observer.

Figure 2. The operation of appreciation as a filtering device (after Lewis, 1991)

Settings of the appreciative system of an analyst not only provide meaning and allow the analyst to 'understand' the world but also restrict understanding (Lewis, 1991). The appreciative system thus acts as a 'cognitive filter' that recognizes certain facts about reality as meaningful data and uses these to create the observer's understanding of reality, but dis-allows the cognizing of those things which are not meaningful in terms of the cognitive categories of which it is composed (Fig. 2).

So a business organization may, for example, in the normal course of events not recognize the existence of hospitals; while individuals within the organization know what hospitals are, there is no stored organizational knowledge relating to hospitals since they have no relevance to the company's interests. If, however, the company enrolls its employees in a private medical insurance scheme then decisions may need to be made which require knowledge about local hospitals and the cognitive category of 'hospital' becomes meaningful with respect to the organization's interests. Attribute values of individual hospitals, such as their location and number of beds, then become data for the organization.

Changes in the cognitive categories through which the world is viewed may also arise from re-definition of organizational missions. For example, a new commitment to providing quality services made by a public transport organization may result in the organization thinking in terms of 'customers' rather than 'passengers'. The idea of a 'customer' has connotations concerning providing a quality service, or even compensation for a poor service, which the more neutral 'passenger' does not. There are clearly corresponding changes in organizational data requirements.

Against the background of such a model of human sense-making much of the practice of data analysis becomes explicable. For example, in practice most effort in doing data analysis is expended, not upon operating the relatively mechanical aspects of techniques such as normalization, but upon building a sufficiently rich understanding of an often novel business situation and negotiating the meaning of data items with the business users. The hours spent with the users to formulate definitions of entities and attributes to include in a data dictionary are not, as is sometimes arrogantly implied, about rationalizing the users' ambiguities and confused terminology but about arriving at a shared interpretation of the problem domain. This may be understood in terms of the appreciative model as being necessary, for the data analyst to understand the appreciative settings of those in the problem situation and so define the problem domain for data analysis.

## POSSIBILITY FOR AN INTERPRETATIVE FORM OF DATA ANALYSIS

The argument that SSM cannot in principle be more closely integrated with data-focused approaches to development is at best unproven because the philosophy of data analysis is so ill-defined. The subjective component of data analysis and the fact that the appreciative model provides a coherent explanation of the social processes involved during data analysis suggest that some closer integration is possible.

We do not believe, though, that such closer integration could be justifiably achieved by simply creating data models of the traditional kind during an SSM analysis; this would merely imbue the SSM analysis with the same ambiguities and non-explicit subjectivity found in conventional data analysis. Instead, the ideas of appreciation provide the possibility of creating an alternative form of interpretative data model. Our own research employs the idea of cognitive categories as the basic components of such a model. One cannot, for example, conceptualize a 'patient care system' without having some ideas about what a 'patient' is, what a patient might do and what might be done to a patient. Each of SSM's relevant systems is then meaningful only with respect to a particular set of cognitive categories and to some assumptions about the associations that exist between those categories. By agreeing meanings for these we may establish a vocabulary for debate. Showing those meanings in a new form of data model (distinguished by the label of a system data model) will not only complement the activity model of SSM and provide further insight into the nature of the system being considered; it might also provide a starting point for later thinking about implementable data storage mechanisms.

Regardless of whether this or some other type of model is used, bringing an interpretative form of data analysis into SSM may, in the longer term, advance the possibility of alternative, phenomenological forms of ISD; in the more immediate future it goes some way towards 'bridging the gap' between SSM analysis and conventional data-focused development approaches.

## ADVANTAGES OF BRINGING DATA ANALYSIS INTO SSM

We have argued so far that it may be feasible to make links between SSM and the data-focused approaches to ISD through a new form of data model. It remains, however, to outline why making such links might be desirable.

## Enhancement of SSM debate

Even if there were not expected to be any subsequent ISD work following an SSM study there could be advantages in producing interpretative data models related to specific human activity systems. Discussion and debate during SSM would be enriched if the human activity system models under debate were accompanied by corresponding data models. These would represent the data storage implications of each human activity system and might therefore provide further insights into whether particular options for change were technically or practically feasible. Such insights might be especially advantageous if SSM is to be employed in strategic information systems planning (Galliers, 1991).

## Identifying 'relevant' object classes

SSM provides the means, through system definitions employing root definitions and conceptual models, to capture and explore different perceptions of the situation; there are many advantages to be gained from identifying data requirements from such explicit, declared models of relevant, purposeful action. An interpretative form of data model, derived from and justifiable in the context of those, might for example provide positive guidance as to what entities or objects should be included in later data models of the more conventional kind.

## Making sense of 'information categories'

Wilsons' SSM based methodology for identifying information requirements from non-contentious 'primary task' models (Wilson, 1990) culminates in the identification of 'information categories'. These are loosely defined descriptions of types of data that are required to perform particular activities and there appears to be no clear relationship between these and the concepts that are the common currency of data analysis. Wilson's information categories do not, for example, even at the highest resolution level, correspond to attributes; nor do they equate to data concerning a single entity.

Wilson's work could be more easily carried forward into detailed design if, in parallel with the identification of information categories, a data model were developed consistent with the thinking that led to the information categories. The system data model mentioned above, for example, would allow a logical defence of the methodology to be continued through to the design stage, through the argument that:

from a given conceptualization of the organization as performing certain activities (those required to enact performance of the primary task) certain information categories are required;

conceptualizing the organization in that particular way makes a particular system data model, and ultimately a particular storage design derived from it, meaningful; and

information categories are particular attribute sets, chosen on the basis that they are required to perform a particular activity—they therefore, broadly, correspond to particular required user views of the storage design.

## Possibility of including different stakeholder perceptions

Where there existed a number of alternative, equally valid, interpretations of the problem situation then developing conceptual models of different relevant systems, data models based upon each of these, and then combining them would provide a data structure which could accommodate different stakeholders' data requirements. The design of data storage mechanisms such as data bases would thus be built up from 'user views' that were based upon explicit models of purposeful user activity, rather than, as at present, inferred from existing report layouts or from vague, possibly contradictory statements of requirements.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper has argued that the accepted view of data analysis as a value-free investigation of an underlying, objectively true, structure of an organization's data may not be sustainable. From its point of initiation data analysis is coloured by the analysts' perceptions of the nature of the situation, and these not only inform but also dis-inform. Subjectively made choices as to what are, or are not, 'objects of interest' are hidden within all the conventional forms of data model.

Instead, from an appreciative perspective, data analysis may be regarded as a process of reality creation, in which the data analyst and users together define a domain of discourse. Understanding data analysis in this way not only provides a better explanation of the practice of data analysis but also suggests that a closer integration of SSM with data-focused approaches to information systems development may be possible through the development of a new form of data model. Finally, we have suggested that such closer integration would be of benefit to the practice both of data analysis and of SSM.
