# Soft systems and systems engineering: on the use of conceptual models in information system development

K. G. Doyle, J. R. G. Wood\* & A. T. Wood-Harper\*

Department of Computer Studies, University of the West of England, Coldharbour Lane, Frenchay, Bristol BS16 1QY and

\*Department of Computer Science, University of Salford, The Crescent,

Salford M5 4WT, UK

Abstract. Many recent studies have shown that computer-based systems continue to 'fail' at a number of different levels (Romtec, 1988; KPMG, 1990) and it is increasingly apparent (Maclaren et al., 1991) that the most serious failures of information technology (IT) lie in the continuing inability to address those concerns which are central to the successful achievement of individual, organizational and social goals. It is the contention of this paper that this failing is precisely because these are the areas which are ignored or inadequately treated by conventional system development methods.

There is, of course, a vast body of literature concerned with the understanding of complex human activity systems. This literature often reflects a mass of contradictions at the epistemological and the ontological level about the behaviour of such systems and has also spawned numerous methods (and methodologies) which seek to guide the individual in making successful interventions into organizational situations (Rosenhead, 1989).

Despite this multiplicity of viewpoints many writers have posited a dichotomy between so-called 'soft' and 'hard' approaches to problem situations and use this dichotomy to inform the choice of an appropriate problem-solving methodology (Checkland, 1985). In this paper we characterize these two approaches as being concerned with either the purpose(s) of the human activity system (i.e. 'doing the right thing') or with the design of the efficient means of achieving such purpose(s) (i.e. 'doing the thing right').

It is our belief that much of the literature and work in either area has not concerned itself with the issues of the other. Writers on 'hard' engineering methods often assume the question of purpose to be either straightforward (e.g. given in the project brief) or, paradoxically, too difficult (e.g. it is not our concern as mere systems analysts). Writers on 'soft' methods on the other hand rarely have anything to say about the design and implementation of well-engineered computer-based systems, giving the impression that this is a somewhat mundane activity better left to technical experts.

This paper, therefore, attempts to set out a rationale for the bringing together of principles from both 'hard' engineering and 'soft' inquiry methods without doing epistemological damage to either. To illustrate our argument we concentrate on JSD (Jackson system development) as an example of system engineering (Cameron, 1983) and SSM (soft systems methodology) as an example of system inquiry (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990). Our general thesis, however, does not depend upon either of these two approaches per se but applies to the overall issue of bringing together insights from two apparently opposed epistemological positions in an effort better to harness the power of IT in pursuit of purposeful human activity.

Keywords: conceptual models, information systems development, soft systems.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT METHODS

The methods of information system development currently available are many and they often vary in approach quite markedly (Olle et al., 1982; Maddison et al., 1983; Connor, 1985). Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald (1982), for example, have characterized six major approaches to systems analysis.

The papers produced for the IFIP Working Conference on the Feature Analysis of Information Systems Design Methodologies differed dramatically in their conclusions of the differences between selected methods (Olle et al., 1983).

One paper which concentrated on an analysis of conceptual and logical models found, by and large, that the similarities between methods in the analysis and design stages of systems development outweighed their differences (Olive, 1983).

However, a different perspective which discussed a sociocybernetic framework for the feature analysis of information systems design methodologies found sharp differences between the methodologies when considering the emphasis on 'doing the right thing' or 'doing things right', the former being epitomized, according to the paper, by Lundeberg's Information Systems Work and Analysis of Changes (ISAC) and the latter by REMORA from Collete Rolland, the University of Paris, Sorbonne (Lundeberg et al., 1981; livari & Kerola, 1983).

This difference of opinion, with one paper addressing techniques and the other frameworks, highlights the need to develop methods which attempt to do 'the right thing right', since doing one without the other would surely produce either a perfectly engineered mess or a poorly implemented good idea (Wood & Doyle, 1989).

This objective could be achieved by using suitable tools and techniques within the framework of the most suitable approach. As well as the development of new tools, techniques and approaches it would seem important to also seek a merging of the best aspects of existing methods. In the context of information systems support for human activity systems it seems reasonable to suppose that a hermeneutic approach to problem-solving (aiming at doing the right thing) coupled with a structured, engineering approach to systems building (aiming at doing things right) could provide a practical and usable starting point.

## MULTIPERSPECTIVE FRAMEWORKS

The Multiview approach (Wood-Harper et al., 1985; Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990) seeks to integrate a number of different perspectives on information systems development, incorporating sections on human activity systems, data analysis, sociotechnical systems design, human-computer interaction and technical design criteria. Doing this, however, places the work of leading exponents in each of these areas into a conventional framework, that of the conventional, sequential, engineering life cycle.

That the initial version of Multiview assumes an early ability to define the desired outcome of the development activity, and hence the system to construct, is made apparent in statements of the kind 'you cannot solve a problem until you know what the problem is', which is in line with the perspective of the hard systems thinker, and the insistence that the analyst must 'avoid jumping to early conclusions about the problem otherwise the causes may never be uncovered'.

By way of contrast, the soft systems approach as advocated by Checkland developed through the experience that

'It was not possible to take for granted the concept of a problem and the activity of trying to solve it and that in problems in human activity systems, the passage of time always modifies the perception of the problem (such problems really do sometimes go away)' (Checkland, 1981)

Elsewhere in the definition of the original Multiview approach it is possible to discern further evidence of a dilution of Checkland's soft systems thinking. For example, the section on resolving differences in views advocates the need to agree a single root definition across different Weltanschauungen. Yet the ability to fashion at least as many root definitions as there are views, and to use these in building conceptual models to promote a debate about desirable and feasible change is a fundamental strength of the soft systems approach. This is stifled somewhat in the Multiview approach which advocates the search for a right answer, 'the alternative root definitions indicate the difficulty of reconciling different viewpoints and yet if one is not agreed it would be even more difficult to agree on the final system'.

## CONSENSUS FRAMEWORKS

In similar vein Wilson advocates the need to search for the common or neutral part of a root definition, i.e. that part of a root definition with which everyone is in agreement and from which a primary task model can be constructed (Wilson, 1987). The cited example is that of a prison which, it is claimed, can be considered as a system for the acceptable, storage and disposal of prisoners. Apart from the unfortunate connotations of the word 'neutral' in this context, basic, as used in his book, might have been more acceptable as a way of capturing the overlapping views represented in this area of agreement (Wilson, 1984). This is obviously an appealing notion when one is attempting to use an agreed conceptual model as a template for a new system design. Wilson's argument, however, which uses Venn diagrams to illustrate how aspects of a root definition may be common across a number of Weltanschauungen seems to ignore the fact that root definitions and conceptual models are abstractions of systems and hence the interaction between, and arrangement of, the components is more important than the components themselves (Angyal, 1941).

In an extreme case it is possible that the common ground might lie in the area of what people are prepared to accept as a compromise given (for each actor) some more desirable outcome. Accepting such a common ground as the basis for a neutral definition, however, could mean that all the participants lose in some way, or that the area of agreement is trivial compared to the areas of disagreement.

These outputs are defined, by an analogy with architecture, as a set of artists' impressions and a scale model to show the client what is proposed, and as a set of plans to give the builder. This analogy implies that the purpose of the conceptual model is to define a more acceptable system than currently exists, i.e. to discover a solution to the problem. Checkland, however, is quite clear that the conceptual model is not a state description of any human activity system and that it is in no sense a description of any part of the real world. Rather, it is simply the structured set of activities which logic requires in the notional system defined in the root definition.

The main purpose of the soft systems approach is to generate innovative thought by selecting some views of a problem situation as being possibly relevant to improving it, working out the implications of these views in conceptual models and comparing such models with what exists in a real world situation. Only after this comparison has been made can discussion and agreement with regard to feasible and desirable change commence. Agreement may lead to action to improve the problem situation and possible actions could include the design and implementation of appropriate computer-based information systems.

SSM, then, provides a learning system which aims to increase one's understanding of some real world situation which is perceived in some way to be problematic. Through increased understanding it becomes possible to define and explore the effect of changes in structure and function on the problem situation. The application of conceptual modelling in consensus approaches is not used, as intended by Checkland, as an aid to teasing out the complexities of reality by focused debate but rather as a means of determining a new logical model of the application area under study. In this respect it is hard to see how such approaches offer anything more than say De Marco's approach to structured analysis and system specification (or any other method of structured systems development) which, at the specification stage of the life cycle, advocates the need to transform the logical definition of an existing domain into a new improved set of procedures (DeMarco, 1979).

## ENGINEERING INFORMATION SYSTEMS

Our work starts from the premise that it is inappropriate to try to force together frameworks that do not complement one another. Therefore, in exploring the links between soft systems thinking and systems engineering it is necessary to be aware of the fundamental differences in epistemology and ontology and to ensure that neither of these elements is compromised when interfacing the application of one approach with the other.

The belief that systems development requires both the study of ill-structured, unbounded problems and the engineering of a computer-based system as part of the intervention provides the confidence that there can be a framework in which both Soft Systems Methodology and the systems engineering approach can be mutually complementary. The evolution of methods brought about structured development methods which sought to incorporate aspects of traditional systems analysis, data analysis and functional decomposition into a number of cohesive development frameworks (JSD, SSADM, Information Engineering, etc.). All of these methods/methodologies contain or require elements which belong to each of the categories identified by Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald (1982) and conform fairly closely to the conventional life cycle approach. The main variations concern

(1) the emphasis placed on the model framework used to abstract from reality; and

(2) the means of manipulating these models to specify a more desirable state and the means of realizing the designed models in the real world. The common deficiency with such methods is the assumption that having defined an existing system or problem scenario, it is then a straightforward matter to define satisfactory improvement.

De Marco's work on system specification which underpins many structured methods of analysis, for example SSADM and its clones, provides a framework which identifies the need to move from an old logical model (of reality) to a new logical model (the specified system) in order to effect improvement (DeMarco, 1979).

This most crucial of all transformations is not directly addressed within the method but is left to the imagination and creative ability of the designer. JSD assumes that the discovery of and consensus over actions, their attributes and orderings is not unduly problematic but merely a question of listening to and abstracting from users accounts of their real world and that user requirements are both given and well understood (Jackson, 1983).

The methods based on the work of Codd (1984) and Chen (1979) tend to pay more attention to the logical data structures necessary to support given requirements efficiently and effectively than to the requirements themselves although the need for flexibility is axiomatic in data analysis.

Topological differences notwithstanding it is possible to discern a core of structured tools and techniques which are widely used both within the frameworks of different structured methods and, perhaps more importantly, in practice (Wasserman et al., 1983). Such tools and techniques include data flow diagrams, systems flow charts, pseudo code, decision tables, process hierarchies, process structure diagrams, process flow charts, entity attribute relationship models, data dictionaries, normalization, data structure diagrams, entity life histories, logical access maps, and data access diagrams.

These tools and techniques rely heavily on one perspective of a rich and complex empirical reality and are, by definition, rooted in positivism. That is, the philosopher's view that the description of phenomena and the order in which they occur is all that human beings can know. Value judgements, statements of belief, expressions of appreciation of aesthetics and so on are thus considered to fall outside these limits. This is clearly in contradiction to the hermeneutic approach advocated by Checkland which relies on an interpretive explanation of phenomena which is both individual and collective and dependent upon the appreciative system of the observer (Vickers, 1970). That is, 'the readiness to note particular aspects of our situation, to discriminate them in particular ways and to measure them against particular standards of comparison which have been built up in similar ways'.

The development of information systems is a purposeful activity which requires an understanding of both objective and subjective issues and ought therefore to make use of appropriate perspectives, models and frameworks, tools and techniques. The soft systems approach could provide useful insights into a framework for tackling subjective issues such as: What are the problem areas to be addressed? How do we prioritize? What changes might be desirable or otherwise? Can change be undertaken successfully or at all? Have we provided 'a cure', etc? Structured tools and techniques on the other hand may be used to model the mechanistic aspects of organizational activity such as formal organization, functions, procedures, information models, data flow and stores, etc., both as a means of fact recording and as part of a realizable specification for the mechanistic components of an information system or indeed the human activity system it is created to serve.

The application of systems engineering principles, tools and techniques is normally undertaken via the use of a particular development methodology (a collection of tools and techniques within the framework of an approach), the most common methodologies in use being the so-called structured ones. These all have a life cycle framework into which activities and associated deliverables can be slotted, with the delivery from 'stage n' usually providing some or all of the input to 'stage $n+1$ '. The application of a structured methodology to a well-defined problem, especially those concerned with efficiency, clearly benefits its user in a number of ways; providing order and control: establishing a standard against which to judge practice: facilitating the import/export of development staff: easing the maintenance burden. These benefits accrue largely because of the decomposition of the development process into smaller more manageable components which are linked via established inputs and outputs and hence possess both a functional and temporal relationship. The modelling techniques used to describe the software and data components of a prescribed system are broadly similar across all the variations of structured design, with entity attribute relationship models used to describe data stores and process specifications to describe individual procedures which are then linked together in some form of process hierarchy. It is the method by which the specified design is put together which differs most.

## GRAFTING AND EMBEDDING

In attempting to merge aspects of SSM and harder systems engineering principles several courses of action are possible (Miles, 1988). At one extreme it is conceivable that tools and techniques from both approaches could be arranged into a newly developed, purpose-fashioned framework.

Alternatively either the SSM framework might be augmented with tools and techniques from systems engineering or vice versa. Our work has led us to believe that the first of these two options promises to be the most fruitful for a number of reasons. Firstly, it is the framework of SSM with its emphasis on exploration, understanding and change which makes it such an attractive proposition and secondly the use of extra tools, methods and concepts is not merely facilitated by SSM, it is encouraged. The second option may reduce SSM to a sparse collection of tools which require assimilation into a highly defined sequential life cycle (we note the uncomfortable manner with which entity life histories sit within SSADM because they are not part of the mainstream transformation processes that make up the method) or may require SSM to be 'bolted' onto the front end of a hard systems methodology. This final category has recently been advocated by the Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency (CCTA), who recommend using SSM as a front end for SSADM (CCTA, 1991).

## ENGINEERING HERMENEUTIC SYSTEMS

In our own work the primary interface which has been explored between SSM and hard systems thinking has been in the area of information modelling, such that the conceptual models of SSM are extended to include details of those information requirements directly associated with the central transformation. These information requirements are, however, not couched in terms of reports or data sets as in Wilson's Maltese Cross, but rather in terms of those 'real world objects' that are the subject matter of the transformation process and those 'real world actions' which impact their abstraction (Wilson, 1984). Consideration of these issues enables minimal entity life histories for each object to be constructed, together with a list of those action attributes which provide further material for focused debate by separating, as far as possible, model from function and which draw a conceptual boundary around the functionality that the system will be able to support, i.e. What do we need to do? How does logic suggest that we do it? What do we need to know in order to accomplish a task?

Entity process models are used within the first steps of the JSD method of systems design. Our research has not used the conceptual modelling stage of SSM to try and define a set of ideal models but rather models have been used to add an extra dimension to the effect change step in order to specify the basis for a newly provided system which is engineered using JSD principles and/or as a guideline for evaluating change to existing systems. In this way the means by which JSD, along with other tools and techniques found appropriate, could be assimilated into SSM rather than being prefixed by it has been explored.

The interface between SSM and JSD was found to be feasible because JSD concentrates on the provision of the software component of a data-processing system but says nothing about organizational problem-solving or the organization of human activity which remains the domain of SSM.

The first stages of JSD normally involve the specification of the models that the system must support. The JSD method, however, offers little guidance as to how one ought to go about this activity except by listening to users' accounts of their own world. Using SSM conceptual models as the basis for providing JSD initial models and including these models in the debate about desirable and feasible change offers an extra dimension to the discussions about 'real world' activity as well as an interface between SSM and JSD of the output–input type used to bind tools and techniques into a methodology. Use of the SSM/JSD framework has not been of the 'bolt-on' fashion, rather the two frameworks have been interleaved, as far as possible, such that there is an area of overlap. This does not mean that upon implementation of some form of computer-based system the task is complete since it is at this point that emergence becomes the spur for the next cycle of exploration, understanding and change. In this way the software component of an information system may evolve to fit closely the requirements of its parent human activity system (Land & Somogyi, 1986).

During the conceptual modelling phase of Checkland's SSM, models are constructed which contain ordered sets of verbs which express in minimal terms what the system is to do. Checkland's view of conceptual models is that they should contain the activities necessary to ensure that they reflect what logic suggests the system must do in order to be the system defined in the root definition. These conceptual models may then be compared with what exists in the 'real world' and used to focus debate on potential change.

Questions such as 'Are these tasks done? If so, are they successfully undertaken? Why are they done in this way? Why is this not done in this way or at all?' may be asked at the comparison stage of SSM so leading to an agreement on desirable and feasible change, at which point the systems described in the relevant conceptual models may be implemented in full, implemented in part, modified or entirely ignored.

These conceptual models are a systemic representation of the activity system defined by the root definitions, and show the minimum set of activities necessary. They are not intended to be objective, rather they are subjective accounts of those relevant systems which the systems thinker views as useful and insightful. They are in no way intended as accurate and correct accounts of some empirical reality. As models of activity systems, conceptual models focus not surprisingly on activity, manifest as ordered sets of verbs. What they do not portray, and of course it is not intended that they do so, is the detailed information used within each activity either for decision-making purposes or as basic operational parameters.

Stowell argues that conceptual models may be expanded in consultation with the user, so that.

The result of this process is the production of an activity model which attempts to solve the problem which is at the same time a solution considered feasible by the user'. (Stowell, 1985)

This approach is clearly attractive and undoubtedly useful in practice but it represents activities which ought to occur in SSM in three separate phases, conceptual modelling: comparison:

feasible and desirable change. It also assumes, in similar fashion to Multiview, that the transformation of the conceptual model into a logical DFD is simply a matter of 'fleshing out' (Stowell, 1985), that is, 'the conceptual model can be expanded to provide a more detailed specification of data using a dataflow diagram'.

The fact that conceptual models are not intended to represent an objective empirical reality but rather a logical description of a particular Weltanschauung for a particular root definition often means that no one conceptual model is implemented as the new set of 'real-world' changes and procedures. Such changes and procedures are dependent upon negotiation, consensus and compromise over what the agreed model should be, and how its activity should be structured. Even if consensus is possible it is no guarantee that the conceptual model, in identifying core activities that everyone agrees should be done, will form a blueprint for how it should be done. Historical, political, social, economic and technical issues will inevitably play a part in the determination of desirable and feasible change.

In seeking to 'define a consensus primary task model of an organization which recognizes, and is based upon, the multiple perceptions of the aims and the missions of the organization according to the various managers involved', Wilson has provided the most coherent account yet of how such a model can be used as the basis for determining information requirements (Wilson, 1987).

The consensus primary task model has at its core Wilson's neutral primary task system or that combination of activities which is common to all relevant conceptual models, such relevance being determined by the systems thinkers which even conceptual model representing a specific Weltanschauung for a given root definition. The remainder of Wilson's consensus primary task model is formed from activities for which there is either 100% agreement over their inclusion or where consensus has been reached through agreement and compromise. Only after a consensus primary task model has been finalized are the information requirements for each activity considered, providing a data model of the system's data requirements and allowing the construction of a Maltese Cross (Wilson, 1984).

This exercise provides a mechanism by which the gap between the old logical and the new logical system may be bridged and thus could be used to determine, based on systems concepts, dataflow diagrams, data dictionaries, process specifications and so on. It requires, however, an early agreement over the consensus primary task model before any exploration of data requirements can be undertaken. The implication of this ordering is that decisions over whether or not to include an activity must be made without formal exploration of its information requirements or provision. Such consideration must at the very least form part of the cost benefit equation and must therefore be part of the debate over desirable and feasible change.

There is also, through the search for a consensus over a single conceptual model, a narrowing of the data analysis to the inclusion of just that data which is required at a given point in time to support the single agreed conceptual model. It is at the conceptual modelling phase of SSM that the richness of the modelling activity may be enhanced through consideration of JSD type actions, attributes and structures. This enrichment of conceptual models is in line with Checkland's own view that conceptual models should be enhanced or expanded at this stage by the application of other systems thinking.

While the consideration of actions, attributes and structures does not fall into the area of what is normally taken to be systems thinking, there is no compromise of perspective or mismatch either ontologically, epistemologically or methodologically, from the inclusion of such considerations at this stage.

Conceptual models are formed from structured sets of verbs, describing in as much detail as thought necessary, the activities that a particular defined system must carry out. These activities may be concerned with monitoring, processing, decision-making, informing and so on. We may consider each activity to fall into one of two broad categories; the monitoring and recording of events, external and internal; and taking action to initiate events, reactive and proactive. The focus of the information systems developer is fundamentally associated with the former, as at the core of any information system must exist some model or models of reality. In addition to the development of this modelling and recording activity, basic functionality, often of a mechanistic or perhaps probabilistic nature, will also be given consideration. These two categories are manifest in the software components of any human activity system as model and function in the terms defined by Jackson (Jackson, 1983).

It follows therefore that the conceptual modelling phase of SSM is a natural point at which to begin consideration of the basic models which the system so described is to support or requires. These models, which are complementary to the conceptual models from which they are derived, provide further insight into the notional systems described in the root definition and may be used to widen the debate at the comparison stage of SSM by providing both a model of the activity system suggested by the root definition together with a realizable model of those computer-based software components necessary to support the activity system. It should be noted, however, that such a model need not necessarily be chosen for implementation.

The information required and provided for each activity may now form part of the debate on desirable and feasible change, thus providing a clearer indication of the interfaces between each notional system as well as an indication of where data or effort is likely to be duplicated or not cost effective. In effect, the models defined as necessary for each conceptual model are views of the wider modelling requirements or local logical models. During the action phase these views may be altered, pruned, merged, extended or ignored to form cohesive global logical models.

Using Jackson's entity action and entity structure steps at this point allows the definition of relevant actions or events, the attributes of each action, and the time-ordered sequencing considered to be important. These actions normally take the form of active verbs and concern entities which undertake or suffer actions in a discernable and significant temporal ordering. The actions which form the structure of a given entity may be initiated by that entity, and are imposed on the entity. Entities may be internal to a system and have no meaning except within the context of that system. Such entities tend to be abstract in type (e.g. the notion of a 'cohort' of students in either a course-based institution as opposed to one which has adopted modularization).

The determination of entity models is therefore inexorably linked to an understanding of the activity systems in which they participate. Entity models developed in conjunction with conceptual models are not intended to form the culmination of JSD phase two or a definitive set of entity structures. Their use lies in an extended perspective of what the root definition implies the relevant notional system to be, by stating not only what it seems the system must do but also what is done (at the level of individual actions) that must be modelled, what it is done by or to and in what order. In addition, specifications of the attributes of each action are produced, thus defining the information requirements for and the outputs from each activity.

The entity models may then, like the conceptual models from which they are derived, be used to focus debate on desirable and feasible change. Further exploration may be necessary, leading to new or changed conceptual models and new or changed entity models. Ultimately, like the conceptual models, entity models may be implemented in full, implemented in part, modified, merged, or entirely ignored.

## Biographies

Kevin Doyle is currently Senior Lecturer in Information Systems at the University of the West of England, Bristol, having recently completed a 3-year secondment as the Head of Administrative Computing within the same institution. He began his career in computing as a database designer with British Aerospace (Dynamics) before joining the then Bristol Polytechnic as a Research Associate. Having completed his MPhil for work on Integrated Information Systems for Institutions of Higher Education, he is now currently pursuing a PhD in Multi-Dimensional Information Systems.

James Wood is currently Senior Lecturer in Computer Science at the University of Salford and, together with Professor Wood-Harper, is Joint Director of the Doctoral Programme in Information Systems. After graduating in operational research he worked in the engineering industry as a systems analyst before undertaking research and joining Brunel University as a lecturer. He holds a Masters degree in business systems analysis and is presently completing his PhD on the methodological basis of information systems development under Professor Frank Land.

Trevor Wood-Harper is currently Professor of Computer Science and Information Systems at the University of Salford, where he leads a research group in information systems methodology, evaluation and quality. His degree is in computer science and he holds a Masters in systems engineering (with Peter Checkland) and a PhD in information systems. Prior to his academic career he was a senior systems analyst with Granada Television and British Airways. He is perhaps best known for his work on the Multiview methodology, and presently holds visiting positions in Denmark and the United States. He has published widely, with more than fifty research articles and five books to his credit.
