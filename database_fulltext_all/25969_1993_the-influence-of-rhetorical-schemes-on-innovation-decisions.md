# The influence of rhetorical schemes on innovation decisions

W. R. King and J. L. Kugler\*

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA 15260, USA, and \*Graduate School of Business Administration, Fordham University, New York, NY 10023, USA

Abstract. Innovation may be viewed as a political process in which rival ideas, sponsored by rival individuals or groups, compete for acceptance and support. Sponsors of a particular idea must build, through argumentation, a consensus to support their idea.

This paper addressed rhetorical schemes as a potentially important element of the innovation decision. While rhetorical actions have been studied in other contexts, they have not been investigated in innovation decision situations.

The construct of rhetorical scheme is developed in this paper as a way to explain the outcomes of the innovation decision process. A scenario was devised in which decision makers face two different rhetorical schemes, emotional/affective and technical/logical, both related to a technology innovation. A hypothesis is tested in order to make a preliminary judgement on the impact of the rhetoric scheme on the financial choices made by the decision makers. The results suggest that the current innovation paradigms might be usefully extended by incorporating rhetorical actions as explanatory elements.

Keywords: adoption of innovation, argumentation, decision processes, framing, heuristics, rhetorical action.

## INTRODUCTION

There is no dearth of research in innovation. Thousands of studies of innovation have been conducted. Thus far, much of the research focus has been on, among others, the dimensions of product-process (Kimberly & Evanisko, 1981; Meyer & Goes, 1988), radical-incremental change (Ettlie et al., 1984), patterns of diffusion (Rogers & Shoemaker, 1971; Mahajan & Peterson, 1985), stages of the innovation process (Zaltman et al., 1973) and the causes of innovation success and failure (Utterback et al., 1976; Hirschman, 1980).

Such studies have provided substantial knowledge concerning the innovation phenomenon. However, there are some important voids in our understanding of innovation adoption. Some researchers have called for a redirection of research towards innovation as a political process of enacting and implementing new ideas (e.g. Frost & Egri, 1991). Similarly, most models of innovation adoption avoid discussion of the need to identify stakeholders' underlying values and assumptions that provide reasons for their actions (Gilbert & Freeman, 1984).

The argumentation process that is demonstrably a key element of any organizational adoption decision has similarly been almost ignored, even though such studies have been conducted in law, public policy and other fields (Riley et al., 1987; Simon-Vanderbergen, 1987). Dutton et al. (1983) state that 'the implications of the language and labels employed to express understanding of decision situations have been markedly neglected' (p. 316).

This paper describes a study designed to preliminarily test the applicability and utility of argumentation theory in the context of an organizational innovation adoption decision.

## BACKGROUND OF THE STUDY

Innovation is the process by which technology is deployed in a given organization (Brooks, 1978). Innovation adoption encompasses a series of mental states that a potential adopter (an individual, a group or an organization) passes through leading to the understanding, evaluation and adoption or rejection of an innovation (Rogers, 1962). Adoption means the acceptance and continued use of a technology, a product, a service or an idea (Rogers, 1962, 1976).

Change, the introduction of something perceived to be new, is fundamental to the concept of innovation. The emergence of an innovation means that a new idea challenges the current order (Van de Ven, 1986). This realization takes place inside people's minds and is a function of the values and interests of individuals or groups involved in the adoption process. Often, executives discuss features of products that have not yet been designed, facilities that have not been built and customer groups who have not been addressed (Stamper, 1987). Top managers manipulate a hypothetical world represented by proposals, reports, schedules and many other managerial concepts: a world that exists mostly in their minds.

While there are criticisms against the notion of innovation emerging from perceptions or the adopters (Kimberly, 1981), it is widely recognized that it is the perception of the dominant decision-making group within the adopting unit that ultimately results in the adoption, or non-adoption, of the innovation (Ramamurthy, 1991).

While the adoption decision may be characterized as a calculation that is future oriented and hypothetical in nature (Rogers, 1976, 1983), contextual aspects complicate the innovation decision process. In an organization, each involved party might see the problem only as it is defined and qualified by the party's personal values and interests.

The strong pro-innovation bias that permeates the innovation literature is built upon the assumption that 'superior' innovations will survive on their own merits. However, a significant body of research tells a rather different story (Frost & Egri, 1991). The innovation decision is not always a rational, goal-directed, methodical endeavour. More often than that, it evolves amidst conflict generated by a multiplicity of goals, values, priorities and foci of attention. Most adoption decisions must be made in relation to political constraints and other factors. Rival ideas, sponsored by rival individuals or groups, compete for recognition and support.

In this context, the sponsor of an idea must build, through argumentation, a consensus to support his or her policy. Organizational decision making concerning the adoption of innovation is an ongoing search for agreement over a mutable set of goals: 'bringing new technology into being is a complex process in which goals are discovered, determined and modified along the way' (Schon, 1971, p. 8).

The notion of agreement is essential to argumentation theory. Ideas do not exist alone, apart from a context. Ideas do not unfold in a social vacuum, rather they must be articulated and advocated by someone:

'Decision and manipulation are usually two sides of the same coin. Any decision of importance is usually the outcome of competitive manipulation; any decision involving others will require some manipulation to put it into effect.' (Schram, 1973, p. 33)

When an innovation emerges, potential adopters are exposed to a variety of stimuli related to the innovation's characteristics and potential implications. They then proceed to develop their own, often idiosyncratic, perception of the innovation. New knowledge related to the innovation is gradually added to the pool of knowledge being shared by the participants.

However, new knowledge and new ideas are not adequate to change policy unless they become an issue for argumentation and unless they are used to gain influence and resources (Schon, 1971). Arguments provide the intellectual means to defend a point of view. $^{1,2}$ The aim of an argument is to promote awareness, goal congruence and agreement on an issue.

The decision-making group may then understand and accept the idea or proposition. As a result of that idea's acceptance, a plan for action begins to take shape in the decision makers' minds.

The ‘champion’ of an innovation creates and uses a participant argument in the attempt to convince others, to make the point of view acceptable and agreeable by them. An argument is therefore the ‘package’ in which points of view (beliefs and opinions) and supporting evidence (factual data) are combined and presented to an audience.

## OBJECTIVE OF THE STUDY

This study addresses a major deficiency of the extant adoption paradigm: its omission of the possible effects of different argumentation strategies. As Rogers (1983) has put it, 'innovations do not sell themselves' (p. 7). The innovation decision process may be strongly influenced by rhetorical action.

Instead of focusing on an innovation's characteristics or an adopter's characteristics, this study investigates how arguments may differentially influence the adoption process. While this rhetorical action perspective has been widely utilized in other areas, it is new in the innovation context. Therefore, this study must necessarily be preliminary and exploratory. Hence, one straightforward hypothesis is addressed in order to ascertain whether rhetorical factors indeed appear to influence innovation choices and outcomes.

If rhetorical action is shown to be significant, the conclusion will be that a potentially important factor has been omitted from prior innovation research. Taken together with the traditional determinants of innovation behaviour – the characteristics of the innovation and of the adopter – this factor might well serve to enhance our ability to model innovation behaviour.

## THE BASES FOR STUDYING RHETORICAL ACTION AND THE INNOVATION DECISION PROCESS

Several factors influence the potential adopter's perceptions and mechanisms of information processing. A number of studies relate innovativeness to characteristics of the adopter (Midgley & Dowling, 1978; Robertson et al., 1984), and to characteristics of the innovation itself, such as cost, compatibility, trialability, effectiveness and comparative advantage (Hopkins, 1980; Calantone & Cooper, 1981; Heany, 1983). Other studies extend the investigation of the adoption process into contextual factors, such as perceived risk (Hirschman, 1980; Robertson & Wind, 1980), levels of cognitive processing (Swinyard & Coney, 1978; Bettman, 1979) and the adopter's expectations and acquisition pattern (Dickerson & Gentry, 1983), among others.

Numerous studies have illustrated that cognitive behaviour is affected by the way in which information is presented. Tversky & Kahneman (1981, 1986) presented empirical evidence on several problems whose context had been manipulated, and in which subjects' choices were greatly affected by the changes in context, or 'framing'. They studied the problem mostly in terms of risk (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, 1982, 1984). Many others have replicated and extended their framework. Most research in this area has operationalized the framing effect in whether a given choice between, for example, risky versus certain options differs as a result of differential framing or phrasing of options (Bateman, 1986; Northcroft & Neal, 1986; Fagley & Miller, 1987; Lewin et al., 1987).

Others have examined the effect of anecdotal information in terms of whether the information presented is 'vivid', 'salient', or 'pallid' (Schwank, 1986). Nisbett et al. (1982) have investigated the effort of 'concrete' (defined as 'emotionally interesting information') versus 'abstract' information (defined in terms of quantitative data, such as tabular frequency data). In a similar way, Fredrickson (1985) suggested that the way an issue is labelled influences managers' behaviour; he reports experiments in which subjects facing the same scenario, labelled either as an 'opportunity' or as a 'problem', have responded in a quite different fashion.

In the information systems field there have been studies on the role of competing modes of information presentation (e.g. graphs versus tables, sentencial versus pictorial information) on learning and performance (Lucas & Nielsen, 1980), in representing judgmental concepts (MacGregor & Slovic, 1986) and in enhancing decision-making effectiveness (Bembasat & Dexter, 1986; Dickson et al. 1986).

Those who study these decision biases, however, seem to ignore the fact that they are studying a rhetorical issue. There is a rhetorical dimension to all managerial behaviours, in the sense that managerial decisions are essentially carried out through communication acts. In the case of innovation, this rhetorical dimension is even more salient, since managers and other organizational actors strive to create and legitimize a vision of an emergent idea, and to induce commitment to it. It is then clear that an argumentation strategy intended to influence the attitude of potential adopters is a necessary and relevant component of the innovation decision process.

Building on Pfeffer (1981), we argue that innovation decisions are the result of symbolic manipulation: the manipulation of meanings and symbols to justify an adoption of a given idea and create commitment. The message should, of course, be adapted to the particular medium being used. Some strategies may suit a specific medium better than others. In the business arena, rhetorical actions are presented in a broad range of forums and media including not only traditional 'speech' (e.g. presentations, debates, public hearings) but also press releases, brochures, electronic mail messages, financial reports, slide shows, several sorts of media advertising and many other forms of corporate communication.

Regardless of the medium being used, the key dimensions in the rhetorical action are argument and evidence ('facts' or 'data'). No evidence has any meaning by itself; to be significant, data or evidence must become part of an argument. Arguments are the essential units of rhetorical action that combine factual data and sets of values and beliefs to express knowledge or commitment on a given issue. The argument is a product of the imagination and ingenuity of an advocate who tries to convince others. It embodies a particular way of organizing information concerning both beliefs and data. The processing of an argument is an inferential process that occurs in the recipient's mind.

Within the realm of rhetoric, the primary resource of interest is the argument, that is the design for conducting a discussion or debate. Argumentation is applied here generically to include all sorts of deliberative interactions, ranging from informal conversation around the water cooler to formal debate in the stockholders' meeting. Any of these interactions is a 'rhetorical action' to the degree that it is motivated by the desire to understand and examine a critical business question. Thus, argumentation is a goal-motivated interaction among two or more decision makers.

## FORMS OF ARGUMENTS

Arguments can be presented in many different ways. The complexity of the argument differs as the goal and audience changes. Decision makers usually try to get their peers to accept new concepts, products and policies. Some arguments are simple chains of reasons, arguing from a specific case to a conclusion. This type of reasoning is evident, for instance, when one notes that three out of five divisions of the company increased their sales, and then concludes that the increases are responsible for large company profits. Another argumentative message may be a complex case outlining a problem and suggesting a new policy to manage the question. For example, a company may present a detailed proposal to its stockholders indicating how it plans to expand its goods or services to meet new markets.

In this paper we adopt the classical notion of (a) emotional, or effective, proof; and (b) logical, or rational, proof. $^{3}$

## Emotional proof

An emotion-based argument is designed to convey emotion to the audience. It embodies appeal to the desires, hopes and fears of the audience. Messages involving emotional tension arouse the audience and stirs its feelings. Emotions have a critical role in decision-makers' cognitive reaction (Holbrook & Hirschman, 1982; Burke & Edell, 1989). People reason differently when they feel deeply about a matter, than when the question at hand has no emotional effect on them.

Politicians are well-known users of emotion-based arguments, especially fear and anger, as demonstrated by analysts of political campaigns and debates. For instance, metaphors of warfare and sports dominate political discussion in America:

'The encounters of the candidates with one another and their tactics are generally violent if not specifically warlike. Moreover the violence is multidirectional; it is directed not only at fellow candidates but at constituencies as well; voters are treated much like a people under seige.' (Blankenship, 1990, p. 164)

The same is true in the advertising arena. Most emotion-oriented appeals in the technical domain aim at the relative, or comparative, advantage (or at the lack of it). Take, for instance, the area of computer systems. Some advertisements emphasize that one should buy a personal computer because others in the same demographic or professional sectors already have them and, thus, possess some type of 'edge'. The implicit concept is that if one does not have such a system or equipment then one is less equipped and, consequently, lacking comparative advantages. Another argument is that one should always use a system that represents the 'state-of-the-art'. Likewise, the implicit concept is that any one person (or organization) that does not have an up-to-date system or equipment, by comparison with others, also lacks comparative advantage (McGary, 1985).

In the business arena, emotional proof is often related to what Schuetz (1990) calls 'company storytelling'. The objective of company storytelling is to influence decision makers by promoting issues rather than facts. Storytelling works as an argument because the stories provide accounts of incidents and accomplishments that are-the grounds for inferences about the values of the company or work group. Stories work effectively as reasons because the narrative links the experiences and values of the storyteller with those of the other decision makers (Schuetz, 1990).

## Logical proof

Various authors (e.g. McGary, 1985; Apotheloz, 1986; Eemeren & Krieger, 1986) agree that a logical argument, when related to technical matters, usually follows the general structure: 'if p, then g (where p is a statement that describes the abilities of the device or system and g is a statement that describes the intended consequence in terms of enhanced productivity or efficiency).

In the business scenario, logical proof is often related to what Schuetz (1990) calls 'justification'. This type of claim deals primarily with facts and often uses financial or technical data to support the claim. Arguments used in economic or technical justification usually follow a relatively simple structure linking cause(s) to effect(s). A typical example would be an advertisement that explains the amount of working memory a computer has; most computer advertisements go on to explain that a better memory capacity implies that the user can run more programs, that they will run faster, that they can be made more user friendly, etc; which means that the business competence of the user will be enhanced.

## DESIGN OF THE STUDY

The innovation decision is a mental process during which a person or group attempts to understand and evaluate a specific innovation (Rogers, 1962, 1983). The outcome of this process might be a decision to adopt the innovation, to adopt it partially, to gather additional information, to postpone the decision or to adopt no innovation. This outcome is often called 'intention to adopt'. The term was coined by Scott (1985), who emphasizes the difference between an ‘intention to adopt’ (the outcome of a decision process involving understanding and evaluating a particular innovation) and ‘adoption’ (actual acquisition and continued, repeated use of the innovation). The intention to adopt has been the focus of studies on consumer behaviour, and empirical evidence provides support on the linkage between intention and actual acquisition (Holak, 1988).

What are the possible effects that emotional and logical arguments might have on adopters' choices? Some social psychologists argue that the persuasiveness of a given argument depends mostly on its logical strength or 'the soundness of the assertion' (Burnstein & Vinokur, 1977, p. 326). In the business field several authors have suggested that training on analytical tools lead business-oriented professionals and students to believe that logic will speak for itself, and that high-quality technical and/or analytical work will undoubtedly be accepted by other peers (e.g. McCauley & Macdaid, 1986; Hill & Somers, 1988). Others believe that either experience acquired in the work environment or exposure to advanced management training (e.g. an MBA programme) may contribute decisively to neutralize framing effects. The following is typical:

'If individuals have been trained in analyzing a certain problem, we would expect the effects of framing to be minimized. For several reasons, managers could be expected not to be affected by frame changes [emphasis added]. In many cases, they have had years of experience in decision making. Additionally, the business environment may cue managers to utilize more complex normative models instead of the simple decision models used by people in their everyday lives. Finally, the higher stakes involved in business decision making could provide a reason to use appropriate normative models.' (Mowen & Mowen, 1986, p. 597)

In contrast, other researchers believe that the impact of feelings and emotions on cognition is too strong to ignore. Much research shows that inducing an affective state influences important elements of human information processing such as evaluation, judgment and recall (e.g. Burke & Edell, 1989).

Technology also exerts a strong, pervasive influence in Western societies. It has been widely recognized that rapid technological change has not been balanced by the capabilities of individuals and organizations to evaluate it and to adopt it (Ellul, 1982; Weinzenbaum, 1984). It may be that, as McGary (1985) suggests, technology has become a strong argument by and for itself. In societies whose formation rests on scientific and technological development, technical proofs seem to have strong appeal. A technical proof is based on the assumption that technology, specifically a 'new' or 'advanced' technology, is always socially desirable:

'Technology, as a proof, seldom asks axiological questions. Indeed, in much of the writing on modern technology, and specifically in writing dealing with computers, philosophical questions are seen as aberrations of 'old fashioned' minds . . . Don't ask questions about the impact of the technology before we invent the technology, ask whether or not we can make the technology.' (McGary, 1985, p. 95)

Meyer & Rowan (1977) elaborate on the reason technologies are institutionalized and become taken-for-granted, almost sacred, means: ‘. . . quite apart from their possible efficiency, such institutionalized techniques establish an organization as appropriate, rational, and modern. Their use displays responsibility and avoids claims of negligence.’ (p. 344).

The business culture in America is strongly shaped by the assumption that proper managerial behaviour should be confined to rational, analytical approaches: 'There is a tendency to avoid emotional expressiveness for instance. The emphasis instead has been on static presentation skills often using charts and graphs rich in quantitative measures to convey ideas.' (Conger, 1991, p. 31).

The notion of managers as rhetoricians might be a radical departure from these practices of business behaviour. Yet it is clear that rhetorical action can affect choice; the only questions are 'How?' and 'How much?'.

To address the problem of how arguments affect the innovation decision process, we use the idea of rhetorical scheme. A rhetorical scheme is a frame generated by the deliberate use of rhetorical elements to persuade potential adopters to commit resources to a given innovation.

We can now address the framing problem from a different perspective. Instead of choices regarding 'gains versus losses' or 'positive versus negative outlooks', as are typically employed, this study investigates the effect of two distinct rhetorical schemes, the first based upon logical/technical proof, the second upon emotional/affective proof.

The null hypothesis offered here is that decision makers facing an innovative choice will not be influenced by differences between arguments based on logical/technical appeal or arguments based on an emotional/affective appeal when they are provided with the same substantive factual information concerning the choice.

This single, null hypothesis is posed to ascertain the potential impact of rhetorical schemes on innovation decisions. If it is not confirmed, further study of this phenomenon will be deemed to be justified.

## RESEARCH PROCEDURE

A laboratory experiment was devised to test the hypothesis. The scenario was pretested and refined by a group of eight faculty members and graduate business students. None of the pretest participants were included in the actual experiment.

The experimental subjects were 49 graduate students, enrolled in an MBA programme at a major university. All of the subjects were also employed in organizations and most had at least several years of business experience.

Each subject was presented with an organizational scenario depicting the history of a hypothetical manufacturing firm. The first part of the scenario described the background of the firm, its current business environment and trends in the industry. The second part of the scenario introduced the prospect of acquiring a new technology, a computer-integrated manufacturing system (CIM).

Each subject was asked to play the role of a corporate executive. The individual had to decide on the degree of commitment of resources to the implementation of the new manufacturing system. This new system could be implemented either in a fully integrated fashion or in a partially integrated fashion, or not at all, as indicated by the dollar amount of resources committed.

To simulate the perception of a scarcity of resources, subjects were asked to distribute a fix investment sum between two projects: (a) the new manufacturing system, to be implemented either fully or partially; and (b) the expansion of the firm's current service infrastructure. The dollar amount invested in the new manufacturing system was then taken to operationalize the individual's commitment to the new technology.

Each subject read and analysed the 14-page scenario on an individual basis; subjects had no opportunity to communicate with others during the experiment.

The independent variable, the rhetorical scheme, was then manipulated to present two distinct conditions. The second part of the case study was presented in two distinct versions, one in terms of logical appeal and the other in terms of emotional appeal.

The logical appeal in favour of the new manufacturing system was formulated in terms of economic justification. Schuetz (1990) suggested that these arguments usually follow a deductive pattern. That is, the idea's advocate puts forth a claim, presents evidence to support the claim and interprets the meaning of that claim for the audience. The arguments are usually presented as a list. The format used in the logical appeal followed closely the examples presented by Schuetz (1990). In this logical condition, there was a reference to the potential impacts of the new system. Impacts were then presented as a list. These consequences have also been explained in earlier sections of the scenario so as to provide informational equivalence on both versions.

In the emotional condition, the argument was framed with emphasis on major corporate values: loyalty to customers and supplies, quick response to customers' needs and a strong sense of pride of the quality of the company's products. The chief executive officer (CEO) presents a short yet compelling comment on what constitutes value and quality for him. The comment is presented in the case in just three paragraphs. These comments were modelled after what Conger (1991) calls 'the language of leadership'. He contends that a powerful, inspiring leader's message should include two components: (a) a definition of the purposes of the organization and (b) use of rhetorical elements that create emotional power. For the sake of illustration, just the second paragraph is presented here:

'... keep in mind that these people who buy corporate planes are guys who enjoy flying. It is a question of convenience, status, and also, pleasure, why not? ... now, if you talk about commuter folks ... most of them are pilots, really good pilots ... you know, I am old enough to remember when all [commuter airlines] owners were also good pilots.'

To ensure informational equivalence, the difference between the two versions of the case study was kept to a minimum: both descriptions had identical sections, identical format and identical numbers of pages. The advantages of the innovation were summarized, in both versions, in terms of projected impacts on several measures of performance (productivity, flexibility, product quality, final cost, timing and financial performance).

To ensure that the experiment was about the effect of different arguments rather than about the effect of tabular versus graphical versus textual data, a table with quantitative data was included in both versions. $^{4}$ The table illustrated the evolution of the total market and the company's market share during the period 1980–86.

In addition, to make sure that the personalities of the CEO and other executives depicted in the case was not a major factor in the experiment, the second part of the case included three memos (Table 1). Thus, both versions were balanced in terms of appeal to figures of authority and expertise.

## RESULTS AND IMPLICATIONS

The results of the experiment for the two treatments are displayed in Figs 1 and 2. A one-way analysis of variance revealed that the experimental manipulation had a primary effect on the investment commitment. Subjects invested more money in the new manufacturing system under the emotional condition than under the logical/technical condition (x=\$37.61 m versus \$35.62 m; $F(1,48)=3983$ ; $P=0.05$ ). The null research hypothesis is thus rejected and the conclusion is that there is a significant difference between the impacts of the two treatments.

These results suggest that, despite the analytical training to which MBAs are typically exposed, the effect of emotional arguments may be quite strong. Indeed, many rhetoricians have known that persuasion occurs through both evidence and argument, ' . . . through the light of logic and the heat of passion' (Campbell, 1982, p. 6). Thus, a rhetorical act must provide justifications that appeal not only to decision makers' cognitive capabilities but also to their affective competencies.

## CONCLUSION

Based on rhetoric theory, this study suggested a potentially important addition to the models used to investigate decisions involving adoption of technology-based innovations. A construct representing the type of rhetorical scheme, emotional/affective versus logical/technical, was operationalized in an experimental scenario.

The results indicated that rhetorical scheme can have a significant impact on decision makers' behaviour in innovation contexts, and that emotional appeals might exert more influence than logical appeals. This result is contrary to much of the 'conventional wisdom' prevalent in the business literature.

Though preliminary in nature, this result may be useful to decision makers concerned with objectively evaluating proposals for implementing new technologies. Business decisions involve constant and inescapable argumentation, whether evaluating new ideas and their possible consequences or assessing old ideas and their successes and inadequacies. Managers often construct and communicate their views of the world as a means to influence others. By using rhetorical analysis to discriminate between the different types of proofs and inference patterns, chances might be enhanced for reaching consensus on what criteria should be used for making a sound group decision.

We also believe that managers who are aware of the potential importance of rhetoric might use these results to guide their decision processes. For instance, if two decision-making groups are provided with the same factual basis and come to the same decision regardless of the rhetorical schemes being employed, one might have greater confidence in the validity of the decision than if the reverse is true. In such a case, the choice might be deemed to be robust with respect to rhetoric. If, as was the case in this study, the rhetorical scheme is shown to make a significant difference, further analysis of the underlying assumptions of the individuals may be useful (e.g. Mason & Mitroff, 1981).

The study also has a theoretical implication. It represents a first step toward the integration of two independent lines of research: innovation adoption and rhetoric. Other models related to decision-making behaviour, such as utility theory and prospect theory, have a major deficiency in their inability to account for the effects of different modes of information presentation and different argumentation strategies. The approach pursued here might be applied in complementing these theories.

This is a preliminary result. However, it suggests that the predictive power of the two major factors that have been previously considered in innovation adoption research – the characteristics of the innovation and of the potential adopter – might be enhanced if rhetorical action was to be introduced as a complementary factor. Further research is certainly indicated, and managers involved in innovation decisions should be aware of the rhetorical element.
