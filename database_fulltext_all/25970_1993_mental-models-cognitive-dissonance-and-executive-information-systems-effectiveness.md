# Mental models, cognitive dissonance and executive information systems' effectiveness

A. Ramaprasad, M. E. Hill\* and D. A. Salach†

Pontikes Center for Management of Information, College of Business and Administration, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA,

\*Department of Marketing, Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA, and †Comdisco, Inc., 6111 N. River Road, Rosemont, IL 60018, USA

Abstract. Recently there has been interest in the interaction between executives' mental models and executive information systems (EIS). As stated by Rockart & De Long (1988), 'the most significant effect of computer support for executives may be in the enhanced mental models of the systems' users' (p. 130).

It is proposed that each interaction between the EIS and the executive will induce cognitive dissonance in the executive. The effect of the dissonance on the mental models will depend upon (a) whether the executive is in an exploratory mode or confirmatory mode and (b) whether the dissonance is below, within or above the threshold range.

Cognitive dissonance serves to instil an opportunity for the creation, confirmation, expansion or reconstruction of one's mental model(s). This increases the likelihood of the executive's effectiveness being enhanced by the EIS and thus would enable better decision making. On the other hand, it can also lead to the rejection of the EIS depending on the mode (exploratory or confirmatory) of the executives and the level of their conviction in their mental models.

An understanding of the cognitive dissonance threshold ranges of executives under different modes of operation is important to prevent rejection of the EIS at one extreme and unquestioning acceptance at the other extreme. Continuous learning engendered by cognitive dissonance within the threshold range, whether in the exploratory mode or the confirmatory mode, is essential for sustaining the symbiotic relationship between the executive and the EIS. Controlling the level of dissonance within certain range limits would allow opportunities for the creation, confirmation, expansion or reconstruction of mental models and thus, through enhanced mental models, enable better decision making by the executive. This better decision making results in increased effectiveness.

Keywords: executive information systems, mental models, cognitive dissonance, effectiveness, decision-making

## INTRODUCTION

Research indicates that executives form mental models of their organization and environment in their normal course of doing business (e.g. Mintzberg, 1973; Jaques, 1976; Kotter, 1982; Isenberg, 1984). The concept of mental models has a long history; it was discussed by Craik (1943), and subsequently by a number of other researchers (e.g. Toffler, 1970; Johnson-Laird, 1983; Young, 1983; Wickens, 1984; Rouse & Morris, 1986; Baggett & Ehrenfeucht, 1988). Johnson-Laird (1983) describes mental models as follows:

Since mental models can take many forms and serve many purposes, their contents are very varied. They can contain nothing but tokens that represent individuals and identities between them, as in the sorts of models that are required for syllogistic reasoning. They can represent spatial relations between entities, and the temporal or causal relations between events. A rich imaginary model of the world can be used to compute the projective relations required for an image. Models have a content and form that fits them to their purpose, whether it be to explain, to predict, or to control.' (p. 410)

Thus, executives' mental models may be thought of as forming the core of their decision making, helping them explain, predict and control events in the organization and its surrounding environment. As such, they help executives to sense problems, formulate them, solve them, implement the solutions and evaluate the impact of the solutions (Ramaprasad & Mitroff, 1984; Ramaprasad, 1987) – in short, to learn.

Recently, there has been interest in the interaction of executives' mental models and executive information systems (EIS) (Rockart & De Long, 1988; Paller & Laska, 1990). As stated by Rockart & De Long (1988), 'the most significant effect of computer support for executives may be in the enhanced mental models of the systems' users' (p. 130). Thus, an effective EIS is one that supports individual learning.

Based on the current extant literature on mental models, cognitive dissonance and EIS, we are presently in a position to explore in greater depth the interaction between an EIS and an executive's mental model(s), and the consequences of the interaction on executives' learning. It is proposed that each interaction between the EIS and the executive will induce cognitive dissonance in the executive owing to lack of a perfect fit between the data provided by the EIS and the mental models. Further, it is proposed that the effect of the dissonance on the mental models, and consequently upon learning, will depend upon (a) whether the executive is in an exploratory mode or confirmatory mode and (b) whether the dissonance is below, within or above the threshold range. These effects are illustrated by anecdotes from a successful EIS development effort.

The purpose of this paper is to provide an understanding of mental models and provide insights into how their development can be enhanced or inhibited by an EIS through cognitive dissonance. As mentioned earlier, the effectiveness of an EIS is directly related to the learning it engenders in the executive. Cognitive dissonance serves to instil an opportunity for the creation, confirmation, expansion or reconstruction of mental model(s). This increases the likelihood of the executive's effectiveness being enhanced by the EIS and thus would enable better decision making.

## EXECUTIVE INFORMATION SYSTEMS (EIS)

In this paper EIS is used in the generic sense to include what has been called executive support systems (ESS), and even some characteristics of decision support systems (DSS). Such use may be supported by (a) popular usage (Turban, 1990) and (b) the fact that the boundaries between the so-called EIS, ESS, DSS and even management information systems (MIS) are becoming blurred by technological developments.

Rockart & De Long (1988) define EIS as 'the routine use of a computer system, most often through direct access to a terminal or personal computer, for any business function. The users are either the CEO or a member of the senior management team reporting directly to him or her. Executive support systems can be implemented at the corporate or divisional level.' (p. 16)

Another definition of EIS is as follows:

'An EIS is a hands-on tool that focuses, filters, and organizes executives' information so they can make more effective use of it. It preserves the benefits of an existing paper reporting system and improves upon it by using electronic information delivery that increases the data's timeliness and usability. An EIS enhances the ease and effectiveness with which an executive can perform information-intensive activities that are inherently part of a company's planning and control process.' (Commander EIS Development Guide, 1990)

Turban (1990) defines an EIS as 'a computer-based system that serves the information needs of top executives. It provides rapid access to timely information and direct access to management reports. It is very user-friendly, supported by graphics, and provides exceptions' reporting and "drill-down" capabilities. It is also easily connected with online information services and electronic mail' (p. 366). He defines an ESS as 'a comprehensive support system that goes beyond EIS to include communications, office automation, analysis support and intelligence' (p. 366).

Ultimately, an EIS, like all information systems, provides descriptive information on (a) the site and (b) changes in the state of the organization and its environment. Quantitative, textual or graphical information on different selected elements is provided for this purpose. An EIS provides insights into factors affecting the state and the changes in the state by highlighting relationships between significant elements at a point in time and over time. Finally, it also provides the capability to model the dynamics of the organization and the environment to understand past behaviour and project future behaviour.

The factors distinguishing an EIS from existing IS can be said to be: (a) active participation by the executive, (b) customization, (c) flexibility and (d) timeliness. An EIS allows for interactive information seeking for unstructured and non-routine searches. It also allows for 'what if' type analysis through modelling capabilities. Both the form and content of information can be customized to an executive; the reports need not be standardized as in a traditional MIS. Executives can freely navigate by themselves to different segments and levels of detail of information. They can also reformat the information and redesign the models. Their attention can be automatically drawn to significant items and relationships. And all of the above can be accomplished without significant delays.

The above four distinguishing factors of an EIS can have profound effects on the interaction of the executive with the EIS. If the form and content of the information provided by the EIS fits the information receptors in the executive's mental models, a truly symbiotic relationship can develop between the two. Such a relationship can lead to the enhancement of both the EIS and the mental models. As discussed earlier, enhancements in mental model(s) would enable better decision making and thus an EIS would contribute to increasing effectiveness, which in turn would strengthen the symbiotic relationship. On the other hand, the absence of such a relationship could be detrimental to both.

## MENTAL MODELS

The notion of mental models comes from the idea that humans are processors of information and have limited processing capabilities. Owing to our limited processing capacity, knowledge structures (e.g. mental models) have been proposed as a plausible explanation as to how we go about handling such situations. These knowledge structures are mental representations of reality and are viewed as being an abstraction of that reality. The more removed they are from reality, the greater the probability of poor decisions being made and, thus, one's effectiveness being reduced proportionately. Johnson-Laird (1983) describes them as follows:

'human beings understand the world by constructing working models of it in their minds. Since these models are incomplete, they are simpler than the entities they represent. In consequence, models contain elements that are merely imitations of reality — there is no working model of how their counterparts in the world operate, but only procedures that mimic their behavior. When men invented numbers, they neither grasped, nor need to grasp, all of their mathematical properties. A limited model of arithmetic is useful, which is fortunate because there can be no consistent formal system that captures all of it.' (p. 10)

Frames, scripts, schemata, cognitive maps, mental images and mental models are closely related (Ramaprasad, 1987). They represent some form of a knowledge structure (cognitive structure). The concept of cognitive structure provides an explanation as to how meaning is given to data.

"Zajonc (1960) defined cognitive structure as an organized subset of the attributes an individual uses to identify and discriminate a particular object or event. Scott (1963) referred to cognitive structure as those structures whose elements consist of ideas consciously held by the person or as the set of ideas maintained by a person and relatively available to conscious awareness. The content of experience is believed to be organized into more-complex structural assemblies, and it is these structures that give meaning to specific elements (for example, particular beliefs, knowledges, values, expectancies)." (Shaw & Costanzo, 1982, p. 182).

A script and a frame are considered to be derivatives of a schema. A schema, as defined by Boland et al. (1990), is 'a general term for the knowledge structure or set of expectations that an individual draws upon to guide interpretation, inference and action in any particular situation' (p. 197). A script is a schema pertaining to mundane knowledge about a stereotyped sequence of actions. For example, when you drive into a full-service petrol station and the attendant comes over to your car, it is not necessary for you to figure out what caused the attendant to come. It is sufficient to have knowledge of the sequence of events pertaining to this situation to behave appropriately. 'This knowledge leaves more cognitive capacity available for use in more interesting tasks' (Galambos et al., 1986, p. 130).

Frames are hierarchical nodal knowledge structures representing a stereotypical entity but are not interconnected among entity representations. Previous researchers have looked upon scripts (Schank & Abelson, 1977; Gioia & Poole, 1984) and frames (Minsky, 1975) as being static in nature.

On the other hand, schemata are more global and dynamic in nature. This is illustrated by Brewer's (1987) definition of schemata. Schemata are:

'unconscious mental structures that underlie the molar aspects of human knowledge and skill. They are modular – different cognitive domains have schemas with different structural properties. Schemas interact with incoming episodic information to modify the generic information in the schema and to produce specific instantiated memory representations of incoming episodic information. An instantiated schema is a specific cognitive structure that results from the interaction of the old information of the generic schema and the new information from the episodic input.' (p. 188)

Frames and scripts are viewed as substructures of schemata. But how are schemata related to mental models? It appears that there is a continuum between the concepts of schemata and mental models. The research indicates that schemata provide the procedures from which mental models are formed (Johnson-Laird, 1983; Rumelhart, 1984; Manktelow & Jones, 1987). As stated by Wilson & Rutherford (1989):

'it appears that the major difference between mental models and schemata is that the latter are taken to be data structures in memory, which can be activated, whereas the former are regarded as utilization of such information in a computationally dynamic manner.' (p. 624)

This implies that schemata are the filters of the mind through which mental models are constructed. Mental models are affected by existing schemata, new information and the situation. Mental models, as well as mental images, which will be discussed further, are specific in nature. However, this specificity of mental models does not mean that they cannot represent a general class of entities. Mental models are viewed as forming the foundation from which mental images are developed. Mental images are particular views of the corresponding mental model.

In addition to the above discussed terms, recent research has been using the term 'cognitive maps' (e.g. Huff, 1990). Stubbart & Ramaprasad (1988) defined cognitive maps as representations of a manager's knowledge of causal relationships. The notion of cognitive maps implies that there is some form of cognitive structure and that this structure can be mapped. The technique of cognitive mapping has been used in research for general management, strategy and marketing (Eden et al., 1983; Huff & Schwenk, 1985; Howard, 1989; Stubbart & Ramaprasad, 1988; Ward & Reinger, 1990). Essentially, cognitive mapping is attempting to map knowledge structures, whether it be frames or schemata. A map tends to imply a two-dimensional representation, and the research using cognitive maps has tended to use two-dimensional representation. However, the concept of mental models tends to imply more than two dimensions.

In summary, there appears to be a continuum, from schemata to mental models. Frames and scripts can be viewed as substructures of schemata and mental images as views of mental models. The notion of a cognitive map centres on the idea that there are cognitive structures and that they can be mapped.

The concept of knowledge structures has significant implications. By assuming that knowledge structures exist, it follows that decisions are based on these knowledge structures and that situational decisions tend to be made based on mental models/mental images. For example, executives may have a mental model as to their organization's position relative to the competition's from which decisions would be made.

The importance of mental models seems evident. But given that they are abstractions of reality, how can they be enhanced so that better decisions can be made? From the previous discussions on schemata it is evident that schemata can change with new information, and as the schemata change so do the mental models. The theory of cognitive dissonance provides insights into a plausible means for providing the opportunities to confirm, expand or reconstruct one's mental models.

## COGNITIVE DISSONANCE

Organizations and the environments in which they operate are complex, dynamic and ill-structured. The amount of information processing required for a complete and correct understanding of the dynamics is beyond the cognitive capabilities of any single executive. Hence, it would be reasonable to assume that an executive's knowledge structure will be only partially complete and correct, and that the extent of completion and correctness will vary across different segments of the organization and the environment.

When executives interact with an EIS it would be reasonable to expect some mismatch between the expectations engendered by their mental model and the actual data presented by the EIS. This mismatch between the expected and the actual results in cognitive dissonance. Cognitive dissonance results in a psychologically uncomfortable state and, thus, motivates one to reduce dissonance and achieve consonance (Shaw & Costanzo, 1982). For convenience, we will consider three levels of dissonance: (a) below a threshold range, (b) within the threshold range and (c) above the threshold range.

The theory of cognitive dissonance basically proposes that there is a tendency towards cognitive consistency or balance. As summarized by Shaw & Costanzo (1982):

'The basic core of the theory of cognitive dissonance is deceptively simple: there exist "nonfitting" relations among cognitive elements that give rise to cognitive dissonance; cognitive dissonance creates pressure to reduce dissonance and to avoid increases in dissonance; the results of such pressures are manifested in changes in cognition, behavior changes, and selective exposure to new information and opinions.' (p. 217)

In order to explain the dynamics of dissonance, let us consider two broad modes of interaction between executives and their EIS: (a) exploratory and (b) confirmatory. The following discussion is summarized in Table 1.

## Exploratory mode

The objective of executives in the exploratory mode would be to learn and to develop their mental model. Learning results in the creation or modification of mental model(s). The exploratory mode is best characterized as being serendipitous, revolving around discovery. Additionally, this mode provides the opportunities to create a new mental model or to confirm, expand or reconstruct an existing mental model. Executives in this mode would be seeking to understand the state of the organization, the changes in the state and the reasons for the same. They are open to incorporating new data elements, modifying existing relationships and incorporating new relationships in their mental model. In this mode executives can be said to be 'applying' (Ramaprasad, 1987) their preliminary mental models to make sense of the data.

Executives may be in an exploratory mode under a variety of circumstances: (a) in the prototyping of an EIS, (b) when they are learning a new aspect of the EIS, (c) during and following a crisis or (d) by conscious choice. Except during a crisis this mode is characterized by a lack of time pressure to decide and act.

Even in the exploratory mode, if the dissonance is below the threshold range, the creative tension and motivation necessary for exploration will be absent. In such a case executives may induce dissonance by refocusing their exploration or by attempting to refine the mental model. Refocusing or refining may push their dissonance into the threshold range.

If dissonance is in the threshold range, an executive in the exploratory mode can be expected to further explore the data to develop a more comprehensive mental model. This further exploration is a result of positive reinforcement, which serves to strengthen the symbiotic relationship. The executive can be expected to seek (a) more detailed data (drill-down), (b) new data, (c) new forms of data representation and (d) new relationships among the data. 'A new relationship may be inferred directly based on manifest covariation. Or it may be inferred indirectly based on [more] complex logical, mathematical, or statistical operations' (Ramaprasad, 1987, p. 144). An EIS's ability to present data in multiple formats would be important for observing 'manifest covariations'; its modelling capabilities would be central to performing the more complex operations. A secondary consequence of such exploration may be to expose the limitations of the EIS, which in turn may lead to further refinement and modification of the EIS.

If dissonance is above the threshold range, executives may be motivated to reject or substitute their mental model. They may also lose faith in the EIS and reject it. Avoiding rejection of the EIS would be critical, especially for the continuation of prototyping. It would also be critical in a crisis.

In this mode executives are more likely to reject or replace their mental models than to reject the EIS. This is a result of the nature of this mode, in that a mental model has not been constructed prior to the interaction with the EIS or that, if a mental model was formed prior to the interaction, conviction in it is weak and, thus, receptiveness toward the EIS is relatively high.

## Confirmatory mode

The objective of executives in the confirmatory mode would be to verify whether the data provided by the EIS match the expectations based on their mental model. Executives in this mode are seeking to confirm their understanding of the state of the organization, the changes in the state and the reasons for the same. They want to make decisions and act. The confirmatory mode also provides an opportunity for executives to confirm, expand or reconstruct their mental model(s) depending on the dissonance level. However, there is also a high probability that executives will choose to retain the presently held mental model(s) and to reject the EIS's data depending on the degree of conviction in their mental model(s). Thus, this mode tends to be characterized by the level of conviction in the mental model(s) being higher than that of the exploratory mode. In this mode executives can be said to be 'attributing' (Ramaprasad, 1987) their mental models to the data. Or, stated another way, the executives are assimilating the data with their mental model(s).

Executives routinely using an EIS for planning, controlling and decision making can generally be said to be in the confirmatory mode. When dissonance is below the threshold range in the confirmatory mode, executives can be expected to proceed to making decisions and acting upon the data presented by the EIS. They may be expected to 'accept' the picture of the organization portrayed by the EIS.

If the dissonance is within the threshold range it could be expected to lead a more detailed exploration of the data to further confirm the mental model. Further exploration of the data would be a result of positive reinforcement, the same as in the exploratory mode.

If the dissonance is above the threshold range, the probability of executives 'rejecting' the picture of the organization portrayed by the EIS would be high. Unless there are compelling reasons, such as a crisis or a long successful history, it is more likely for the EIS to be rejected than for executives' mental models to be rejected.

## EXAMPLES

## Example 1: A matter of definition

The company had introduced a new product (let us call it X) and, naturally, the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) was very concerned about how X was going to be received in the marketplace. The marketing executive in charge of X had provided reports to the CEO that X was doing fairly well and was beginning to penetrate what could evolve to be a large market. The CEO requested that the EIS development team track the performance of X on many specific attributes, including customer base, order volume and dollar value of orders.

The EIS team began the development project by conducting extensive interviews with those responsible for X's technical design, information system support, marketing and management. On the basis of these discussions, the team created a prototype of the EIS for tracking X and demonstrated it to the CEO, Executive Vice President of Marketing and the Senior Vice President (who was also the executive sponsor of the EIS in the company). The three suggested some minor improvements but, on the whole, were very pleased with the prototype. The prototype was later enhanced based on the suggestions made by the executives and a final review was conducted with sample data.

As the EIS team proceeded with the development, they found that several EIS-generated reports about X's performance were not as favourable as those the CEO was receiving from X's marketing executive because of the amount of 'soft' data manually included by X's marketing executive but not included in any database. The team attempted to reconcile the two reports, but found it impossible because of the subjective processing of the 'soft' data. Based on the team members' diverse backgrounds in EIS, accounting and finance, the team came up with a final version of the EIS for X. Although the EIS reports did not support the CEO's mental model of the performance of X, the finished EIS for X was presented. During the demonstration, the CEO remarked that 'these numbers look quite a bit different'. The team leader explained, 'These revenue figures agree with what is being reported in the database. These figures, however, differ from the marketing reports you've been receiving for many reasons, the major one being the manual inclusion of sales thought to be generated by X'. After a detailed demonstration, the CEO warmly thanked the team leader.

Immediately after the team leader left the CEO's office, the CEO called X's marketing executive and said, 'Take a look at these EIS numbers of X. Your charts show increasing sales from X, increasing customer base, etc., and the EIS is showing a little different picture'. The CEO was concerned and asked that the marketing executive review his information.

Subsequently, as the EIS team developed the application further, they worked with the marketing department to more accurately identify, validate and capture the 'soft' sources of sales from X. This enhancement provided some leeway for sales not originally included in the database source. There were many grey areas of measurements that staff of the marketing department were interpreting to their advantage; the EIS team was interpreting the same measures more conservatively. In the end, rather than trying to resolve all the differences in measurement, the EIS team gave the users the option of generating reports with different assumptions and at different levels of detail.

On the whole it was interesting that the CEO, operating in a confirmatory mode, when confronted with EIS reports that contradicted his existing mental model did not reject the EIS but was receptive to the team leader's explanation within the framework of the CEO's current mental model. The EIS team leader felt that the CEO's 'mild' response may have been because during its year-and-a-half lifetime the EIS had been very accurate, and the CEO had faith in the EIS team's diligence in design and development of the applications. Had the EIS's reputation been the opposite, the outcome may have been quite different.

## Example 2: Fact or fiction

Often EIS prototypes are built with fictitious numbers. Novice users may concentrate on the numbers themselves, as if they were real, and not on the functional and logical aspects of the prototype. Considerable dissonance can be induced in a user who cannot distinguish between the fictional and the factual aspects of a prototype, and the consequences of such misperception can be significant.

Once, the EIS team leader was demonstrating a prototype to one of the company's Senior Vice Presidents (not the executive sponsor of the EIS mentioned in Example 1), who reported to the CEO. For demonstration purposes, some numbers used in the prototype had been extracted from a test database and had not been verified; and some values had been simply assumed. The Senior Vice President (VP) immediately concentrated on the numbers. He went to a particular line item and started to chuckle quietly and said, 'This number is wrong, it's way off'. He was almost ready to discount the EIS completely at this point – the picture it was portraying did not fit his mental model; it was causing too much dissonance.

The Senior VP had not been the first to jump on the EIS bandwagon. Not being very computer literate, he had wanted to wait and see if the EIS was going to be valuable to him. He was a very outgoing person who had worked his way up through the ranks and was very successful at performing his marketing responsibilities.

In viewing the prototype the Senior VP was operating in a confirmatory mode rather than an exploratory mode (as perhaps an executive more sympathetic to the EIS may have operated). It took the EIS team leader a while to get the Senior VP back on track, to reduce the latter's level of dissonance and to get him to explore the EIS. The leader explained that the numbers were not real. Once real numbers were put in, he was won over. The EIS really made sense to the Senior VP and to this day he uses it quite often.

## Example 3: Confirming the suspicion

This incident took place around the time the recession hit the Midwest in general and the company in particular. A Vice President, who was a strong support of the EIS, would call the EIS team leader often to discuss enhancements or discrepancies in the EIS. One day the VP called and insisted to the EIS team leader: 'There's something wrong with your numbers. There's definitely something wrong'. The EIS team leader said, 'Hang on, let me pull them up on my screen'. When the EIS team leader saw that some of the key figures were off by an order of magnitude, he said, 'You're right, these numbers do look unusually low', and he promised to verify the numbers. The VP ended by saying: 'If these numbers are correct, we'll need to review these numbers in detail and take the appropriate action'. The VP did not appear to have any doubt that the numbers were wrong.

The EIS team leader checked and found that the numbers were in fact correct – the company was feeling the impact of the recession, perhaps combined with the effects of a bad week. The verification required a lot of research, almost two full days of effort by the team collecting all the details, compiling them into summary reports, and comparing them with the previous year's data. The team leader reported the VP's call and the subsequent findings to the Senior VP (the executive sponsor).

The sharp drop caused considerable dissonance in the VP's mind. He may have come close to but did not actually reject the numbers and the EIS. But when the numbers proved to be correct he realigned his mental model of the situation (as did the other senior executives) and sought an explanation. Perhaps the VP and the other senior executives had a sneaking suspicion that the situation was bad but did not know how bad it was. The suspicion may have cushioned the magnitude of the dissonance and therefore led not to a rejection of the EIS but to further exploration of the data and a search for an explanation for the drop. In the end the incident probably reinforced the integrity of the EIS.

## MEASUREMENT

The purpose of the above model is explanatory. It is to help the designer understand the dynamics of an executive's interaction with an EIS. By classifying the state of the executive into one of the six categories, the designers will be able to frame the issues arising out of a particular interaction in a meaningful manner.

The ability to quickly obtain insights into the dynamics is particularly important in prototyping, the most frequently used method of EIS design. The short periods between successive prototypes and the close interaction between the designer and the executive provide an opportunity for (a) repeated measurement and validation and (b) a cumulation of errors and frustration, if the dynamics of the executive's interaction with the EIS is not well understood.

To use the model proposed in this paper, one has to be able to determine whether the executive is: (a) in an exploratory or confirmatory mode and (b) below, within or above the threshold level of dissonance. Such measurement will help the EIS designers, developers and implementors to understand the dynamics of the executive's interaction with an EIS. Both can be determined, albeit subjectively, by observing and probing the executive's behaviour.

It would be ideal if the designer could administer a questionnaire or conduct a structured interview at each ration of the prototyping cycle, or periodically after the system has been implemented. But the characteristics of the subjects would almost definitely preclude such a possibility. Senior executives (the users of EIS) are generally very busy, impatient and unreceptive to repeated measurement with questionnaires and interviews.

Thus the designer will be forced to rely on less obtrusive measures. Such measures may be based on: (a) the spontaneous responses of the executive, (b) the responses the designer can elicit by probing the executive, (c) observation of the executive and (d) tracking the keystrokes of the executive. From such data the designer will have to infer the executive's mode and level of dissonance.

The data on spontaneous responses, elicited responses and observations of the executive will obviously be very subjective. But the reliability of the judgments based on the three types of data can be enhanced by analysing them systematically. The three types of data are products of three different methods of measurement. If more than one person is involved in the measurement (say the executive sponsor and the operating sponsor), then one will have simultaneous multiple measurements. And, finally, over many cycles, one will also have repeated measurements. In other words, one has repeated multiple measures by multiple methods. Although the reliability of any one of the above measures may be low, by systematically combining the many measures one would be able to get indicators of the mode and the dissonance level at which an executive is operating.

In observing executives one can draw upon the literature on cognitive dissonance to interpret their behaviour. For example, if they spend too little time on a task it is highly probable that their dissonance level is either below or above the threshold. More time spent on the same task as a result of changes in the EIS may be an indication of the movement of the dissonance into the threshold range.

Tracking keystrokes is the fourth type of measurement. From keystrokes one can determine whether an executive is traversing familiar territory or exploring new territory. This method will help assess the relative emphasis on exploration and confirmation. It will also help determine (a) what new areas executives are exploring, (b) what areas they have stopped exploring and (c) what areas they have explored have been incorporated into the confirmation routine.

The objective of the measurement, it should be remembered, is not to obtain a precise numerical value of the level of dissonance. It is to be able to categorize the state of the executive at any one point in time into one of the six cells in the model. Each category then evokes a different framework for interpreting the executive–EIS interaction.

## CONCLUSION

Cognitive dissonance is double-edged. On the one hand, it provides the creative tension necessary for the iterative development of the mental models and the EIS. On the other hand, it can also lead to the rejection of the EIS depending on the mode (exploratory or confirmatory) and the level of convictions towards one's mental model(s). Managing the cognitive dissonance by managing expectations and providing explanations such that the dissonance is within the threshold range of the executive will be important for a successful and continuing design, development, implementation and operation of an EIS.

Mental models and EISs are not monolithic. However, they might be concatenated. Parts of each may be better developed than others. There may be a better fit between some parts of mental models and EIS than other parts. Thus, different aspects of an EIS may cause different levels of dissonance.

In the same vein, executives may flip back and forth between exploratory and confirmatory modes. They may adopt an exploratory mode for some aspects of the EIS and a confirmatory mode for others. The mode they adopt may also be a function of their traits and skills and the conditions under which they are operating.

Thus, there may not be a single dominating effect of cognitive dissonance due to executive-EIS interaction. The cumulation of effects over (a) different components of the mental models, (b) different components of the EIS, (c) different executives and (d) time will determine the trajectory of the interaction. It is this cumulation of effects that an EIS manager has to manage.

An understanding of the cognitive dissonance of executives under different modes of operation is important to prevent rejection of the EIS at one extreme and unquestioning acceptance at the other extreme. It is important for the EIS manager to ensure that executives continue to operate under both exploratory and confirmatory modes, simultaneously as well as switching between them as necessary. Continuous learning engendered by cognitive dissonance within the threshold range, whether in the exploratory mode or the confirmatory mode, is essential for sustaining the symbiotic relationship between the executive and the EIS.

Cognitive dissonance can also be managed by providing executives with easy access to pinpoint the source of and the reasons for the discrepancy between their expectations based on the mental model and reality. Drill-down capabilities, colour coding and textual explanations on screens are examples of such facilities. If a screen does not 'look right', the executive's dissonance will be inversely related to how easily they can determine why it does not 'look right'.

Cognitive dissonance needs to be given greater recognition and attention in the development of EIS. The developer has to be sensitive to the reactions of the user. Controlling the level of dissonance within certain limits would allow opportunities for the creation, confirmation, expansion or reconstruction of mental models and thus, through enhanced mental models, enables better decision making of the executive. This better decision making results in increased effectiveness.
