# Evaluating organizational efficiency resulting from information technology investment: an application of data envelopment analysis

M. A. Mahmood

Professor of Information Systems, University of Texas, El Paso, El Paso, Texas 79968-0544, USA

Abstract. Even during this recessionary era, information technology (IT) expenditure in most organizations continues to grow at a high rate. Because of this increased IT expenditure, more and more senior managers are demanding that IT play a greater role in determining their firm's success by helping them increase organizational efficiencies and perhaps even achieve competitive advantage. The existing information systems literature on IT investment and organizational strategic performance, however, provides very little help to senior managers in making before-the-fact IT investment decisions. The present research study puts forth some 'hard' evidence relating IT investment to organizational strategic and economic performance by using data envelopment analysis (DEA). Eight IT investment measures were used as inputs and 10 organizational strategic and economic performance ratios were used as outputs for the DEA model. The results indicate that two-thirds of the organizations in this research study are deemed efficient by DEA. A clear distinction exists between the efficient group and the inefficient group in terms of IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance. The firms in the efficient group had a much higher return on their information technology investment than the inefficient group. In addition, the DEA results pinpoint the inefficient inputs and deficient outputs for an inefficient firm allowing a senior manager to take corrective actions to compensate for the situation.

Keywords: data envelopment analysis, information technology investment, organizational strategic performance.

## INTRODUCTION

'More pressure. More money. More business focus. The new reality is unmistakable: Information Technology has become a much higher-stakes game' (Sullivan-Trainor & Maglitta, 1990, p. 12).

Even during this recessionary era, information technology (IT) expenditure in most organizations continues to grow at a high rate (53% of the information systems managers state that their budgets have increased, on an average, by over 17%; only 15% state that their budges have actually decreased; Computerworld, 1990).

This increased IT expenditure, however, comes with a string attached. More and more senior managers (referred to herein as strategic managers) are expecting IT to play a greater role in determining their firm's success. They are demanding that IT investment help them increase their organizational efficiencies and achieve competitive advantage. As a result 'IS managers are feeling more top managers breathing down their necks' (Sullivan-Trainor & Maglitta, 1990, p. 12).

In order to make informed before-the-fact investment decisions, the critical problem that confronts strategic managers is how to determine the contribution of IT investment towards organizational strategic and economic performance. Unfortunately, not much progress has been made in the area. Many chief executives are 'less than completely satisfied with the methods used in their organizations for assessing IT performance' (Wilson, 1988, p. 3). Even the information systems (IS) managers are not very confident about the situation. More than half the respondents among those IS managers who implemented programmes to measure the effectiveness of IT said their 'programs [are] only somewhat successful' (Sullivan-Trainor & Maglitta, 1990). Needless to say, most of the decisions, so far, are based on faith. Even though many strategic managers still believe in the idea of using IT to get ahead of their competitors and are still willing to take risks searching for potential and sometimes elusive competitive advantage ('during the last 12 months, two-thirds of the respondents (IS managers) installed information systems with the express purpose of providing competitive advantage'; Sullivan-Trainor & Maglitta, 1990), unable to identify clear returns on their earlier investments, many chief executives 'have clamped down, . . .' (Kutler, 1988, p. 5). They are becoming more and more reluctant to invest money in IT based on faith alone.

Even though many of the spectacular successes obtained through IT investment are readily documented after the fact (e.g. American Airlines, American Express, Citibank, Dun and Bradstreet and Federal Express), there is a critical shortage of research studies to help strategic managers measure the contribution of an IT investment to organizational performance before, not after, the IT investment decision is made (Stabell & Foresund, 1982; Jonscher, 1983; Strassman, 1985; Turner, 1985; Mahmood & Mann, 1991). Clearly there is a need for before-the-fact research studies to evaluate the actual performance of IT in organizations.

The present research study attempts to help strategic managers make these before-the-fact IT investment decisions by relating IT investment to organizational strategic and economic performance through the use of data envelopment analysis (DEA). More specifically, the present research seeks to identify whether IT investment measures and organizational performance measures can be used to identify the relative efficiency of an organization. The next section reviews the relevant literature on the applications of DEA to IT investment and organizational performance decisions (it is realized that most of the studies included in this review of previous research do not deal with the impact of IT investment on organizational strategic performance per se; for a further review of IT investment and organizational performance literature, see

Alper & Kim, 1990; Barua et al., 1991; Banker et al., 1993). This is followed by the discussion of the methodology used for the research study. The subsequent section presents the research findings and discusses the results. The paper concludes by outlining some limitations of the study.

## PREVIOUS RESEARCH

A number of research studies relating to the application of DEA to measure the effect of IT on organizational performance were found in the information systems literature. These are mostly empirical studies dealing with cost–benefit analyses and field surveys. They are found to be representative in terms of variable definitions and operationalizations, measuring methodologies and findings, and as such they are discussed in what follows.

Project managers use project size as a means to maximize software productivity. Unfortunately, research studies in this area are not very conclusive. Some say economies of scale exist; others say diseconomies of scale exist. Banker & Kemerer (1989) have used the DEA technique to identify the most productive scale size for a project. They utilized professional work hours as the input and the size of the project (in terms of lines of code) as the output to determine the relative efficiency of eight different previously published data sets. They concluded that the local economies or diseconomies of scale depend on the size of the projects.

Chismar & Kriebel (1985) used DEA to measure the relative efficiency of 19 firms. Two output measures (total sales and return on investment) and five input measures (non-production labour and capital, production labour and capital and IT expenditures) were used to measure the effect of IT on organizational performance. The results indicate that 9 of the 19 firms were very efficient (as indicated by a performance rating of 1), with the remaining firms having performance ratings between 0.867 and 0.997. There was, however, no clear relationship between the level of IT expenditure and performance.

Elam et al. (1984) used DEA to assess the relative efficiency of 10 data centres. The data centres were treated as production centres. They utilized information satisfaction as the output measure and IT investment, technology level, IS employee satisfaction and performance and task complexity as the input measures. They found a positive relationship between the data centres' relative efficiency and the input measures.

Software productivity is barely able to keep up with hardware productivity. Software project managers are naturally interested in increasing the productivity of software programmers. Kemerer (1987) first identified factors that are relevant to measuring software productivity. The author then used the DEA technique to measure the relative efficiency of 15 large software development projects. The DEA results, according to the author, help software managers efficiently utilize resources available to maximize software productivity.

Kauffman & Kriebel (1988) applied the DEA technique to an automated teller machine (ATM) network to assess the economic performance of different ATMs in the network. The ATMs were rated as efficient or inefficient by using working capital and maintenance trips as inputs and ATM interchange transactions, deposit contribution and labour contribution as outputs. They further showed how DEA results can also be used to design policies to improve the operating efficiency of ATMs.

Sherman & Gold (1985) used DEA to measure and evaluate the operating efficiency of 14 bank branches. The output measures used in the study were the number of transactions in four transaction categories from the most resource-consuming type to the least resource-consuming type. For example, the most resource-consuming transaction type included loan applications, new passbook loans and life insurance sales. The input measures included labour (full-time equivalent members of staff per branch), office space [rent paid for each branch is used as a surrogate for office space since the space used by each branch (in square feet) is not available] and supply costs (total costs of supplies used). The DEA results provided insights into the bank branches' performance by identifying inefficient branches.

Stabell & Foresund (1983) used DEA to relate the relative efficiency of 82 large Norwegian firms to their information technology use. As measures of IT use (inputs), they utilized the number of non-production employees and the ratio or non-production employees to total employees (they call this 'administrative intensity'), total expenditures on IT, the ratio of IT expenditures to sales, number of different applications, total number of terminals and number of terminals per 100 non-production employees. They used the sum of total labour costs and net profits as the output measures for a firm. Their results show that the 'administrative intensity' and the relative IT expenditures are related to a firm's relative efficiency. This efficiency is, however, not related to the firm's size or any absolute measure of its IT use.

To summarize, previous research studies in this area have made some contributions toward verifying a relationship between IT investment and organizational performance. However, they have stopped short of investigating whether certain IT and organizational strategic and economic performance measures are useful in measuring the overall efficiency of an organization. In addition, they did not show how the efficiency of an organization can be improved by identifying its inefficient inputs and deficient outputs. The present research tries to rectify these shortcomings by putting forth some 'hard' evidence relating IT investment to organizational strategic and economic performance.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Organizational efficiency has commanded considerable attention since the inception of organizations in our society. This interest has generated multiple models, multiple dependent variables and multiple independent variables to measure this efficiency. Unfortunately, these have obfuscated a clear definition of organizational efficiency and, as a result, no singular, parsimonious models or theory has emerged (Goodman et al., 1983). In the next few sections, the input and output measures used in this study and the model used to determine organizational efficiency are delineated. Rationales for choosing these specific measures and the model are also discussed.

## Organizational strategic and economic performance measures

To measure an organization's excellence, Peters & Waterman (1982) and Woo & Willard (1983) urged for the use of profitability measures that entail return on sales, return on investment and growth in revenue. Chakravarthy (1986) also recommended the utilization of cash flow to investment as a profitability measure. He argued, however, that such conventional profitability criteria, by themselves, are inadequate to determine an organization's strategic performance. As such he recommended that, in addition to the profitability measures, the criteria should include (a) an organization's ability to raise slack resources, (b) the wise use of these resources and (c) productivity. Sales revenue by total asset is a measure of an organization's capital productivity. Sales revenue by employee is a crude measure of its productivity.

An organization's market to book value and its debt over equity are indications of its ability to raise capital resources (Bourgeois, 1981). Its working capital to sales and dividend payout ratios, at the same time, should provide an indication as to its ability to invest such resources wisely for future gain (Bourgeois, 1981).

Based on the aforementioned studies, the present research uses 10 direct ratios to measure organizational strategic and economic performance: return on sales (ROS), growth in revenue (GINR), sales by total assets (SBYTA), dividend payout ratio (DPAYR), sales by employee (SBYE), total debt to equity (TDTOE), working capital by net sales (WCBYS), return on investment (ROI), market value to book value (MVTOBV) and cash flow to investment (CFTOI).

## Organizational IT investment measures

To measure an organization's IT investment, the present research study also utilizes eight ratios: IT budget (ITBUD) for the organization, IT budget as percentage of the organization's revenue (ITBUDBYREV), total value of the organization's overall IT investment (ITVAL), total value of the organization's IT investment as a percentage of its revenue (ITVALBYREV), percentage of IT budget spent on IT staff (ITBUDBYSTA), percentage of IT budget spent on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA), total number of PCs and terminals in the organization (TPCS) and number of PCs and terminals as percentage of total employees (PCSEMP).

The first two ratios, obviously, answer questions such as how much is an organization spending on information technology and how does this expenditure measure relative to the organization's revenue. The next two measures reveal the currency of its information technology. The organization's relative investment in training IT staff is provided by the staff training criterion. The last two measures indicate the extent to which the organization has made IT available to its end-users.

These measures are used in the present research mainly based on the results of studies conducted by Computerworld (1988). Their conclusions are also supported by research studies conducted by Benjamin (1982) and by Davis & Olson (1985). Benjamin (1982) predicted that by 1990 end-user computing expenditures would exceed 33% of total IT investment. By 1989 over 50% of the organizations had end-user tools (Reed, 1989). Most end-users use PCs and terminals. It is, therefore, imperative that total IT investment include investments in PCs and terminals.

It is commonly recognized that some end-users will be able to perform mundane programming tasks. However, for difficult application most will need expert assistance from end-user support specialists (Davis & Olson, 1985). To maintain their knowledge of current developments in information technology, these specialists must be trained on an ongoing basis, and this training involves cost. Total IT investment should also, therefore, include percentages of IT budget spent on IT staff and their training.

IT obviously includes hardware, software and communication technology. Investment in these items should, therefore, be included as a part of total IT investment.

## Measurement model selection

Once the measurement criteria are selected, one must choose a model to measure organizational efficiency. In the present study, DEA is used to measure the relative efficiency of different organizations included in the sample. The DEA approach works in the following way: it converts multiple input measures and multiple output measures into a single measure of relative efficiency (Charnes et al., 1978). This is done by identifying peer groups and by constructing an efficient frontier. Each organization is judged efficient or inefficient by comparing it against its peer group. The efficient organizations lie on the frontier and inefficient organizations lie within the frontier. DEA uses the following definition to determine the measure of relative efficiency for an organization (Charnes et al., 1985):

'100% relative efficiency is attained by any DMU [decision making unit] only when comparisons with other relevant DMUs do not provide evidence of inefficiency in the use of any input or output.' (p. 96).

A number of approaches, besides DEA, are available for determining relative efficiency. They all, however, suffer from one form of inadequacy or other. For example, conventional ratio analysis is dependent on the arbitrary assumption that one-ratio-at-a-time comparisons can determine relative efficiencies (Sherman, 1984). 'Extensions to index numbers involve a priori weighing schemes with accompanying assumptions of stability in the sizes of inputs and outputs utilized' (Charnes et al., 1985, p. 96). The regression approach requires explicit specification of functional relations between multiple inputs and multiple outputs. DEA bypasses these problems but not requiring this 'a priori choice of weights or explicit specification of functional relations' (Charnes & Cooper, 1985, p. 61). Sherman (1984) and Banker et al. (1986) have demonstrated the superiority of DEA over alternative methods.

In addition, DEA is an innovative and preferred approach for measuring organizational performance (Epstein & Henderson, 1989) for a number of reasons. First, it allows strategic managers to distinguish between efficient and inefficient organizations (Kauffman & Kriebel, 1988). Second, it allows strategic managers to pinpoint sources and amount of inefficiency for each inefficient organization. Third, DEA may be able to detect inefficiencies that may not be identifiable by alternative approaches (Epstein & Henderson, 1989) such as the regression approach or ratio analysis.

## The sample

Eighty-one firms out of a total of 100 reported by Computerworld in its 'Premier 100' list as the most effective technology users were used as the DMUs in the sample utilized for this research study (Computerworld, 1988). The remaining 19 organizations could not be used because their organizational strategic and economic performance data were not available in the DISCLOSURE database. The objective of the present research was to investigate whether these effective firms were also efficient.

The data on IT investment measures for the present research were collected from the Computerworld study. The data on organizational strategic and economic performance measures was obtained from the Compact DISCLOSURE database, which provides financial and operating information on approximately 12000 companies. Since the average for these ratios varied from industry to industry, they were first standardized before the actual data analyses were undertaken. Chakravarthy (1986) used this procedure to compensate for the inter-company variance. For ease of interpretation, however, results were presented in terms of original values instead of the standardized values (for example, see Tables 2–6 and Appendices A and B).

## RESULTS

Using DEA, data were analysed in two steps: first, the relative efficiency for each organization was obtained. Second, the sources and amount of inefficiency were investigated for each inefficient organization.

## Organizational efficiency

Based on the inputs (eight IT investment measures) and output (10 organizational performance measures), DEA gauged the relative efficiency of all organizations included in the sample. The results show that relative efficiencies for the 81 firms included in the sample ranged from 0.49 to 1.00. All organizations that rated 1.000 (an absolute standard) occupy positions on the production frontier and as such are described as efficient. They consume the least resources for their level of outputs. Table 1 shows the DEA relative efficiency results for all organizations included in the sample.

The results show that 54 (66.67% of the total) firms were deemed efficient by DEA as indicated by a performance rating of 1.000, while 27 firms (33.33% of the total) were categorized as technically inefficient with relative efficiency ratings below 1.000.

Once organizations were identified as efficient or inefficient, they were then investigated as to their level of IT investment and organizational performance. Table 2 provides a comparative analysis of efficient and inefficient organizations with regard to their IT investment level. The results show that, regardless of their size, all efficient firms invested less in information technology (as indicated by their lower means on different IT investment measures) than the inefficient firms. With the exception of percentage of IT budget spent on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA), the variability among the organization's IT investment was also relatively low (as indicated by their relatively lower standard deviations).

The next obvious step in the analysis was to investigate the efficient and inefficient groups of firms in terms of their organizational performance. Table 3 shows the results. As expected, the efficient firms outperformed the inefficient ones in all categories except in sales as a percentage of total assets (SBYTA). In other words, they had higher return on sales (ROS), growth in revenue (GINR), sales by employee (SBYE), working capital by sales (WCBYS), market value to book value (MVTOBV) and cash flow to investment (CFTOI). They paid higher dividends (DPAYR) and higher return on investment (ROI). They also had higher total debt to equity (TDTOE). The inefficient group outperformed the efficient group in only one category, namely sales by total assets (SBYTA).

With the exception of two ratios (total debt as a percentage of equity and market value as a percentage of book value), the variability among the firms in the efficient group (as shown by their higher standard deviation) was higher.

## Comparative analysis of most and least efficient firms

Next, the six least efficient and the six most efficient firms were defined and compared. The least efficient firms include Bell Atlantic Corp., Caterpillar, Inc., First Wachovia, McDonnell Douglas Co., Southwestern Bell Co. and the Travelers Corp. One thing must be made clear here: DEA does not rank order the firms' relative efficiency rating. Rather, it suggests the degree of inefficiency a firm is suffering from compared with other members in its efficiency reference set (includes firms which are judged to be comparable to the firm in question). The least efficient firms are, therefore, defined as those firms that performed least efficiently compared with other members in their efficiency reference set. For example, Bell-Atlantic Corporation, a member of the least efficient group, is about 51% efficient compared with other members in its reference set (which include Sara Lee Corporation, Burlington Industries, Shell-Oil Co., Penzoil Company, Texas Utilities Co., Amoco Corporation, Dun and Bradstreet Corp. and Minnesota Mining and M).

The most efficient firms include AT & T, General Signal Corporation, General Mills Inc., Penzoil Co., Sara Lee Corp. and Unocal Corp. These firms showed particularly good performance and frequently formed the efficiency reference set for inefficient firms (see Table 4); as such, following the guidelines suggested by Smith (1990), they were defined as most efficient. Table 4 shows the relative efficiency rating, efficiency reference set and total appearances in different efficiency reference sets for all member organizations in the sample. The relative efficiency rating column indicates how an organization has performed relative to other members in its efficiency reference set. The relative efficiency reference set column portrays the organization number of member organizations in the efficiency reference set of a firm. The total appearances column shows the total number of appearances a firm has made in different efficiency reference sets for different organizations.

Once the most and the least efficient groups were defined, the performances of their member organizations were investigated. Table 5 shows the mean and standard deviation of information technology investment measures for the two groups of organizations. The results indicate that most efficient firms, overall, spent less money on information technology. More specifically, they spent less on IT budget (ITBUD). Their overall IT investment value and total number of PCs and terminals in the organizations were relatively smaller. Their ratios of IT budget as a percentage of revenue (ITBUDBYREV), overall IT investment value as a percentage of revenue (ITVALBYREV), IT budget spent on IT staff (ITBUDBYSTA), IT budget spent on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA) and number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees (PCSEMP) were also relatively lower.

Similarly, Table 6 shows the mean and standard deviation of the two groups in terms of organizational performance measures. As expected, overall, the most efficient firms had a much higher performance in terms of return on sales (ROS), growth in revenue (GINR), sales by total assets (SBYTA), dividend payout ratio (DPAYR), sales by employee (SBYE), total debt to equity (TDTOE), working capital by net sales (CWBYS), return on investment (ROI), market value to book value (MVTBV), and cashflow to investment (CFTOI). However, performance variances among these organizations were also higher (as shown by their higher standard deviations), indicating that some organizations had performed better than others.

## Excess inputs based on DEA analysis

Once the inefficient organizations and their efficiency reference sets were identified, the next logical step was to investigate the magnitude of these inefficiencies. DEA provides additional insights into this matter. Appendix A shows the performance relative efficiency rating and inefficiency inputs (slack) for organizations in the sample. The column 2 result from the table indicates that Abbot Laboratories (No. 1), for example, will need to reduce its overall inputs by about 7.5% to become an efficient as its reference set consisting of organizations with identification numbers 51, 17, 43, 55, 68, 63 and 32. Compared with the composite of its reference set organizations, it used excess inputs for IT budget (ITBUD), IT budget as a percentage of revenue (ITBUDBYREV), total value of the organization's overall IT investment (ITVAL), total value of the IT investment as a percentage of its revenue (ITVALBYREV), percentage of IT budget spent on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA) and number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees (PCSEMP). More specifically, column 3 of the table indicates that the firm could reduce its IT budget by 24 millions, ITBUDBYREV by one-sixth of a per cent, ITVAL by 47.8 millions, ITVALBYREV by 1.7%, ITBUDBYTRA by 2.2%, and PCSEMP by 8.1% and still provide the current level of outputs.

## Deficient outputs based on DEA

Appendix B shows the deficient outputs (slack) for organizations in the sample. The column 3 result from the table indicates that, for example, American Airline (no. 6) will need to increase its overall outputs by about 16% to become as efficient as members in its efficiency set. More specifically, compared with the composite of its reference set organizations, it will have to increase its return on sales (ROS) by about 2.2%, sales by employee (SBYE) by \$229 945, total debt to equity (DBYE) by 90.3%, working capital by net sales (WCBYS) by 5.2%, return on investment (ROI) by 1.0%, market value to book value (MVTOBV) by 99.4% and cash flow to investment (CFTOI) by 0.6%. The adjustments suggested in Appendices A and B, if achieved, will make the inefficient firms efficient (Charnes et al., 1985).

## DISCUSSION

The DEA results of the present research study indicate that, among the most effective users of information technology, two-thirds of the organizations are also efficient. The results also indicate that a clear distinction exists between the efficient and inefficient groups as well as the most efficient and the most inefficient groups in terms of the IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance. Given the presence of existing difficulty in quantifying the contribution of an IT investment to organizational performance before, not after, the IT investment decision is made, the results are very encouraging.

They also had higher variance among different performance measures, indicating that some of the firms in this group performed better than others. Interestingly, even though the inefficient firms had a higher sales volume relative to their total assets, their sales per employee and return on sales were lower than their counterparts in the efficient group (a clear sign of inefficiency indeed!).

The results also indicate that firms with superior organizational strategic and economic performance did not necessarily spend a lot of money on IT to achieve this performance. As a matter of fact, the inefficient firms outspent the efficient firms on all measures. Overall, the variance among the inefficient firms' spending in information technology was also higher, indicating that some of the inefficient firms spent much more than others within the same inefficient group.

Similarly, the firms in the most efficient group, without any exception, outperformed the most inefficient group on all strategic and economic performance measures. These firms also spent less on information technology that their counterparts in the most inefficient group. There is a reason for this apparent paradox. The author contends that organizational strategic and economic benefits from information technology investment are derived more by using the technology efficiently than by just investing more in the technology. Of course, one has to invest before one can use the technology and achieve superior organizational strategic and economic benefits. The author believes that the most efficient firms, in the past, have invested sufficiently in IT allowing them to have a good IT infrastructure, which in turn allowed them to use the technology more efficiently.

The author also agrees with Mahmood & Mann (1991) when they stated that an appropriate level of IT investment must be accompanied by an organizational strategic plan (which describes its IT investment goals, objectives and strategies). What is more important here is to determine 'how you spend what you spend'.

Other factors such as economic and competitive environments, managerial astuteness, firm's history, its IT investment programmes and quality of its present information technology may positively or negatively contribute to organizational strategic and economic performance. These factors are beyond the scope of the present research and, as such, they will not be discussed further. Strategic managers must, however, take these factors into consideration in making an IT investment decision.

To summarize, the DEA results indicate that, within the most effective users of information technology, two-thirds of the firms are either efficient or most efficient and are achieving higher organizational strategic and economic performance than their counterparts in the inefficient and most inefficient groups. This coincides with the findings of Nolan, Norton, and Co. (1985) who have empirically shown that leading-edge firms to the use of IT are more profitable than laggards.

Some of the findings were not expected. For example, it is not quite known why the most efficient firms (see Table 3) had, relative to the efficient firms (see Table 2), higher value for their overall IT investment (ITVAL) and higher value for their total number of PCs in the organization (PCs). Our conjecture is that the most efficient firms have a good built-in IT infrastructure. This increased the value of their overall IT investment and total number of PCs, relative to the efficient firms. In other words, as the firm becomes more and more efficient, its IT infrastructure gets better and better and it is able to use IT more and more efficiently.

It was also not expected to find that most inefficient firms (see Table 5) outspent the inefficient firms (see Table 2) in all but one category [percentage of IT budget spent on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA)]. It is, however, quite possible that over the years most inefficient firms fell badly behind and are now trying to catch up with the efficient ones. These firm's IT expenditures, consequently, were considerably higher — much of which was catch-up expense.

This result also seems to indicate that as the firms become more and more inefficient they spend less and less on the training of their IT staff and more and more on other IT expenditures. Obviously, state-of-the-art technologies will be of no use unless these firms have properly trained employees to utilize and take advantage of them. Higher IT expenditures combined with lower productivity have made these firms most inefficient.

One of the most significant aspect of DEA results is that it takes the guesswork out of a firm's performance and tells a strategic manager how exactly the results at the firm level can be used to improve the performance efficiency of the firm. As such, DEA results provide a focus for management review and internal audits of the inefficient firms to determine the extent to which the inputs level can be reduced or the outputs level can be increased.

The DEA results indicate that, among the 27 inefficient firms, 78% (21 out of a total of 27) spent more money on IT budget (ITBUD), 63% spent more on IT budget as a percentage of revenue (ITBUDBYREV), 85% spent more on total value of its overall IT investment (ITVAL), 78% spent more on IT budget as a percentage of its revenue (ITVALBYREV), 30% spent more on percentage of IT budget expended on IT staff (ITBUDBYSTA), 52% spent more on percentage of IT budget expended on training IT staff (ITBUDBYTRA), 81% spent more on total number of PCs and terminals in the organization (PCs) and 48% spent more on number of PCs and terminals as percentage of total employees (PCSEMP) than required for achieving their level of organizational performance. As stated earlier, DEA specifies exactly by how much an organization can reduce its level of input on a certain measure and still achieve the same level of output performance.

The DEA results also indicate that 59% of the inefficient firms were deficient in return on sales (ROS), 48% in growth in revenue (GINR), 22% in sales by total assets (SBYTA), 33% in dividend payout ratio (DPAYR), 44% in sales by employee (SBYE), 78% in total debt to equity (TDTOE), 41% in working capital by sales (WCBYS), 78% in return on investment (ROI), 63% in market value to book value (MVTOBV) and 56% in cash flow to investment (CFTOI). The inefficient firms will need to reduce these deficiencies before they can become efficient.

## CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH ISSUES

The results of this research have practical implications for understanding the role of information technology investment on organizational performance. It is the author's contention that, since strategic managers are held accountable for IT investment success, understanding what it is and being able to evaluate it (before the investment decision is made) are crucial to their personal as well as organizational success.

The results indicate that two-thirds of the organizations used for this research study are deemed efficient by DEA. A clear distinction exists between the groups of efficient and inefficient firms in terms of their level of IT investment and their organizational strategic and economic performance. The firms in the efficient group had a much higher return on their information technology investment than those in the inefficient group. In addition, the DEA results pinpoint the inefficient inputs and deficient outputs for an inefficient firm, thereby allowing a strategic manager to take some corrective actions to compensate for the situation.

The present research study had put forth some 'hard' evidence relating IT investment to organizational strategic and economic performance. Future research studies should further explore this issue in a non-threatening environment in which faults, problems and weaknesses with these studies are not overemphasized. The focus should be on providing further before-the-fact 'hard' evidence and guidelines (even when they are not 'perfect') for strategic managers to assess IT investment with respect to its impact on the organization. Tempting as they may be, after-the-fact case-based documenting the spectacular successes should be de-emphasized [the information systems literature is replete with these case studies; may the author even suggest a moratorium on these studies so that before-the-fact studies (which are so badly needed in the area) can catch up with them]. The future studies should also investigate the impact of information technology on the community, society and even the environment. The success of all these, in our opinion, will determine whether IT will be in the forefront in helping organizations achieve superior strategic and economic performance and perhaps even competitive advantage or will go back to the back room for mundane data processing.

Finally, a few words about the limitations of the research study must be stated here. First, the research is based, in part, on the data provided by Computerworld in a non-random sample of organizations. Second, some of these data were estimated by Computerworld or its sources. Third, some of the organizational performance measures may have been influenced by factors not related to IT investment. These need to be factored out before more specific relationships can be identified. Fourth, the present research study is a cross-sectional study and as such must be interpreted carefully. A subsequent research will embark upon a longitudinal study. In all fairness, given the present constraints in obtaining IT investment data (most organizations are either not collecting this type of data or are not willing to disclose them), the present study has made a positive contribution towards furthering research in the area.

## Biography

Mo Adam Mahmood is Professor of Information Systems at the University of Texas, El Paso. He received his PhD in Management Information Systems from Texas Technical University and MBA from California State University. His current research interests include information technology use for increasing organizational strategic and economic performance as well as national and international competitiveness. Dr Mahmood has published in MIS Quarterly, Decisions Sciences, Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, INFOR—Canadian Journal of Operation Research and

Information Processing, Information and Management, Journal of Computer-Based Instruction, Journal of Microcomputer Systems Management, Journal of Systems Management, Data Base, International Journal of Policy and Management and others. He had presented papers, served as both a reviewer and discussant, and chaired conferences, tracks and sessions for several regional, national and international conferences. He is currently either an associate editor, a member of the editorial review board or reviewer for a significant number of information systems journals.
