# Rapid adoption of information systems in an organization with poor management: the case of fund-holding in UK primary health care

J. A. A. Sillince & C. E. B. Frost

Sheffield University Management School, Sheffield, S10 1FL, UK

Abstract. Reorganization of the UK primary health care system to create an internal market for health services depends upon local family doctors (general practitioners) taking on budgetary responsibilities and purchasing services from hospitals. These budgets will be monitored by local committees. The success of the internal market is heavily dependent upon computerized management information systems. This paper investigates the introduction of information systems into an organization that is ill prepared for change.

Keywords: systems adoption, information system strategy, medical information management, GP systems.

## INTRODUCTION

Recent reforms in the UK National Health Service (NHS) have involved hospitals beginning to opt out of local health authority control and becoming self-governing trusts. Within the primary care sector of this system, general practitioners (GPs) will become consumers (DoH, 1991a–c), managing and controlling their budgets, and purchasing services from hospitals. This reform involves GPs becoming fund-holders. The first wave of budget fund-holding from April 1991 was implemented with an experimental group of 400 general practices which were chosen mainly for their familiarity with computer systems.

Earl (1989) has suggested that under ideal circumstances there are three aspects of information systems strategy. One aspect uses business goals and plans to clarify policy in a top-down manner. Top-down policy making uses analytical methods to deduce policy from goals. Another aspect builds policy evaluatively from knowledge of the current system in a bottom-up manner. This uses surveys and audits, and information from users and specialists. A third aspect makes use of information technology opportunities by means of experiments and product champions, in a creative, inside-out manner.

The contention of this paper is that two of the three aspects of strategy formulation towards management information systems, those of top-down and bottom-up policy formulation, were problematic in systems adoption for GP fund-holding in primary health care in the period 1991–92. Figure 1 shows criticisms of GP systems adoption as they emerged via top-down policy making and via bottom-up evaluation.

## CRITICISMS OF TOP-DOWN POLICY MAKING

Information management problems manifested themselves in top-down strategy in several ways:

Poor information on costs. Auditors sought costs, yet while some costs were easy to determine (e.g. prescriptions) others (e.g. hospital costs) were unavailable in an accurate form. Clinical information devalued. Management information (rather than clinical information) was considered to be the object of primary care computerization.

Data incompatibilities. Data could not flow easily around the system: the same information was defined in different ways at different parts of the system.

No data model. No data model existed for the primary health care system.

Poor information on health outcomes. In order to measure effectiveness of the primary health care system some outcome data were needed. Yet these were unavailable in an accurate form.

Poor information on health needs. There was no theory of health need against which to measure effectiveness of the primary care system.

## BOTTOM-UP EVALUATION

In bottom-up policy formulation, there were also some problems:

Not enough time. There was a lack of time for introduction of systems to GPs' practices. Not enough money. There was a lack of money for introduction of systems to GPs' practices. GPs not using computers. GPs tended to underuse the information systems once they had been installed.

## IT OPPORTUNITIES

There were several experiments, mainly of a technical nature. These were generally successful in providing information about the feasibility (e.g. electronic data interchange) or infeasibility of particular approaches. For example:

Electronic data interchange (EDI) was rapidly developed for procurement (the NHS aimed at 80% use of EDI by its suppliers by 1993) and for links between GPs and other parts of the health care system.

Electronic mail was developed for links between primary and secondary sectors of the health system.

Smart cards were developed for providing patients with access to their own health data, and for primary–secondary links.

Online databases were used for GPs and their patients.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE NEW SYSTEM

## The main actors

The actors and their roles are as follows:

GPs, besides their pre-reform medical responsibilities, must manage and control their budget, and purchase services from hospitals by means of one-off contracts or, for convenience, block contracts. These services comprise inpatient, outpatient and diagnostic services and exclude emergency and medical services. Because GPs purchase services from hospitals, they must make use of accurate information about hospital costs. Considering the health information system as a whole, GPs are the major data entry point. All patients may only register with one GP. Every patient has a unique NHS number. Patient registers are kept at each GP's practice. They accurately record all patients' non-clinical details (name, address, age, etc.) and the NHS number. They are sent to Family Health Service Authorities by GPs.

The Family Health Service Authority (FHSA) invoices the GP for drugs dispensed, monitors expenditure against budget, and draws up contracts. It reimburses the pharmacist for dispensed drugs. It provides indicative prescribing budgets for GPs, oversees medical audit (by the GP's peers), liaises with GPs to produce collaborative care plans on specific problem areas, and has responsibility for introducing information technology for GPs. In 1992 (rather late in the day from the point of view of GPs' acquiring IT skills to implement the 1991 reforms) the Department of Health (DoH) began a community information system project (CISP) to provide overall direction to IT in FHSAs.

The Registrar General aggregates patient registers at national level at the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys (OPCS) from data sent in from FHSAs and entered on a core master patient index.

The Audit Commission and accountancy firms act as independent financial auditors of GP budgets.

The Prescription Pricing Authority (PPA) operates the indicative prescribing scheme (IPS), which monitors prescribing and dispensing on a monthly basis and which was fully automated from its inception. It handles 400 million prescriptions annually (Prescription Pricing Authority, 1991). An important element in GPs' budgets is taken up by prescriptions — £2.5 billion in 1990–91 (Prescription Pricing Authority, 1991).

## GPS' INCOME

GPs get their funding according to two elements of their budgets, one positive and one negative. The positive element includes an amount per person on the GP's patient register. The method at present used to calculate payments to GPs over and above capitation is the Jarman index, which takes into account special needs of deprived areas that require a GP to undertake extra work. The positive element includes an element for items of service such as immunizations and cervical smears. Patient registers, as well as providing the starting point for any auditing activity, are a good means of avoiding errors in local population estimates that arise because of migration, and form the basis of the Registrar General's intercensal population estimates. The negative element represents a discouragement to overspend on the GP's budget. In theory, GPs can overspend by up to 5% in any one year (compensated by an underspend in the following year). A greater than 5% overspend will trigger an audit by the FHSA. If a greater than 5% overspend continues for 2 years then the GP may lose his right to make his own budget.

## PRESCRIPTION COSTS

Different prescription rates are one of the sources of cost variations between GPs. Besides the budgetary mechanism of control, the FHSA is responsible for counselling of GPs who overprescribe. Peer group pressure at the level of the FHSA (Peter et al., 1989) or the practice (Beardon et al., 1987) may produce positive results, as may more interchange of data between GPs and pharmacists (Neville et al., 1989). A study of US and UK doctors using computer-based information systems found that most doctors do not feel they are supplied with enough information about what their colleagues do and how their colleagues use resources, and that they want more information and would find it useful (Martin, 1987). Among aids to reducing prescription rates, GPs are most approving of a psychology service to enable GPs to wean some drug-dependent patients off drugs (Hamilton et al., 1990).

## REFERRAL RATES

Cost variability attributable to GPs also arises from different patient referral rates. Referral rates vary widely (Wilkin & Smith, 1987). Unlike prescription rates, which are being discouraged from being too high, there is no simple policy which says that referral rates should be lower. Exchange of information between GPs and District Health Authority (DHA) seems to have beneficial effects (Nicklin et al., 1991). GPs want feedback from consultants about the appropriateness of their referrals and what patients and their relatives have been told, and aggregate information on comparative referral rates. It might be possible for DHAs to identify where the primary care sector can deal with care more cost-effectively than can hospitals. DHAs would like to be able to identify those GPs who have higher referral rates than their colleagues. However, this could have adverse effects on the absence of a norm or criterion to establish one (Wilkin et al., 1989), since GPs might mistakenly infer from this that high referral rates are always bad.

## CRITICISMS OF GP SYSTEMS ADOPTION

## Criticisms of top-down policy making

## Poor information on costs

At present, control of costs is the main financial auditing aim, and medical audit is mainly aimed at checking for adverse effects of this preoccupation with costs. However, information systems do not exist for an accurate knowledge of hospital costs (House of Commons, 1991). Yet such information is important if GPs are to use the internal market most effectively (referring their patients to the most cost-efficient hospitals). Once such information is available, the opportunity will exist for both medical as well as financial audit trails.

## Clinical information devalued

It has been suggested that the new information systems indicate that ‘policy objectives appear to be subsumed by financial objectives’ (Morris, 1990). These criticisms have arisen as a response to concern shown by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) about resource and financial management in the NHS, and to the government’s claim that the main purpose of new information systems within the NHS is to ensure budgets are kept to. This concern expresses itself in the criticism that the DoH pays more attention to management information than to clinical information, whereas clinicians will only be won over when they see clinical benefits (Anon., 1990).

## Data incompatibilities

Various problems of incompatibility of data exist. These will tend to decline as systems proliferate and become more mature, and as sharing of data increases. Several systems enable GPs to supply data for subsequent sharing (e.g. Hargrave et al., 1988). In Scotland the General Practice Administrative Statistical System (GPASS) enables GPs to access data and to produce analyses and reports (Milne et al., 1991; Taylor et al., 1991). Such systems enable the data to be correlated and anonymized before onward transmission. Such data are then available for use for resource management, local performance monitoring and demographic and epidemiological analysis. However, such systems built around data sharing depend upon GPs creating clinical and other data in a comparable form. Clinical information about individual patients needs to flow freely between primary and secondary health care facilities. Such free flow is often hampered by incompatible data formats and codes.

## No data model

Although data modelling and data management for the secondary care sector were initiated in 1985, primary care data modelling began only as late as 1990 (Kay, 1990). There is a need for a data model for primary care, which unfortunately is still under active development within the NHS Information Management Group (IMG) at this time (February, 1993, almost 2 years since fund-holding began to be introduced).

## Poor information on health outcomes

All doctors must participate in medical audit according to Working for Patients (DoH, 1991a). It is important to remind ourselves here that this is not the same as financial audit. The NHS reforms have firmly endorsed the right of doctors to be reviewed only by their peers on clinical matters. This medical audit is undertaken by the FHSA. It would seem that unsolicited feedback does little to alter the behaviour of GPs (Hamley et al., 1981). It is desirable that the outcomes of primary care should be evaluated, although this is not a reasonable prospect in the foreseeable future. As yet data simply do not exist for this (Gillam, 1991). Relevant data include smoking rates as a response to health promotion activity, changes in immunization rates, cervical cytology coverage, reduced waiting times or reduced attendance rates for self-limiting illness, and patient satisfaction and understanding of GP-provided information.

## Poor information on medical needs

Patients' health needs vary from area to area. Unfortunately, information on health needs is even poorer than that on costs. Currently, needs are indicated by usage statistics derived from GPs' consultation records and from hospital episode data. The activity measures are compared with inputs to obtain performance indicators. But the large variation in utilization across districts, both in absolute terms and for different degrees of case complexity, suggests that existing measures of service use are poor indicators of need except perhaps in more straightforward specialties (George & Brazier, 1991). Thus, needs have been ignored (Birch & Maynard, 1986) and will continue to be ignored in the foreseeable future since the first priority of districts is to establish contracts. The Audit Commission in 1990 advised that 'due to the relative weakness of the information systems on which managers must make their decisions, the government is surely right to ask health authorities to proceed cautiously in introducing all but the simplest form of contract' (Audit Commission, 1990).

## Bottom-up evaluation

Bottom-up evaluation gave rise to criticisms that there was not enough time or money for systems adoption and that GPs were not using computers as much as was necessary.

## Not enough time

'Most of the problems arise, not from fundamental flaws in the contract, but from a lack of preparation due to the very short time scales allowed for its introduction' (Hayes, 1990). Indeed, the health minister specifically rejected the idea of pilot studies of IT support for fund-holding in primary care and was criticized in Parliament for his hastiness (Hansard, 1990a–c). Yet this pattern of rushed computer implementation as an unanticipated implication of cabinet decisions to reform institutions is not new. Computer errors caused innumerable problems for reforms of housing benefit and rents. And it is not so long since local government was threatened with a cash flow problem because its computer systems were not ready for change from rates to poll tax collection.

## Not enough money

Critics (including software developers involved in developing new GP budget management systems) say that not enough funds are being made available for the development of information systems. One problem is the DoH's insistence that it owns all health care data and therefore that no NHS personnel should have to pay for data. (The FHSA is the legal owner according to DoH legal advisers, but this is disputed by GPs' representatives, who claim that they own the data.) Such a policy by the DoH precludes software developers reducing costs by means of getting access to GPs' data of use to pharmaceutical companies.

## GPs not using computers

The change towards GP fund-holding depends upon GPs rapidly acquiring and using computer systems. In April 1989 a DoH survey found that 25% of GPs used a computer; by April 1990 this had risen to 50%, and by September 1991 this figure was 79% (Anon., 1991). However, a survey of 562 GPs in September 1991 (Goves & Davies, 1991) showed that only 37% used (38% intended to use) computers for patient registration, 22% (29%) for partial clinical records, 9% (17%) for complete clinical records, 19% (31%) for referral letters, 29% (36%) for recall of patients, 17% (28%) for audit, and 32% (37%) for repeat prescriptions. The survey warned that GPs resisted sharing data: only 49% of GPs let hospital doctors see their clinical notes, and only 70% wanted access to hospital clinical data on their patients (Goves & Davies, 1991). A smaller survey of all 91 GPs in East Surrey Health Authority a year later, in September 1992 (Shum, 1992), found that computerization of complete clinical records was still at only 23%; and half of GPs (49%) expressed difficulties because of lack of knowledge of computers. These statistics are worrying bearing in mind the fact that the DoH has published guidelines suggesting that computerization of complete clinical records is ‘essential’ (DoH, 1989). The statistics suggest that computer use and competence will lag behind their availability within GPs’ practices. Thus, many GPs were not in a good position to embark on fund-holding either in the first wave of 1991 or in 1992.

## IT opportunities

Several experiments that have been undertaken are of considerable interest to GPs. These experiments were useful in increasing the amount of information available about solutions. Also, there was a competitive supplier environment in which some 35 GP management information systems were developed and marketed, and which again increased the variety and experimentation in the primary care system.

One of the earliest experiments of the use of EDI was the linking of six GPs' practices in Devon in 1988 (Brown, 1989). A nationwide FHSA network called Healthlink was developed and operated by Racal. Pilots were run in the Oxford and East Anglian Regional Health Authorities, initially to supply GPs with up-to-date figures of hospital waiting lists for outpatient appointments and inpatient waiting lists. The feasibility of GPs using electronic mail to request tests for pathology laboratories was studied. Other schemes linking GPs to their hospitals included the Derbyshire FHSA network to improve the care of diabetic patients and electronic mail for all 118 GP practices in Sheffield. The use of EDI, and the advent of the 'smart card', which can carry personal and clinical data about the owner of the card, raised serious questions about the need to standardize GP-created data.

Some experimentation occurred with regard to use by GPs of online databases (e.g. Jones et al., 1992). Over 1000 medicine-relevant databases are now available to GPs through dial-up links. Intelligent gateways such as ISTEL'S Infosearch can drive such links. It should be routine for GPs to consult databases to find information such as opening times of laboratories, immunization and vaccination schemes, and specimen preparation requirements. An example of the way ahead is an information system for the provision and monitoring of immunization of children, which has existed for 20 years in computerized form. It is the Immunisation and Vaccination System, and it is part of the Standard Child Health Computer System (Morris, 1988). However, a survey of 562 Welsh GPs in 1991 found that only 24% had modems (Goves & Davies, 1991). Alternatives to online databases include CD-ROMs, which are expensive but quick and easy to use, and tailor-made decision support systems for primary care, such as the Oxford System of Medicine (Fox, 1990) or the Psion Organizer program (Corbett & Clark, 1991). It has been estimated that a complete knowledge base for primary care would require ten million medical facts (Gordon, 1991).

## EXPLANATIONS

What are the explanations for the difficulties in introducing primary health care systems? Figure 2 shows the strategic management aspects of policy formulation according to the tripartite Earl (1989) model. In terms of top-down policy development, several aspects could be influenced by those at the top of the NHS. Both management and strategy were undervalued, and there was much political uncertainty. In terms of aspects relevant to bottom-up fact finding and auditing, the dependence of systems suppliers on a start date (however infeasible) was a source of instability, since GP systems would not get built unless firm dates were forthcoming. Also, there was some concern from the grass roots about the confidentiality of patient data. All these aspects were problematical, as the following will show.

## Causes of problems arising from top-down policy making

## Management undervalued

The NHS has traditionally undervalued management, which has been synonymous with clerical roles: 25–40% of non-medical staff currently spend their time filling in forms (Bertrand, 1991). In general, the quality of management information in the NHS is poor (Kay, 1990). The ethos of the organization has been predominantly one that emphasizes the patient–doctor relationship; the model has been the independent-minded medical consultant. Pay for managers is poor, except for those in a handful of top ‘figurehead’ posts. The management layers of the organization have been unprepared for the massive changes introduced by the government during the 1980s and unable to collect, analyse and communicate the data required by moves to an internal market. One simple example of this is that ‘market research data’ revealed that the total NHS expenditure was three times any previous estimate, thus revealing the poor state of audit within the NHS (Anon., 1992). This failure has affected the attitudes of computer professionals within the NHS. The consultation process for the working paper Framework for Information Systems: Overview resulted in only seven written replies (Hansard, 1990d; Pounder, 1990). This is in an organization employing many hundreds of computer and information specialists and whose yearly IT expenditure is approximately £1 billion or 2.5% of operating costs (Anon., 1992). For too many computer professionals within the NHS consultation was seen as a waste of time. This systemwide malaise is suggested by the fact that ‘insufficient attention has been paid to the limitations placed upon the theoretical possibilities of information technology by the characteristics of the health system of which the information system is but a part’ (Sandiford et al., 1992). The administrative system has been unable to keep up with a rapid adoption of information technology and, as a result, much hardware and software has been imperfectly specified (Hansard, 1990e). In a period of rapid IT change the NHS spends only £5 per employee on training (Anon., 1992). Yet today the NHS faces massive information management problems.

## A lack of strategic management

But these are the lower level administrative problems. Very little work has gone into discovering the aims of the primary care system: demand for and utilization of health care have been the focal points of interest and not need and quality of outcome (Carr-Hill & Sheldon, 1992). This has arisen from the assumption that such decisions are medical ones and to be made by GPs, rather than the preserve of administrators. Yet efficiency objectives and resource limitations, together with the moves towards more parsimonious resource allocation, inevitably raise the question about what the priorities for primary health care should be. This question has not yet received a definitive answer. Information systems cannot remove the reality that such an answer must be based upon a value judgement. Politicians tend to accept the pressures put on them by their constituents, so that it is accepted that those who pay should in general receive better health care. This ‘inverse care law’ has recently been provided with evidence (Anon., 1986). Democracy and equity conflict here in a very clear way. Underlying this source of conflict and uncertainty is the natural wish for GPs to remain independent from administrative control or interference. There has been some research on implications of management control systems upon professional behaviour by doctors (Eisenberg, 1986) and the attitudes of doctors to administrators (Schultz & Harrison, 1986) and the effect of centralization–decentralization on these issues (Brommels, 1989). One interesting aspect of this in relation to the primary care sector in the UK is that, whereas the doctor–administrator conflict in the hospital sector has reflected doctors’ wish to retain independence against what they see as greater centralization, this cannot be argued to be the case for GPs, who have received relatively more resources and responsibilities in the post-1991 internal market.

## Political uncertainty

Up until the April 1992 general election there was uncertainty about whether or not the government's NHS reforms would be implemented. Opposition within the medical profession was thought to be strong. One element of perceived political strength is a commitment not to back down, which in this case on the part of the GPs was a commitment not to prepare for fund-holding. The fact that GPs were unprepared strengthened their case that the reforms were being introduced too hastily.

## Causes of problems arising from bottom-up evaluation

## Uncertainty about patients' access rights and confidentiality

Another dimension of complexity is the uncertainty caused by the rights of patients to see their medical records and, if necessary, to demand the correction of inaccurate or incomplete data. Until 1990 the Data Protection Act applied only to computer records, but in that year the Access to Health Records Act extended patients' rights to all records. Even as late as 1991 there was concern about whether administrators would have the same access rights as doctors, partly an expression of anxiety by doctors about the growth in administrators' powers (Data Protection Registrar's Office, 1991). If administrators were able to see records, it was suggested, then their format as minimum data sets could be used to identify particular patients' identities, giving rise to concerns about confidentiality. This represented a rapid change of attitude on GPs' part since Micros in Practice: Report of an Appraisal of GP Microcomputer Systems (DHSS, 1985) which noted little concern for patient confidentiality among GPs.

## Inertia in the absence of a deadline

GPs did not take computers seriously when they were not fund-holders. And software developers did not take GP systems seriously until they had been given a deadline for starting fund-holding. Both these factors meant that the deadline, when it did come, would be an imposed one for which GPs had not adequately prepared. Fund-holding requires computerized records for the various accounting procedures to be practicable. The lack of diffusion of computer awareness among GPs and the lack of infrastructure (standardized commercial requirements definitions and designs, a minimum number of software developers with tested products, credible and effective government inducements for GPs to computerize) made the new system in primary care verge on the infeasible. It was not until the need for information directly affected GPs' potential income (the critical period was 1990–92) that they responded. Before that computers were a time-consuming and unnecessary luxury for most GPs (for which they only received 50% of the money back from the government) and consequently GP systems were commercially unattractive to software suppliers.

## IT opportunities

The pilot GP practices were carefully chosen to maximize the potential success of the early stages of fund-holding. GPs chosen were the most experienced computer users. They tended to be GPs whose financial management skills were already well developed, and who belonged to group practices. Less computer-literate GPs who did not belong to group practices (for example, in inner cities) were under-represented.

## CONCLUSIONS

In terms of Nolan's (1979) model of IT adoption within organizations, information systems within UK primary care during the period 1985–92 show aspects of being in an 'initiation' phase: a technology focus in the exploration of devices and communication protocols, an absence of cost-benefit justification for provision of computers to GPs, and requirements superficially characterized in transactional terms. Management commitment was not complete, since objectives and plans were unclear. GPs were not favourably inclined to developing and using computers. The systems were not properly justified, and there were no clearly defined priorities. These are all characteristics of an 'initiation' stage of systems adoption.

Similarly, the period was one characterized by 'technology identification' rather than 'technology transfer' (McFarlan & McKenney, 1983). Smart cards, Read codes and communications links were some of the technologies identified and investigated purposefully. Pilot and experimental schemes abounded, partly for data collection and partly for political (test the water) reasons. In Earl's (1989) tripartite model of information system strategy formulation the experimentation or 'inside-out' aspect worked perfectly. However, the top-down and bottom-up aspects of the policy-making model were problematical and failed to support the adoption of systems in primary care both at the level of information systems (Fig. 1) and in terms of strategic management (Fig. 2).

However, a wider perspective is justified. The information systems within the post-reform NHS are a crucial component in the internal market. If information about needs or costs is not available or incorrect, then the internal market will be failing in its function of enabling rational resource allocation.

The case of NHS primary health care systems shows that the software adoption process cannot be divorced from the organizational context in which it is working. Although many factors affect the quality and success of software adoption, one of the necessary conditions is a favourable organizational environment. In primary health care, politically innovative changes originated from the cabinet and therefore the political aspects of the NHS reforms were sophisticated and robust. However, the implications for management information systems were left as an afterthought. This reflects the history of undervaluing strategic management within the NHS, the equating of information systems with transaction processing rather than with management information and the lack of forethought about the effect of the reforms upon GPs' information management requirements.

Management information systems are now becoming a major tool in political and institutional change, and thus an important precondition for the success of institutional reform. The naive view of computers is that they enable central powers to enforce and monitor change more easily. But this overlooks the important analogy between democratic process and software product life cycle. Consultation and specification of requirements are crucial elements in making new political institutions acceptable and new information systems realistically implementable.

## THE FUTURE

On the negative side, questions need to be answered about whether current information systems that are based upon usage (e.g. waiting lists) and patient satisfaction (e.g. complaints to MPs) are in the long run able to evolve into systems based upon information about need and outcome. Such a transformation will require major rethinking of what comprehensive health care means and how it can be objectively measured and used.

On the positive side, the DoH is aware of the shortcomings of current systems. As recently as 1989 its view was that information systems were not adequate to meet the need to set and monitor GPs' budgets (DoH, 1989). What has been slowly emerging over the whole health system (and what will eventually include the primary care sector) is a more balanced tripartite approach on the lines mapped out above. There is therefore the potential for a strategic plan. Some suggestions have been already made by the authors (Sillince & Frost, 1993). What follows is a summary.

The eventual shape of top-down clarification will be determined not by some political directive, but by business objectives formulation, which has been a part of NHS planning for some time. These originate in ministerial suggestions, which are embroidered at regional and district levels. Such a process is having an effect upon the determination of information requirements for the secondary health care system.

A second, bottom-up approach involves consultation with individual health care workers and patients. There are signs of the beginnings of a shift from laboratory research to social survey research. In the US, for example, elaborate surveys are undertaken to monitor the population's health on such indicators as health behaviour, incidence of specific diseases and key variables such as blood pressure. The shift towards surveys, which is indicated by recent policy (DoH, 1992), is associated with the realization that health spending will always be limited, that drugs and surgery are yielding diminishing returns on extra resources compared with preventative and behavioural programmes, and that much health variation is influenced by income.

A third, experimental approach has, in general, been very strong for several years. Experiments within primary care information systems have included: methods of giving patients access to their health records, smart cards, electronic mail, electronic data interchange and GP decision support programs. Many mainstream projects such as hospital information support systems (HISS) and resource management initiatives (RMI) have been piloted. The extent to which these three approaches to strategy formulation will balance in the primary care system of the future depends upon the extent to which the factors identified here continue to be emphasized.

## Biography

John Sillince is Lecturer in Information Systems in the Management School, at the University of Sheffield. He holds a BA and PhD in social science, a BSc in mathematics, and an MSc in computer science. His research interests are intelligent argumentation systems, text retrieval, manufacturing and medical information systems, and system adaptability to changing requirements.

Brian Frost is Lecturer in Information Systems at the Management School at the University of Sheffield. He has a BSc in economics and an MSc in computer science. His research interests are information systems to support primary care prescribing and fund-holding, and many other aspects of medical economics and medical information systems.
