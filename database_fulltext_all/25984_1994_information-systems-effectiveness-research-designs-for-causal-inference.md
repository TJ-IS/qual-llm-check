# Information systems effectiveness: research designs for causal inference

W. J. Haga and M. Zviran

Department of Systems Management, Naval Postgraduate School, Monterey, California 93943, USA

Abstract. This paper examines the capacity of the research designs of 37 empirical studies of information systems (IS) effectiveness to provide a basis for the development of theories about behaviour related to IS effectiveness. The power of each study to support causal inference was evaluated in terms of (a) its handling of the time dimension, (b) its ability to weigh differences and (c) its resistance to internal validity threats that pose alternative explanations for its reported findings. Of the reviewed studies, 29.7% could account for the time dimension, 32.4% employed a comparison group and 16.2% were not susceptible to any internal validity threats. Only 13.5% of the studies combined an accounting for the time dimension with the use of a comparison group. Of these, however, only 5.4% were also invulnerable to internal validity threats. The research designs of nearly 95% of these published studies were deficient in supporting causal inference. In those studies, suggestions that one variable was causally related to another variable could not be substantiated. Encouragement for the future capacity of IS effectiveness research to support causal inference was found in a trend towards the use of quasiexperimental designs. Recommendations are made regarding ways to increase the inferential capacity of research designs employed in the study of IS effectiveness.

Key words: effectiveness, effectiveness measurement, productivity, productivity assessment, research design, validity.

## INTRODUCTION

The measurement of information systems (IS) effectiveness is an important issue for both IS researchers and practitioners (Alavi et al., 1989). It has been in the top ten list of critical IS problems since 1980 (Ball & Harris, 1982; Dickson et al., 1984; Hartog & Herbert, 1986; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987; Haga & Zviran, 1993). Senior line managers and IS managers wonder whether they are getting a pay-off from their investment in information systems, especially since the arrival of the personal computer (Strassmann, 1985; Bowen, 1986;

Maginnis, 1986; Pournelle, 1987; LaPlante, 1988). General managers surveyed recently (Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1987) ranked the measuring of IS effectiveness as the fourth most critical problem they face in managing information systems. Sixty per cent of the IS executives polled in that survey considered the measuring of effectiveness to be a critical problem. According to a Morgan Stanley economist (Roach, 1990), 'the service sector has devoted far more effort in deploying computers than in determining the precise ways they can be used to enhance white collar productivity'.

The definition of effectiveness in IS has itself been a topic of considerable research and discussion (Carlson, 1974; Radecki, 1975; Ginzberg, 1978; King & Schrems, 1978; Kleijnen, 1979; Chandler, 1982; Markus & Robey, 1983; Maggiolini, 1986; Dominick, 1987; Trice & Treacy, 1988). Most researchers are in agreement that IS effectiveness focuses on the organizational effects produced by information systems. However, these effects may be viewed from a variety of perspectives that include: usage of the information system itself (Colton, 1973; Swanson, 1974; Lucas, 1975a, b, 1978; Fuerst & Cheney, 1982; Hogue & Watson, 1985), user attitudes toward an information system (Lucas, 1974; King & Rodriguez, 1978; Robey & Zeller, 1978; Neumann & Segev, 1979; Ball, 1980; Altmeyer & Bozeman, 1981; Bruwer, 1984), value of the information produced by a system (Gallagher, 1974; King & Epstein, 1983), cost savings (Hall & Lincoln, 1976; Edelman, 1981; Englander & Englander, 1985; Kapsales, 1986), use of the information produced (Barkin & Dickson, 1977), quality of decisions made using a system (King & Rodriguez, 1978; Aldag & Power, 1986; Gallupe & DeSanctis, 1988), problem-solving ability (Mason, 1978), profit (Sullivan & Secrest, 1985), value of a system to an organization (Robey, 1979), usefulness of a system (Franz & Robey, 1986), performance factors (Mansour & Watson, 1980), usefulness of the information produced by a system (Larcker & Lessig, 1980), return on investment (Cron & Sobol, 1983), benefits produced by a system related to its costs (Lincoln, 1985), quality of writing (Durand et al., 1987) and implementation of plans (Raghunathan & King, 1987).

This paper looks at the capacity of studies of IS effectiveness to contribute to the construction of formal theory. It reviews 37 empirical assessments of IS effectiveness. (We reviewed 36 separate studies. However, we counted Gallupe & DeSanctis (1988) as two studies because they employed different research designs in two stages of their research. Therefore, while we looked at 36 studies, we speak of them as 37 studies.) We examined the formal designs of these studies for their capacity to meet two prerequisites for supporting claims to causal inference: how they handled time in causal order and how they dealt with rival explanations for their reported findings. The intention here was to examine the potential for the development of theory in one area, IS effectiveness. To do that, we profiled the research designs that provide both the limits and the foundation for that potential. Our point was not to criticize individual studies but to portray the state of methodology and theory development in a subdiscipline.

## THEORY

A theory is a set of testable, falsifiable propositions that are related in such a way that some may be deduced from others. Together, they make sense out of what is observed (Homans, 1964). For example, a researcher can deduce a proposition 3 from propositions 1 and 2 while deducing a proposition 4 from propositions 2 and 3.

Propositions are statements of relationships between concepts (Mill, 1950). Concepts suggest operational activity and measurement. At the conceptual level, a propositional statement takes the form, if A, then B. For example, if (A) user involvement is high, then (B) a system implementation will be successful.

Only falsifiable propositions (Popper, 1968) have utility for the development of theory. For example, if we assert that using a decision support system improves the quality of decisions, then the variables use of a decision support system and quality of decisions must be operationalized in such a way that the assertion can be found to be false. In this instance, conditions must exist under which the use of a decision support system does not yield better decisions. Science advances by attempts at disproof rather than by attempts at verification. Research designs that cannot account for time order, measure differences or resist alternative explanations hamper the capacity of an empirical study to disconfirm hypotheses. Otherwise, as researchers, if we cannot disconfirm hypotheses, we are fabricating tautologies.

## TESTING CAUSAL INFERENCE

We focused on two conditions that must be met for X to be accepted as the cause of Y:

(a) Time order: a change in X must precede a change in Y.

(b) Rival explanations for observed changes in Y must be excluded.

Our concern is limited to weighing the capacity of empirical studies of IS effectiveness to support causal inference. However, that limit is not a loophole through which these studies can evade our criticism. It can well be said that correlational studies of, say, factors associated with user satisfaction are interesting in their own right. Or, it might be said that IS effectiveness researchers are justified in pursuing taxonomies. Taxonomies, after all, can be a first step in the construction of theory. Would such claims be a reason not to assay the capacity of IS effectiveness to handle causal inference: We say no. The business of academic research is, finally, to provide a basis for building theory. We want to find out how much of IS effectiveness research is moving beyond interesting correlates or taxonomies.

## Time order

Concepts must be expressed in causal order. In a proposition of the form, if X, then Y, X is assumed to be the cause of Y. We follow Blalock (1964) in holding that if X is a cause of Y then a change in X must precede a change in Y. X must not merely associate with a change in Y. Likewise, the design of an empirical investigation must account for time order between two variables (Blalock, 1964; Rescher, 1970; Denzin, 1989). A researcher must collect data at more than a single point in time. If a researcher is interested in the effect of an intervening variable X, then the design must collect data before (pretest observation: $O_{1}$ ) and after (post-test observation: $O_{2}$ ) the appearance of X:

$$
O _ {1} \times O _ {2}
$$

Other things being equal, a researcher may attribute a difference between $O_{1}$ and $O_{2}$ to the intervening effect of X.

## Rival explanations for observed changes

Other things, of course, are seldom equal. Alternative explanations arise to account for differences between $O_{1}$ and $O_{2}$ . Rival explanations that are consequences of research design have been categorized as threats to internal validity by Campbell & Stanley (1966).

Internal validity deals with whether observed changes in a dependent variable are due to the effect of an experimental variable X or to other factors for which no accounting was made in the design of a study. Some threats to internal validity deal with spurious conclusions based upon observations made before and after the effect of an intervening experimental variable. Others deal with erroneous conclusions based upon supposed differences between comparison groups.

What follows is a brief description of each of the internal validity threats identified by Campbell & Stanley (1966).

## History

Events occurring between pretest and post-test observations that affect an observed phenomenon along with an intervening variable X are the history effect. Designs that collect data at both pretest and post-test points, but lack a parallel control group, cannot take account of the effect of history on a study's findings. Designs lacking a pretest point but involving a comparison group, while weak in other respects, can control for the effect of history.

## Maturation

Changes within respondents themselves that affect measurements between pretest and post-test observations (e.g. ageing, growing up, becoming bored, tiring) constitute the maturation effect. Designs that collect data at both pretest and post-test points, but lack a control group, cannot account for the effect of maturation. The capacity of a design to exclude maturation as a rival explanation for its findings is not clear (from the design alone) when a design lacks a pretest point but involves a comparison group. Maturation among respondents does not affect designs that use randomized comparison groups. With randomized participation, the effect should appear in both the experimental and the comparison groups.

The time series design, while lacking a comparison group, shields a study against the maturation effect. Campbell & Stanley's (1966) rationale is that changes arising from maturation, testing, selection and mortality would not occur at the $O_{2}/O_{3}$ period that had not occurred already at $O_{1}/O_{2}$ .

## Testing

When respondents become skilled at taking a test, filling out questionnaires, answering interviewers or taking part in experiments, Campbell & Stanley (1966) call it the testing effect (also known as retest effect, test sophistication and testing reactivity). Such increased skill causes changes in indicators at a second observation that a researcher might confuse with the effects of an experimental variable. A design that lacks a pretest stage, yet uses comparison groups, controls this effect. Designs that involve a pretest and post-test but lack a comparison group cannot protect their findings against the testing effect.

## Instrument calibration

When people are the instruments of observing, recording and measuring, changes within them can produce changes in their reports of events. This effect is known as instrument calibration (also called instrument recalibration and instrument decay). Examples of human recalibration are fatigue, an interviewer's growing skill at conducting interviews, observer boredom or developing self-confidence by observers in their own skills. Designs that involve comparison groups with randomized assignment of members or, if not, have no pretest stage can deal with this effect.

## Regression

When a researcher selects comparison groups precisely for their extreme differences in scores on tests or surveys, a regression effect is likely to occur. Generally, respondents with extreme scores on one occasion will not produce the same extreme on a later occasion. Statistical regression toward a mean score is likely in the post-test observation of such groups. In the case of a one-group pretest/post-test design, the design alone does not reveal whether statistical regression affects a study. Designs involving randomized comparison groups are able to account for the effect of regression.

## Selection bias

Selection bias is a rival explanation for a study's findings when comparison groups assumed to be equivalent are actually different as a result of biases operating during the selection of respondents. These differences can account for the differences in reported data between the two groups. Any design with a pretest and a post-test can control for this effect. Those lacking a pretest or randomized comparison groups are vulnerable to selection bias.

## Differential mortality

Between non-randomized comparison groups, there can be selective drop-out of participants from a study. Researchers may assume that any such participation mortality is random. This differential drop-out effect can lead to differences in reported data that could be mistaken for the effect of an experimental variable. Any design with a pretest and a post-test can control for this effect. Those lacking a pretest or randomized comparison groups are vulnerable to differential mortality among respondents.

Interactions of selection with other effects

In multigroup designs, a researcher could mistake the interaction of a selection bias and, say, maturation for the effect of an intervening experimental variable.

## METHODOLOGY

## Selecting studies for review

We selected journals in IS and related fields, published in English between 1970 and 1990, as the source from which we reviewed studies of IS effectiveness (see Appendix A). These are the journals regarded as appropriate professional reading material and publishing outlets for IS academics (Hamilton & Ives, 1983; Vogel & Wetherbe, 1984; Alavi et al., 1989).

An on-line search reported 128 items when we used and—or combinations of the key words 'productivity, effectiveness, cost–benefit, evaluation or success' as applied to assessments of information systems. Out of that set, 18 articles appeared to report empirical assessments of IS effectiveness as determined by a reading of their titles. None of them, despite having been published in IS journals (e.g MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information and Management, Data Base), are among the 36 empirical studies of IS effectiveness discussed here. Some focused on the development of survey questionnaire instruments, while others were from trade and industry publications aimed at MIS practitioners. They did not report empirical studies of system effectiveness.

We suspected that the empirical literature on IS effectiveness was more extensive than what the on-line search yielded. Our manual search brought forth the difficulty with our on-line search: deficient key words. Authors used key words such as 'manufacturing and engineering systems, return on investment, utilization process, human information processing, information system design, information system investment, cost/benefit calculations, management reporting, management issues, accuracy, content, information characteristics and user evaluation'. Such key words were, in retrospect, appropriate for describing various aspects of evaluating IS effectiveness. They simply were not the ones that we imagined would identify reports of IS effectiveness in the academic literature. Therefore, we undertook a manual search of every issue of the journals listed in Appendix A, going back to 1970 where applicable. We identified 118 articles (by title, key words and abstracts) as dealing with the evaluation of IS effectiveness. The reference lists in these articles, in turn, identified 31 more articles, bringing the total items in the set to 149. We read these 149 articles and categorized them according to the characteristics of their reported research designs. Most did not report empirical studies.

Excluded immediately were non-empirical analyses of the assessment of information systems (Chervany & Dickson, 1970; Coleman & Riley, 1972; Mason & Mitroff, 1973; Carlson, 1974; Radecki, 1976; Soergel, 1976; Strassmann, 1976; Tharp & Taggart, 1976; Dickson et al., 1977; Arnovick & Gee, 1978; Ginzberg, 1978, 1980; King & Schrems, 1978; Mason, 1978; Kleijnen, 1979; Worthley, 1980; Hamilton & Ives, 1982; Keim & Janaro, 1982; Borko, 1983; Markus & Robey, 1983; Mason, 1984; Bearman et al., 1985; Calista, 1985; Gremillion & Pyburn, 1985; Lay, 1985; Maggiolini, 1986; Martin & Trumbley, 1986; Rouse, 1987; Marsden & Pingry, 1988; Trice & Treacy, 1988).

We also excluded reports of studies involved in validating attitudinal questionnaires and other instruments, even though the instruments they were developing were aimed at gauging aspects of IS performance (Larcker & Lessig, 1980; Mick et al., 1980; Chandler, 1982; Epstein & King, 1982; Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Srinivasan, 1985; Snitkin & King, 1986; Dominick, 1987; Hill et al., 1987; Miller & Doyle, 1987; Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Money et al., 1988; Montezema, 1988). We wanted studies that assessed IS effectiveness per se rather than reports of efforts that developed techniques by which to carry out such assessments.

Removed, too, from the original 149 articles was a miscellany of pieces with attractive titles that actually dealt with other issues. We excluded articles addressing evaluations of the technical performance of hardware (e.g. computer processor performance in millions of instructions per second). Articles dealing with efficiency in software development (e.g. programmer productivity in terms of lines of code written per day) were excluded as well. We later removed two case studies (Matlin, 1979; Decker & Plumlee, 1985) because they lacked structured data collection designs of the sort that we intended to evaluate.

Our research efforts yielded 36 articles reporting empirical studies of IS effectiveness in its various formulations. (As noted earlier, while we reviewed 36 articles, we refer to them in this paper as 37 studies because of the double counting Gallupe & DeSanctis, 1988). We may have missed some studies. For that we apologize in advance to the researchers we might have overlooked. If we have not discovered every empirical study of IS effectiveness, we believe we have found a set that represents the genre.

## Evaluating research designs

By reading the descriptions of research methodology in each study, we determined whether a study:

1. employed an explicit, experimental variable;

2. gathered data before the administration of an experimental variable;

3. gathered data after the administration of an experimental variable;

4. gathered data from comparison groups;

5. randomized the membership of comparison groups.

From these elements we classified the research design in each study according to the Campbell & Stanley (1966) taxonomy (see Table 1).

## Time dimension

From the Campbell & Stanley (1966) taxonomy, we identified research designs that employed pretest and post-test observations (one group pretest/post-test, true experiment, time series, non-equivalent control group and separate sample control group). Studies that used these designs clearly accounted for the time dimension. Designs that recorded multiple observations even when all of them were post-test (post-test only control group in repeated factorial observations, post-test only time series with non-equivalent control group and post-test only time series) also accounted for the time dimension. Studies involving observations at a single point in time did not.

Guided by the Campbell & Stanley (1966) taxonomy, we assessed the ability of each study to handle the internal validity threats (scored as the -1s, 1s and blank cells in columns A–H in

Where Campbell & Stanley (1966), for example, assign a –I to studies that are vulnerable to, say, the testing effect (because respondents learn the test-taking game), we left that cell blank in Appendix B (meaning that testing was not an issue) where a researcher collected data from, say, archival sources.

## Non-experimental designs

## 1. One-shot study

## [X] $O_{1}$

Table 2 shows that the one-shot study was the research design in 51.4% of the surveyed studies of IS effectiveness. Campbell & Stanley (1966), in an unfortunate choice of words, call this design a one-shot case study. This invites a confusion of this research design with the conventional meaning of a case study as a comprehensive, in-depth report of an event or specific organization. To avoid the confusion, we have taken the liberty of calling this design a one-shot study.

In the estimate of Campbell & Stanley (1966), rival explanations for the findings reported by one-shot studies are so numerous as to render them useless in disconfirming or accepting a causal linkage. According to Denzin (1989), the one-shot approach makes no attempt to approximate the virtues of experimental design: it has no comparison group and no time dimension.

In a later article on the use of case studies, Campbell (1975) appeared to modify his disdain for one-shot studies. However, in that instance, he was dealing with true case studies in the anthropological sense of sustained participant observation.

Because a one-shot study has no laboratory-like, controlled administration of an intervening variable, a researcher implicitly invites you to assume, as indicated by the brackets around the X, that subjects in a one-shot study were exposed to the intervention of some naturally occurring variable.

... a carefully studied single instance is implicitly compared with other events casually observed and remembered. The inferences are based upon general expectations of what the data would have been had the X not occurred (Campbell & Stanley, 1966)

A one-shot study fabricates an experiment not through design, but through analysis, specifically multivariate analysis. This technique creates analytic groupings out of collected data after the fact, groupings that a researcher did not identify a priori. Nagel (1961) concluded that the difficulties of basing causal inference on multivariate analysis 'are notorious'.

The difficulty with asking subjects to recall something at $O_{1}$ is that human recall is notoriously unreliable (Haberman, 1963). For that reason, one-shot questionnaire data collection is ‘the weakest form of survey design’ (Denzin, 1968). Fundamental to the gathering of scientific evidence is the need to record differences (Campbell & Stanley, 1966) by making comparisons. No comparisons are possible in one-shot studies.

## 2. One-group pretest/post-test

$$
O _ {1} \times O _ {2}
$$

The one-group design, found in 5.4% of these studies, has the virtue of a deliberate attempt to measure the differences between a group observed at $O_{1}$ and at $O_{2}$ . Unfortunately, its lack of a comparison group subjects it to a distracting set of rival explanations for its findings. Chief among them are other effects, besides the X variable, transpiring between $O_{1}$ and $O_{2}$ .

While the one-group pretest/post-test design is superior to the one-shot study, the only recognition it merits from Campbell & Stanley (1966) is that it is worth doing only where researchers can do nothing better. They employ the one-group study as an example of a weak design that deludes researchers by its closeness to a true experimental design. Its sole virtue is that it accounts for the time dimension.

## 3. Static group comparison

$$
\frac {X O _ {1}}{O _ {1}}
$$

The static group comparison accounted for 5.4% of the studies reviewed here. This design offers a basis for examining differences. However, because respondents were not randomly assigned, the horizontal line indicates non-equivalency of comparison groups in Campbell & Stanley's (1966) notation.

Static group findings are vulnerable to the rival explanation that observed differences between the groups predated the intervention of an X variable. A researcher faces the difficulty of attributing observed differences between comparison groups to one group's exposure to an experimental variable.

One form of this difficulty is the possibility of biased drop-out after the X variable intervened. For example, assume that a standard test of creativity showed that employees in general scored higher than employees who were participating in structured re-engineering of business processes. Researchers might be tempted to conclude that process re-engineering destroyed creativity. An alternative explanation could be that creative people withdraw from re-engineering exercises leaving a residual set of less creative participants.

When researchers deliberately select comparison groups for their extreme differences, as opposed to their equivalency, rival explanations gain potency to undermine reported findings.

## Experimental designs

4. True experiment

$$
\begin{array}{c c c} R & O _ {1} & X \quad O _ {2} \\ R & O _ {1} & O _ {2} \end{array}
$$

The true experiment, having pretest and post-test data collection points paralleled by randomized comparison groups (indicated by the Rs), controls for all threats to internal validity. Of the studies reviewed here, 2.7% employed a true experiment design.

## 5. Post-test only control group

$$
\begin{array}{c c} R \times O _ {1} \\ R & O _ {1} \end{array}
$$

The post-test only design nearly matches the true experiment in its capacity to handle threats to internal validity. Its use in the studies of IS effectiveness reviewed here was 10.8%.

6. Post-test only control group in repeated X factorial design

$$
\begin{array}{c c c} R & X & O _ {1} \qquad O _ {2} \\ R & & O _ {1} \times O _ {2} \end{array}
$$

Of the studies of IS effectiveness that we reviewed, 2.7% used this design.

## Quasiexperimental designs

Experimental designs are the methodological ideal: they control for all of the internal validity threats. However, researchers can usually approximate that degree of control only in a laboratory setting. Indeed, the IS studies reviewed here that employed experimental designs did so in either simulations or in a laboratory. In the field setting, which was the setting for 83.8% (see Table 3) of the reviewed studies, such control over variables and environmental influences is unlikely. Nonetheless, information systems are situated in the real world with its environing elements: users, vendors, management, software developers and system analysts. This setting provides a rich ground for IS effectiveness research. Campbell & Stanley (1966) put forth as an objective for research in the social sciences both that it be done in the field and that it reduce its vulnerability to internal validity threats by approximating features of the true experiment. To this end, they identified research designs that approach this objective, calling them quasiexperimental designs.

## 7. Time series

$$
O 1 O 2 \times O _ {3} O _ {4}
$$

Appearances to the contrary not withstanding, it is not the case that a time series design shares the vulnerabilities of the one-group pretest/post-test. Repeated measurements, before or after an experimental variable, shield a study from internal validity threats. To repeat what was said earlier, Campbell & Stanley's rationale (1966) is that changes arising from maturation, testing, selection and mortality would not occur at the $O_{2}/O_{3}$ period that had not occurred already at $O_{1}/O_{2}$ . Of the studies reviewed here, 2.7% used a time series design.

8. Non-equivalent control group

$$
\begin{array}{c c} O _ {1} & X \quad O _ {2} \\ \hline O _ {1} & O _ {2} \end{array}
$$

In this design, the experimental and control groups do not have equivalence through randomized assignment of members. In a field setting, such groups might represent natural assemblages such as user groups or organizational departments. Control groups are made as similar to experimental groups as their availability permits, but they are not similar enough to be a true experiment. Nonetheless, Campbell & Stanley (1966) regard the non-equivalent control group as a design well worth using in circumstances where a true experiment or a post-test only with control group are impossible. In our review, 5.4% of the studies employed this design.

9. Post-test only time series with non-equivalent control group

$$
\begin{array}{c} X \quad O _ {1} \quad O _ {2} \quad O _ {3} \\ \hline O _ {1} \quad O _ {2} \quad O _ {3} \end{array}
$$

We do not know whether Campbell & Stanley (1966) intended their taxonomy to be inclusive of all known cases. What we do know is that the creativity of IS effectiveness researchers has exceeded the bounds of the taxonomy. The post-test only time series design is the first of three research designs for which we developed descriptions and notation. It consists of repeated observations following the intervention of an X variable. Similar to the non-equivalent control group design, it has a comparison group that was not exposed to the X variable. However, that group is one that is naturally occurring in a field setting rather than having its members assigned. Of the reviewed studies, 2.7% used the post-test only time series with non-equivalent control group design.

## 10. Post-test only time series

$$
X O _ {1} O _ {2} O _ {3}
$$

This design makes sense when researchers have discovered that an X variable has already intervened in a setting and they want to reduce their vulnerability to the internal validity threats facing a one-shot study. We have scored it as being similar to the time series. It resists most internal validity threats except for the history effect, to which it is vulnerable, and the instrument calibration effect. As Table 2 shows, 8.1% of the reviewed studies used the post-test only time series design.

## 11. Pretest/post-test with separate sample control group

$$
\begin{array}{c c c} R & O _ {1} & X \\ R & & O _ {2} \end{array}
$$

Campbell & Stanley (1966) regard this design as a variant of the pretest/post-test with separate sample control group. The effects of history and maturation still intrude as they do in any time series design collecting data directly from human respondents. Yet, the design provides a strong defence against internal validity threats. Of the reviewed studies, 2.7% used this design.

## Time dimension

We categorized each of the 37 studies according to whether its research design permitted an accounting of the time dimension (Table 3 summarizes the findings in columns I–N of Appendix B): 29.7% of the studies accounted for time. The remaining 70.3% employed signs that collected data only at a single point in time. They had no way to account for time because of the structure of their research designs. In the words of Denzin (1968), studies of this sort take a 'snap shot' at one point in time and leave it to each respondent to weave a tale about events preceding an interview or questionnaire.

## Intended causality

One can ask whether any of the studies reviewed here had a concern with the development of theory at all. How can we judge researchers on something they never intended? As Table 3 shows, 43.2% of the reviewed studies presented visual models employing arrows to illustrate the direction of effects of the variables being studied or described their intentions in language that suggested causality (e.g. impact, determine, enhance, influence, predict, affect, lead to) (Powers & Dickson, 1973; Swanson, 1974; Gallagher, 1974; Lucas, 1975a, b, 1978; Schewe, 1976; Edstrom, 1977; King & Rodriguez, 1978; Robey, 1979; Altmeyer & Bozeman, 1981; Bruwer, 1984; Franz & Robey, 1986; Baroudi et al., 1986; Tait & Vessey, 1988; Cronan & Douglas, 1990).

Of the studies that suggested causal inference was their intent, 18.8% accounted for time in their designs. Another 18.8% employed comparison groups. Only 6.3% used designs that shielded them from all internal validity threats. None of the studies implying causality in their findings enjoyed a combination of time order, comparison groups and protection against internal validity threats. Some seemed to be unaware that their reported finding of empirical support for their models was scientifically impossible.

## DISCUSSION

The picture that emerged here is one of bad news and good news. The bad news is that nearly half of the reviewed studies were in the one-shot mode and only 5.4% of the studies accounted for time order, used equivalent comparison groups and resisted all internal validity threats. The good news is that a third of the reviewed studies were sound, creative, experimental and quasiexperimental designs. The best news is that things are getting better.

## Need for theory

When we encounter research colleagues employing deficient research designs, their apology has been an assertion that they have no interest in building theories about behaviour around information systems. They do not embrace theory construction as the point of their academic research enterprise. We indulge a digression here to document the views of those who hold otherwise, particularly for IS academic research.

Nagel (1961) claims that the paramount aim of social science is to establish general laws that can serve as instruments for systematic explanation and dependable prediction. Bariff & Ginzberg (1982) have acknowledged that IS, being an applied discipline, 'has no basic, core theory'. They called for IS research to contribute to the development of a behavioural framework that will give IS a capacity to describe, explain and predict behaviour around information systems with something more substantial than intuition. They explicitly called for IS behavioural studies to meet the standards of formal research formulated by Campbell & Stanley (1966).

Alavi et al. (1989) argue that a lack of theory in MIS results in important relationships among variables not being discerned by MIS researchers. 'Theories are needed to guide research and the choice of research methodologies', they say. Theory guides researchers to hypotheses worth testing and shows how to causally link them. Without theory, researchers are inclined to formulate hypotheses post hoc because they are the hypotheses that pass statistical tests of significance [see Baroudi & Orlikowski (1988) on the fixation with statistical significance testing among social researchers].

Kleijnen (1979) argued that IS researchers must match tools with theories that provide frameworks, clues, guidelines and ideas. Ginzberg (1980) decried the mischief wrought by 'atheoretical' research in IS. Ives & Olson (1984), in a preface to their analytical structure for IS, stressed the need for a theoretical framework that can generate research hypotheses. Jarvenpaa et al. (1985), in talking about studies of computer graphics, lamented that the absence of theory contributed to the current state of inconclusive results. Hummel & Gunn (1989) said there can be no validity to the claims of IS researchers until they answer the question of what constitutes a valid theory of the relation between computers and human users. Dickson et al. (1977 called for 'a better ground of our research on a theoretical base'. According to Amoroso (1990), theory is crucial to making sense of observed IS phenomena; researchers cannot draw implications or make predictions without it.

To deny theory is to miss the point of academic endeavour.

## Bad news: IS effectiveness research lacks a basis for causal inference

An examination of the scoring of the 37 studies in columns I–N of Appendix B shows that 29.7% of the studies accounted for time, 32.4% used a comparison group and 16.2% randomized assignment to comparison groups. However, 13.5% of the reviewed studies accounted for time and used comparison groups. Sixty per cent of that set randomized assignment of respondents, getting down to 8.1% of all the studies. A look at columns A–H shows that 67% of that set are among the six studies that resisted all internal validity threats. The bottom line is that only 5.4% of the 37 studies (Aldag & Power, 1986; first stage of Gallupe & DeSanctis, 1988) possessed an airtight case for supporting causal inference. This inability to accept or reject assertions of causal inference in so many studies removed a foundation upon which to build formal theory around IS effectiveness. The root of this finding is the 51.4% of the studies that indulged the one-shot design.

## Good news: there is a lot about which to cheer

While it is true that roughly half of the reviewed studies resorted to the one-shot study, it is also true that roughly half did not. Indeed, in addition to the 2.7% of the studies that used a true experiment, roughly 35% of the reviewed studies used a quasiexperimental design. We contend that the research most likely to inform us about IS effectiveness will be done in field settings. It is there that quasiexperimental approximation of laboratory controls is appropriate and fruitful.

Several of these effectiveness studies were textbook examples of how to do research. King and Rodriguez's (1978) study of the usage of a strategic information system was the first to randomly assign respondents to comparison groups. Moreover, they triangulated their data collection with both attitudinal and behavioural indicators. While we described their research design as post-test only, it was cleverer than that, employing three comparison groups:

$$
\begin{array}{c c} R \times O _ {1} \\ R \times O _ {1} \\ R \quad O _ {1} \end{array}
$$

Lucas's (1978) evaluation of an optical scan order entry system was the first to approximate a true experiment with pretest and post-test observations for both experimental and control groups. He lacked only randomized assignment of respondents to ensure equivalency of his comparison groups. Aldag & Power's (1986) study of the effectiveness of a decision support system randomized assignment to their comparison groups. Moreover, they did so in an ingenious factorial design that alternated the administration of their experimental variable (use of a decision support system). Durand et al.'s (1987) examination of the effect of word processing upon the quality of writing randomized assignment of respondents to comparison groups. Gallupe & DeSanctis's (1988) evaluation of a group decision support system employed the most complex design of all of the reviewed studies, using eight randomized comparison groups in a combined true experiment and post-test only design:

$$
\begin{array}{l l l l} R _ {1} & O _ {1} & X & O _ {2} \\ R _ {1} & O _ {1} & & O _ {2} \\ R _ {2} & O _ {1} & X & O _ {2} \\ R _ {2} & O _ {1} & & O _ {1} \\ R _ {3} & & X & O _ {1} \\ R _ {3} & & & O _ {1} \\ R _ {4} & & X & O _ {1} \\ R _ {4} & & & O _ {1} \end{array}
$$

They also triangulated their data sources between attitudinal and behavioural indicators.

## The best news: an emerging trend

Examining the chronology of studies in Appendix B offers more grounds for believing that IS effectiveness research is already moving in the direction of supporting causal inference through research designs. Of the last 13 studies reviewed in this study, only four were one-shot designs. Compare this with the first eight studies, which were all one-shot studies. Four of the last eight studies (double counting Gallupe & DeSanctis, 1988) defeated all threats to internal validity.

## RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE IS EFFECTIVENESS RESEARCH

## Time dimension

IS effectiveness research can support causal inference only if its research designs gather data at more than a single point in time. Editors of IS journals and manuscript reviewers should reduce the portion of studies of IS effectiveness that have no concern with time (two-thirds in this review).

## Quasiexperimental designs

Chervany & Dickson (1970), Carlson (1974) and King & Rodriguez (1978) have called for controlled experimentation in IS research. While academic IS learns a great deal from laboratory experiments, researchers will do richly descriptive and theoretically fruitful research on IS effectiveness in field settings. Bariff & Ginzberg (1982) urged IS researchers to move to field settings. Of the studies reviewed here, 83.8% were done in field settings. The challenge is to combine the real-world setting for field research with research designs that increase the capacity of the findings to support causal inference.

Quasiexperimental designs meet that challenge by providing a route to gaining the richness of field settings while approximating the rigour of experimental controls. What is 'quasi' in quasiexperimental designs is a lack ' . . . of full control over the scheduling of experimental stimuli (the when and to whom of exposure and the ability to randomize exposures) which make a true experiment possible' (Campbell & Stanley, 1966). In field settings, IS effectiveness researchers must collect data before and after the appearance of an experimental variable. Linson & Barclift (1988) studied the input/output productivity effect from automating government purchases. They gathered archival data on the inputs and outputs of a contracting office for one year before a system implementation. They also gathered input and output data for the year following. They compared inputs and outputs between the two time periods to assess the productivity effect of the automated system.

Where randomization of comparison groups is not feasible in a field setting, an alternative is naturally assembled groupings such as occupational categories, user groups, local area network clients and departments, divisions or firms that perform identical or similar functions. An ingredient for the inferential success of quasiexperimental efforts is for IS effectiveness researchers to be aware of exactly which variables they do not control. They should also be aware of the variables by which comparison groups differ. This was done by Lucas (1978) when he employed a non-equivalent control group design in the assessment of an order entry system. Englander & Englander (1985) used records of welfare cases in New Jersey as an experimental group before and after the implementation of a case-tracking system. They used records of welfare cases in the entire United States as a control group. Ginzberg (1981) used security portfolio managers as a pretest and post-test experimental group to examine the effect of on-line portfolio management software while using a group of investment experts (who did not have the software) as a control group. Sound research designs are possible in a field setting. These examples show that they are already being employed. The critical question is why any IS effectiveness researchers continue to employ the limited one-shot design.

## Triangulation

Studies of system success must approach empirical assessment with more than one concept of effectiveness. A researcher can study a system's performance, for example, in terms of user attitudes, usage rate, costs and benefits, or the economic value of information. Moreover, researchers can study any particular IS implementation from several analytic levels: individual actor, small group, organization or society (Gorry & Scott-Morton, 1971; Nolan & Wetherbe, 1980; Bariff & Ginzberg, 1982). As we noted earlier, Gallupe & DeSanctis (1988) employed both attitudinal and behavioural indicators of the success of a group decision support system. Aldag & Power (1986) used both attitudinal and behavioural measures in evaluating the impact of a decision support system. Edstrom (1977) triangulated individuals' self-reports of user's 'influence' with estimates from others around them. King & Rodriguez (1978) evaluated a strategic information system by triangulating attitudinal responses from questionnaires with archival data on system usage and an assessment of decisional performance by a panel of experienced manager-users.

## Longer time frame for field studies

As a stop toward building scientific credibility, IS effectiveness research must commit to going beyond the short term (see Bariff & Ginzberg, 1982). IS effectiveness researchers need to match cross-sectional analysis with longitudinal designs (see Vitalari, 1985). Indeed, it is difficult for a researcher to make a contribution to an understanding of IS effectiveness that is at all helpful unless his or her work has been over the long term.

How long should a longitudinal study be? A researcher might need 2–3 years to cover the implementation of an information system from proposal to user acceptance. After a researcher has covered the introduction of a system, he or she must continue with follow-up studies throughout the system's life cycle. Considering that the useful life cycle of information systems is shorter than for most other capital investments (some scarcely last 3 years before succumbing to obsolescence), this is not a burden. Once a researcher has gained entry to an organization to study its information systems, the greatest cost has been paid. Unless researchers have a propensity to be wearisome to their hosts, our grasp of IS effectiveness will benefit from extended research relationships.

The pay-off from this is the discovery that different things happen to information systems in the long term than are ever imagined in the short term. Potent revelations in social research have come from the longest studies: communities, organizations, cohorts of people over a lifetime. The same is surely true of IS effectiveness. IS researchers need multiple post-test observations to tap long-term effects of a system implementation such as learning curves and adaptive behaviour. Linson & Barclift (1988) urged follow-on research to their office automation study to see if a new system's efficiency, in itself, would provoke customers to make greater demands on it in years ahead.

## CONCLUSION

We said that we wanted to find out how much of IS effectiveness research is ready to move beyond taxonomies and correlates to support causal inference. Now we know: 83.7% of these empirical examinations of IS effectiveness cannot state, with confidence, that their findings are the result of causes claimed by the researchers. They lack a basis for disconfirming or accepting declarations of causal inference. They cannot be the foundation upon which to build formal theories of behaviour around IS effectiveness.

The import of our findings, by themselves, is elusive. There is no basis for believing that a similar review of, say, organizational psychology studies of employee satisfaction would reveal better handling of the time dimension or internal validity threats through research design. On the contrary, we suspect that our findings do not characterize IS effectiveness research alone. Far from it. Baroudi & Orlikowski (1989) reviewed the problems of research methodologies in the reference disciplines. Academic IS research inherited many of its shortcomings from management studies, which are prone to the same deficiencies we have lamented here [see, for example, Toronto (1975) criticized by Eoyang & Haga (1977)]. Bear in mind that Campbell & Stanley (1966) were instructing educational researchers and Denzin (1989) was addressing sociologists. Having borrowed behavioural theory concepts from our reference disciplines, Bariff & Ginzberg (1982) suggest that it is time that IS researchers started to stand on their own methodological feet. The most encouraging of our findings is that IS effectiveness research has reached a stage in supporting causal inference that gives us a basis for paying our debt of theory.

A concern of this paper has been to channel future research on IS effectiveness into research designs that will support causal inference. An ancillary aim has been to provide IS practitioners with a means to judge the soundness of IS research designs. Sound research designs can provide the data that can answer the managerial question: 'What is the pay-off from information systems?'.

William James Haga (PhD Illinois) is visiting the information systems faculty at the Naval Postgraduate School, Monterey, California, USA.
