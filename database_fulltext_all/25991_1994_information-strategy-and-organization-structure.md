# Information strategy and organization structure

E. Jordan

City Polytechnic of Hong Kong

Abstract. The information systems literature has taken little recognition of developments in the areas of organization structure and design. The concentration of professional practice has been on management information systems for 'big business' and 'book-keeping' systems for smaller businesses. This is a very limited view of the overall needs for information in organizations. Organizations may take many forms, and the nature of information systems that are needed will vary depending upon the particular form taken. This paper works from the organization structure literature towards a theory of information systems structure and strategy. Illustrative cases that support the hypotheses are described.

Keywords: alignment, configuration models, generic strategies, organization structure, strategy

## 1 INTRODUCTION

From the viewpoint of Williamson (1975), organizations come into their very existence because of information. He asserts that the uncertainty of the marketplace, characterized by information about transactions, drives individuals into forming or joining organizations, while the continuing uncertainty in the environment of the organization leads it to changes in its strategy and structure. This view reduces all business activity to transactions between individuals and groups, with information as the controlling resource. While such a simple and powerful mechanism, giving primacy to information and economic activity, is attractive, it is too simplistic and fails to deal with the real social, psychological and political settings of most organizations. A wider view of information may be obtained by looking at it from the organizational design or general management perspective.

Information technology has been widely credited with having significant impacts on the structuring and performance of organizations. A particular change has been the 'flattening' of organizational hierarchies by enhancing the functioning of management with better information and control processes. However, not all organizations are hierarchies. This paper investigates the relationship between the structural form of the organization and the potential application of information technology. This is related to, but quite distinct from, the problem of how to structure the information technology function itself.

Information is a recurring theme in the organizational literature. Organizational structure and design, organizational behaviour, the management process and decision making — in each area information, information flow and information technology have to be considered. While Bacon's dictum 'Knowledge itself is power' is frequently misquoted as 'information is power', it is commonly recognized that information is a source of power within the organization.

As a typical example of the importance of information in an organization, consider Galbraith's (1977) approach to organization design, which has given key areas of choice:

task;

structure;

information and decision processes;

people; and

reward systems.

Galbraith views each of these as variables and available for independent decisions, however the word ‘independent’ is not used in the statistical sense, since relationships between the areas are recognized. Indeed, Galbraith looks for and describes ‘optimal’ configurations in two or more of the five dimensions. Such approaches are seldom known to information systems designers, whose work will typically be quite isolated from the organizational designer. Galbraith fails to make the link across to information systems design. Miller (1986) who describes configurations of structure and strategy that are appropriate for particular environments, with information systems as one of the organizational variables, also fails to make the link. Mintzberg’s (1979) theories of organizational structure hold, that structure is dependent upon several environmental factors, which include, as intermediate forms, the flows of information within the organization — these will be explored below. Child (1984) also stresses the importance of information and information technology as driving forces in the design of effective organizations.

While the organizational theorists and strategists have included information systems in their domains, there has been some small incursion of wider organizational views in the information systems field. Structure is typically considered only at the stage of systems proposal or evaluation of implementation success. Davis (1974), for example, discusses the evaluation of information systems proposals using criteria of technical, economic and operational (organizational) feasibility. King's (1978) 'strategy set transformation' introduced the idea of alignment of information systems with organizational objectives, which Bowman et al. (1983) included in the first stage of their MIS planning model, while stopping short of dealing with organizational structure speciality. Markus and Robey (1983) list 'fit with the structure' as one of their four organizational validity components, and Mansour and Watson (1980) emphasize that organizational structure variables, particularly those concerning the organization of the MIS function itself, are the most important in predicting 'performance' of information systems in organizations.

Ein-Dor and Segev (1982) reviewed and extended the research on the relationship between the structure of the information systems function and the organizational context, including the size, age and degree of decentralization and organization. They reported that the degree of centralization of the MIS function was strongly correlated with that of the organization, with older and larger organizations being more centralized. Limitations in the research methodology are, however, extensive: the sampling frame was restricted to organizations with a mature and 'effective' MIS function in one geographical area; individual students carried out the study on one organization each without repeats or cross-checking. Samples were not randomly selected; there was no validation of the construct measurements and value scales were used extensively without attempts to standardize the multiple raters. Thus the results cannot be regarded as conclusive, merely indicative.

Leifer (1988) used Mintzberg's organizational forms in an attempt to match information systems structures to organizational structures. He placed information systems structures into four mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories, and proposed matches between these and organizational structural forms. This theoretical work was used by Burn (1989) in her empirical research into the effect of information technology on the structure of the organization in Hong Kong, with limited findings reported. Feeny et al. (1989a,b) also looked at matching organization structure to information systems architecture in large organizations.

Thus the fit between organizational structure and the information systems in an organization appears to be a valid area of investigation. The assessment of the fit may then serve as a component of information systems planning and business strategic planning, an area where alignment is now frequently sought. In particular, this may be seen as an extension of Miller and Mintzberg's (1988) work, which includes information systems planning. An organization's structure is held together by information; without information the organization cannot function. A model is developed that may be applied during information systems planning.

In the next section Mintzberg's basic concepts of organizational structure are reviewed, along with brief descriptions of the standard organizational forms. Readers familiar with Mintzberg's work may safely proceed to the next section, where general hypotheses relating organizational structure to information systems are developed. The last part of this paper will then demonstrate these hypothesized configurations by using illustrative cases.

## 2 STRUCTURAL FORMS OF ORGANIZATIONS

## 2.1 Basic parts

In his seminal work on organizational structures, Mintzberg (1979) breaks down the organization into five basic parts: the strategic apex, middle line, operating core, technostructure and support staff (Figure 1). The operating core carries out the elementary functions of production of the organization and is supervised directly by the middle line, while the strategic apex includes senior management and members of the board. The technostructure consists of analysts, technologists and other specialists (including IS staff) who are concerned with designing the work processes of others (mostly the operating core), generally to effect standardization. The support staff provide support to the organization outside the flow of productive work — everything from legal services to canteens, print room to cleaners.

## 2.2 Control mechanisms

Any of these five basic parts may become dominant, depending upon the control mechanism that is appropriate to the complexity and dynamic nature of the organization's environment and the interdependencies of the work tasks being undertaken in the operating core: what Mintzberg calls the ‘contingency factors’. The particular control mechanisms are:

direct supervision;

standardization of work processes;

standardization of outputs;

standardization of skills; and

mutual adjustment.

These five mechanisms then allow the strategic apex, technostructure, middle line, operating core and support staff, respectively, to become the dominant parts of the organization. For each of these, there is then a corresponding form of the organizational structure. Mintzberg names the characteristic organizational structures as simple structure, machine bureaucracy, divisionalized form, professional bureaucracy and adhocracy, respectively.

The choice of control mechanism is determined by the operating environment. Mintzberg proposes that the organization's structure, functioning and behaviour are affected by the following contingency factors:

age and size of an organization;

technical systems (production technology);

stability, complexity, hostility and market diversity of the environment;

locus of power (external, internal or fashion/culture).

Mintzberg's analysis relies on significant research results from the Aston programme, Khandwalla, Woodward and others (Mintzberg, 1979, Chs 13–16).

## 2.3 Simple structure

The simple structure is coordinated through direct supervision; the driving force is from the strategic apex attempting to centralize. The metaphor is centralization. This means that the person running the business makes most of the decisions and attempts to retain as much control as possible. This configuration is most commonly found in new or small organizations when the environment is simple and dynamic, or in larger ones where the environment is hostile. The technical (production) systems are typically simple, and do not have a regulating effect on the operational work.

The design characteristics include little training, indoctrination, specialization of jobs, formalization of behaviour, or planning and control, and few liaison devices. This general absence of formalism is complemented by organic functioning in a nevertheless centralized framework with wide span of control and functional groupings of personnel. As the organization grows it may drift towards machine bureaucracy if the work can be standardized and analysed (Mintzberg, 1988, p. 298).

## 2.4 Machine bureaucracy

The machine bureaucracy is coordinated through standardization of work processes; as the simple structure organization grows older and larger it becomes unmanageable by an individual. Analysts and technologists are employed to design, measure and control the work of others, so that their work may be coordinated by being standardized. Thus the organizing force is from the technostructure tending to standardize. The machine bureaucracy, as a form, arose in the last century through the emergence of very large organizations. Typical examples are fast-food restaurant chains, airlines and production line organizations. Characteristics of the machine bureaucracy are significant horizontal and vertical job specialization, much formalization of behaviour, bureaucratic (as opposed to organic) functioning, functional groupings and limited horizontal decentralization.

The machine bureaucracy operates in a stable, simple environment, where whatever change that is taking place is evolutionary so that adaptation is without difficulty. It flourishes when the source of power is technocratic or external, as in the familiar bureaucracies of government. The technical (production) systems will usually be regulating but neither sophisticated nor automated (Mintzberg, 1988, p. 298).

## 2.5 Professional bureaucracy

This organization is one in which the members of the operating core, the people actually doing the productive work, are highly skilled and highly trained. They are recognized as such by external organizations, such as professional associations. The other parts of the organization, such as the strategic apex and the technostructure, are weakened as a result. Hospitals, legal practices and educational institutions are typical examples. This type of organization is coordinated by the standardization of skills, where the operating core has a force for professionalism. It is characterized by much horizontal job specialization, much training and indoctrination and bureaucratic decentralization both horizontally and vertically.

Professional bureaucracy is called for whenever an organization finds itself in an environment that is stable yet complex. Complexity requires decentralization to highly trained individuals, and stability enables them to apply standardized skills and so to work with a good deal of autonomy. To ensure that autonomy, the production system must be neither highly regulating, complex, nor automated (Mintzberg, 1988, p. 299).

## 2.6 Divisionalized form

In much the same way that growth of the simple structure led to the development of the machine bureaucracy, growth of the machine bureaucracy by acquisition or diversification led to the divisionalized form. This, also known as the M-form organization, is the subject of Chandler's (1962) work, Rumelt's (1974) study and much other research. Williamson (1975) describes the divisionalized organization as having internalized the capital market so as to reduce that form of uncertainty. The divisionalized form is coordinated through standardization of outputs from the divisions, with the force from the middle line staff to establish their own territory, as almost antagonistic kingdoms. Characteristics are marked grouping (such as product and geographic divisions), much performance control and limited vertical decentralization. There are few liaison devices as the divisions operate as almost distinct businesses.

The technical systems are similar to those of the machine bureaucracy, because many divisional forms are like collections of bureaucracies, except that if the divisional form has developed through related industries the technical systems must be divisible between the units. The environment is simple and stable, although there may be diversified markets. Many American industrial organizations are today organized in this way. The power of the divisionalized form rests with the middle line, as the output of each division is under its own control, and it is this output that is standardized as the key coordinating mechanism (Mintzberg, 1988, p.299).

## 2.7 Adhocracy

The adhocracy exists in a complex and dynamic environment, in which highly trained people work together using cooperative methods such as liaison devices and organic interaction. The adhocracy is coordinated through mutual adjustment, where the characteristic force is from the support staff working to collaborate. In this case there is much horizontal specialization; organic, functional and market groupings; small unit size throughout; many liaison devices throughout; and selective decentralization. The metaphor is support.

The adhocracy structure appears in film crews, high-technology start-ups and other areas where many areas of expertise need to cooperate. Industrial organizations in which the production process is highly automated, such as petrochemicals and cigarettes, may become adhocracies when the support functions, such as marketing and legal services, overwhelm the rest of the organization. There are two distinct forms of adhocracy, depending upon whether its output is for customers or for the rest of the organization: the operating adhocracy and the administrative adhocracy respectively.

## 2.8 Missionary

In the later versions of his work Mintzberg suggests a sixth fundamental structural form (Mintzberg, 1988), in which the organization is driven by its mission alone and the key coordinating mechanism is the standardization of norms; this form is named the missionary. This is particularly characteristic of religious orders and other organizations not driven by the environment around them but more seeking to rearrange the environment. None of the people within the organization is the key component; that role is reserved for the ideology that it supports. In this paper the missionary form is not considered since our principal concern is with business organizations and the ideology or norms of the organization are dealt with by considering the corporate and national culture in which the organization finds itself. Also, many religious or similar organizations resemble professional bureaucracies in many ways, especially because what is regarded as training by some may be regarded as indoctrination by others. The functioning of nursing, teaching and other religious orders may be quite adequately considered as professional bureaucracies with enhanced (different) cultures.

## 3 GENERAL HYPOTHESES

## 3.1 Information variations with organizational structure

The fundamental types of organizational structure are now examined in order to define the generic information system that would be appropriate to them. Mintzberg (1979, p. 286) has a set of contingency factors determining the method of coordination, which in turn determines the organizational structure and the functioning of the organization. These contingency factors are the age and size of the firm, the technical system, the environment and the centre of power. As far as information technology is concerned, the contingency factors of most significance are the technical system and the environment. The technical system refers to the means of production, whether it is simple or sophisticated, and whether the technology of production is controlling or not. Fully automated production dispenses with the need for an operating core, while very simple production technology (cooking hamburgers) may be standardized. The environment has four key dimensions: stability, simplicity, market diversity and hostility. Stability leads to predictability, which in turn leads to standardization; simplicity leads to rationalization and analysis into easily comprehensible segments; increasing diversity of markets increases the range of work to be done; while hostility leads to unpredictability and the need for fast strategic actions. The relationship between the technical systems and information technology is through the information content of the technical system and of the product and the rate of change of the systems, as shown in Figure 3.

Thus, the four dimensions of environment and the two dimensions of technical system are all related to information technology. Environmental factors, technical systems and information technology are interrelated, as shown in Figure 4.

Mintzberg sees the traditional management information system as being a characteristic of the machine bureaucracy (Mintzberg, 1979, p. 342) and then, by extension, the divisionalized form (1979, p. 390). The need for control in both of these configurations, together with the simple, stable environment, leads to a significant 'system of regulated flows'. The technical (production) systems in the machine bureaucracy are regulating but neither automated nor very sophisticated (Mintzberg, 1979, p. 467), which limits the degree of penetration of information technology into the work process. In addition, the simple, stable environment suggests a 'factory' or 'support' role (McFarlan & McKenney, 1983), in which the strategic impact of the portfolio of future applications is low. Typically a machine bureaucracy is old and large, which would suggest that information technology may well have been established long ago, with evolution and maintenance being hallmarks. The MIS is a product of the technostructure, the component of the organization that is in the ascendant in the machine bureaucracy, whose mission is to measure, standardize and control through close monitoring.

The divisionalized form may frequently be regarded as a collection of machine bureaucracies (Mintzberg, 1979, p. 393) and so has all the above characteristics as well as its own significant addition of performance control imposed from divisional headquarters. Thus, the management information systems must also be collecting performance information; in fact, this will be most important. An additional significant factor for the divisionalized form is the divisibility of the technical systems, which also extends into the information systems. Interconnectivity between divisions is not normal; they should be able to stand alone. It is normal then for divisions to have quite separate systems; the output that is in common is reports on divisional performance, such reports representing only a small fraction of the quantity of data processed.

The other three forms do not have management information systems in the above, formal, sense. In the simple structure all decision flows are from the top down; the administrative work takes place in the strategic apex and the technical systems are both simple and non-regulating. There is no technostructure and an insignificant system of regulated flows. Centralization is the key, with little planning and control. The non-regulating technical systems suggest that information technology will neither control nor direct the operational work, nor will there be a large information component in the product or service. The dynamic and sometimes hostile nature of the environment may be attributable to information technology, but this will not intrude into the operational work. As all the administrative work takes place in the strategic apex, it is here that computer systems will be found, but they will be administrative, not controlling.

In the professional bureaucracy the technical systems in the operating core are neither regulating nor sophisticated, so the prime source of information for formal management information systems is weak. The environment is also stable, presenting few opportunities or threats from information technology. However, the environment is complex, which also insulates the organization from the effects of simple changes in technology. Mintzberg (1979, p.467) observes that the system of regulated control flows will be insignificant except for the support staff, which may well be organized as a machine bureaucracy. Each individual in the operating core will be carrying out skilled, standardized work with significant autonomy, so information technology may be used locally. With case-based work there may be an information flow that follows the cases, enabling the following professionals to do their standardized routine.

The adhocracy, like the professional bureaucracy, employs highly skilled staff, but their work is not standardized as it is project based. Within the project team are support, technostructure and operating core workers as well as middle line staff, all generally experts. If the operating core is automated, as in the 'administrative' adhocracy, only the other three areas will be involved. Typically coordination is mutual adjustment, with project group meetings being normal. Other liaison devices will be used throughout the organization in order to coordinate its activities. This is not the situation for formal bureaucratic information systems, in fact Mintzberg (1979, p. 467) notes significant informal information flows throughout such organizations. Within the liaison group, information technology may be used as a contribution from the techno-structure staff. The technical systems are very sophisticated but not regulating (the work of others) in the administrative adhocracy, and thus may involve significant information technology. In the other form of adhocracy, the 'operating' adhocracy, there is neither sophistication nor regulation from the technical systems, suggesting little opportunity for using information technology.

Trippi and Salameh (1989) examine the effect of the dynamic nature of the environment on the key application areas and the types of strategic information systems needed. They hypothesize that for more turbulent environments the emphasis should move away from transaction processing systems and management information systems towards decision support systems and expert systems/artificial intelligence applications. Unfortunately, while Trippi and Salameh's work may be used to contrast the machine bureaucracy and adhocracy structural forms, the range of validity is limited because only one environmental aspect (its dynamism) was considered. While their work makes few references to established theories it does support the broader suggestions of Mintzberg.

## 3.2 Hypothetical information structures

In the simple structure the generic computer system (note that this may be only a small part of the information system) is a small business system or turnkey system, typically minicomputer or microcomputer based. It will be running administrative, 'bookwork', applications, maintaining the central records of customers, products and activity. These applications are justified individually by having grown to such an extent that paper-based systems are inadequate. In other words, depending upon the number of customers, products, employees, etc., those 'lists' are automated. Once an initial system has been installed, additional applications are more easily justified. There is little pressure to integrate these applications unless cross-referencing is explicitly a critical area. As the market strategy varies then pressure builds up to develop particular applications. The computer system should be able to be operated by many staff, with the primary design and selection decisions coming from the chief executive officer, the individual who runs the whole organization. The organizational information system will be centred on the boss, with many parts of it only in his/her mind.

The generic computer system for the machine bureaucracy will be a wide-reaching transaction processing system (TPS), because computer input and output are excellent forces for standardization, the driving force of the machine bureaucracy. This TPS will feed into a formal management information system for the purposes of control. Each computer user will only access a limited number (perhaps only one) function within the system and be ignorant of others, through specialization. Individual subsystems need not necessarily be integrated as the important task is to standardize the individual function and only permit limited horizontal decentralization. Areas not computerized within the information system will be processed by forms. Routines for processing such forms will be precisely specified with rigid chains of command and authorization. The part of the technostructure that is in the ascendancy may well be the data processing or MIS department. The need to get management control information would have been paramount in the design of computer systems. There will be little organizational performance information produced by this MIS.

The generic computer system for the divisionalized form will be a series of distinct computer systems in each division and often in departments within the individual organization. The principal features in common will be the output required by head office. The exact nature of the output may not be constant; this represents more the function that the output is required to perform. The performance of the division needs to be monitored and then controlled. Headquarters will need dollar and volume performance data to be compared with previous periods, budgets and other divisions — which may be competitors. Some divisions may be ‘carbon-copy’ divisions, in which case headquarters will require identical systems. Pressure will exist for all computer systems to be management information systems, as management-level information is the only information required to ‘coordinate and control’ — as this takes place at the highest level. The force from the middle line managers to establish their own sovereignty will lead to distinct and separate systems whenever they can get away with it. The types of organizations making up the components (divisions) may be very diverse, in terms of both their own coordination processes and market positioning. This may then mean that the distinct divisions are able to have quite different systems. Headquarters may use some divisions for experimental systems, pilot studies or similar. In general, return on investment will be a frequently used criterion in computer system purchase.

The generic computer system for the professional bureaucracy will be one that does not reach the operating core. The training and indoctrination and specialization will create an atmosphere in which it becomes unthinkable to computerize at the core. Administration and support systems will exist in order to attempt to minimize costs in these 'overhead' areas, but few data will be gathered that will reflect on the performance of the operating core — their professionalism would resist this type of monitoring or control. A professional body of workers, operating on a human clientele, may encourage client support systems — not those that would displace the service of the professional but only those that would enable a specific professional to be selected. As professionals establish their status with their own professional organizations outside their employment domain, links with similar professionals in outside organizations or with the professional bodies would be attractive. These are links not for carrying out operational activity but for enhancing professionalism.

The generic computer system for the adhocracy is the network, which reflects the group support metaphor. It may be a local area network to support the small-size unit that is focused on a particular function or market, or a larger network to facilitate liaison and coordination among larger groupings. These networks mimic, supplement and support the human networks that are an essential part of the adhocracy. The growing popularity of the adhocracy is shown by the increase in interest in computer-supported collaborative work (CSCW) and group decision support systems (GDSS). The software on the network is not specified, although there is a need for some transferability and transportability of information. The criterion for selection of the software should be the extent to which it supports and enhances the performance of the team, in other words the software may have to be regarded as another team member. Thus the hardware network mirrors the human networks that are the characteristic of this organizational form. An administrative adhocracy will have an information system incorporated in the automated production process. This may be as explicit information or as control and monitoring information built into hardware devices.

These information systems and structures are summarized briefly in Table 1. While the table may be more convenient, it is the more detailed descriptions above that form the hypotheses.

Just as Mintzberg recognizes that many organizations do not adopt ‘perfect’ standard structural forms, so these generic descriptions will only indicate broad parameters of design. Particular organizations may be variants or combinations of structural forms, with information attributes varying correspondingly.

## 4.1 Simple structure

The low-price strategy adopted by MechElec (company names have been disguised in this section, but are real cases), a work tool and work tool consumables company, requires it to have large numbers of customers and products in order to survive. Stocks, sales and customer activity need to be monitored closely for volume, cost exceptions (discount tracking) and optimum stock levels. The monitoring is done by the business owner and his direct assistants. In addition, as landed net cost must also be minimized, an executive-level information system comparing freight rates, alternative suppliers, import costs, duties and currency exchange rates is an essential part of the information system. This activity takes place at a much lower level in a larger organization but here remains an activity in the hands of the entrepreneurial individual who drives the company. It would certainly not fit the title of 'executive information system' elsewhere, merely part of the purchasing function. In MechElec, where buying and selling are conducted on very slim margins in order to attempt to be the lowest cost, this part of the information system is critical to the success of the organization, although it may not be implemented in a formal part of the computer system. It is, in effect, a small machine bureaucracy, in a market place which is not large enough to support a bigger company. The information systems are all in support of the strategic apex, where all important decisions are made.

## 4.2 Machine bureaucracy

The machine bureaucracy in the minimum net cost marketplace is the epitome of this organizational structure. The standardization of work processes is one that leads easily to the attempt to minimize costs. A widely known example is McDonald's fast-food chain, where processes are formally standardized so that in all countries, in all branches, the product will be uniform and low cost. The information system operation is standardized, as is each worker's activity. Each person's task definition includes formalized information handling. This has no meaning other than 'it has to be done as part of the job'. Managers at the individual restaurant collect data for their reports, which will include local control information as well as data required at a higher level. Everything works because everyone follows the routines. The routines are designed by the technostructure, here comprising food technologists as well as economists and information systems specialists.

Machine bureaucracies do not always aim for lowest costs; an organization such as a three-star hotel would be an appropriate example. The computerized part of the information system extends to the limit of technology that is cost justifiable, which means seldom being the innovator in new information technology applications but simply adopting them as soon as the cost is low enough. The three-star status requires that standards of service, including technologically based services, must be at the norm or better. The added value is created by offering certain services that are the standard for the most exclusive and expensive hotels. Some of these services may be information technology based, such as rapid check-out. The bureaucracy is affirmed by the standardization of activities, facilities and work processes in the hotel. The information system is overwhelmingly a transaction processing system with standardized routines to be used by individual operators. A more up-market hotel chain will use information technology whenever it can offer a distinguishing service, in particular 'keeping ahead of the pack'. Elements of the information system will be designed to monitor service level, as this is a critical issue. Information technology may assist in the attraction of customers, through links to other up-market service industries, offering the potential customer an integrated package of (high-cost) leisure, finance and personal services. Timing of the introduction of information-based services is critical as a distinguishing feature.

A particularly interesting case is Mrs Fields, Inc., whose world headquarters has established standardized information systems for each of its biscuit shops. The additional use of electronic mail enables this machine bureaucracy to be run like a simple structure (Melymuka, 1990). All the work is standardized but the technostructure is simply part of the strategic apex.

## 4.3 Divisionalized form

A regional waste collection service (in which the author was responsible, as a consultant, for IT review), part of a national transport company, attempts to offer low costs by maximizing collection efficiency, routing and disposal. Information technology could assist through management science applications such as route scheduling and pricing algorithms. Given that the headquarters requirements are only for high-level, summarized information, it is more valuable from headquarters' point of view to go for carbon-copy systems in all similar units. The optimization of routing creates localized units in different areas, each of which headquarters will require to operate an identical system (with the local manager fighting the move). It will also be valuable to integrate widely across applications, but this may be particularly difficult to accomplish through the efforts of middle managers to rule their own territory. Headquarters derives information benefits from having identical systems within each unit, so in this case the purchase was encouraged by a deep discount subsidized by headquarters.

## 4.4 Professional bureaucracy

Tertiary educational institutions in Hong Kong can be seen to span a range of market positions. Cost-effectiveness is a characteristic of the polytechnic education sector both in Great Britain and in Hong Kong. The University of Hong Kong is the established front-runner, while the recently established University of Science and Technology is focused on a narrower target population. The degree of focusing is measured by the number of courses or number of disciplines that are available for study, while costing is determined by the funding levels received per student from the central funding body, the Universities and Polytechnics Grants Commission.

In all of these organizations, there is no clear management information system. The formal information systems that do exist encroach little on the normal working patterns of the 'operating core', the academic, lecturing staff. Class lists are produced for their guidance and results sheets need to be submitted to examination boards and student records systems, but otherwise there is little link between the academics and the computer systems that administer the organization. Educational institutions compete to attract the 'best' students by offering two items: status and course specialization. Some institutions may offer a range of specializations sufficient to attract the numbers, while the elite institutions have more funds available to carry out activities that will enhance status. The low-cost organizations, the polytechnics, are also able to achieve their lower costs by applying economies of scale. Increases in student enrolment lead to smaller increases in academic workloads and administration loads if suitable strategies are adopted. This will then free up more funds for some status-enhancing activities.

This gives some indication of where information systems fit into an educational institution. The polytechnics need to reduce unit operating costs through economies of scale, so administrative systems and clerical, operating procedures need to be run with machine bureaucratic style. These will be extended as far as possible into the academic areas, so that staff may support larger numbers of students. Examples include class administration, timetabling and grade processing. Notice that direct computer control of classes or the educational process is generally unacceptable. Computer-aided instruction, while technically possible for a long time, has been very slow to grow. It is against the ‘professional’ ethos. Status in educational institutions is generally achieved through research, which information technology may support indirectly, through electronic mail, and directly, with dedicated computing resources for research purposes. These results were obtained in a pilot study of the research framework in educational organizations in Hong Kong (Jordan, 1989).

## 4.5 Adhocracy

It is particularly common that the adhocracy takes a market niche as its target. This is attractive in focusing the expert team in its product design and development effort. This market niche may also be defined by a low cost, such as the attempt to bring a very new technology into a particular area of application where the existing method or product is expensive. Thus the aim is to introduce new, high technology into an existing area, where the competition will be on the basis of cost rather than functionality. This is also a characteristically entrepreneurial area (Jordan, 1992). It is extremely risky: you may be beaten in the race to release the product, the product may not work as anticipated or the development costs could eat up the variable capital before any results are achieved. On the up-side, you may define a new market, be the new standard or your product brand name could be the word used to describe the generic product (and besides you could make a fortune). The competition and development of computer memory from drum and core to high-density dynamic random access memory (DRAM) has been always at the leading edge of technology but also with the cost per unit of memory as the most significant characteristic. The teams that work on such products need sophisticated prototyping facilities and modelling and design tools that will give them competitive precision. The research teams need every assistance to be able to 'go the extra mile' in bringing a product to commercial reality from the laboratory. High-technology support will be much in evidence.

On the other hand, the adhocracy with a speciality market for its products or services has a smaller number of customers but is freed from the constraint of needing to make the low-cost product. The product may well be distinguished on the basis of new technology bringing new functionality. This is a scenario which is very familiar, including the introduction of such products as audio compact disc, video cassette and the personal computer. In each case a new technology was brought to the market place, defining a new product and eventually a new set of standards. The costs in bringing the product to market were great, as were the potential profits. Problems exist in many areas, including bringing a new technology to fruition, defining and marketing a new product. These many special problems may be assisted wherever possible by the available information technology, but there is no overriding mission for the IT itself apart from enhancing the teamwork (Jordan, 1992).

## 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The hypotheses describe relationships between organizational structure and information systems opportunities. Descriptions of generic information systems for organizations with relatively pure forms of organizational structure are given. Organizations with mixtures of basic forms or complex structures will need information systems that vary accordingly, with the underlying basic forms of Mintzberg as the guide. While these hypotheses are not yet fully tested, they are examined by the consideration of various cases. A research programme is in progress. The goal of the research is to develop tools which may be used by information systems strategic planners to facilitate more effective strategic information systems planning (SISP).

The cases described above highlight the differences between information needs that are attributable to organization structure. However, in some sections the variety of cases illustrate the need for a second dimension of analysis. This second dimension needs to reflect the market strategy of the organization, which leads to major differences between organizations having similar structures. It has been proposed (Jordan, 1989) that appropriate analysis may be performed using Porter's (1985) competitive strategy model. This need is highlighted by the scant mention of the customer and product in the above analysis, except when referring to market needs.

Few textbooks that are used to support the teaching of the information systems refer to the work of Mintzberg in organizational structure. It represents a clear approach to the alignment of information systems and organizational structure that would enhance the design of systems that are produced. With an increasing pressure for organizations to become more entrepreneurial, systems need to be put in place that do more than simply control, coordinate and reduce costs. Incorporation of the Mintzberg's basic concepts of organizational structure into information systems design courses will be a step towards improving the understanding of the organizational context of information systems.
