# Going to work on an Apple: factors affecting microcomputer use in business organizations

M. R. Jones

Management Studies Group, Department of Engineering, University of Cambridge, Mill Lane, Cambridge CB2 1RX, UK

Abstract. The use of computers in organizations may be influenced by both technical and social factors. In addition, the way in which these factors operate in any specific organization may be affected by the particular type of computer being used. This paper reports the results of semistructured interviews with users of Apple Macintosh computers in three different organizations. The relevance of factors identified in earlier interviews with academic Macintosh users to the use of microcomputers in the business context is considered. It was found that, while there was considerable individual variation in attitudes to microcomputer use, many of the factors identified in the earlier study were also present in the business organizations. Some reasons for the attitudes found are discussed.

Keywords: Apple Macintosh, microcomputers, social and organizational factors, technical factors, user perceptions.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Clegg (1992) argues that research on the use of computers tends to fall into two distinct categories: the human-computer interaction (HCI) literature, which typically considers the detail of the interaction processes of individual users; and the organizational/management literature, which is usually concerned with the use of computer-based information systems (IS) within a broader organizational context. It may be argued, however, that the use of computers in practice involves both elements. Technology has certain characteristics which may affect individual patterns of use, and this may be mediated by the influence of organizational context. There is a need, therefore, for research which seeks to integrate the two perspectives.

Both the HCI and organizational literature, however, generally neglect the influence of the specific type of computer on user perceptions and behaviour. From the HCI standpoint it is assumed that it makes no difference which sort of computer is used, providing that it offers the required functionality, whereas from the IS standpoint the computer is seen simply as part of a system to perform some application. There is substantial anecdotal evidence, however, to suggest that all computers are not equal in the eyes of the user and that certain brands are regarded differently from others. While there are certainly fans of Sun workstations, Amstrad

PCWs or even Spectrum ZX80s, the Apple Macintosh appears to have attracted a particularly loyal following, especially among non-traditional computer users, as a number of writers have noted (Poynor, 1990; Curry, 1991). Given the continuing growth of computer use in organizations and the development of microcomputers as a consumer product, both directly and in their marketing to organizations, then these brand effects may be expected to have an increasing importance in future.

To date, however, there has been little empirical research which has attempted to understand the nature of users' perceptions of specific computer systems, or to relate these individual perceptions both to the technical qualities of the technology and to features of the organizational context within which they are being used. An earlier study (Jones, 1990) sought to identify a range of technical and organizational factors affecting the use of Apple Macintosh microcomputers among academics. However, the small sample size of that study (10) and possibly atypical situation of academics as microcomputer users laid the generalizability of the results open to question. It was therefore decided to carry out a follow-up study among industrial and commercial users of Apple Macintoshes to investigate the extent to which the factors identified in the earlier study could be carried over into the business context.

## 2 RESEARCH METHOD

## 2.1 Company selection

Contacts were established with three companies with a known commitment to Macintosh use operating in substantially different economic sectors. In two cases the Macintosh user group was a subunit of a larger company in which other types of microcomputer system were used in other units.

The three sites selected were: company A, the insurance division of a multinational; company B, a division within an R&D establishment with an interest in human-computer interaction; and company C, a publisher. In each case contact was made via a senior individual in the company or division who identified suitable candidates for interview. The choice of candidates was made as varied as possible, encompassing a range of roles, skills, experience and seniority within the company. Most of the interviewees were using standard applications packages with System 6 on Mac SEs and Classics and Mac IIs with large screens.

## 2.2 The interviews

A total of 20 semi-structured interviews were held with Macintosh users in the three different companies. The interviews took place in the users' workplace and lasted approximately 1 hour. A number of topics were covered in the interviews as shown in Table 1. The topics discussed were identified from the earlier academic study, but interviewees were encouraged to expand on comments they made, and to discuss any other issues relating to their use of the Macintosh which they considered important.

## 3 TECHNICAL FACTORS

A conclusion of the earlier paper was that the technical quality of the interface was closely associated with social factors. For example, perceptions of the quality of the user interface were associated with positive attitudes toward Apple as a company committed to design quality. The influence of technical factors on user perceptions will therefore be considered separately before moving on to a discussion of social/organizational factors.

## 3.1 The quality of the interface

As with the academic users, the quality of the Macintosh interface was commented on positively by most interviewees, with almost half specifically identifying ease of use as the most significant factor in their evaluation of the Macintosh. Several also cited the ability of their young children, or of friends with no computing experience, to learn to use the computer in a very short space of time as evidence of the quality of the interface in this respect.

A number of interviewees mentioned the visibility of actions and options as positive features. The visibility of actions was related to the direct manipulation of icons, which created (apparently) a close correspondence between the users' actions and the task they wished to carry out. The visibility of options concerned the continuous accessibility of available commands via the menus. This visibility was linked by interviewees to two other important features of the interface: its intuitive nature and its consistency. The desktop and most, though not all, Macintosh applications were felt to be fairly intuitive, and hence easy to use. The consistency of the 'look and feel' across different applications was also said to make it easier to learn new applications by creating a sense of familiarity. In consequence, deviations from the standard conventions were highly conspicuous and led to strong criticism and a poor opinion of 'offending' applications.

The potential to control the appearance of the screen was mentioned as another advantage of the Macintosh. This was seen in two ways. The first, and more widely mentioned, was the ability to customize the layout of files and windows to suit an individual's preferences and 'personality'. For example, some interviewees liked to have the screen filled with many active files, while others operated a strict control of layout, locating particular types of files in different areas of the screen. The other aspect of the appearance of the screen was the use by some interviewees of customized start-up screens, sounds and backgrounds to individualize their computer. A number of interviewees reported that they had experimented with such customization in their early stages of Macintosh use but had quickly reverted to the standard interface.

In the academic study concern had been expressed regarding the suitability of the Macintosh interface for use by experienced users of command-based systems, and it was suggested by one interviewee in the present study that there might be limitations in the use of Macintoshes by experienced secretaries with speed typing skills. One person with extensive $L^{AT}_{E}X$ experience did express reservations about the comparative ease of use of Macintosh and command-based systems for document preparation, and another whose work involved almost exclusively the use of spreadsheets found the combination of mouse and keyboard ‘physically a nuisance’ to use. The two secretaries interviewed, however, were generally very favourable toward Macintoshes, particularly when fully familiar with keyboard alternatives to use of the mouse.

## 3.2 Physical qualities

Comments on the physical appearance of the Macintosh were less frequent among business users than academics. This may be attributable to the absence of design experts among the business user group interviewed. Several interviewees, though, expressed positive views about the small size of the original Macintosh, arguing that this made it less obtrusive on the desk and reasonably transportable (either for home working or for off-site demonstrations). However, this was more than matched by a greater number of adverse comments about the small screen size. One technical expert in company B described it as 'intolerable', and in many cases some or all users in the company had upgraded to A4 and even A3 screens.

The closed box design of the early Macintoshes also attracted mixed comments. Most of these suggested that this was a drawback of the Macintosh, closing off opportunities for third-party hardware add-ons and restricting the potential of the system. One interviewee argued that this had been a crucial strategic error on the part of the Apple company which had held back widespread adoption of the Macintosh in the business community. This view contrasted with another interviewee, who suggested that it would have been a mistake to have made the Macintosh more sophisticated and flexible and that the closed design had enabled Apple to avoid major compromises and to produce a machine which fulfilled its (limited) range of tasks very well.

## 3.3 Presentation quality

A factor mentioned by most interviewees as being a positive aspect of Macintosh use was the quality of the output. In some cases this could be attributed to the use of a laser printer with a

Macintosh compared with the dot-matrix or daisy-wheel printers which had been used with previous systems. However, for most users there was also felt to be a substantial improvement in the presentation quality which could be achieved using the Macintosh with alternative fonts and font sizes and greater control over layout. The high-resolution screen and 'what-you-see-is-what-you-get' character of applications were also felt to facilitate the achievement of high standards of presentation, although one or two more experienced users were concerned that the implementation of these features had its limitations. For example, screen resolution did not match printer resolution, and printed output was often subtly different from the screen display.

## 3.4 Performance and reliability

Other technical factors mentioned in several interviews were the performance speed of the Macintosh and the reliability of hardware and software. Many of the more experienced users expressed reservations about the 'lack of processing power' of the Macintosh (even if they themselves used top of the range models). It was suggested that this was usually masked in the initial stages by 'amazement' at what you could do and how easy the Macintosh was to use. Once users began to explore the capability of the system, however, they were often disappointed at the slow performance and lack of genuine multitasking, although this was usually recognized to be a product of rising expectations and the increasing complexity of the applications in which they were being used.

Experience of the reliability of the Macintosh was polarized, with one company reporting themselves exceptionally satisfied with hardware reliability, while the other two expressed concern at high failure rates for some hardware items. Some interviewees also questioned the reliability of some software 'at the limits'.

## 4 SOCIAL ASPECTS OF COMPUTER USE

## 4.1 Changing attitudes to computer use

The length of time that interviewees had been using Macintoshes varied widely, from more than 5 years to less than 5 months. The range of previous experience of computers also varied widely, with some having worked with computers for more than a decade on a variety of systems, while for others the Macintosh was their first computer. Not surprisingly, therefore, there was a diversity of responses to questions concerning changing attitudes to computer use, and for some their memory of their attitudes prior to Macintosh use were inevitably rather distant.

## 4.1.1 Controlling technology

The initial response to use of a Macintosh was described by several interviewees as being one of 'shock' or 'surprise' at being able to use the system so easily. Expectations built up from previous systems or received views of the ‘difficulty’ of computer use were discovered to be unfounded. The ease of use and the familiarity of new applications provided by the consistency of the interface helped to overcome the fears of novice users and created a sense of control over the technology. There appeared to be two aspects to this sense of control, as illustrated in the discussion of controlling screen appearance above: docility, an ability to get the computer to do what one wants; and pliability, an ability to adapt the system to one’s own requirements (Jones, 1990). Combined with the small size of the original Macintosh, this led two interviewees in company B to describe the Macintosh as ‘meek’, ‘non-threatening’ and ‘safe’.

The sense of control was felt by interviewees to be an important source of motivation to use computers in their work, making them, as one interviewee described it, 'enticing' and encouraging them to explore the opportunities for doing more and more of their work with the Macintosh. This 'snowballing' of Macintosh use was particularly marked in company C, where financial constraints were the primary factor controlling the growth in the number of users. This was contrasted with the situation in another branch of the same company, where PC-compatible based computing had not taken off in anything like the same way.

The greater personal control over their use of the Macintosh was also a positive factor, enabling the interviewees to have more control over their own work without being dependent on computer specialists or corporate computing facilities. For example, data could now be prepared at their own desks when it suited them and with easy-to-use applications rather than having to move to an unreliable mainframe terminal with a very primitive user interface. Most interviewees welcomed this greater autonomy.

## 4.1.2 Engagement with the work task

The ease of use of the system was described by several interviewees as providing a transparency of interaction which made the computer ‘invisible’ in regular work tasks (although one interviewee disagreed and said that awareness of the applications was never entirely lost). This sense of direct engagement with the task was compared by one interviewee to driving a car, with the necessary actions becoming ‘second nature’ in most situations. Another described the use of the Macintosh for word-processing as a ‘magic typewriter’.

## 4.1.3 Fear of freedom

In the business context the ease of use and greater user control of Macintosh systems created potential problems, particularly for system managers, with some interviewees arguing that the Macintosh was almost ‘too easy to use’.

There were several areas in which greater user autonomy was a source of concern. In the learning of new applications, many interviewees stated that they rarely used the manuals and learnt to use applications either by trial and error or by seeking assistance from informal experts, usually within their own office. Concern was expressed that this could lead to suboptimal use of applications. In addition, the scope for damage to the system, whether deliberately or by accident, was seen to be increased by encouraging inexperienced users with a possibly exaggerated view of their own competence and a very shallow understanding to develop their knowledge by trial and error.

Another area of concern was in the preparation of documents, where a proliferation of styles was observed. Some interviewees also expressed concern at the amount of time which could be 'wasted' in learning new applications by trial and error and, as one person put it, the 'compulsion towards perfectionism' in the presentation of documents.

In all three companies there was felt to be a need for greater standardization to maintain compatibility, to impose appropriate design principles and to ensure that applications were used to their full potential. In two of the companies substantial training programmes had been initiated to address these issues.

## 4.1.4 The Macintosh as a non-computer

A feature of the study of academics was their characterization of the Macintosh as a non-computer. Although this was less marked in the business organizations, a number of interviewees expressed the view that the Macintosh was in some ways not a real computer. This was also mentioned as the view of other organization members who did not use Macintoshes, often in a disparaging sense.

Most interviewees used the Macintosh solely for applications, and very few attempted any programming on it (and then mostly in Hypertalk), including those for whom programming was a major element of their work. Since, in many people's minds, programming was seen as a defining aspect of computing, its absence as a feature of Macintosh use served to differentiate the Macintosh from real computers. The black-box nature of the Macintosh was also seen as making it less computer-like as experienced computer users were used to being able to observe and understand what was 'going on inside the system'.

Other characteristics of the Macintosh which were mentioned as being un-computer-like were its ease of use (one interviewee suggested that 'computing' had to be 'difficult, irritating') and the transparency of the interface, which led to it becoming seen as just 'part of the furniture' or as a 'tool' to get on with the task in hand. In contrast to the academic users, however, most business users felt that they never entirely lost their awareness of its being a computer. This may have been influenced by the fact that Macintosh use was not discretionary in the business organizations.

## 4.2 The experience of Macintosh use

The interviews sought to investigate not just the practical aspects of Macintosh use, but also their affective aspects.

## 4.2.1 Fun

More than half the interviewees mentioned that they enjoyed using the Macintosh or described its use as 'fun', although some said that this was limited to the initial stages of use of a new application. A number also referred to 'playing' with the computer either deliberately through the installation of games on their work machine or as a description of their process of learning new applications. This was seen by interviewees as a useful source of motivation to increase their knowledge. In only one company, however, was this considered a legitimate feature of Macintosh use. In the other two, security concerns led to a ban on games to shield the network from viruses and unauthorized access.

There were mixed views about the humorous side of the Macintosh interface, such as monkey or Harry Secombe sound effects, with some interviewees regarding it as an irritant and others mentioning it as a positive feature (although one of these also admitted that the humour could rapidly wear thin — if not for the user, then for his or her colleagues). In the group in which there was management approval of ‘whimsical’ use of Macintoshes, however, the use of humour was felt to be an effective way to encourage users to ‘feel that the computer is theirs’, even though it was not felt to affect productivity either way.

## 4.2.2 Aesthetics

The quality of output achievable has been mentioned previously as a technical feature of the Macintosh. However, a number of interviewees also identified this as a feature of their feelings and Macintosh use. The ability to produce what they regarded as ‘aesthetically pleasing’ documents was valued by users, enabling them to achieve pride in their work. This had led several users in the publishing company to take on responsibilities for the production of publicity, which they would previously have sent out to specialist designers, and this had produced a very substantial improvement in the interviewees’ productivity and job satisfaction. There was also admiration for the design of the computer itself, which was described approvingly by several interviewees as ‘clean’.

## 5 THE ORGANIZATIONAL CONTEXT OF MACINTOSH USE

The main contrast between academic and business users of Macintoshes may be expected to be in the influence of the organizational context on use.

## 5.1 The politics of Macintosh use

The adoption of a new computer system involves the utilization of organizational resources not just of money, but also of equipment, staff time and managerial skills and attention. These resources are the subject of continuing contestation within organizations. The introduction of the Macintosh within the organizations studied may therefore be seen to have had political consequences.

## 5.1.1 Introducing the Macintosh

The process of introducing the Macintosh varied between different companies. The division of company A recognized the need for increased business use of computers and appointed a new manager with responsibility for information technology; Macintoshes were installed all at once, the strategy being justified on the basis of speed of successful take-up by users. In company B there was initially a steady growth of individual Macintosh use justified on technical grounds as the individuals were human-computer interaction researchers and needed to study the most up-to-date interfaces. Once this had created a 'critical mass' of users on which to justify the switch, an initiative by the head of the division was able to get the Macintosh adopted as the divisional standard. In company C Macintoshes were initially introduced for secretarial use, but were taken up with such alacrity by other staff that it was decided to adopt them as the microcomputer standard and to reorganize the organizational computing strategy around them.

In both companies A and B there was also an element of opportune timing in the introduction of Macintosh systems. In company A, a change in top management had led to a major corporate restructuring exercise aimed at reducing hierarchies and using technology to assist organizational development. In company B, the availability of sufficiently sophisticated Macintosh models for the business environment coincided with the phasing out of a corporate office automation system.

## 5.1.2 Resistance to introduction

In company B the introduction of Macintoshes was a gradual process over a period of more than 3 years. The initial adopters of Macintoshes were therefore bringing in a new and non-standard machine and faced resistance from users of the old systems then in use. This led to difficulties in purchasing Macintoshes and a lack of technical support for their integration with organizational systems. Only when Macintoshes had been made the divisional standard did this change. Some resistance from other groups in the organization continued, however, especially from those with a more technical orientation.

Resistance may also arise within the group. Two types of user could be identified from the interviews as being less enthusiastic about Macintosh use: older, often more senior staff with no computing experience (who basically did not want to use computers at all) and technically oriented staff with considerable experience of other systems (who preferred these, usually more powerful, systems). Some of the concerns in both cases could be attributed to fears of deskilling. As one technical expert put it '[seeing how easy it was for novice users] scared the hell out of me, because I thought what's the use of me learning to use computers if you can learn them that way?' (although this was seen as a positive feature rather than a criticism). For the staff with no computer experience the view was expressed that their skills should not and, they argued, could not be computerized and that the Macintosh was therefore largely irrelevant to their work.

This resistance may not involve overt refusal to use the Macintosh. With technical experts it was typically expressed as technical reservations about the performance of the machine, especially the perceived lack of processing power, and in disparaging remarks describing it as a 'Noddy' machine or not a 'serious' computer. For the inexperienced users resistance was often expressed in terms of difficulties in using the computer and lack of time to learn.

## 5.1.3 The Macintosh as a symbol

The Macintosh also appeared to play a number of symbolic roles in organizational power relations. In some cases this related to other tensions within the organization, rather than to specific characteristics of the Macintosh itself. For example, resistance to Macintosh use could be linked to resistance to other organizational changes associated with the introduction of the Macintosh or other animosities between Macintosh proponents and resisters. In other areas, however, the particular features of the Macintosh were the focus of attention.

The resistance to Macintosh use by technical experts described above had some important symbolic aspects. Both technical experts interviewed in company B stated that 'real programmers don't use Macintoshes'. Despite the jocular tone of the remark it may be considered to express real sentiments. Resistance to Macintosh use could therefore be seen a sign of allegiance to the programming community. This was illustrated in the comment of one technical user. 'I've seen more and more people being interviewed [on television], people generally in a position of influence, and they seem always to have a Macintosh in the background. You never see a Sun or a Symbolics behind them ... [but] you do see those sort of things on a Horizon [science] programme'.

For most of the human-computer interaction researchers in company B, however, the Macintosh was a positive symbol, illustrating their commitment to 'practise what they preach'. In some cases symbolism acted at the group rather than the individual level. For example interviewees in company A described the Macintosh as 'advanced', 'modern' and 'trendy', and saw its use as symbolizing their division's technological sophistication and superiority. In the other companies, groups with high levels of Macintosh use also felt themselves to be 'ahead of the field', and this was considered to contribute to group identity and cohesion.

Another interesting symbolic aspect of Macintosh use identified by one interviewee was the role of screen size as an organizational status symbol, with senior staff having larger screens. It was also suggested by two interviewees in different companies that the presentation quality achievable with the Macintosh gave documents produced on it a greater credibility with decision-makers even though their content might be no different from one produced by hand or on a different computer system.

## 5.2 The practice of Macintosh use

Once adopted, computer systems contribute to the production and reproduction of organizational practice. The interviews suggest that this can work both for and against the use of Macintoshes in organizations.

Organizational practice in computer use becomes institutionalized through the adoption of standards. In the early stages of the introduction of the Macintosh this was a significant factor in deterring more rapid uptake by individuals, especially in company B, where PC-compatibles were, and still are in some other divisions, the standard. Interviewees concerned professionally with standards and with a need to maintain compatibility with other colleagues within the organization were especially affected.

Having become adopted as a standard, however, the Macintosh then becomes institutionalized itself. A number of interviewees described themselves as non-discretionary users and stated that their use of the Macintosh was primarily determined by the fact that it was 'available' and that 'having got it, I wanted to use it'. The standard served to reinforce Macintosh use by ensuring that new users adopted Macintoshes if they wished to be compatible with their colleagues. In addition, the introduction of Macintosh-based email systems increased the costs of non-use and incompatibility. In time, the standard also leads to the development of a reservoir of specialized expertise for users to draw on if problems arise and to a group of users committed to Macintoshes by familiarity and their investment in learning to use them.

## 5.3 Macintosh culture

The perception of the Macintosh as having a distinctive culture was less marked among the business users than among academics, with more emphasis being placed on its functional performance, although some of the symbolic aspects of the Macintosh discussed above do reveal some influential associations. Interviewees' images of the Apple computer company, however, did reveal some similar perceptions to those identified by academics.

Words associated by interviewees with Apple included creative, innovative, user-centred, whacky and youthful. These were seen as part of an ‘American West coast’ culture with an emphasis on individualism and self-expression, as exemplified by the presence of individual developers’ names in the information about software. Apple was also felt to be a very unified company, and this ‘togetherness’ was liked by one interviewee as paralleling the atmosphere within his own company. Interestingly, another interviewee described Apple as very secretive and identified it as similar to his own company in this respect. Generally, though, Apple was seen in a positive light, as ‘the good guys … on your side’, particularly when compared with other computer manufacturers.

Only about a quarter of the interviewees had any detailed knowledge about the Apple company. Most of the users in company A had seen an Apple corporate video as part of their training, and one or two individuals in companies B and C had read several articles and books about Apple and were very knowledgeable about its history. The more knowledgeable interviewees tended to be rather more cynical about the Apple culture, and some felt that the company had become much more ‘corporate’ in recent years. However, this did not seem to have dented their enthusiasm for the Macintosh, but rather was seen as a necessary step in the development of any major company.

## 6 DISCUSSION

The evidence of both this study and the earlier one of academics (Jones, 1990) would appear to support the findings of Margono & Shneiderman (1987) and Diagnostic Research Inc. (undated) that suggested that the ease of use of the Macintosh, which they identified primarily as a product of the interface design, leads to greater user satisfaction and to the use of a greater diversity of applications. Although no direct assessment of user satisfaction and range of application use was made in this study, managers in both companies A and C strongly emphasized the positive effect of Macintosh use on these issues. In company B, reactions were more mixed, perhaps because the users were generally more experienced in computing and had used other, often more powerful, systems in aspects of their work. They were therefore more aware of the limitations of the Macintosh and had prior experience of a variety of applications on different machines.

The findings also provide evidence of variation in users' reactions to the technical qualities of the interface. For example, attitudes towards direct manipulation varied depending on users awareness of, and concern with, the 'deceptiveness' of its representation of technical functionality. Most users believed that they had greater control over the computer, at least for the purposes for which they used it, and did not question whether this was technically the case. We do not claim that such findings are new; indeed, in some cases they reflect deliberate decisions of the original designers (Kay, 1990). They are reported, however, as observations of the interviewees about their perceptions of Apple Macintosh use.

What these studies have also illustrated though, is that the technical quality of the interface interacts with a complex set of social and organizational factors which influence the attitudes of users towards computers and their role in their work. Such phenomena are not addressed in traditional analyses of usability (e.g. Ravden & Johnson, 1989), which therefore give a partial picture of the factors affecting use. There would appear to be a number of aspects to this. The first relates to user control of technology.

The relationship between user and technology is not socially neutral. Shallis (1984) develops the argument of Ellul (1964) that in modern societies technology is seen as sacred, to suggest that we have created a 'silicon idol', that computers have become venerated as symbols of technical rationality. The language associated with computing (such as 'artificial intelligence' with its suggestions of superhuman capabilities) may also serve to reinforce users' feelings of inferiority with respect to information technology. Another source of user concern may be computer culture which, with its jargon and plethora of acronyms, often alienates novice users (Kiesler & Sproull, 1987). Users therefore come to believe that they cannot learn to control IT, and such beliefs may acquire an almost mythic status in organizations despite little evident grounding in personal experience. By its ease of use, therefore, the Macintosh may help users to overcome these preconceptions and hence demystify computing. The comments of users about controlling the computer may thus mark an important shift in perceptions.

Whether this feeling of control is transferred to other computer systems and other technologies, though, would seem likely to depend on the extent to which the users perceive the Macintosh as typical of computers in general. Most novice users in this study appeared to relate their new-found skills specifically to the Macintosh, distinguishing between use of the Macintosh and 'computing' in general. The degree of transference may therefore be small. In addition, having become adopted by novice users, the Macintosh may then serve to define for them the nature and capabilities of computing. This was a source of some criticism from more experienced computer users who recognized the Macintosh's limitations.

A second aspect of changed attitudes which the ease of use of the Macintosh appeared to facilitate was greater user autonomy and engagement with the work task. This may be seen as supporting currently influential theories of organizational change (Dichter, 1991; Ostroff & Smith, 1992), which suggest that organizations are becoming flatter with greater use of team working by 'empowered', multi-skilled employees. Only in company A, however, was the introduction of Macintoshes explicitly associated with organizational change involving the flattening of hierarchies and greater use of information technology by professional staff. In all companies, though, interviewees suggested that they recognized a qualitative improvement in their job satisfaction and an enlargement of their work roles and autonomy with the use of the Macintosh and a couple explicitly identified this as a reduction in alienation. One manager stated that this had had a significant effect on morale in the company, with staff being more relaxed and efficient since the introduction of Macintoshes. The non-discretionary nature of computer use in the three companies, however, meant that the employee autonomy did not extend to whether they used computers or not, and it appeared that this restricted users' engagement with their work task in some cases.

Leadbeater (1989) argues that another important feature of these organizational developments is a shift from the collective to the individual as the focus of social action. There would appear to be some evidence of this in the greater individual control over computing provided by the Macintosh, both at a microlevel in terms of customization of screen layout and more generally in terms of greater work autonomy. More individualistic behaviour also appeared to be encouraged, in some companies at least, for example the 'fun' aspects of Macintosh use and positive attitudes towards the 'West coast' culture associated with Apple. In general, though, the individualistic nature of Macintosh use was less emphasized in the business organizations than in the study of academics. For example, none of the business interviewees referred to Macintosh use as 'subversive' as some academics had done. Indeed, the Macintosh culture, as reflected in ideas about the Apple computer company, was seen by two interviewees as mirroring their own corporate culture. Thus, Macintosh use appeared to be seen in some cases as a symbol of conformity rather than individualism.

Whether or not the growth of Macintosh use marks a transformation of organizational structure, the greater autonomy brought about by Macintosh use may be expected to increase the power of individual users relative to that of the organization. Within the academic environment this shift of power appeared to be compatible with the high levels of autonomy traditionally afforded to academic staff. In the business environment, however, it may give rise to a number of social, technical and organizational tensions. Socially, greater individual power may be difficult to integrate with organizational norms of behaviour which may tend to emphasize conformity and pursuit of formal, functional goals. Non-functional, informal aspects of computer use, such as play, may therefore be discouraged.

From a technical standpoint greater individual autonomy may give rise to concerns about standards which may serve in the first instance to control the adoption of new types of microcomputer and later to restrict their customization. Security considerations may also lead to controls on user autonomy. From an organizational perspective the enlargement of work tasks, for example by users taking on responsibility for production of their own publicity materials, may also be inefficient if specialist services are consequently underused and domain experts 'waste' time on other activities, such as document design, for which they have not been trained. Thus, while greater user autonomy may be considered desirable in principle, this may conflict with organizational norms.

Another effect of Macintosh use which may give rise to conflict is the changing status of different types of users. A typical classification of personal computer user types based on levels of experience and system knowledge identifies five main categories: application users, command-level users, end-user programmers, functional support personnel and end-user support personnel (Bullen, 1986). The findings of this study, though, would appear to indicate that an alternative approach may be needed to describe categories of Macintosh user, as there was little programming and most users found it relatively easy to learn applications without much support. The equivalent classification might therefore be resisters, application users, multiple task users and enthusiasts.

Among the interviewees there were a small number of resisters and single application users, but the majority of interviewees were multiple task users. In each company there were also a few enthusiasts who strongly identified with the Macintosh, sometimes in an almost emotional way: 'as soon as I saw it, I thought that's the way things should be. [It was] effectively a gut feeling — it looked right', as one put it. They had often explored the potential of the Macintosh in their own time, and become local experts in particular aspects of Macintosh use. However, most were not computer specialists and did not wish to be identified as such.

In the conventional classification, in contrast, computer specialists are usually seen as the 'highest' group of users (Maclean et al., 1990). In this study, though, they were split between the resisters and the enthusiasts. Some technical experts used their knowledge to become enthusiasts, while others were reluctant to spend time on what they perceived to be underpowered systems compared with their usual workstations, unless forced to by the need to maintain compatibility with their colleagues. With the Macintosh, therefore, greater knowledge of computers did not always lead to greater use or influence. Conflict around the introduction of the Macintosh may therefore be related to this disruption of the established hierarchy of computing in organizations.

## 7 CONCLUSIONS

While there were some important differences, for example in relation to standardization of use, the findings of this study appear to provide confirmation of the results of the earlier one of academics (Jones, 1990). Both indicate that technical and social factors need to be considered in developing an understanding of how and why computers are used in organizations. It would also seem that this may be affected by the particular brand of computer that is used, a factor that does not usually seem to be given consideration in the IS literature. Thus the Apple Macintosh appeared to have specific symbolic associations that influenced its adoption in each of the companies.

A number of factors suggest that these findings may be, at least in some way, connected with the particular qualities of the Macintosh. For example some of the characteristics mentioned by interviewees, such as user control, invisibility and 'meekness', may be argued to be derived from its specific technical qualities. Similarly, the 'fun' and aesthetics of the Macintosh were fairly distinctive amongst computer brands. The findings also provide support for the reports in the non-technical press and anecdotal evidence that suggests that the Macintosh is seen in a different way from most other computers. Certainly, the generally positive tone of the interviewees' responses reported in this paper was not solicited and gives, as far as possible, an accurate reflection of their views. Indeed, several interviewees appeared to be delighted to have the opportunity to express their enthusiasm and pleased that someone had identified it as a subject worthy of investigation.

As we did not interview users of other brands of computer, however, it must be acknowledged that we cannot be certain that they might not have given similar results. A useful extension of this research might therefore be to conduct an equivalent study with users of other brands. In view of the increasing use of graphical user interfaces similar to that of the Macintosh with other hardware platforms it would also be interesting to see if the same associations are found with Microsoft Windows on a PC for example, or if they are Macintosh specific. We would argue though that such analyses could only be by way of a general comparison rather than a statistically matched test, since the users' contexts and personal experience cannot be replicated.

Whether or not these studies establish a unique ‘Macintosh effect’ is, however, secondary to our concerns in this paper. What is more important is that users associate certain ideas with computers (arguably related to their specific brands), and that these form a potentially influential part of the individual’s attitude towards IS. This is not to suggest, for example, that installing Macintoshes, or any other particular brand, will eliminate resistance to IS introduction, but that computers need to be recognized as commercial products for which this sort of brand identity may be a factor in users’ perceptions. Moreover, in this study a number of the characteristics identified by the interviewees, such as individuality and style, reflected values that Apple has traded on in its advertising. Whether this was the result of Apple’s advertising, or the adverts were simply reinforcing intrinsic qualities of the product, they now form part of the brand identity which appeared to contribute to users’ perceptions.

As we have argued, some of these characteristics may also reflect currently influential views on organizational change. It is beyond the scope of this paper to discuss why, and perhaps even whether, these changes are taking place, but again they may be seen to provide associations which are drawn on by users. Franz & Robey (1984) described IS as ‘icons of rationality in cultures committed to the idea of intelligent choice’. What this paper seeks to suggest is that the particular computers on which IS rely, may also be seen as icons of style, autonomy and various other qualities in cultures which, it may be argued, are more typically committed to rather different goals.
