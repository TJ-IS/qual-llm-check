# Timing the introduction of information technology

G. V. Post, K.-Na. Lau\* & A. Kagan†

Department of Finance & CIS, Western Kentucky University, Bowling Green, KY 42101, USA, \*Department of Marketing, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, Hong Kong, and †SABER, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287-3306, USA

Abstract. Deciding when to adopt or introduce new information technology (IT) is a relatively difficult problem. This paper presents a method that can be used in many circumstances to evaluate new IT. The model is based on modelling the response of customers and rivals to services provided by IT. The methodology recognizes competitor reactions on two levels: within the local market and actions by rivals in the international market. The model examines behaviour, costs, and benefits over time. Several different types of models are examined, ranging from competitive (Nash) equilibriums to joint ventures. This model is illustrated by examining when banks should implement new technology. The results indicate that large banks can effectively utilize technology as a strategic weapon. Smaller, local banks are more limited in their control of technology. The model reveals some strategic uses that are not evident from existing models.

Keywords: banking, information, international, rivalry, strategy, valuation

## INTRODUCTION

A difficult decision in the application of information technology is the question of when to implement new technology. There are many situations where it would be useful to estimate the best implementation time and potential returns to introducing new technology. Three complications make this question difficult to answer. First, the benefits of new technology are not easy to measure. Second, introduction of new technology is a strategic application which requires analysing the potential responses of competitors. Third, the economics of information technology indicate that the price of new technology will decline over time. These three issues create an interesting problem: being the first adopter of new technology can be more costly and riskier, however the potential exists to gain a competitive advantage.

This paper presents a methodology that allows businesses to determine the optimal time to introduce new information technology. The method utilizes the principles of economic rivalry and strategic analysis of customers, suppliers and competitors. Additionally, the method is specifically designed to examine alternatives for different categories of businesses. In many industries, there are two distinct types of businesses: the large firms that dominate the international market and smaller firms that cater to national or local customers. The two types of firms tend to have different customer demands and different asset levels, hence they should consider different approaches to technology. This new model is especially useful for situations involving changing market structures. For example, the opening of US local banking markets to national banks or the opening of 'local' markets to international competition in European Community markets.

## Existing literature

There has been considerable discussion in the literature on how to evaluate IT, but few answers or solutions have appeared. Zmud (1979), Ives and Olson (1984) and Srinivasan (1985) discuss much of the literature regarding the evaluation of IT. Two methods have been suggested to evaluate IT: measures such as net present value (NPV) and user 'satisfaction'. As summarized by Srinivasan (1985), economic measures range from cost–benefit analysis (King & Sehrems, 1978; Alpar & Moshe, 1990) to information economics (Maish, 1979; Ahituv, 1980). User satisfaction measures have been rationalized on the grounds that the users are in a position to evaluate the benefits subjectively. Much of the literature has been devoted to creating user satisfaction instruments. See Weill and Olson (1989) for illustration via examples, and see Davis (1989) for a version of a user satisfaction survey.

Another approach to analysing IT minimizes the issue of evaluation. Porter (1980) explained how firms could focus on strategic alternatives to gain a competitive advantage in the market. Writers such as Parsons (1983), McFarlan (1984), Clemons (1986) and Scarbrough and Lannon (1988) have applied Porter's work to the competitive uses of IT. Lederer and Mendelow (1988) provide a more complete summary of the literature.

Porter's (1980) approach to strategic application showed that strategies involve external agents such as competitors, customers and suppliers. Parsons (1983) noted that potential strategic opportunities could be found by examining changes in products and service, operating costs and by searching for effects on consumers and suppliers. McFarlan (1984) further identified switching costs and barriers to entry as important strategic variables. Table 1 indicates how these ideas are incorporated into this model.

These strategic issues have surfaced in other disciplines, such as marketing's analysis of consumers. Four papers useful to our analysis appeared in the 1970s regarding financial institutions and consumer usage statistics of automated teller machines (ATMs) (Mears et al., 1978; Morris, 1978; Walker, 1977a, 1977b). Also, Cox and Lasley (1984) examined consumer attitudes and responses to the introduction of new products (without reference to IT). Additionally, Parker and Coulter (1987) analysed consumer attitudes toward large and small banks. Hannan and McDowell (1990) statistically determined that IT in the form of ATMs influenced the market positions of US banks. They also statistically showed that IT effectiveness depended on the initial size of the firm.

## Financial institutions

An introduction to the topic of bank modelling can be found in Santomero (1984). There are highly sophisticated models such as that of Sprenkle (1987) which incorporate uncertainty on liabilities and assets. Banks have faced several strategic IT decisions in the last few years, such as expanded account offerings that required substantial increases in IT and ATMs. An issue of the same or greater magnitude that is now surfacing is point-of-sale POS terminals and debit cards. In each IT decision there is a trade-off between the cost of the IT and return on the investment. In most cases, the decision is complicated by the question of how the firm's rivals will react. Adding even more complexity is the fact that the decisions are not static — costs and benefits change depending on when each firm chooses to implement. Although it provides little guidance, a survey by Roth and Van Der Velde (1989) illustrates that these strategic trade-offs are considered to be important issues. It also summarizes current investment plans for many banks.

Barua et al. (1991) postulated a model that is worth examining in more detail. They examine IT investment in the banking industry within a Nash/Stackelberg game, using a simplistic demand and cost model with linear reaction functions. Their static approach postulates that all actions by rivals and consumers occur in a 'single-period game'. To simplify their analysis, they assume that firms already possess all necessary technology. The only remaining issue that will affect the outcome is customer reaction, which is defined in terms of a fixed 'cross-elasticity of substitution'. Traditional economic literature has shown that it is difficult to obtain generalizable conclusions from this type of model. Most of the 'propositions' Barua et al. derive are true only for their restrictive choice of functions.

Our approach differs from that of Barua et al. in several respects. Our model is both broader and more detailed, which makes it applicable to a wider range of problems, and capable of producing more relevant information. It will also be estimated using actual data to demonstrate its power. The objective is to produce a non-linear model that accurately describes interactions of rivals, consumers and technology over time.

## 2 MODEL

The model has four components. First, there are four firms: two international and two local firms. Second, customer sensitivity to IT services and other variables are modelled. Third, hardware costs decline over time. Fourth, software design costs are higher for the first firms to implement technology.

## Consumers

Marketing theory and research shows that consumers are attracted to firms for a variety of reasons. This attraction is modelled by noting that customers make decisions on the basis of their economic utility functions. Since utility cannot be observed, a common practice is to use aggregation to determine market shares, by examining the probability that customers will switch firms. Derivation of this relationship requires the first 10 equations.

Deterministic utility is denoted by $_{k}V_{ij}(t)$ , which represents the value received by consumer k for switching from i to firm j at time t. The utility function depends on three factors:

1 A switching cost $(_{k}C_{ij})$ , which includes features such as convenience and friendliness.

2 Prices of products $(r_{i}^{l}(t))$ and purchases from suppliers $(r_{i}^{d}(t))$ . For banks, these are interest rates on loans and deposits.

3 The variety and quality of services provided by the time at time t. The variety and quality of services is a function of the IT offerings and the effectiveness of this technology.

For the model to accommodate multiple technologies, consumer k's perception of IT services is

$$
_ {k} \mathsf {I T} (t) = \Sigma_ {m} [ _ {k} b _ {i, m} X _ {i, m} (t) ]\tag{1}
$$

which is summed over the number of different technologies. The $X_{i,m}(t)$ are binary variables representing the presence of the mth technology in firm i at time t. The bs represent the consumer perception of the usefulness of this technology.

As indicated by the literature on strategic uses of IT, the switching cost can be affected by the implementation of IT. To incorporate this effect, the value of $C_{ij}$ can increase by some percentage for every time period after the technology is implemented.

Writing the deterministic utility as a function of the three variables discussed above

$$
{ } _ { k } V _ { i j } ( t ) = { } _ { k } f _ { i j } [ C _ { i j , i } ^ { d } ( t ) , r _ { j } ^ { d } ( t ) r _ { i } ^ { l } ( t ) , r _ { j } ^ { l } ( t ) , I T _ { i } ( t ) , I T _ { j } ( t ) ]\tag{2}
$$

In general form, the total switching utility $(_{k}U_{ij}(t)$ is a function of deterministic utility and stochastic utility (unobservable):

$$
_ {k} U _ {i j} (t) = _ {k} V _ {i j} (t) \cdot_ {k} \epsilon_ {i j} (t)\tag{3}
$$

Since the international market consists of two firms (form 0 and firm 1), these functions are defined for $i,j = \{0,1\}$ . In the local market, there are two small firms plus the two international firms, hence $i,j = \{0,1,2,3\}$ .

Given these utility functions, it is possible to examine the probability that customers will switch firms — for example because of a change in technology. Let $_{k}P_{ij}(t)$ be the probability that consumer k switches from firm i to firm j at time t. That is

$$
_ {k} P _ {i j} (t) = \operatorname{Prob} (_ {k} U _ {i j} (t) > _ {k} U _ {i, b} (t)), \forall b \neq j\tag{4}
$$

In accordance with McFadden's (1974) derivation of the logic model, assume that the stochastic utility $(\log_{k}(\varepsilon_{ij}(t)))$ follows the type I extreme value distribution to get

$$
{ } _ { k } P _ { i j } ( t ) = _ { k } V _ { i j } ( t ) / \Sigma _ { b k } V _ { i , b } ( t )\tag{5}
$$

where the denominator is summed over the total number of firms in the given market.

Customers are heterogeneous in terms of their utility parameters. Assuming that

$$
{ } _ { k } V _ { i j } \sim \operatorname{Gamma} ( \mu , \mu V _ { i j } ) ,\tag{6}
$$

where $\mu$ is the scale parameter of the game distribution, and $\mu V_{ij}$ is the shape parameter, then $V_{ij} = E_k(\kappa V_{ij})$ is the average deterministic utility for all customers in the market. Then

$$
{ } _ { k } P _ { i j } \sim \mathsf { B e t a } ( \mu V _ { i j } , \mu \Sigma _ { h \neq j } V _ { i , h } )\tag{7}
$$

The average switching probability for all the customers is given by:

$$
P _ {i j} (t) = E _ {k} [ _ {k} P _ {i j} (t) ] = V _ {i j} / \Sigma_ {b} V _ {i, b}\tag{8}
$$

This probability represents the primary observable consumer response to technology (and interest rates). It will directly affect the market share of the firms involved in the market. Given the initial market share, the market share for firm b can be found by:

$$
M _ {b} (t) = \Sigma_ {i} [ P _ {i, b} (t) M _ {i} (t) - 1) ]\tag{9}
$$

To operationalize the model, $V_{ij}$ is specified as

$$
\begin{array}{c} _ {k} V _ {i j} (t) = \tau_ {j} ^ {d} r _ {j} ^ {d} (t) + \tau_ {j} ^ {l} r _ {j} ^ {i} (t) + \delta_ {j} \mathsf {I T} _ {j} (t) + 1 / C _ {i j} + h _ {i j} ^ {d} [ r _ {j} ^ {d} (t) - r _ {i} ^ {d} (t) ] - h _ {i j} ^ {l} [ r _ {j} ^ {l} (t) - r _ {i} ^ {l} (t) ] + \\ g _ {i j} [ \mathsf {I T} _ {j} (t) - \mathsf {I T} _ {j} (t) ] \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

## Costs

One of the most direct effects of new technology lies in its cost. An intriguing feature of information technology is that costs decline over time. Costs tend to drop for two reasons: (1) improved production techniques and economies of scale and (2) as other organizations gain experience with the technology, later adopters have the opportunity to learn from their mistakes, or hire their employees. The rate of decline due to the general structure of the industry is

$$
_ {i} C _ {i} (t) = C _ {0} \exp (- \beta t)\tag{11}
$$

This equation states that the cost to the first firm adopting the technology (firm i) will decline at the rate indicated by $\beta$ . The second reason for the drop in costs due to learning effects is

$$
_ 2 C _ {j} (t) = C _ {0} \exp (- \beta_ {1} / t _ {1} - \beta t)\tag{12}
$$

## Cost sharing

Since cooperation between the international firms is unlikely, consider the case of cost sharing by the local firms. In stage 2, the local firms maximize the sum of discounted profits $(S_{2} + S_{3})$ . It is also possible to consider an unequal split of total costs (SC), described by the portion paid by firm 2 $(PP_{2})$ . The two firms can share costs, but still implement at different times, which might occur when companies agree to share the cost of telecommunication facilities or off-site hardware (e.g. for a POS implementation). In terms of the model equations, if firm 2 implements at time=10, and firm 3 implements at time=15, costs can be written as the sum of shared and non-shared costs:

$$
C _ {2} = \left[ \text { total\_costs } _ {1 0} * \text { SC } * \text { PP } _ {2} \right] + \left[ \text { total\_costs } _ {1 0} * (1 - \text { SC }) \right]
$$

$$
C _ {3} = \left[ \text { total\_costs } _ {1 0} * \text { SC } * (1 - \text { PP } _ {2}) \right] + \left[ \text { total\_costs } _ {1 5} * (1 - \text { SC }) \right]\tag{17}
$$

This case is solved by examining $S_{1} + S_{2}$ at each of the combinations of implementation times, for various values of SC and $PP_{2}$ . For example, this paper examines SC = {0.25, 0.5, 0.75} and $PP_{2} = \{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$ .

## INITIAL EXAMINATIONS AND MODEL VALIDATION

To test the model, it was applied to the implementation of ATMs by the US banking community. The first step is to estimate the base parameters of the model. Then the model is solved to determine strategies for large and small firms. Lastly, these results are compared with the actual decisions made by firms over that time period.

## Estimation

The first step in the use of this model is to estimate as many of the parameters as possible. In many cases, estimation is as simple as obtaining current values of balance sheet and income statement items. In other cases, it may be exceedingly difficult to obtain statistical estimates of some of the parameters — notably customer sensitivity to technology. In these cases, it is best to select wide ranges of the variables and solve the model several times across the range of values.

To demonstrate the process and illustrate the usefulness of the model, we examined the adoption of ATMs by the US financial community from the early 1970s until 1990. Some of the data values shown in Table 2 are subjective estimates and aggregations, so the results may not apply to a particular bank. The coefficients should be tailored to each specific application.

Two papers by Walker (1977a, 1977b) provide insight into the introduction of ATMs in the early 1970s and contain data on ATM costs and consumer reactions. Three basic sets of data need to be acquired or estimated: (1) cost behaviour or decline over time; (2) consumer response to technology; (3) balance sheet data. Behaviour of hardware and software costs is found in the Walker papers, supplemented with data to 1990 obtained directly from ATM manufacturers IBM and Diebold. Equations (11) and (12) are estimated via regression. Total implementation costs for a large-scale POS-EFTS (electronic funds transfer system) were estimated by Morris (1978) to be around \$20 million. These costs are scaled down for smaller firms. Also, to focus on ATMs (not the more expensive POS), the base model utilizes a total implementation cost for the large firms of \$10 million.

## Large firms

The base results indicate that in a competitive market (Nash equilibrium), the earliest the international firm will implement the new technology is at time period 2, and the two local firms will implement no earlier than time period 11. In the base case, one of the international firms implements at time 2, the other at time 3. The firm that moves first gains a slight advantage in profits. Also, if the large banks form a joint venture, they will not implement the technology. This conclusion agrees with traditional antitrust conclusions regarding the effect of collusion on the consumer. In other words, large firms are strategically forced to implement earlier to attract customers or to prevent them from leaving, even though it is more expensive. Consider how these results match with Walker's (1977b) comments about banks that chose to implement ATMs first:

In 1976, as in 1974, banks with EFTS machines had lower demand and time and savings deposit growth rates than all banks. The banks with EFTS machines are earning more revenue than comparable groups of banks, but the additional costs EFTS banks are incurring more than outweighs the larger revenues.

## Small firms

The local bank's optimal implementation occurs at time period 11 or 12. When the large firms jointly choose not to implement the ATMs, the smaller firms delay only slightly, choosing to implement the technology at period 16. The local firms are forced to implement technology earlier because of the presence of the large international firms. Second, even without the large firms, the small firms obtain a competitive advantage through the implementation of technology (but not as much as the large firms). Third, the smaller firms are highly dependent on costs.

The model can be examined in greater detail by changing the base coefficients. For instance, if customer sensitivity to technology is increased slightly (from 0.10 to 0.30, which is still only one-third the value for an interest rate change), then the large firms will implement at time 1 and the local banks will implement at time 2 or 3. Walker (1977a) found that in 1974 there was only minimal implementation of ATMs by all banks. Yet by 1976 (Walker, 1977b), there was a large increase in the implementation of ATMs. In particular, Walker seemed surprised that most of the gain was attributable to implementation by small banks:

Small banks with less than 2 branches in unit banking states seem to be the ones that are most inclined to install retail EFTS machines. It may be that they are so concerned about 'future competition' from larger banks that banks with assets under \$50 M are determined to be first to offer EFTS services in their markets.

The model is useful because it can examine a wide variety of situations, which allows decision-makers to evaluate technology and strategies before they are implemented.

## Evaluation of main parameters

One of the strengths the model is the ability to examine how changes in the underlying parameters affect the results. It can be used to identify which parameters generate the most powerful effects. Table 3 summarizes how the results change as a result of changes in individual parameters. Shown in the first row of Table 3, an increase in the base cost ( $C_{0}$ in equation 11), causes both the large and small firms to implement at a later time. The profit changes are ambiguous; they tend to decrease for some values, but eventually increase.

System development costs will decrease over time at some rate ( $\beta_{0,0}$ in equation 11). As costs decline at a higher rate, all firms tend to implement earlier, which decreases profits for the firms, but not by a large amount. The decline in costs makes the project profitable sooner.

If the costs of switching ( $C_{ij}$ in equation 10 from one large firm (firm 0) to the other large firm (firm 1) increase, then firm 1 will resort to earlier implementation to compensate for the increased switching costs. Firm 1 will still have lower profits than firm 0, but it is still its best alternative. In the local market, if the cost of switching from an international company (firm 0) to a local firm (firm 2) increases, then the small firm will implement the new technology at a later time, with a resulting loss in profit. This action differs from the large companies largely because the local firm is responding more to cost factors than to strategic factors. The loss of customers implies that the local firms have less money to invest in the technology.

Theoretically, the model should be relatively sensitive to customer valuation of the technology ( $\delta$ in equation 11). Surprisingly, initial changes in this parameter show that there is little change in the implementation time. However, as customers become more sensitive to the technology, the large firms will garner more profits, at the expense of the smaller firms. That is, since the large firms tend to implement before the small firms, any increase in customer sensitivity will result in more customers switching to the larger firms, hence more profits. The important conclusion of this result is that in a realistic setting, we do not need highly accurate estimates of customer sensitivity to technology. Other simulations indicate that technology sensitivity interacts in a complex manner with the other parameters.

## RESULTS

The model can be used to evaluate various strategies for European firms that may be facing increased competition from the restructuring of the EC. Since the US financial industry is unique in that it supports a large number of small banks, it is necessary to consider a different set of underlying parameters than those estimated in the prior section. In particular, the size of the local banks was increased relative to the size of the large banks. Also, the rate of cost decline was increased slightly along with an increase in customer sensitivity to technology.

This model has the potential to examine many questions regarding the strategic uses of information technology. Space constraints limit the discussion to a small subset. This paper focuses on the strategic use of technology and joint ventures for the local or national firms. Many other alternatives were not considered. It is possible that non-IT solutions will be more profitable.

The initial results indicate that the local firms do not typically use technology as a competitive weapon. Some interesting questions arise from this observation. What causes this difference between the international and the local banks? Is there a situation in which technology would be used as a strategic weapon by the small banks?

## Differences between small and large firms

In terms of the first question, there are three primary differences in the model between the two types of companies. First, this model defines a structural difference by forcing the large international firms to make the first decision. This model has two parameter level differences between the large and small firms: (1) number of customers and (2) initial capital. Of these, the number of customers (market size) has the strongest effect. When the starting capital is increased so that it is equal to that of the large banks, there is no substantive change in the small banks' actions, although they do receive higher profits. On the other hand, increasing the number of customers in the local market does encourage the small banks to implement earlier.

## Strategic use of IT for small local firms

## Introduction

Another interesting question is whether or not technology can be used as a strategic weapon by the local firms. In the initial results of the competitive model there is little strategic use of IT by the small firms. Changes in most of the model parameters do not alter this result. Yet, the EC introduction of increased international competition (or the opening of the US industry to national banking) changes the situation. European banks have been insulated through political regulations and cultural habits. In the US, interstate banking is limited by several regulations. These situations can be examined by adding a model restriction that controls when large banks are allowed to enter the local market. The goal is to answer the question of whether or not the local banks can compensate for the opening of their markets with technology. The local banks are allowed to share a portion of the development costs. For example, the small firms might join together to establish a POS network among local retailers. This situation was examined under various levels of customer loyalty and customer sensitivity to technology.

To study these issues, the model was examined for the following parameter changes: big banks were restricted to entry in periods 0, 5, 10, 15, 20. The percentage of costs shared by the small banks was 0, 50, 75, 100. Customer sensitivity to technology was 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0. Customer loyalty to the local firms included neutral, medium, high and very high loyalty. These last descriptive categories were created by controlling the switching costs between the four firms. In the higher loyalty cases, the customers prefer to do business with the local banks.

An increase in loyalty (moving down the columns) tends to result in a delay in implementation of new technology. Logically, if customers are inherently more loyal, there is less need to entice them through the use of new technology and the resulting services. Table 5 indicates that, as loyalty increases, there is a need for increased restrictions on large firm entry and for increased sharing of costs before the strategic use of technology is warranted. In summary, when customers are more loyal to small banks, implementation of new technology will be less likely and the local banks are 'less competitive'. The importance of this statement lies in the ability of the model to delineate exactly what is meant by 'less competitive', and to determine precisely when a bank should resort to competitive use of the technology.

The model allows for investigation of more complicated interactions. For example, an increase in technology sensitivity has two different effects depending on the level of customer loyalty. If customer loyalty is not high, an increase in sensitivity leads to earlier implementation (requires lower cost sharing), whereas for higher levels of loyalty an increase in sensitivity requires a higher level of cost sharing to generate earlier implementation times. To see the effect, examine the last two columns of Table 5 for each of the rows. At lower levels of loyalty, an increase in technology sensitivity results in more competitive use of technology, but at higher levels of loyalty the same increase causes a decrease in the strategic use of technology.

One possible explanation for the interaction between loyalty and technology sensitivity is that the two parameters have opposite effects on customers. Increased loyalty keeps the customers at the smaller bank, whereas the more technology-sensitive customers will switch to the larger banks (who almost always implement technology earlier). At low to moderate loyalty levels, the local banks can use the technology to compete against both each other and the large banks. When the loyalty effect becomes stronger, the technology effect is overshadowed. Since the small banks already have most of the local customers, trying to attract the remaining local customers through technology is not as profitable. In fact, the technology effect is counterproductive because it means the customers are less loyal.

If the firms continue to share costs (even in the presence of a decrease in profits) as technology sensitivity increases, the percentage drop in profits tends to be greater. One explanation is that, if the customers are more sensitive to technology, the banks are forced to implement earlier in an attempt to gain or hold market share.

## ASYMMETRIC LOCAL FIRMS AND JOINT VENTURES

It is worthwhile to examine what happens when local firms are different sizes. Consider four levels of loyalty vis-à-vis the large firms: very high, high, medium and neutral loyalty. With two local firms, dropping the duplicate cases leaves 10 unique sets. Since the firms are different sizes, it is important to allow a cost sharing that is not necessarily equal. As explained in the model section, there are two basic parameters of interest: (1) the percentage of the costs that can be shared and (2) how that percentage will be split between the two firms.

The cost-sharing parameters are treated as independent control variables. In particular, it is best to treat the percentage of costs that are paid by each firm as an 'offer' from firm 2, which is an offer that firm 3 may accept or reject compared with the non-sharing solution. Additionally, firm 3 chooses the amount of costs that will be shared. In other words, although the companies are working on a joint venture, they have the ability to negotiate for the optimal terms. The resulting situation is a Nash game that determines what will happen to both firms if they share costs.

Initial results indicate that total profits for the two firms will usually be higher if they share costs, however it is possible that the Nash game will yield a profit that is lower for an individual company. Therefore, it might be in the best interest of the 'losing' firm not to participate in the Nash joint optimization game.

Table 6 shows the results of the Nash simulation when the loyalty of firm 2's customers is very high and that of firm 3's is high. Notice in this case that there is only one cell where there is an incentive for both firms to accept the cost-sharing offer, row 3, column 4, where all of the costs are shared but firm 2 pays 60% of them. More importantly, note that there is no easy way for the two firms to reach this cell. If firms treat the cost sharing as a Nash game, the banks will choose the zero cost-sharing position. This conclusion does not hold in all 10 cases.

In general terms, the acceptable cost-sharing solutions are highly dependent on the relative sizes of the two firms. If there is a large difference (very high-neutral, high-neutral, very high-medium and high-medium), then the only acceptable solutions arise when there is minimal cost sharing (25%) and firm 2 pays the bulk of the costs (60–80% or more).

When firms are similar in size (very high–high, medium–neutral), as in Table 6, only one solution is acceptable to both firms. If the firms are the same size, there is a higher chance that they will be willing to share the costs. Typically, sharing 50% or more of the costs, with the costs shared equally (40%–60%) is acceptable to both companies. However, the important conclusion is that the firms will not be able to reach this optimal point through a typical Nash negotiation strategy — they will end up with no cost sharing. In other words, this new model allows the firms to locate a better strategy than they would normally reach through negotiation.

## LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Because of the complexity of the model, only a small set of results has been presented here. The model is capable of answering many other questions. The model can be applied to other industries by altering the utility functions and profit equations. However, difficulties and limitations will arise any time a model is applied to solving practical problems.

## Application to actual cases

The first problem in applying a model is to determine if the problem can benefit from the approach. In particular, this model assumes that the implementation will be successful, however many management and human factors can cause projects to fail. Similarly, real-world projects often take longer and cost more than expected. In many cases, these factors will be the most important determinant of whether or not to implement a new technology. In fact, these costs are likely to be higher for early implementors. If a firm does not have the management expertise or trained staff, it may be wiser to forget about strategic IT implementations. On the other hand, if the potential gains are high enough, it might be worthwhile to invest in a new staff or to pay someone else to develop the project.

A complete analysis of any competitive situation will reveal alternatives to the use of IT. In many cases, it will be more profitable to choose from these alternatives. However, making that determination requires an estimation of the potential gains from the IT, which is the purpose of this model. Similarly, the model can be used to evaluate competing IT projects.

Another complication is the need to collect the data and estimate the underlying functions. It can be difficult to estimate the sensitivity of consumers to services provided by IT. Although this model does not eliminate the problem, it does provide evaluation of the conclusions for different levels of the parameters. As a result, managers can determine which of the parameters are most important and spend additional resources identifying more precise estimates.

## Additional strategic effects

As illustrated here, the primary strategic effect is the ability to attract customers with new services. As noted in the introduction, the strategic use of technology can have many other effects. They are summarized in Table 1. For example, McFarlan (1984) showed that technology may affect the switching costs of the customer. As mentioned in the model section, it is straightforward to allow the technology to increase the probability that a customer stays with the firm.

In a firm that uses physical products as inputs, it is important to consider the quality and timeliness of delivery as well as the cost of the inputs. These factors can be incorporated into the supplier and profit functions. These factors are often strategic variables that can be affected by the implementation of technology.

Parsons (1983) included some more subtle strategic effects. For example, IT may change the structure or size of the market. Market growth rates can be added to the model, and they can be linked to the implementation of the technology. It is considerably more difficult to evaluate changes in market structure — largely because these changes may be unpredictable. The most likely change to the model is that the profit function would change when technology is implemented. In other words, the model would require two profit functions: (1) current profits and (2) a formula to calculate profits (and costs) if the entire production process is changed.

## CONCLUSIONS

This paper discussed the development of a dynamic model used to evaluate strategic uses of information technology. The model was validated using data from the US banking industry. The model can be applied to other industries. One of the contributions of the model is its proficiency in evaluating the strategic effects of technology on customers and competitors.

The model emphasizes the dynamic nature of the problem by directly incorporating the price changes typically exhibited by IT. Additionally, the model captures the competition between local firms as well as international firms. By directly modelling costs, benefits and profits over time, the model can be applied to complex, realistic problems.

In practice, many factors will influence the timing of IT introduction. Some of them, such as lack of capital or inadequate IS staffing, may be insurmountable. Similarly, more traditional actions may be more profitable than IT implementation. However, in almost any situation, it will be worthwhile to have an estimate of the costs, customer responses and reactions of rivals to potential IT investments.
