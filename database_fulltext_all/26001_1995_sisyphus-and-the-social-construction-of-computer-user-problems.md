# Sisyphus and the social construction of computer user problems

K. Grint

Templeton College, Oxford, UK

Abstract. This article argues that one way of understanding the role of technical developments in computers is to consider them as solutions looking for problems, rather than as solutions to pre-existing problems. This reversal of popular opinion is premised on an analysis of the way that boundaries are drawn around the domain of the 'technical' such that computer users can be kept at a distance and their interpretation of computer user problems 'managed' more or less successfully by those purporting to resolve such problems. The result is that users are faced with a labour of Sisyphus: just when they thought they had resolved their problems another technical 'advance' suddenly appears and generates another problem. An awareness of the social framework for such developments may facilitate a clearer appreciation of the role of the problem solver in the construction of the problem itself.

Keywords: computer user, constructivism, problems, Sisyphus.

## INTRODUCTION

Graphical user interfaces (GUIs), particularly Microsoft Windows 3.1, are big business: Windows is currently the fastest selling business package ever and has been since Windows 3.0 took off. The popular explanations of this phenomenon tend to associate it with the resolution of long-standing technical problems in user interfaces, especially the 'notoriously unfriendly' nature of the command line-based operating systems such as MS-DOS. This assumes that the problems sit within the technology and that, once discovered, can be resolved. But each wave of technical advances appears to bring with it further technical problems that require greater expense to resolve. Those who bought MS-DOS as the solution to CP/M (or its alternatives) may have bought Windows 1 as the solution to MS-DOS and so on. Yet, paradoxically, many users argue that none of the commercially successful interfaces has been technically the best.

An alternative perspective suggests that the problems experienced in interfacing with computers are not so much discovered by users as created by technical advances. Thus, rather than seeing, for example, Windows as the resolver of the command line problem, we might profitably consider it as the creator of the problem — which it then resolves. That is, the producers of technical developments actively persuade us that our existing technology is inefficient (even if there is little way of assessing this claim) and then persuade us that they have the solution to the problem we were hitherto unaware of. Very fortunate, you might think. In effect, although the specific problems which various 'experts' address are different (command line systems, unfriendly computers, memory shortage, etc.), the structure of their arguments remains very similar. Rather than the conventional sequence of events being one in which the technical advance resolves a long-standing technical problem, this approach suggests that the problem can be created by the technical advance. If this is so then future technical developments will create problems for contemporary Windows users — problems that currently do not exist — and they will simultaneously provide solutions to those problems, at a price. Just as Sisyphus rolled his stone up the hill only to see it roll back down again each time, so technical advances appear to help Sisyphus but actually ensure that his task is never-ending.

The model of computer user problem developed here is supported by empirical evidence from a series of different sources, including a pilot survey for a much larger project, interviews with computer journalists and documentary materials drawn from a wide variety of texts. Particular attention is focused on the development of PC operating systems. Operating systems were chosen as the primary case study mainly because of the plethora of reports in the trade journals and papers which took the development of Windows 3 as a radical and commercially adept solution to the 'problem' of MS-DOS and the equally problematic 'solution' to the MS-DOS 'problem', OS/2. Since the manifestations of the problems of MS-DOS had never affected me or anyone that I knew personally, I began to investigate the issues and discovered to my horror that I too had a problem with MS-DOS and really ought to do something about it. My simple problem was that my PC would not support Windows 3. So there was I, a PC user for almost a decade, unwittingly ignorant of a problem that everyone, well at least everyone in the computer industry press, now recognized as manifestly obvious. How could I have been so ignorant? In what follows I try to trace out my ignorance and develop a model which explains it.

## A CONSTRUCTIVIST PERSPECTIVE ON TECHNICAL PROBLEMS

In contrast to many conventional accounts of computer user problems, which tend to assume a fixed and determinate capacity on the part of the computer, a constructivist approach suggests that the capacity of technology — what the computer will do — is the upshot of social accounts. That is, our knowledge of the capacities of technology is derived from various representations of them (texts, speech, etc.), and these representations are not reflections of technologies but constitute them; hence technology is not transparent but necessarily opaque; it is a social construction. What the technology appears to do, then, depends upon the social context of its production and, equally importantly, its consumption (Kling, 1991).

This does not mean that the technology is irrelevant — it is not that technology does what we will it to do – but it does imply that the nature of the technology, and indeed the boundaries between the technical and the social, are social constructions. It is not, however, that the technical is subordinate to the social, nor that the technical and social carry discrete but equal significance, rather it is that the division between the technical and the social is a socially constructed division; in short, what counts as the technical is a social decision (see MacKenzie & Wajcman, 1985; Bijker et al., 1987; Grint, 1991; Murray & Woolgar, 1991; Woolgar, 1991; Woolgar & Grint, 1991; Grint & Woolgar, forthcoming; Grint & Woolgar, 1992. What interests the constructivist is how certain technologies come to be associated with specific 'effects', 'technical capacities', and, for our purposes here, 'computer user problems'.

A preliminary point is that this perspective suggests that the problems associated with computers are also social constructions; computer user problems, in this perspective, do not lie 'in' the computer — awaiting their resolution by experts of various kinds — but are constructed by users, purchasers, designers, etc. Indeed, one of the features of software which is held to be inherent to it is its very flexibility. Although software is packaged and sold as having particular capacities and limits, users invariably appear to 'discover' new aspects and facilities (as well as bugs) which the designers and producers appear to be unaware of at the time of launch. One might want to argue here that such 'discoveries' do not undermine the assumption that technology has inherent capacities – they merely confirm that designers and users take time to 'discover' these capacities. The alternative approach is to suggest that what are taken as the 'capacities' of the technology only become apparent through representations of such capacities that are themselves subject to doubt. For example, whether a certain software will run in a particular way, and whether it outperforms a rival, is the result of certain powerful actors and networks of actors persuading others of their own representation of the capacity, and problems, of the technology.

Such a concern about 'the problem of problems' is not (or at least not just) idle speculation. The costs of computer user problems are enormous. In the UK alone, a Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) survey in 1988 suggested that the cost of correcting software errors in marketed software was approximately £500 million; adding the cost of in-house software errors pushed the estimated total to £2–3 billion. In 1988 the Bank of America finally dumped Masternet, a customer asset management system, which it had already spent £20 million implementing. The computerization of the British Passport Office in 1989 cost £7 million and was considerably slower than the manual system it replaced. In 1989 Barclays Bank scrapped two projects worth £10 million and had to pay an extra £10 million on a third project that was accumulating 2500 system stoppages a month. And the most recent computer shambles is the Taurus project to computerize London's share registers, which failed in March 1993 at a cost of around £400 million.

According to Meiklejohn (1990), a major cause of the problem is commercial pressure, especially the costs involved in quality control, which can effectively double the cost of a project. Retrospective repairs to software can cost up to 100 times the original cost of writing it. According to Croucher, chair of the British Computer Society's Quality Management Group:

The real difficulty with software is that we don't understand how to capture the requirements of a complex system. A lot of people don't realise the difficulty of the communication process between users and suppliers of software. There is enormous potential for misunderstanding and misinterpretation (quoted in Meiklejohn, 1990).

For Croucher and Cameron Low (DTI project director on software quality guidelines) a further problem lies in the overambitious nature of software project directors and, linked to this, their general assumption that all problems have a technical solution. Croucher and Low assert that the major issues are managerial not technical. It is ironic, then, that many software houses have begun to turn to semiautomated production of software, adopting software engineering tools to compensate for software engineering tools. The issue may be more subtle than this. At one level the problem is that software writers tend to define problems in different ways from users: for software writers it is a problem if the software will not do what the writers want it to do — it has low specified functionality. But user-defined problems are often radically different: these relate not to whether it will do what the writers say it will do but to whether the users can get it to do what the writers have said it will do and perhaps whether the software is easy to learn, easy to use and flexible, etc. This mirrors the current fascination in more general management literature with total quality management (TQM): the reversal of the producer-oriented firm to a consumer-oriented firm. For example, the primary concern of the software writers ought to be, under TQM, 'How can we make a product that the customer wants?' not 'Having made a product what can we do with it?' At this level, then, the problems of computer users are qualitatively different from those of the software writers.

But there is more to it than this. The customer/producer or writer/user division does not question the nature of the problem, only the differential significance it has for the parties involved. What I want to suggest here is a rather different approach to the issue of problems: not that the problems are different but that what counts as a problem itself is an interpretative process. Hence, as an illustration, the orthodox differential problem approach might suggest that poor execution is a problem for writer and user, while poor documentation is a problem only for the user, i.e. that the problems lie in the technology of the software or the documentation and are demonstrably 'objective': they lie in the technology itself. The more radical approach suggests that the problems lie not in the technology but are the upshot of social constructions: if all the users are adept users of the system then the documentation is not a problem; if the program operates to the satisfaction of the users then whatever the extent of its alleged bugs these are not a problem to the users. Problems, then, lie in the interpretative actions of the users not in the technology itself. The 'fact' that PCs approaching 10 years old seem to be 'objectively' slow, and consequently this 'technical problem' of speed is one in dire need of resolution, cannot be dissociated from the increase in processing speed and decreasing cost of chips since then. The technical problem of speed is a socially constructed problem, developed through comparisons with other more powerful machines. Such a problem did not actually exist, lying dormant in the technology, 10 years ago, merely waiting for researchers or users to 'discover' it now. On the contrary, the 'problem' was constructed post hoc. The problem appears to lie within the technology but its appearance cannot be isolated from the social nature of its origins.

It follows that to concern oneself with the way computers generate problems for their users is something of a labour of Sisyphus. Since the issue is one of problem construction rather than problem discovery, a more sceptical and constructivist approach may be useful: problems do not so much generate their own solutions; solutions spawn a rash of problems. ## ACCUMULATING GENERAL COMPUTER USER PROBLEMS

Of course, not all software and not all operating systems are deemed to have problems or to be unreliable. But part of this argument is that such reliability also reflects the social nature of stabilization rather than the inherent quality of the technology. In short, what counts as a reliable or unreliable operating system, what counts as a problem, what counts as a solution — and how stable the solution is — are all social constructions. Even convincing people that you have a problem can be a problem. As John Macey argues after adopting Unix-based accounting software and encountering problems: 'They (software suppliers) are no better and no worse than any other software house. You have to convince them that you have a problem (Financial Times, 14 June, 1991).'

The difficulty tends to be compounded by the popular assumption that technological innovations generally, and computers in particular, are often endowed by their human commentators with such levels of predictability and an error-free nature that to computerize a previously manual system is often regarded as rational in and of itself: the further humans are from the process involved, the less chance there is of error creeping in (Rosenbrock, 1990). This becomes most obvious in terms of catastrophic plane accidents (Brown, 1992) or banking computer crashes, the blame for the disaster is usually deposited firmly at the feet of the human operator or the computer programmer; after all how can a machine make a mistake: surely the real problems relate to human users not to the technology? (see Forester & Morrison, 1990; Cantwell Smith, 1991).

Yet the closer one gets to the manufacturer of hardware or software, or those who review such items, the more unlikely humans appear to be regarded as the cause of significant problems. Woolgar & Russell (1990) certainly suggest, from their analysis of 'the software crisis', that most program writers and systems analysts relate software 'problems' to software bugs not human operators. Computer users appear to have the same non-social experiences.

Let us delve a little deeper into the software to see whether the constructivist approach can, indeed, shed new light on some of these problems or, through shedding light, create new problems, especially the way in which 'technical problems' in operating systems are retrospectively constructed by software journalists and reviewers.

## OPERATING SYSTEMS

Operating systems ostensibly fulfil several roles: they are said to provide an interface between the hardware and the application software by facilitating access to data files on disk, they control communications with other devices, they provide housekeeping services and security measures and they facilitate use of the computer by the (often inexpert) user.

Operating systems for personal computers only really took off in 1974 when Gary Kildall's new company, Microcomputer Applications Associates, wrote PL/M (programming language/microcomputers) for the inventor of microprocessors — Intel. Kildall subsequently developed an operating system for micros in PL/M called CP/M (control program/microcomputers) and founded Digital Research. The convention is that CP/M was 'inherently' technically crude from the very beginning:

The success of CP/M owes nothing to technical excellence and everything to market forces ... So how can a million CP/M users be wrong? CP/M hit the big time because it was able to overcome one of the biggest problems facing the early microcomputer industry. It provided a means of transporting application programs from one manufacturer's hardware to another (Lewis M., review of CP/M in Practical Computing, April, 1984).

CP/M then became the standard operating system for the Intel 8080/8085 and Zilog Z-80 chips. This standard was then subverted or superseded by the MS-DOS and PC-DOS systems developed for the IBM PC. Whether this standard was any better or not was unclear. As Lewis put it: 'Keep firmly in mind that the most suitable OS (Operating System) for you is not necessarily the one that is technically the best' (Lewis, M. review of operating systems in Practical Computing, April, 1984).

When Windows 3.0 appeared to displace OS/2, as the inheritor of a mantle already slipping gradually from MS-DOS, a similar dispute about the technical merit of the 'victor' arose. As one OS/2 devotee put it: 'OS/2 users feel Microsoft is abandoning a technically superior product in exchange for short-term gain. Microsoft has spat in the eye of developers who put a lot of work into keeping ahead of the market, and now it has decided the market is somewhere else' (PC User, 19 December 1990 — 1 January 1991). Such invective might seem out of place in the columns of a computer magazine, but it actually has quite a long history. As Rodwell argued almost a decade ago, operating systems — and their problems — take on a moral flavour that smacks more of religious or political zealotry than objective assessments:

For reasons which totally escape me, operating systems, like programming languages, religion and politics, seem to inspire quite ridiculous degrees of fanaticism, frequently bordering on bigoted blindness, if some of the hate mail we receive here is anything to go by. (I cannot understand why anyone should get so upset about a subject like operating systems, which, in the global scale of things, are pretty insignificant — but people do.) (Rodwell, P, review of MS-DOS and CP/M-86 in Personal Computer World, October, 1982).

Over seventy million PCs currently use DOS as their operating system (either the Microsoft original or a clone), and fewer than 700 000 use OS/2, originally developed by IBM and Microsoft together. OS/2 was launched in April 1987 in conjunction with the new IBM PS/2 PC compatibles. It was intended to take 90% of the PC market by 1990. By October 1988, less than 21% of The Times Top 1000 companies had decided to adopt OS/2 as their standard, at a rate of change of around 3.5% over the coming 6 years (Inset, 1988). Approaching seven million use Microsoft Windows 3 (launched May 1990). In May 1991, Microsoft claimed that by 1992 the sales of Windows 3 would have outstripped those of MS-DOS (Financial Times, 14 May, 1991). Currently, both IBM and Microsoft are working on the next version of their own products [OS/2.2 and Windows NT (new technology)].

How significant are social aspects to the general assessment of these different operating systems? Indeed, to what extent is the assessment itself a social accomplishment rather than a technical analysis?

## THE SOCIAL BASIS OF SOFTWARE ANALYSIS

Perhaps the easiest way to recognize the social basis of software capacity is to scrutinize the way software reviewers operate. If we might be justifiably sceptical of the claims made by the actual producers of software, are the reviewers, however objective they strive to be, able to provide a more or less objective account? It has to be admitted that my evidence here is limited, but it is nonetheless illustrative of the social processes I am concerned with. As one journalist interviewed responded:

We try and pretend that each product that comes in is equal in the eyes of our magazine, whether it comes from the smallest or the largest. Obviously some of the larger companies are going to have certain advantages. Their products, because they are used more widely, you're going to be able to get trained on them more easily, and the dealer who sells it to you can help you out with it ... whereas a small company might not be involved in the same degree of service. But on the whole we try to treat everything as if it were a level playing field (D.B. interview, 16 August 1991).

It should be clear from this that whatever else is involved there is considerably more to establishing the nature of a piece of software than objectively measuring its 'technical' performance. Whether the product comes from a 'reputable' stable and whether training and technical support are provided are social aspects only indirectly related to the actual product itself. But is there anything more to the social beyond this relatively obvious point? Well, it becomes clear that what counts as 'good' or 'bad' is seldom derived from the opinion of individual 'experts' but is the result of considerable negotiations. For example, as D.B. noted: 'If I was going to write about something and I thought there was a real glaring flaw I'd probably ask somebody else in the office, if it was going to ... I mean, give it a bad review, I would ask someone else in the office' (D.B.). Or as A.G. accepted:

Well I think what tends to happen is that people will read quite a few other magazines and talk to editors of those magazines. I think in journalism as a whole there is a fairly good exchange of ideas . . . if someone's been in the business for a relatively long time then they probably — I mean if you were reviewing a piece of software from Microsoft then you would probably have a preconceived idea of it being good, it generally is (A.G. interview, 15 August 1991).

But note that, even when ‘experts’ like this one need to develop a network of similar responses before determining what the technology will actually do, they appear to assume that other experts act objectively: ‘in the case of most the stuff which we do with freelancers, at the end of the day it’s what they say (we) have to go on that . . . they are the only ones who will be able to judge it properly’ (A.G.). Yet such expertise has to be assessed in the light of readers, rather than any allegedly objective nature of the technology as D.B. admitted:

We assume that the Macintosh way of doing things is superior, because the people that we're writing for have already bought Macintoshes and they don't want to be told that, well actually, you've wasted your money, you should have gotten a IBM PC instead... It's partly that and it's also partly because we really do believe it, I think we do ... right Chris? (Chris affirms belief) (DB interview, 16 August, 1991, my emphasis).

This search for allies in the construction of software reviews is not unique to the world of computing. On the contrary, it is through the development of alliances and associations that claims to expertise in many other if not all fields of study tend to be created and sustained (see Latour, 1986, 1987).

## THE PROBLEM OF USER-FRIENDLINESS

It may seem bizarre to those of us who have watched computers allegedly becoming friendlier with time that there was a time when user-friendliness seemed guaranteed. Have personal computers and their operating systems actually become more unfriendly as they have become more technically sophisticated? Well you might think so reading this review of the original IBM PC:

The key-board has a 16-character buffer which is pretty exciting when using the word processor — you have to keep a careful count of the number of times you hit the delete key! ... Easywriter is an interesting word processing package because it is entirely memory resident. This means that individual text files can't exceed 18,500 characters ... (but) once I got the hang of it, I found this package quite pleasant to use and I think that many people will find it offers all the facilities they're likely to need ... Since the functions within the macro languages are all written in machine code they operate at very high speeds ... With 256k I don't suppose that long variable names would be a problem ... Although I've not covered the DOS in detail, you can see that it provides everything that a user is likely to need (Review of IBM's PC in Personal Computer World, November, 1981, my emphasis).

Either computers have become more unfriendly since those halcyon days of just a decade ago or what counts as friendly has shifted with the increasing experience and sophistication of the user. A mere 3 years later, in a comparative review of operating systems in April 1984, Practical Computing regarded the three leading contenders at the time as: 'notorious error messages ... hard for newcomers to use' (CP/M); 'still unfriendly' (MS-DOS); and 'rather unwieldy for simple end-user tasks' (Unix).

One area where systems appear to be more unfriendly or friendly is in the viability of multi-users. Unix can support multiusers while MS-DOS cannot, and this has often been given as a reason for the switch from the latter to the former. Again, the issue can be seen as a problem inherent to the software of MS-DOS: MS-DOS has never been able to support multiusers so the problem has always been there. Or you can view it as another example of the way problems are retrospectively inserted into software. Before the development of software applications which facilitated multiusers, and before the 'problem' of single users was perceived, MS-DOS was, allegedly, perfectly adequate; only when other applications are developed are the 'problems' of MS-DOS apparent. We do not, at least at the moment, regard the inability of current popular PCs to embody software which translates spoken Japanese into desktop-published English (with different notation styles for different journals and American English rather than traditional English) as a problem of unfriendly or inadequate systems. However, we may read such problems back into current PCs a few years from now should such software and hardware make this kind of facility available.

But although the precise source of user-friendliness is as ambiguous as a definition of the phenomena, one thing appears to unite the users — or at least the writers — Unix is the unfriendliest of all. Unix is often represented as an inordinately unfriendly operating system: "Unix: the operating system we all love to hate" is a typical sentiment from Gopsill (1989). Or rather more graphically:

Somebody once described a Unix-based multi-user as a two men and a dog set up. The two men are the multiple users, Unix is the dog. No doubt many dogs are warm, responsive and altogether delightful creatures, but a different canine image dogs Unix. It is commonly seen as the bare-fanged slavering variety that would take your leg off as soon as look at you (Gues, D., review of multiuser systems in Computer Systems Europe, November, 1988).

In its defence, Steve Prentice, responsible for product marketing at the Santa Cruz Operation which claimed 45% of Unix licences in the UK in 1988, argues that user-friendliness is not something of great significance anyway:

You have to understand the role of an operating system. An operating system isn't for the end-user. Its purpose is to provide an environment for applications developers, whose requirements are for an environment rich in features. Unix does this (quoted in Gues, D., review of multiuser systems in Computer Systems Europe, November, 1988).

Note again here that the response to criticism is one that deploys the 'try a real expert tactic' already seen above. Another exponent of this line of defence is Russell Jones, for: 'Unix was built by programmers for programmers: it provides a veritable battery of programming aids and tools, but is not exactly renowned for the accessibility of its user interface' (Practical Computing, April, 1984). Or, clearer still, is Donald Norman's assessment of Unix:

For programmers, Unix is easy to understand and modify ... For all its virtues as a system — and it is indeed an elegant system — Unix is a disaster for the casual user. It fails both on the scientific principles of human engineering and even in just plain common-sense (Practical Computing, March, 1982).

The response of Michael Lesk, from Unix's parent, Bell Laboratories, is equally revealing: the 'caustic remarks about command names and other aspects of the human interface ... might be ignored, since he has no experimental tests to justify them; or they might even be taken as flattery of Unix since he does not name any system he likes better (Practical Computing, March, 1982).

Now one might want to suggest here that were Unix to have a friendlier interface — for the uninitiated — its merit as a programmers' tool might be perceived as slipping. Equally, were the esoteric mystique associated with Unix also to be replaced by 'child-friendly' interfaces then, again, something of the value and power associated with Unix might disappear. In effect, one might want to argue here that user-friendliness is not just a relative concept but one that embodies disparate and perhaps dichotomous notions of computer user skill and consequent problem. If computers are so friendly that even a child could use them why do we need to pay programmers skilled adult salaries? More importantly here, the problems of 'experts' and 'novice' are inversions of each other: a friendly interface is not a solution to an 'expert's' problem of 'inadequate' programming tools. Conversely, the absence of such tools is not a problem for the novice — but the perceived unfriendliness of the system may be.

This issue resurfaces in terms of visible or invisible interfaces. At one level (novice), operating systems are regarded as at their most friendly when they are invisible. Indeed, Unix is regarded as a problem for novices because it is so opaque. The new user is frightened off by such visibility, hence the need to hide the unfriendly 'kernel' under the rather more friendly operating system shell, with only the latter visible and directly accessible. However, descriptions of the graphical user interface (GUI) used for OS/2's Presentation Manager appear to invert the whole relationship of friendliness and covertness. In the words of one reviewer:

'If DOS seems less than user friendly, OS/2 is worse. But the Presentation Manager window-and-icon front end transforms the forbidding, naked operating system into an attractive package that everyone will be itching to play with' (Malone, S., OS/2 1.1 review in PC Magazine, February, 1989, my emphasis).

Compare that with Aldred's comments a year later:

Since this type of environment is graphically rather than character based, and completely hidden from the user, it is much easier to use ... But it is only in the last few years that all the major hardware and software companies have begun working with graphical operating environments. Maybe they suddenly noticed the unfriendly, difficult to understand, hard to use, character based operating systems were losing business (PC Business World, 27 February 1990, my emphasis).

Two things are apparent from this. First, GUIs are associated with friendliness and, second, this 'discovery' appears to be a very recent one. Let us look a little closer at 'gooeys'.

## COMMAND LINES VERSUS GRAPHICAL USER INTERFACES (GUIs)

GUIs originated in the Xerox's Palo Alto Research Centre in the 1970s with the Xerox Star Workstation, from which sprang the Lisa Personal Office System in 1983, then the Apple Macintosh in 1984. The same year Digital Research announced GEM (Graphics Environment Manager) which uses a windows-icon-mouse-pointing system (WIMP) similar to that which front-ends MS-DOS. Finally, Microsoft's Windows first made an appearance in 1985, with Windows 3, being launched in 1990 to rapturous applause from some:

'It's brilliant. It's the first Windowing system I've seen that really knocks the spots off the Mac ... there were a lot of problems with Windows 2 ... it's going to solve a lot of problems we currently have with Windows 2. These mostly revolve around memory and clipboard restraints (PC Business World, 22 May, 1990).

GUIs are graphically oriented interfaces that primarily use icons and facilitate direct manipulation of on-screen elements. They have become divided into two rather different formats recently: the 'familiar' or 'common' interfaces, which share characteristics across programs and are deemed suitable for 'sophisticated' users of programs such as Presentation Manager; and the 'intuitive interface', which assumes no prior skill or subsequent expertise and which is directed at very specific user groups (executive information systems, point of sale retail systems, etc.).

Prior to the development of GUIs most PC operating systems had operated via a command line (as non-Windows MS-DOS still does). The conventional approach to this would be to assume that GUIs solve an inherent technical problem of command lines — the absence of GUIs. As one interviewee, asked about current problems, noted:

The one (problem) that really springs to mind is the way people actually interface with computers. Previously it was always, with a PC anyway, it was always on a command line basis and people had been complaining for years ever since it came out that it was unhelpful and now with Windows 3 out it makes it a lot easier with having pictures on it to use (A.C. interview, 26 July 1991, my emphasis).

Note here the assumption that the 'problem' of interfacing via a command line has always been there — it is technically derived from the software itself. Indeed, it was because of this inherent problem that an alternative was sought from the very beginning. Yet the possibility that alternatives are actually generated by companies and, in the same process, create a problem for the previous software is also considered:

all the time there are people coming up with wonderful new technical devices, sometimes you get called into companies saying 'Look we've done this really great thing but we don't know what to use it for, we don't know who's going to buy it, we don't know how to sell it' ... People don't understand it, people haven't caught up with the technology (A.C. interview, 26 July, 1991).

## Or, as D.B. accepted:

the problem for the manufacturers is that computers are becoming much more capable and we still can't think of anything to do with these extra capabilities ... people are being sold (the ideas) to get them to buy more powerful equipment so that they can use a windowing interface. Well, once they've sold us on that what will they sell us on next? ... I don't think that users ... know what they really want ... users by and large swallow what the manufacturers are pushing (D.B. interview, 16 August, 1991).

So is the 'problem' of command lines one that was apparent right from the start, as A.C. suggested? This is always difficult to know. Certainly, my analysis of the review literature suggests not. In an extensive review of DOS, 5 years after PC-DOS was launched in August 1981, Ted Mirecki (1986) made no reference at all to the command line 'problem' which appears to be so self-evident to Window enthusiasts today. This, of course, does not mean that nobody regarded the issue as a problem; it means either that the articulated complaints have evaded my trawl through the computer press or that the issue was not articulated in the source — in which case perhaps it was not regarded as a sufficiently significant problem to merit professional attention. It could also mean that the issue was not in fact a problem at all. For example, Bidmead's review of MS-DOS in April, 1984, suggested that MS-DOS's superiority over CP/M lay, among other things, in its 'editing (of) the command line: long, incorrectly entered commands do not have to be retyped in full' (Practical Computing, april, 1984). Clearly, then, there are things in the command line system that frustrated some users, but it is not clear whether these frustrations predated the development of alternatives. There is certainly no mention of the lack of a GUI, and the consequent retention of the command line, as a problem of MS-DOS. Similarly, in reviewing OS/2 in June 1988, Eva White took no exception to the 'problem' of the missing GUI and argued instead that: 'The command interface of OS/2 is very similar to DOS... This allows users already comfortable with DOS to ease themselves slowly into OS/2' (Byte, June, 1988).

If, then, the 'missing' GUI in MS-DOS and CP/M is eventually solved by Windows or a Macintosh, what evidence do the journalists cite to advise their readers how to circumvent the problem of command lines? Well, the authoritative sources cited are rather thin. According to Skok:

Studies by Peat Marwick have already shown that Apple Macintosh users, with the benefits of GUI and transparent data interchange, use two or three times more software per workstation than PC users and, therefore, make a comparably greater use of the machine (Skok, M., in PC Business World, 29 August, 1989).

And a survey is cited by the same magazine the following year:

A US survey (undertaken by Temple, Barker and Sloan) published by Microsoft says GUIs help users to work faster and more accurately than character-based systems. It also says GUIs give lower levels of frustration and fatigue and that users need less training and support. On average, experienced GUI users completed 35% more tasks than character based users in the same period (PC Business World, 19 June, 1990).

But other 'experts' pose questions that cast at least a small shadow over such research — without denying the results. PC Magazine, for example, was quite clear that GUIs were the way to the future but denied the possibility of proving this:

The real issue is what they can do for everyday computing. Save time? Make programs less difficult to use? Ease the learning curve? Make programs more powerful? The answers are yes, yes, yes and yes. Because it offers standardised commands from one program to the next, a good GUI will save you time in both the learning of the application and their use... You won't find any benchmark tests in this survey. Such tests just can't measure the look and feel of a GUI; nor can they weigh the subjective benefits — such as ease of use and a shortened learning curve (PC Magazine review of GUIs, October, 1989).

Not everyone was enthralled by the Windows 3.0 icon-based system, however; and the arguments against it are often quite varied. Michael Skok, for example, attacked it for only offering precisely what many regarded as its most significant advantage:

When you cost what it takes to upgrade to Windows 3.0 the only benefit is the graphical user interface. Beyond that you gain nothing. The fact is obvious to me that customers are shocked at having made this investment. We can't live with DOS as an underlying platform for the future (PC Business World, 18 September, 1990).

Others attacked the GUI (this time the one used in IBM's Presentation Manager) not because it was the only benefit but on the grounds that it was not a benefit at all. Thus, in their review of operating systems in June 1991, Henning and Jackson argue that:

Another useful feature is the use of DOS batch files from an OS/2 command line. I've long managed my PC by the use of batch files ... and to my mind is much easier than using icons ... I hope this (OS/2's Presentation Manager) isn't the thin end of a graphics-only wedge (Henning, E. and Jackson, P., review of operating systems in PC User, 22 May–4 June, 1991.

The sceptical approach to GUIs is further developed in terms of denying the problem which GUIs appear to solve:

there is a commonly held belief that graphical systems are easier to learn — more 'intuitive' to use the jargon — than the DOS style of command line entry. I'm not entirely convinced of this. All most people need from DOS are commands to change directory, copy files, list a directory and run programs . . . I find the mouse preferable for most things, but as you get to know the many shortcut keyboard commands the habit of using them begins to creep in. Even with such mouse-intuitive operations as switching windows I occasionally found myself pressing Alt-Tab instead (Malone, S., review of OS/2 1.1 in PC Magazine, February 1989).

Yet, however much individuals resist the move to the Windows solution (to a problem they may not recognize), the development of Windows as the new PC standard implies that such MS-DOS stalwarts may have little choice in the future but to 'up' grade. But, while Malone argued that using the mouse may be a problem, some reviewers began to argue that double clicking the mouse was the real problem.

a nice touch (of the Windows 286-or 386-based Windows Manager) is that you need only to single click on an icon to run it. The draw back is that the MS-DOS Executive, over which Windows Manager has no control, will always need a double click [Dubash, M., review of hDC (MS-Windows Suite) in Personal Computer World, October, 1989].

One wonders whether the user of the original IBM PC would agree with this assessment of a technical problem — we have moved from a point were a 16-character buffer in 1981 is a solution to the technical problem of memory limits to one in 1989 where having to click a mouse twice is a technical problem awaiting a technical solution. I would suggest that we might profitably start thinking about these as social constructions rather than inherently technical problems.

## PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS

One implication is that the development of technology may be as much associated with the creation of new problems 'as the resolution of old ones. If managers or computer users are hoping that the next technical fix will indeed prove to be the end of their problems, then this approach suggests that they are going to be sadly disappointed. In short, Sisyphus rules OK! — at least as long as we continue to see software and hardware (or should it be 'riskware'?) as technical artefacts rather than cultural products.

Second, and relatedly, it implies that users should take a much more sceptical approach to the hype surrounding computer developments, especially those which promise to solve technical problems for you. It is not a question of denying some technical assessments in favour of others, but of emphasizing the social origin of these assessments, and recognizing that solutions generate problems. This is not a call for Luddism: it does not mean throwing Windows 3.1 out and digging up your old CP/M machine. It is not that CP/M is technically better than Windows 3.1, rather it is a plea to consider the development of problems and solutions as social not technical issues. The warning is not 'don't buy it!' but 'caveat machinae emptor!' (let the buyer of the machine beware!)

Third, users should perhaps pay more attention to history in that the social construction of problems tends to occur over time and in relation to related technical developments elsewhere. To return to the beginning, the mismatch between the 'demands of the situation' and the capabilities of the human operators is only a mismatch if we, or rather influential actors, construe it as such at that time. The problem may be undermined, or at least held up for closer scrutiny, if we problematize the problem itself.
