# Designing with dialogue charts: a qualitative content analysis of end-user designers' experiences with a software engineering design tool

L. J. Calloway & G. Ariav\*

Pace University, the School of Computer Science and Information Systems, 1 Pace Plaza, New York, NY 10038, USA, and \*University of Tel Aviv, Racanati School of Management, Ramat Aviv, Israel

Abstract. Software engineering tools used by designers are critical to most systems development methodologies, and successful methodologies are critical to improved productivity. However, the way in which designers use and relate to software engineering tools, whether computer assisted or not, has received little attention in the design literature. The purpose of this study is to gain insight into how people perceive the process of using design tools. The study is a qualitative analysis of interview information from participants in a field experiment. Four teams of student designers used various design tools during the development of interactive information systems typical of those that might be developed by sophisticated end-users. The research reported here is an exploratory study aimed at understanding how designers use one of these tools, the dialogue charts. The broad range of purposes included the uses predicted by the reference literature on design. However, the end-user designers also used the tool opportunistically — they found a broader range of tool usage than the literature on design tools predicted. For example, they consistently used the tool as a communications vehicle among different phases of design and development. The results show that the relationships these 'end-user designers' developed with the target tool are expressed in highly emotional language. These attitudes are tightly coupled with the purposes for which the designers use the tool. The methodology uses a field experiment as a treatment and a semistructured interview with a hidden agenda for gathering data. The data analysis techniques draw on the concepts of discovering grounded theory as described by Glaser and Strauss. They further draw on the concepts of qualitative content analysis synthesized by Krippendorff and the qualitative data analysis methods described by Miles and Huberman.

Key words: CASE, dialogue design tools, qualitative methodology, system design processes.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Improving the process of design is critical to improving systems development productivity in management information systems. Design tool vocabularies used by designers to plan and design these information systems are intended to improve productivity. However, little is known about how designers use software engineering tools, whether they are traditional or computer-assisted techniques. Productivity is used in the management information systems literature to mean the timing, cost and quality of products and service delivered by the information systems function (see Henderson & Cooprider, 1990). The aim of this research study is to use qualitative and exploratory interviews to generate insight into the way designers use tools during the process of design. The particular tool studied is the Dialogue Charts (DCs), a design vocabulary that models the conceptual structure of user-system dialogue in interactive systems (Ariav & Calloway, 1988).

This topic is of importance to users and vendors of tools in a context where software engineering tools and computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools are seen as a key element in improving software development productivity. Little is known about how designers actually use software engineering tools — whether traditional or computer assisted — to plan and design information systems. The research focus is often on the tools, not how people use the tools. In this study, the focus is the reverse.

The main findings of the study show that the relationships that people developed with the tool vocabulary are rich and varied, and the extraordinary variety appears to be related to the designers as well as to the specific tool. Designers express strong attitudes about their tools during process of use, and they describe them in emotional terms. Designers use the tools opportunistically; the most salient examples are their use for communication and for coding. Designers discovered the value of this tool late in the system development process, and did not delve into the effects of the tool on the final product from an engineering point of view.

The research reconstructed and studied, unobtrusively, tool usage experiences of 16 student systems designers who were divided into four teams. Each team planned, designed and implemented an interactive system during an undergraduate upper-level course in database and communications systems. The student designers used dataflow diagrams, entity relationship diagrams, pseudocode and DCs, which were the focus of this study. The DCs (Ariav & Calloway, 1988) are representative of a class of design vocabularies used for modelling the conceptual structure of user-system dialogue in interactive systems.

Design tools are vocabularies of abstract symbols representing some aspect of system structure along with a formal or informal set of rules about how these symbols are combined. The essence of a design tool is in the vocabulary it provides for modelling design problems, the rules of grammar for tool usage and the framework it sets for arguing about possible design solutions. Dataflow diagrams, flowcharts and programming languages are design tools.

Appendix 1 introduces the vocabulary, grammar and framework for the dialogue charts. The DCs were first documented in the Communications of the ACM's publication SIGCHI, (Ariav & Calloway, 1988). They were developed as a conceptual design tool for interactive dialogue. The DCs are a paper and pen production technology. According to Henderson & Cooprider (1990, p. 232), a production technology has 'functionality that directly impacts the capacity of an individual(s) to generate planning or design decisions and subsequent artifacts or products'. The end-user also used the DCs as a coordination technology. A coordination technology is defined as 'functionality that enables or supports the interactions of multiple agents in the execution of a planning or design task' (Henderson & Cooprider, 1990, p. 233). The end-user designers applied the tool to the entire development process, and also used it to communicate and coordinate their analysis and development work.

Section 2 briefly discusses the research strategy developed for the study. In section 3, the results from the combined analysis of the four teams are presented. In section 4 the research findings and the implications for managers, practitioners and educators are explored. The paper concludes with a discussion of further research directions in section 5.

## 2 RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

There is a growing sense in the management information systems (MIS) research community that new ways of discovering knowledge are needed, and qualitative approaches to MIS research seem to offer some possibilities. In December 1990, a conference on research challenges, perceptions and new approaches in the information systems arena was held in Copenhagen, Denmark. The objective of the conference was to discover ways to balance technical knowledge with understanding of complex human, organizational and societal contexts. The conference resulted in a book (Nissen et al., 1991). The book includes a detailed description of the methodology used in this study in 'developing and using a qualitative methodology to study relationships among designers and tools' (Calloway & Ariav, 1991). One area in which little basis for comparative analysis exists is the area of end-users' responses to design methodologies developed by the information systems and the computer science communities. The research methodology described here was conceived to explore how designers perceive using design tools during system development. New design tools are created and marketed every day, but there is little research available to enlighten information systems designers and managers about the effects or implications of incorporating these tools into their design environments. This situation is relatively common in the information systems design field, where, in spite of intensive research activity centring on information systems and information system design, relatively little is known about these topics (Lyytinen, 1987; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989).

The research methodology draws on the work on generating grounded theory by Glasser & Strauss (1967), the work of Miles & Huberman (1984) in formulating more rigorous qualitative analysis methods and the work on replicable research methods using qualitative content analysis by Krippendorff (1980). The hybrid methodology is fully documented elsewhere (Calloway, 1988; Calloway & Ariav, 1991). The methodology is encapsulated in Table 1.

## 2.1 Treatment

In order to simulate a production environment in which end-user designers would use a design tool as one tool among many productivity aids, we organized a field experiment. In a field experiment, the context of the situation is experienced as real to the participants, although the actual situation is experimentally designed by the researcher (Campbell & Stanley, 1983; McGrath et al.).

The subjects in this field experiment were candidates for the Bachelor of Arts degree in information systems at a large urban university. Essentially, the subjects studied five courses in the information systems area at the university. Required courses were (1) introduction to computer science, in which the students learned Pascal; (2) fundamentals of computer systems, that included hardware, software and assembly language programming; and (3) systems analysis and design, that included Hierarchical Input/Output (HIPO) charts, entity relationship diagrams, dataflow diagrams and decision modelling and analysis techniques. All of the students had two additional electives. Most of the students chose the same two electives: (4) artificial intelligence, in which they learned logic and prolog programming; and (5) decision support systems, which included design and delivery of a fourth-generation language system.

The course used for this research was database systems and data communications. During this course the students were taught to use dialogue charts. This course required the students to break up into teams and to select an interactive system that could be modelled and developed in a third-generation language (Pascal or Cobol) and which had complex queries (those that accessed more than one file) and a menu structure at least two levels deep. The designs were evaluated by the instructor, and any misuse of the design languages had to be corrected before the team proceeded to implementation. The system that each team built had to work. The queries had to be demonstrated to the class. They were required to use entity relationship diagrams, pseudocode and dialogue charts in the design of their systems.

Using the DCs imposes a philosophy of top-down, nested systems design. It imposes a design strategy that meshes with the philosophy of stepwise refinement. The DCs move from a broad breakdown of interactive tasks to be accomplished jointly by the user and the system to a specific decomposition of these tasks into subtasks. Decomposition continues until the joint user/system tasks are separated to user-only and system-only tasks. The decomposition is hierarchical and must take place in a specific control structure that limits decisions to those used in most structured programming languages and in the Pascal syntax charts (Jensen & Wirth, 1978).

Using a term project as the deliverable allowed us to create an environment in which the task took 3 months, and we could generate experiences about designing and delivering a complex system. We used teams of designers rather than individuals because it better simulated a production environment. All teams followed a structured design method. Each team selected its own application, in order to add variety to the experiences.

These subjects were not employed as designers, although they were preparing to enter the marketplace after graduation at the end of the term. However, although they had less experience than professional designers, they were similar to any end-user or professional designer with less than 2 years' work experience using a new tool for design. The subjects in the study are thus referred to as end-user designers, since they meet or exceed the qualifications usually possessed by professional end-users who assume direct responsibility for systems development and data processing tasks for the service of their own direct benefit (see Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990).

Each design team is treated as a site in the study.

A single site is the phenomena in a bounded context that make up a single 'case study', whether that case is an individual in a setting, a small group, or a larger unit such as a department, organization, or community (Miles & Huberman, 1984).

## 2.2 Initial data capture

We selected a post-implementation interview with an agenda that was hidden from the respondents as the instrument to capture the experiences of the design teams. Each team was asked to respond to a semistructured interview after the term was over and the projects were demonstrated, submitted and graded. The students had no knowledge that we would be interviewing them until a week before the term ended. Then they were told that if they participated in the interview they would get three points extra on their grade.

In a semistructured interview the questions used to elicit perceptions are defined broadly, but the answers are open-ended. The agenda that was 'hidden' was our interest in the dialogue charts. It was kept hidden by treating the responses to all the tools they used as equally important.

## 2.3 Data reduction

The data reduction relied on the combined methods of grounded theory generation and content analysis. Content analysis is used on symbolic and semantic information rather than on numerical data, and it is especially useful when the extent and nature of the information cannot be fully characterized in advance. Both quantitative and qualitative content analysis depend on defining a unit of analysis. Quantitative content analysis usually uses a word, sentence or paragraph as the unit of analysis when dealing with written or printed materials. In qualitative content analysis, the idea or thought is the unit. Some content analyses create a complete coding scheme to apply to the units of analysis before beginning the data reduction. Other studies, including this study, develop a set of categories from the literature. The literature categories are compared with the data and modified by the data (after Miles & Huberman, 1984). The exact categories are not known in advance and neither is the coding scheme. Reliability depends on inter-coder agreement that the categories are appropriate. Because of the qualitative nature of the research and the data, there is no formal inter-observer reliability measure. Consensus consisted in agreement among the researchers that the coded categorizations realistically captured aspects of the design process indicated by the mentions. No attempt was made to agree that every possible category was coded, only that those coded were appropriate. In qualitative studies, the context functions as the equivalent of the dependent variable, and the data function as the independent variables. Thus, if the related experiences indicate that the tool is perceived as useful, then usefulness is considered as a dimension of the design context.

There are no concepts of counting the occurrences or incidents that fall into a category in grounded theory generation, since the purpose of the category is conceptual. One incident or many incidents in the data might provide the material for defining the category. The category is illustrated by incidents in the data.

## 2.3.1 The unit of analysis

The fundamental unit of analysis in this study is defined as a mention. A mention is a group of words uttered by the end-user designers about the DCs. One might argue forever about the boundaries of mentions. The rule of thumb we followed was to try for the smallest mentions that made sense in relation to the categorizations. Mentions often occurred in groups. A group of mentions is found by finding a set of mentions surrounded by words which were not about the dialogue charts.

The taped interviews were analysed using qualitative content analysis. First the tapes were scanned for any mention of the dialogue charts. When a mention was found, it was transcribed. Each mention was categorized by studying its relationship to the categories. When (and if) a suitable primary category was found, a subcategory was located or created based on keywords from the mention. For example, the mention

'... we did a rain dance.'

'It was just ritual.'

'We did it because we had to hand it in.'

'Yes. We did the dialogue charts because it was called for...'

was categorized under 'purpose'. Then we created the subcategory ritual: rain dance.

A mention can relate to more than one category (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Since the process of qualitative data reduction and analysis is iterative, mentions were sometimes merged or sets of mentions repartitioned. Ideally, data are continuously collected in grounded theory until no 'new' categorizations are found and additional mentions are not adding any new information to a category. Content analysis is normally done on historical information that does not change. We analysed the interviews as though they were historical data, since the information in the interviews could not change.

## 2.3.2 Researcher as data analyst

Assessing the contribution of qualitative studies requires, in part, understanding the academic context, values and experience of the researcher(s) (Morey, 1986). In ethnography, a closely related method of enquiry, the researcher is the research instrument. The investigator, therefore, may state his or her assumptions up front, before the data analysis if possible, and gives some sense about what he or she expects to find in the study. If categories are expected to be uncovered that describe the context of experience, the research documents the categories by providing references and possible exemplars of data that are expected to fit into the category. Stating the assumptions and the theoretical orientation of the researcher helps reduce bias and also helps to reveal researcher bias to the reader.

In this study, both the researchers were of the opinion that dialogue charts are a useful design tool for interactive systems. The opinion is based on the fact that the tool includes vocabulary, grammar and rules found in numerous tools used for similar purposes (see Appendix 1). The opinion is also based on the fact that the dialogue charts were developed by the researchers especially to meet the design requirements of human-computer interaction dialogue.

To reduce bias, the researchers created a complex task that required several design tools, and used an interviewing technique that allowed the respondents to say whatever they wanted to say about all the tools, including the dialogue charts. Thus, the respondents were not limited to answering previously (possibly biased) questions. All comments about the dialogue charts were transcribed and analysed.

Additionally, in the nature of qualitative analysis, much of the information that surfaces will be outside the theoretical expectations of the study. It is, therefore, especially critical that the information is not stripped of its context; otherwise, the conclusions the researcher promotes might mislead the reader. For example, in this study some results appear to be understandable in terms of psychology or organizational behaviour. These are outside the expertise of the researcher, and must be reported only as raw data that could be subjected to further analysis and further exploration within the domain of expertise. Furthermore, the researcher must minimally document the procedure of investigation on a level that allows some insight into the internal decision making during the research process (Kirk & Miller, 1986).

## 2.3.3 The seed categories

After Miles & Huberman (1984), the study adopted a set of five broad research categories filtered from the literature on software engineering and dialogue design. The five initial categories were stated as research questions. Within each question, subcategories were suggested that were drawn from the literature on design. The five categories captured the recommendations, suggestions and expectations about when designers would use dialogue design tools, how the tools were expected to be used and what effects on the design and the design process they were expected to produce. For a complete description of the derivation and analysis of the categories, see Calloway, 1988.

Q1 Purpose: For what design purposes are the dialogue charts used?

Gathering intelligence, goal elaboration, design generation, design evaluation, communication.

The objective of question 1 was to capture the purpose of design during the entire design and development process. Malhotra et al.'s (1980) empirical studies showed that the same essential design activities of goal elaboration, design generation, and design evaluation are repeated during various stages of system development and at various levels of hierarchy and design abstraction. We also included intelligence from Simon's (1960) decision analysis framework and communication, the activity of transferring information from one task or person to another. Communication is discussed in the literature as a way of creating a document for users (e.g. Moran, 1981) and for maintainers of systems (e.g. Brown & the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, 1982).

Q2 Stage: In what stages of system development do designers find the dialogue charts to be helpful?

Documentation and analysis, logical/conceptual design, implementation design, programming/coding, testing.

This question was used to keep track of those instances when the chronological stage of design of development was mentioned. The initial set of categories came from the information systems development life cycle according to Couger (1982).

Q3 Product: What are the perceived effects of the dialogue charts on the products or results of the design effort?

Hierarchical and modularity structure, control structure, data structure/architecture.

This question is aimed at learning the relationships between using the tools and the (possibly intermediate) products created during a development life cycle. There are four dimensions of structure described by Couger (1982). They are the general modular and hierarchical structure, the control structure, the dataflow structure and the data structure.

Q4 Process: What are the perceived effects of dialogue charts on the nature of the design process?

Design philosophy, constraints.

Pursuant to developing a product, certain processes and procedures occur. This question keeps track of references and mentions about how the design processes and procedures are constrained, dictated or ordered by design tools. We postulated two categories in the absence of a reference set of processes. These were constraints, which limit design choices available to the designer through strictness or rigidity of the tool vocabulary or rules, and design philosophy, which determines the direction of the design, such as top-down or bottom-up or outside-in.

Q5 Attitudes and attitude patterns: How do designers feel about the dialogue charts and about using them?

Learning, task performance, subjective satisfaction, retention, errors.

The initial set of categories is based on Shneiderman's (1984) list of measurable human factors and includes: learning, which is the time required to learn the tool; task performance, which is the ease of performing the task or the time required to perform the task; errors, which includes the kinds and numbers of errors; and subjective satisfaction/dissatisfaction, which collected the designer's feelings and reactions about using the tool, especially when they were not directly related to learning or task performance.

To summarize, the methodology was designed to:

1 Provide a treatment that would generate complex behaviour similar to what would be expected in a commercial environment.

2 Use an unobtrusive instrument to capture symbolic data.

3 Reduce the data in a precise and replicable fashion.

## 3.1.1 Purposes

The variety of purposes that the design teams mentioned was diverse, and some were unanticipated. Forty per cent of the mention codings were about the purpose of design. A third of the purpose categorizations were about communication.

Goal elaboration, requirements analysis. The process of designing with the DCs imposes a top-down philosophy of design, and the teams commented on it.

You start out with the very simplest, the highest level ... break that down, and you go down and down and down until you hit the lowest level (1.30).

Requirements document. The results of the initial design are a requirements document for the systems functions. The usefulness of this document surprises end-user designers and leads to subjective satisfaction as expressed by comments such as:

They literally tell you what your system is going to do (3.17)

and

It really is a true map, which is surprising (1.24).

Design generation—implementation design. The teams used the DCs in implementation design of menus and complex queries. All four teams talked about menu design with the DCs, but one team was not successful in using them. Three teams were positive and the fourth was neutral.

We had gone over the dialogue charts... The menus were derived from that (1.25).

From there [dialogue charts] ... we know what to code into our menus, and where to have subordinate menus (2.14).

Two teams used the dialogue charts in planning their 'complex queries', and two spoke of them as similar to structured English.

It's structured English to a user interface, that would be the parallel (2.23).

Design generation—programming designs. All of the teams mentioned the DCs in relation to coding, and three were positive.

That's what I programmed from (3.6).

The dialogue charts are a way of programming your system — without programming your system (2.22).

We took our dialogue charts, and our files, and our menus that we designed (from the Dialogue Charts, by the way), and we — actually started to code them (1.27).

The fourth team was not successful in trying to code from the dialogue charts.

Design evaluation. Two teams used the DCs to facilitate design evaluation. As an example, one team used them to decide where to go in case of an error.

... should we go back to the very beginning menu? Or should we go back to where they made the mistake? (1.33).

Communication. Communication was a key use of the DCs by these teams. Previous comments that imply communications describe the DCs as a map of the system, an outline of the system, looking top down, the names of program modules, and a guide to designing menus and evaluating them. The teams were able to use the dialogue charts during the entire development process for a variety of purposes. Although the design teams found the DCs easy to use and applied them throughout the development process for numerous tasks, they nevertheless report surprise and amazement that they were useful.

It – usually, it seems like it's a tool, right, and we all say it's a tool, and we all agree, and we all do the exercise, and it seems like the whole value of the exercise is making you think about how to draw this little diagram. But this diagram [dialogue charts] ... it had a lot of value in that, but, then, it had applications further than that (1.40).

That's why it's so surprising. For once, we actually used it further on (1.41).

The teams used the DCs in coding.

... procedure for procedure, it's just straight off the Dialog Chart ... (3.10).

That's what I coded off of (3.2).

It made coding really a breeze (2.10).

It helped in the implementation (1.23).

One designer hoped to use them for system maintenance.

In terms of helping them maintain their system, I do keep in the back of my mind the dialogue charts, which I thought were great. In terms of helping explain myself to them, what ideas I had. Whereas before maybe it was just kind of haphazard. Now, I have some structure for explaining, and why I'm thinking what I'm thinking (1.48).

## 3.1.2 Processes of design

Philosophies. The design teams identified the philosophies of top-down hierarchical decomposition of tasks within specific control structures. For example, one team described dialogue charts as

an outline of the general system. Sort of like looking-top-down (3.17).

The process was described as iterative.

As you go through them and through them and through them... You realize things that you haven't thought about before... (1.20).

They identified an outside-in philosophy, recommended by Parnas (1969) and Keen & Gambino (1980), for designing the user interaction first.

... usually, the user interface is like the last thing you think about ... (3.19).

Constraints. One team found the constraints helpful because it forced them to put their ideas into

something concrete (1.14).

and provided

a strong control over what was going on (1.35).

Another team, team 4, found them difficult to follow:

sometimes you had to go around the DCs (4.14).

## 3.1.3 Products of design

The process comments were tightly woven with the perceptions of the design products.

Documentation. All four teams noted that the DCs provided a document of the system. Exemplar remarks are:

It's like diagrams of how the system should work (1.5).

It was all very well laid out (2.18).

It's a map of the system (1.24).

One team spoke several times about 'seeing' the system. The DCs helped them to

see the control (1.35).

Hierarchical and modular structure. Two teams used DCs as guides for modularization and coding. For example:

... we started doing it, almost like in modules. almost like a factory assembly line (3.20).

## Another team said

I think dialogue charts were basically names of program modules . . . Well, they were a little bit more than that (3.11).

Control structure. One team mentioned that the DCs explained the control structure for them. The DCs showed them

at what point you would intercept the user to get a response. And based on that response what would be the next step (1.5).

You kinda see the flow of everything (1.6).

You have to diagram where things are coming in from and going through (1.35).

## 3.1.4 Attitudes and attitude patterns

Almost half the mention codings reflected attitudes about using the tool vocabulary, and half of these were about satisfaction or dissatisfaction.

Subjective satisfaction/dissatisfaction. Most of the mentions expressed subjective satisfaction. Team 1, which had the most mentions in general, mentioned that there was value in drawing the DCs, but the true value was discovered later, when the charts were used in the more detailed levels of design.

Like I said, dialogue charts were, surprisingly, very helpful. They really gave us the basis for so many of the next steps (1.38).

Many mentions indicated surprise that the tool was useful. Team 1's comments emphasized the surprise that the tools were useful in later stages. A typical comment was

these seemed to be the most helpful, because when we did get into the later stages, we did actually use them. Much to my surprise (1.10).

Team 2 was also amazed that the DCs were helpful. The coding task was recalled as

all strangely easy (2.19).

and

amazingly simple (2.28).

Another team called them 'fantastic' and 'tremendous' (3.19). Coding the interface was 'really a breeze' (2.13), 'a pleasure' (2.16) and 'strange' (2.17). If being alive is considered satisfying, then one another team was satisfied.

I would've been dead without the dialogue charts (3.6).

Task performance. Teams found the experience of creating the DCs to be mixed. Dialogue charting is said to be

trouble (2.4).

## They

took a long time (2.1).

## - and they

didn't take much time (2.30).

It appears that the perceptions of how long they took and how difficult they were to create changed. When the team discovered some value in the DCs, they began to think they were easier and faster. Another team found the DCs both easy and fast:

we blew those off in no time (3.4).

And the last team found the DCs 'easy' (but not subsequently useful).

We did it because we had to hand it in (4.4).

Learning. Interestingly, the teams did not understand the value of the DCs until they learned they were useful. This learning led to comments such as:

Dialogue charts shouldn't have been hard (2.5).

Team 1 similarly revised its opinion of the DCs and also redid them. They made six references to recognizing the value. The following mention is an example, a response to being asked what they might do differently, the same or not at all if the step were repeated.

... if each of us would have prepared a little more separately, especially for the dialogue charts.

Yes.

We may have recognized the value of it sooner (c. 21).

## Another comment was

Once we got through the first three applications... They were all the same; the structure was similar (2.34).

## 3.2 Multicoded mentions

The categories were related. After all of the mentions had been categorized and reviewed by a second researcher, the mentions were charted to see if any correlations among different categories appeared. Mentions that were multicoded were those that could not meaningfully be divided. There were 56 multicoded messages, constituting 45% of the mentions.

All but one of the mentions that were multicoded shared either a purpose category of an attitude category or both. Most of the multicodings were subcategories within the purpose category, or two attitude categories, or an attitude category with a purpose category. This indicates that the codings within the purpose and the attitude categories were very tightly linked, and that the purposes of design discussed by the team members were closely related to an attitude. The multicodings within the purpose category reinforce the concept of Malhotra et al. (1980) of design as a series of nest iterations between goal elaboration, design generation and design evaluation.

Designing with the DCs imposed certain philosophies and constraints on the process of design. The products and documents of the design appear to be clear and easy to understand. Stages of design were captured as part of the purpose of design. Reported use of the DCs was tightly coupled with attitudes and attitude patterns (see Fig. 1).

## 4 DISCUSSION OF THE RESULTS OF THE STUDY

The results of the research are discussed in relation to four areas. First, the themes and patterns that emerged about how designers used the DCs are discussed, especially those that appear to extend beyond the literature (section 4.1). Next, some ramifications of the delayed discovery of the value of the tool are discussed (section 4.2). Then, some suggestions are drawn regarding educational issues about design tools and the process of design (section 4.3). Finally, some implications for managers who select and manage design environments are discussed (section 4.4).

## 4.1 End-user designers and their tools

As can be seen from the examples in section 3, the majority of the characteristics suggested in the literature for design tools are confirmed by the results of this study. However, the study reveals some gaps in the previous literature. First, most of the comments that the end-user designers made about using the dialogue charts are about the attitudes and attitude patterns they developed towards the tool. Second, although communication is part of the recommended requirements for a complete software engineering methodology, the idea that the end-user teams would use whatever tool seemed useful for communication has not been addressed. This leads us to the next gap, which is that users appear to use tools opportunistically.

## 4.1.1 Attitude dimensions

The end-user designers described DCs with strong feelings and in emotional language. The literature relating to attitude patterns and software tools is sparse and normative. According to Jacob (1986), for example, a good design tools should make the task easier and not harder. End-user designers described tasks in which they used DCs as 'amazingly simple', 'a breeze', 'very easy', 'terrific', 'a pleasure', 'strangely easy', and 'fantastic'. These positive emotional expressions coincide with the descriptions of the tool as helpful, valuable, important and useful. Some team members were surprised as well as amazed that the tool was useful. Furthermore, comments expressing strong attitudes were commonly intertwined with comments about using the tool for a purpose.

## 4.1.2 Communicating with tools

The end-user designers used the tool for communicating information among all of the stages of design, for interpersonal communication about design decisions, and even suggested using the DC descriptions of the system as a vehicle for educating users and maintainers. Some of these uses are suggested in the literature. Both Moran (1981) and Jacob (1986) hold that the specification should function as a user manual, and, according to Brown and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (1982), the specification should be useful as a design document for the systems maintainers. However, most design tools create barriers between team members and between phases of the development cycle. Each team member often has his or her own personal view and domain representation. Furthermore, information must be passed from vocabulary to vocabulary between phases of the development cycle, because the life cycle coverage of each tool vocabulary is limited (Webster, 1988).

In this study, however, end-user designers describe the DCs as a requirements document or map, and maintain that they contributed to all subsequent design activities in physical design and coding. The end-user designers spoke of the DCs as practically identical to several design activities: They were a way of 'programming your system without programming your system', like 'structured English to a user interface', and 'basically names of program modules'. These uses indicate that the dialogue charts filled a need the end-user designers had for a coordination technology (see Henderson & Cooprider, 1990).

## 4.1.3 Opportunistic use of tools

The extensive use of the tool for communication purposes reveals a third important area of interaction with tools and tool vocabularies. The dialogue charts are a tool specifically developed for creating conceptual design and implementation design decision structures for interactive systems. Nevertheless, they were used enthusiastically for other purposes, for example to design the code, to determine program modules and to assist in evaluating the system design. Either the dialogue charts are an appropriate tool for communication and coordination, or end-user designers will use whatever tools they have that they feel will meet their needs, regardless of any side-effects of using the tools, and regardless of whether or not the tool was 'supposed' to meet the application need.

## 4.2 Delayed discovery of the value of the tool

The end-user designers discovered the value of the DCs late in the development life cycle. Their realization came when they used DCs for communication, programming generation and design evaluation. The specification of the interface was used by the end-user designers as an outline of the system. It was used as a modular decomposition for coding.

A natural question to ask is whether or not the information that is being communicated is necessary and sufficient for these unanticipated purposes. The team members expressed very positive feelings about the value of the DCs in the tasks for which they were used. Nevertheless, there remain some questions as to whether or not the end-user designers (or professional designers) would be aware of the limitation of their tools. The responsibility for creating complete tools, therefore, is better determined through the informed analysis of the tool maker. The subjective satisfaction with the tool and the possible lack of awareness of the scope of early decisions make it critically important that design tools create designs and enforce design decisions that are valid, at least theoretically, for the particular application.

## 4.3 Implications for information systems educators

Some design tools that are taught by IS educators are seen as ritualistic in that the exact value of the tool is not apparent to the designers. A basic assumption that underlies qualitative work is that people act towards reality in a way that is consistent with their perceptions of reality (Frake, 1966). We might expect a designer, therefore, to most use a tool whose value is most perceived and understood. Educators might focus specifically on the use of the design tool being taught as well as the relationships, if any, among the tool's vocabulary and abstractions to the rest of the design process.

Additionally, students in IS should be educated as to the structural implications of their tools. Dialogue charts enforce a design that uses basic program structures of sequence, repetition and choice, and the designs are hierarchical. These structured enforcements ensure that the final products will be modular and loosely coupled. They also, however, could lead to obtuse, deep hierarchies of choices that are difficult for end-users. In this study, the design vocabulary seemed to make these difficulties apparent to the designers, who made several comments about adjusting their design's dialogue structure in order to design a system that would be easier to use. These results indicate that the design curriculum should continue to invest time in teaching the concepts of abstract modelling to educate designers about the implications of early design decisions.

## 4.4 Implications for information systems managers and practitioners

Information systems managers and practitioners will find that the results of this study point to interesting issues of designer productivity, work satisfaction and the selection of design tools and methodologies.

## 4.4.1 Designer productivity

The DCs are a paper and pen design tool, aimed at increasing the productivity of designers. The impetus behind both upper and lower CASE technologies is the hope of increased productivity. Estimates for productivity increases through tool automation range from 15–40% at Arthur Anderson & Co. (Voelcker, 1988) to 300% and more from advertisers of products (Norman & Nunamaker, 1989). Most design tools, however, create communications barriers because their limited scope and coverage of the development process leads to difficulties communicating from vocabulary to vocabulary (Hackathom & Karimi, 1988; Webster, 1988). Selecting a tool that designers use throughout the design process should improve productivity by improving communications and coordination during the design process (Henderson & Cooprider, 1990).

## 4.4.2 Work satisfaction

The end-user designers expressed strong attitude patterns when discussing design tools, including the DCs. The idea that end-user designers are very involved with their tools on an emotional level may indicate that the tools contribute towards the end-user designers' attitudes towards their work.

## 4.4.3 Selecting tools and design methodologies

The results of this research show that design tools should be carefully selected for their relationships to designers as well as for their technical competencies. Aspects to consider include the ability to use the tool for communication and coordination, the breadth of coverage over the more detailed designs such as implementation design and coding designs and the perceptions and attitudes that the designers express about the tool. It would thus seem wise to test out a design tool on a comprehensive mini-project to determine whether or not there is sufficient value to subsequent steps to include the tool in a design methodology.

## 5 OPPORTUNITIES FOR FUTURE EXPLORATIONS

The extraordinary variety of uses of the DCs and attitudes towards the DCs would not have been predicted from the existing literature, yet the issues were quite saliently important to end-user designers. These research findings strongly suggest that in new areas in which there is little theoretical or empirical knowledge it is highly useful to pursue a qualitative approach towards enquiry. Much of the design literature and most journalists are very dogmatic about tools and their virtues, while ignoring their uses. This 'one-size-fits-all' approach towards tool construction and assessment ignores both the individual and the group variation in needs and emotional responses.

This study reveals attributes and characteristics that would almost certainly be missed with standard empirical methods. For example, the experienced designers and practitioners in Henderson & Cooprider's (1990) report on functionality characteristics of computerized production and coordination technologies did not consider the strong attitude component in learning from experience. The pervasive nature of these attitudes, attitude patterns and beliefs that accompany tool usage in design should be investigated in more structured studies of computerized and non-computerized software engineering tools.

The results of this study reveal that, in the highly creative process of design, end-user designers use their creativity to adapt design tools to their perceived needs as well as to adapt themselves to the requirements and constraints of their tools. This mutual adaptation indicates that usefulness in design tools might be highly individualized, and substantiates the need for flexibility in CASE tools found by Henderson & Cooprider (1990). The results also confirm that design tools should not be studied in isolation: design is a cooperative activity.

More research is needed into the organizational environment in which design tools are deployed and into the learning and attitude formation behaviour of the tool users. United States software productivity is widely seen as being in crisis. For over 30 years, the search for methods and tools has focused on improving software engineering through more comprehensive technical support of design activities, from IS planning to systems maintenance. Perhaps more focus on how people see design methods, relate to tools and respond to designing tasks can help support these emerging environments.

## APPENDIX 1: THE DIALOGUE CHARTS

The dialogue charts (DCs) are representative of a class of dialogue modelling languages that are intended to aid in the design of interactive computer systems. These tools design the interactive control structure of the interface (what happens next) by limiting the interaction structures to those found in structured programming: sequential actions, case selections, if-then-else choices and do-while or do-until activities. Similar vocabularies are general transition networks (Kieras & Polson, 1983) and augmented transition networks (Cheriton, 1976). For a more complete comparative analysis of the dialogue charts and other tools see Green (1986) and Calloway (1988).

In addition to structuring the dialogue, the DCs also allow the designer to begin the conceptual design by considering a task that is interactive as a composite set of activities to be decomposed into system and user pieces at the leisure of the designer. Composite tasks can remain intact through various level of hierarchical decomposition.

The dialogue charts were first conceptualized by Gad Ariav in 1982 in order to meet the requirements for designing highly interactive systems. It is useful to look at the DCs within the dialogue management framework that partitions systems-user dialogue into three linked generic functions: the handling of syntax, the handling of control and the handling of the applications. This conceptualization essentially underlies a wide array of contemporary views of dialogue management (e.g. Parnas, 1969; Britton et al., 1981; Norman, 1983; Ariav & Ginzberg, 1985; Hayes et al., 1985; Olsen, 1985; Gaines & Shaw, 1986; Green, 1986; and Hix & Hartson, 1986). The set of dialogue concerns breaks down as follows: the syntax defines the valid set of user inputs ('gestures') and captures presentation aspects, including delivering outputs to the user; handling applications entails the definition of the interface to the required application modules and passing information to and from these modules; finally, the control aspect of dialogue management is concerned with maintaining and enforcing the dialogue structure, practically defining the set of interaction contexts and the permissible sequences of user-system activities. A model of the control structure of the dialogue is an abstraction of the dialogue: it outlines possible sequences of system-user interactions without being bound to the specific implementation details such as interaction style or implemented appearance of the user interface.

Six distinct constructs make up the DC notation. They are associated with a set of graphic symbols (Fig. 2).

2 A non-decomposable user activity, i.e. a 'terminal' (an oval).

3 A decomposable system activity, i.e. a program (a double box).

The arrows represent the directions of the sequences. They limit the repertory of flows to those commonly associated with structured programming approaches. Junctions in the diagrams represent decision points, and are resolved by whoever holds the initiative at that point. The party (i.e. either user or system) whose range of actions is specified in the routes that branch out of the junction holds the dialogue initiative and selects the actual dialogue path to be followed. This approach requires the adherence to a consistency constraint, namely that all the paths that emanate from a junction will be either all user led or all system led. It also brings out the fundamental decision on the assignment of dialogue initiative, and explicitly calls for its resolution.

The major manipulation in dialogue charting is the transformation of a box in a dialogue chart into a more detailed chart. The rules are that any box can be further decomposed. It can be decomposed into more boxes or into boxes and ovals, or into ovals. However, once a box is either 'all user' (i.e. single-lined box) or 'all system' (i.e. double-lined box), it can only be decomposed into more boxes and ovals of the same kind. Ovals are atomic and cannot be further decomposed. A common issue in conceptual design is how deeply should the structure be decomposed. A related issue is when to declare an element as terminal rather than as further decomposable. There is no clear stopping rule for the elaboration process. Design common sense indicates that the process should stop either when further decomposition does not offer new relevant insights or when the element represents a library module or 'canned' procedure.

The DCs follow the ideas embedded in the syntax charts of Jensen & Wirth (1978) by supporting the concept of designing only the permissible dialogs. Error handling procedures are added to the DCs as annotations at the appropriate system level.

## Biographies

Linda Jo Calloway is an associate professor with the School of Computer Science and Information Systems, Pace University. Dr Calloway holds a masters and a doctorate in information systems from the New York University Stem School of Business, where she also served on the faculty.

Before returning to university life, she managed software development for all ITT World Communication's real-time telecommunications systems. During the Apollo project, she was a scientific systems analyst at Cape Canaveral. She has worked as a researcher and teacher in China, Ireland and The Netherlands. Her recent publications include Hightech Comes to War Coverage: Information and Communications Systems in the Gulf War, The 1000 Hour

War (Greenwood Publishing Group), and papers in Public Relations Quarterly, Information Systems Research and Journal of Information Technology.

Gad Ariav holds a PhD in decision sciences from the Wharton School of the University of the University of Pennsylvania. His research has focused on decision support and temporally oriented database management. Before joing Tel Aviv University he was on the faculty of NYU's Stern School of Business. Dr Ariav has been dealing professionally with computers and information systems since 1967, in Israel, Europe and the US. He has published articles in the Communications of the ACM, Data and Knowledge Engineering, Journal of MIS and ACM Transactions on Database Systems. He is currently completing a book on the design and implementation of DSS (with Michael J. Ginzberg), to be published by McGraw-Hill.
