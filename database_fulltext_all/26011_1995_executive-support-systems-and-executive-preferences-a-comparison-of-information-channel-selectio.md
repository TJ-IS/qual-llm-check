# Executive support systems and executive preferences: a comparison of information channel selection theories

Betty Vandenbosch & Chris Higgins\*

Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio 44106, USA \*Western Business School, University of Western Ontario, London, N5Y 3H6 Canada

e-mail: wand@unixg.ubc.ca

Abstract. A number of theories have been put forward to explain how managers make information channel choices. These include channel disposition, media richness and social influence. Each is assessed in terms of its usefulness for providing insight into why executives choose to use or not to use executive support systems (ESS). The results of a survey of 66 executives indicate that social influence theory may provide significant insight into information channel selection. A secondary analysis provides evidence that there are no significant relationships between demographic characteristics and several perceptual measures of ESS performance.

Keywords: information channels; executive support systems; information richness; social influence

Executives have a great deal of latitude in their selection of information sources (Rockart & DeLong, 1988). Certainly, far more information is provided to them than they would ever have the time or inclination to absorb. In addition, a great deal of information is potentially available to them from several sources. In some instances they can obtain the same information directly from their subordinates, from written reports and from computer printouts, to name just a few.

In efforts to improve executives' access to information, companies have spent and are continuing to spend large sums to provide top management with computerized support to monitor both the external environment and internal operations (Lynch, 1989). Recently much of the effort has been in the development of executive support systems (ESS). These systems have been designed to provide top management with information in a form that is easy to access, easy to use, of high quality and relevant to decision-making (Houdeshel & Watson, 1987; Matthews & Shoebridge, 1992; Rockart & DeLong, 1988). ESS are typically, but not necessarily, used directly by top management and focus on a manager's or group of managers' information needs across a range of areas. Rather than being limited to a single recurring type of decision, they are flexible tools that provide information support and analysis capability for a wide range of executive decisions (Scott Morton, 1984). Despite their obvious potential, executives have frequently been slow to embrace this technology. Paper reports prevail (Fitzgerald, 1991) and face-to-face encounters continue to be the mainstay of executive information gathering (Mintzberg, 1990).

Given the substantial resources, in terms of both expense and executive time, required to develop these systems (Paller, 1990; Watson, et al., 1991), more insight is required into the perceptions executives hold about their value as sources of information. This research was designed to determine how executives view ESS in comparison to more traditional information channels available to them.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

To date, limited empirical research has been directed towards understanding why executives choose to use or not to use ESS to augment or replace more traditional channels of information. It is clear, however, that executives have widely varying preferences and habits in their use of information channels (Martensson & Mahring, 1992). A number of researchers have put forward models to explain how managers make information channel choices, including channel disposition (Swanson 1982, 1987), media richness (Daft & Lengel, 1984, 1986) and social influence (Fulk et al., 1987). Each of these may be useful for providing insights into why executives make the choices they do. What follows is a brief discussion of each of these theories and their extension to ESS channel selection.

## Channel disposition

Channel disposition is 'a psychological cost-benefit utility assessment in which a user implicitly trades off the benefits of a channel's information against the associated cost of access' (Swanson, 1987; 133). In other words, it is the net utility that an information system has for a user. When managers have information needs, arising within organizational contexts, they are motivated to seek information from one or more of the information channels available to them. They will be more or less predisposed to use certain channels depending on their assessment of the relative quality and accessibility of those channels. Based on these predispositions, and the specific information need at hand, they will make their selections. The nature of their experience when using a specific channel will influence managers in their future channel choices.

Of course, quality and accessibility are relative terms and perceptions about them are more important in determining the use of a channel than are objective facts. For example, someone who has never used a computer or a mouse may find the cost of accessing an ESS much higher than someone with ten years of spreadsheet experience. Similarly, perceptions of quality, or 'how good the information is' are dependent upon a comparison with alternatives available and the use to which the information is to be put. A controller usually has different metrics from a strategic planner by which to measure quality. Hence the definitions of accessibility and quality are dependent, to a large extent, on who is being asked. Individual perceptions notwithstanding, a significant amount of research has been undertaken to determine whether quality or accessibility is more important in the selection of an information channel.

In a survey of 163 subjects, O'Reilly (1982) found that, although the importance of information sources was related to the perceived quality of the information available, frequency of use was largely a function of the perceived accessibility of the sources. His rationalization of why accessibility dominated quality is the ambiguous nature of much information coupled with the need to produce results. In a study of the information use of a sample of 362 professionals from various levels in two organizations, Culnan (1983) found that the use of information channels was related to both perceived accessibility and perceived environmental uncertainty. She did not test the perceived quality of the information provided. Swanson (1987) divided quality into two components. Attributed technical characteristics comprised reliability, precision, accuracy, timeliness, comprehensiveness and conciseness. Attributed information value related the quality of the information to its specific user. This construct consisted of value, usefulness, relevance, importance and meaningfulness. He found that attributed information value plays a more significant role than accessibility in the selection of an information channel.

There is some support for the hypothesis that accessibility is more important than quality in the use of an information channel, and also some evidence that the information value component of quality is more important than accessibility. These equivocal results may indicate that the choice of an information channel is affected by more than just accessibility and quality. It is also possible that the relationships between accessibility and quality are more complex than researchers have investigated thus far. For example, managers may not differentiate among levels of accessibility or quality above or below certain thresholds, or they may place such a high premium on one that they are fairly insensitive to changes in the other. Finally, as Swanson suggested, they may make trade-offs between the two. A particularly accessible information source may be used more than its quality warrants, and high quality may increase tolerance of poor accessibility.

In the case of senior managers, accessibility may be moot. Executives typically have rapid access to whatever information channels they require. Subordinates are regularly called away from their activities to inform senior managers. It is difficult to imagine any ESS users who are faced with limited accessibility because they have to share terminals or use systems that are not virtually always on-line. Hence channel disposition theory would predict that relative quality will be the principal criterion that determines how much use managers make of the information channels available to them.

## Media richness

Media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1984, 1986) states that media richness (characterized by speed of feedback, number of cues, language variety and personal focus) and the equivocality of the issue to be resolved interact with each other in the selection of information/communication channels. Less rich media are suitable for processing standard data and well-understood messages, while richer media are required to deal with equivocal or ambiguous messages and issues. Media form a richness continuum from face-to-face contacts, through telephone, addressed written communications and formal unaddressed documents to computer output. Managers select media based on the equivocality of the messages they are receiving/transmitting (Daft et al., 1987). Rice and Shook's (1990) meta-analysis of 40 studies found that media usage was highly correlated with organizational level, lending support to the theory of media richness. Higher-level managers, whose tasks tend to be more equivocal in nature, use face-to-face communication more frequently, while lower-level managers are more prone to use the telephone and written communications.

Zmud et al. (1990) investigated media choice using both traditional and new communication channels. Their candidate criteria included items from both the channel disposition and media richness theories. In their study of 158 managers are a single large manufacturing firm, they found that feedback, accessibility and quality all played a role in channel selection. ESS were not among the channels Zmud et al. investigated.

Based on media richness theory, one could posit that ESS and executive support requirements are an equivocality/richness mismatch. The systems may be helpful to executives in dealing with uncertainty, but the media may well lack the richness to be particularly useful for dealing with equivocality. Certainly researchers have found that executives show a marked reluctance to use, and a disdain for, computerized information sources (Mintzberg, 1990; El Sawy, 1985). At the same time, it would seem that executive support systems are a potentially richer medium than paper reports because of their capacity to personalize formats and the information accessed. Consequently, it seems that media richness theory would predict that, in general, ESS are superior to paper reports and inferior to face-to-face contacts for equivocal information.

## Social influence

The social influence model of technology use (Fulk et al., 1987) was developed in response to anomalies associated with the media richness model and newer technologies. Specifically, media richness theory has not proved particularly successful in the prediction of the use of newer media such as electronic mail. A number of studies show that electronic mail, which has relatively low media richness, is often used for tasks that one would typify as having a high personal requirement (Fulk & Boyd, 1991).

The social influence model proposes that perceptions of media (such as richness) are socially constructed, at least in part. Four forms of social influence are in evidence: co-worker opinion, vicarious learning, norms and social definitions of rationality. The theory predicts that there will be similarity in use of particular media within organizations, but different patterns across them. In the case of ESS, determining whether or not social influence is at work is confounded by the varying quality of ESS implementation across organizations. In addition, social influence at the executive level may be a less relevant phenomenon, as executives typically have few 'valued others' inside the organization. Finally, even if social influence is at work, the 'valued others' are likely to develop media usage patterns based on their perceptions of relative advantage (Zmud et al., 1990).

Norms and social definitions of rationality may play a greater role than 'valued others' in the selection of ESS as an information channel. Given the long tradition that paper has as an information channel, and the difficulty humans have in changing habits (Lewin, 1947; Coch & French, 1948), paper reports may be considered a more useful information channel even though they have several shortcomings in relation to ESS, for example, ESS are typically more timely, and can be tuned to individual information requirements and presentation preferences.

The norms arising from a particular corporate culture can also play a role in the selection of ESS as an information channel (Green & Murphy, 1994). If an organization has or develops a culture that encourages ESS use, perhaps through the expectations of the most senior executive, it is likely that the channel will be selected more frequently than alternatives. For example, Bergeron et al. (1991) found that organizational norms (social factors in their terminology) were significantly correlated with internalization of the ESS (defined as dependence upon the system, ownership felt by the user and routinization of usage), lending support to the importance of social influence.

In summary, channel disposition theory seems to predict that the relative quality of the ESS in comparison to other information channels available determines its use, while media richness theory seems to predict that ESS would generally be preferred to paper reports but found inferior to face-to-face contacts. The social influence model seems equivocal. If executives have few 'valued others' to rely on, channel disposition and media richness theories may prevail. However, given, the strong tradition of paper reports, social influence predicts that they may be considered a more valued channel than ESS. Since there is no strong or consistent theoretical support for a clear ranking of information channels for executives, the research was designed to determine whether or not and how executives distinguish among face-to-face contacts, paper reports and ESS as information channels. A secondary analysis was conducted to determine whether or not demographic factors have any relationship to perceptions about ESS.

## Methodology

A mail questionnaire was designed to ascertain the differences that executives perceive between ESS and the more traditional information channels of face-to-face contacts and paper reports. In addition, several questions were used to test whether or not perceptions vary along demographic lines. The survey approach was selected because, of the methods available (i.e. surveys, interviews and observation), it is the most efficient way to obtain information about perceptions from a large group of executives.

## Study sample

Contacts were made with ESS software vendors and consultants to obtain the names of companies that had ESS that had been installed for at least three months. In addition, the popular press was scanned for companies with ESS they were willing to talk about.

Nine companies from various industries including natural resources, chemicals, banking, utilities and industrial products, agreed to participate in the study. 1990 mean sales for the non-banking companies was \$2.3 billion and the mean number of employees was 5,035. Two of the companies in the sample were banking institutions. The mean of their 1990 total income was \$5.7 billion and the mean number of employees was 21,408.

In each company a contact person was interviewed to ensure that the ESS had been in full operation for at least three months and was available for use by the most senior managers of the organization. The contacts were explicitly asked to send questionnaires to all people who had access to the system, whether or not they actually used it. All respondents were guaranteed confidentiality. Non-respondents were contacted once by the company liaison officer.

One hundred and sixty-four surveys were distributed and 66 were returned, for a response rate of 40%. The mean age of the respondents was 43, and the mean number of layers to the president was 1.96. The presidents of six of the nine companies completed the survey. Of the respondents, 30% reported to the president and 24% to a direct subordinate of the president's. The mean tenure in the company was 16.9 years and the mean tenure in the job was 3.1 years. The mean time the ESS had been available for use was 16.8 months. The respondents had a wide range of system usage, varying from no use at all to four hours per day. The mean daily use was 0.67 hours with a standard deviation of 0.68. Only one respondent was a woman, so gender was dropped from the analyses.

## Construct operationalization

Channel characteristics. As mentioned above, O'Reilly (1982), Culnan (1983), Swanson (1987) and Zmud et al. (1990) have empirically investigated criteria used to differentiate information channels. No clear definitions of the candidate attributes of quality and accessibility emerge from a comparison of the scales. For example, in his accessibility scale, O'Reilly asks about the difficulty of obtaining meaningful information and how often required information is available from a particular source. In Swanson's operationalization, meaning is a component of information value and required information available was not part of any of his scales. Similarly Zmud et al. (1990) included dependability as a component of accessibility and reliability as a component of quality, while Swanson considered both part of technical quality and O'Reilly included only reliability.

As it was outside the scope of this research to develop general definitions of the criteria, O'Reilly's ten items were modified slightly and included in the survey. Four additional items were added to incorporate major components of the Swanson and Zmud operationalizations that were absent from the O'Reilly scales. Zmud et al. found feedback to be an important criteria in the differentiation of information channels. The operationalization of feedback was a single adjective pair (delayed feedback/immediate feedback). This did not seem an appropriate question to differentiate the channels being assessed in this research, as only face-to-face contacts provide feedback in the sense intended by Zmud et al.

The respondents ranked each of the three information channels (face-to-face contacts, paper reports and the company's ESS) on five-point Likert scales for each of the fourteen attributes. In addition, two summary questions asked the respondents to rank the channels from most to least useful and from most to least used. ## PERCEPTIONS OF ESS SUCCESS

A secondary analysis was undertaken to understand whether or not demographic factors have any relationship to perceptions of the success of an ESS and consequently, the role ESS plays in executives' information channel choices.

A fundamental issue facing all MIS researchers is the selection of a dependent construct to measure system success (Keen, 1981; Srinivasan, 1985; DeLone and McLean, 1992). The ultimate aim of most systems is to improve organizational effectiveness, performance and profitability. However, because of the many confounding factors, alternate explanations and time lags associated with the measurement of effectiveness, researchers are forced to develop surrogate measures. User information satisfaction (UIS) and system use are two of the most common (Keen, 1981), but these measures are not ideal and their appropriateness is often dependent on the situation under investigation (Ives et al., 1983). Certainly, in the case of ESS, traditional measures may not be appropriate.

For example, Kim (1990) identified five dimensions of UIS: input procedures, processing capabilities, output quality, overall effectiveness and organizational factors such as top management support. These dimensions may or may not be relevant to a particular situation. With ESS it would seem that input, processing and output are far less relevant in the evaluation of satisfaction than the results obtained by using the system. Similarly, assessing top management support when the system is being evaluated by top management may not be helpful.

Use is also a construct whose meaning is dependent on context. High use may indicate a successful system, a system that is very difficult to use, or a strong mandate from management. In the case of executive support systems, use may be as much a function of time available as of the success of the system, or it may be nothing more than forced compliance because needed information is no longer provided through other media. In other words, executives may use ESS a lot even though they are dissatisfied with them and may not use them much even though they are satisfied.

Since the conceptual meanings (Bagozzi, 1984) of use and satisfaction are tightly linked to the context in which they are being evaluated, it is not surprising that there is support in the literature for both high and low correlation between various measures of user satisfaction and use (Sanders, 1984; Davis, 1989). There is no clear precedent for the superiority of use or satisfaction as a context-independent surrogate for system effectiveness. Hence, the selection of a dependent construct requires an understanding of what constitutes effectiveness within the context being studied. In the case of executive support systems, no such understanding has been developed so far.

Perceived usefulness (Davis, 1989) may also be suitable as a dependent construct to measure ESS effectiveness. Davis defines perceived usefulness as 'the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance' (p.320). Davis developed a multidimensional scale of the construct, fested it in two studies, and found that perceived usefulness and use were highly correlated. His work found that perceived ease of use may be a causal antecedent to perceived usefulness and presumed that perceived usefulness is an antecedent to use. However, Bandura's (1986) social cognitive theory provides support for an alternate explanation of the correlation between perceived usefulness and use. Bandura's theory proposes that the environment, behaviour and cognitive and personal factors all interact, based on triadic reciprocity. This reciprocity may be at work between system use and perceived usefulness. While it seems obvious that if someone finds a system useful he or she will use it, using a system more may also cause someone to find it more useful. The relationship between use and usefulness may be that of correlated dependent constructs or constructs mutually causing each other, rather than that of a unidirectional antecedent and consequent.

All three 'success' constructs were employed to tape respondents' perceptions about the success of the ESS: usefulness, overall user information satisfaction and use. In addition, respondents were asked about their perceptions regarding ease of use.

Perceived usefulness. Davis (1989) followed a careful process to develop a reliable and valid scale to measure perceived usefulness. In two separate studies the scale attained exceptionally high reliability scores (Cronbach's alphas were 0.97 and 0.98) and high scores on measures of convergent and discriminant validity. Davis's final questions were incorporated into the questionnaire to measure perceived usefulness.

Perceived ease of use. Davis's (1989) six-item scale for perceived ease of use was incorporated into the questionnaire as it exhibited high reliability (Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.86 to 0.94), convergent validity and discriminant validity in two separate studies.

Overall user information satisfaction. Sanders's (1984) six-item scale for a user's perceived overall satisfaction with a system was employed. This scale focuses specifically on the system's ability to improve job performance. It is appropriate in this context because of its brevity and clarity of purpose. Other UIS scales (e.g. Ives et al., 1983) assess a myriad of dimensions. While

Sanders did not report reliability or validity for the scale, Higgins et al. (1990) found that the scale had high reliability in their application (internal consistency was 0.9 and Cronbach's alpha was 0.86).

Use. The survey contained three questions about executive support system use. The first asked the respondent to indicate the average amount of time spent using the ESS daily and the second asked about the specific amount of time spent on the last working day. These questions were worded to elicit a specific response and thereby avoid a potential floor or ceiling effect from a calibrated scale. Because varying demands on an executive's time and organizational norms may have as much impact on how much the system gets used as the attributes of the system itself, a third question asked the respondent to rate his or her use on a five-point Likert-type scale anchored by 'Not at all' and 'Very heavy' (adapted from Igbaria et al., 1989).

Finally, the survey contained several demographic questions. Experience, other computer use, tenure in the organization, tenure in current position, organizational level and gender were operationalized via single questions. Training was operationalized following Igbaria (1990). The questions asked respondents to indicate the amount of computer training they had received from five sources: college or university, hardware or software vendors, consultants, company training programs and tutorials.

## RESULTS

The data were analysed in two steps. In the first, correlations between demographic factors and ease of use, usefulness, overall user information satisfaction, use, and the rankings of ESS in terms of use and usefulness were calculated. In the second step, the data were tested to determine if the respondents differentiated among the channels for the 14 attributes.

Cronbach's alpha was calculated for the multi-item constructs. All exceeded Nunnally's (1978) 0.8 guideline for basic research: ease of use had an alpha of 0.92; usefulness, 0.97; overall UIS, 0.91; and use, 0.83. Given their high reliability, the means of the individual items comprising these constructs were used in the subsequent analysis. Because the data did not exhibit bivariate normal distributions, the Spearman rank order correlation coefficient was employed to determine the relationship between demographic factors and the various measures of success. The correlation matrix (Table 2) indicates that there are no significant relationships between demographic characteristics and the various perceptual measures of ESS performance. These findings have some important implications.

That age and tenure were not correlated with use, usefulness and satisfaction implies that resistance to computers by those who were not brought up with them has been broken down with easy-to-use ESS technology. Similarly, that computer training and experience with computers were not correlated with ease of use implies that ESS have surmounted the technology hurdle. Not having an extensive computer background does not appear to limit the use that people make of ESS. Finally, the number of layers to the president has no relationship to perceptions about the system. While this finding does not have implications for ESS positioning in organizations since only four layers appear in the sample, it does provide justification for treating the entire sample as a single set with common opinions about ESS.

Paper reports were considered the most accurate, followed by ESS and then face-to-face contacts. Not surprisingly, ESS were considered to be the most easily available channel of communication. They were also considered to be the most efficient. Face-to-face contacts were considered to be the next most available and efficient. Face-to-face contacts scored the highest on all remaining attributes with significant differences (helpfulness, meaning, meeting requirements, specific to needs and timeliness). Except for accuracy, where it had the highest rank, and helpfulness, where paper and ESS were close, paper scored the lowest on all attributes. No significant differences were found among the channels for the attributes of accuracy checking, information availability, interpretability, relevance, reliability and suitability.

The results of the summary questions on use and usefulness were inconsistent with the individual attribute rankings. Paper ranked ahead of ESS on both.

## DISCUSSION

As the media richness theory predicts, the results of this research clearly support executives' preference for face-to-face contacts over other media. Both individual attribute rankings and overall usefulness and use rankings placed face-to-face contacts ahead of the other channels. The results for the selection of ESS and paper are equivocal. ESS are preferred over paper for the majority of the attributes, but paper reports are found more useful and are used more overall. Nevertheless, the results may shed some light on the usefulness of the channel selection theories described above for predicting relative preferences for ESS and paper reports.

Channel disposition theory does not seem to provide insight into ESS use. Even though ESS ranked higher than or equal to paper for all quality and accessibility attributes with the exception of accuracy, paper ranked higher on the overall measures. It is not likely that accuracy is the only relevant dimension in the selection of an information channel, since face-to-face contacts were deemed the least accurate, yet the most useful and the most used. It would seem that, at the executive level, accuracy is not as important as other dimensions (cf. Weick 1985). Similarly, media richness theory predicted a preference for ESS over paper, which was not borne out by the survey. Even though ESS are more personal than paper, they are not considered to be as useful and are not used as much as paper reports by the survey respondents.

Social influence theory may provide significant insight into executive channel selection. It may be that paper reports, because of their tradition, are preferred to ESS, in spite of their obvious weaknesses. However, there may be alternative explanations. Paper reports have shortcomings in comparison to ESS, but they also have advantages. Paper is more mobile and it is easier to scan and annotate. One of the executives who completed the survey implied that these characteristics are more relevant to executives than those in which ESS excel: 'As a VP, I do not need up to the minute data ... and I prefer paper to analyze and annotate following up with face-to-face contacts. I find most of my time is spent figuring out the future, not looking at the past.'

Another manager wrote: 'An ESS needs to make information available before printed reports are distributed to various levels of management. Otherwise, paper is just a lot easier to deal with.'

A third said, 'I don't want a paper reporting system converted to look-up screens.'

It is also possible that ESS implementation in the companies studied was not sufficiently complete to replace paper entirely. For example, one respondent wrote, 'The lack of completeness and suspect credibility of the data significantly detracts from the acceptance and use of the system.' Several others echoed these concerns. One manager went so far as to write, 'A company ESS needs to cover all aspects of information required by management; if it doesn't, managers will keep on using alternate sources of information.' However, given the number of companies that responded and the length of time their systems have been in place, if this explanation is correct, then ESS may never be sufficiently robust to replace paper and one must question more explicitly their usefulness in information communication in organizations.

## CONCLUSION

The results of this research indicate that arguments about executives not being interested in using computers because of their demographic profiles do not hold water. Executives will use ESS regardless of their demographic characteristics, as long as the system provides them with information they find useful. The research also provides an indication of what 'useful' means.

Face-to-face interactions, which were considered the most useful channel by the executives in this survey, provide the most meaningful information, are the most helpful and the most specific, and also provide the most timely information. ESS, which are considered the least useful channel, are believed to be the most available and the most efficient. Paper is considered the most accurate. Hence it would seem that paying heed to the traditional metrics of quality and accessibility will not necessarily result in a successful ESS. Developers must build systems that provide information that is helpful and meaningful to those who have access. Unfortunately, this challenge may be rather daunting as 'helpfulness' and 'meaningfulness' are concepts that are dependent on both context and the perceptions of those making the assessment. In addition, what is helpful and meaningful now may not be so in the future.

Channel disposition and media richness theories did not appear to explain executives' channel preferences vis-à-vis ESS. The social influence model of technology use seemed to be a more appropriate theory; however, alternative explanations exist. It may be that the dimensions that were used to compare these channels are not the ones that are relevant to executives' decision-making process. Since executives never have been and never will be limited to a single information channel in any case, it may be fruitful to investigate the conditions under which they prefer to use paper, ESS and face-to-face interactions.

In any event, the research contains an implicit warning for practitioners. Given executives' preference for paper reports, in spite of the clear superiority of ESS along several quality and accessibility dimensions, companies should be cautious in their assumption that the technology will be embraced by the executives it is designed for. And they should also be cautious in their assumption that accuracy, timeliness and reliability of information are the primary measures of a good system. Finally, they should be wary of efficiencies resulting from paper reduction as a justification for ESS development. The paper does not necessary go away.
