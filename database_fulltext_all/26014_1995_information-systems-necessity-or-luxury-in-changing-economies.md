# Information systems: necessity or luxury in changing economies?

Jakov Crnkovic & William K. Holstein\*

Computer Information Systems Department, College of Saint Rose, Albany, New York 12203, USA \*School of Business, State University of New York at Albany, Albany, New York 12222, USA

Abstract. This paper explores the role of information technology and systems in countries that are undergoing rapid social and economic change. The countries of East and Central Europe and the former Soviet Union are good examples. We focus on the most important needs for management information in such countries, which are often quite different from those in Western countries, and on the difficulties of implementing information technology. We suggest that improved information systems will be critical for future success in changing economies and that the processes for developing systems and applying information technology should follow quite different paths from those followed in the US and Western Europe. We also present several examples of problems and possible solutions.

Keywords: changing economies, information system development and implementation, role of information technology, soft systems methodology.

## INTRODUCTION

Our experience in Eastern and Central Europe in the past several years has led us to three interesting and provocative questions:

1 What is the role of information technology (IT) and information systems in economies that are undergoing rapid and difficult economic and social change? Can such countries develop domestic markets and compete effectively in world markets without making substantial investments in IT and systems?

2 Must the implementation of IT and systems in changing economies go through the same lengthy development and growth phases that have characterized US and West European experience or can the process be cut short and made much quicker and more effective?

3 How can the development and implementation of information systems in changing economies be improved to increase the chances of achieving 'competitive advantage' from (obviously quite expensive) systems investments?

In this paper we provide a framework for thinking about these questions and share our views, impressions and experiences in a manner that we hope may provide some useful perspective. We use several examples drawn from actual practice to illustrate the most important points. Although our experience is drawn primarily from Yugoslavia and Hungary, we believe that our points are applicable to most of the countries of Eastern and Central Europe and those in the former Soviet Union. Most of these countries, developed or developing, are experiencing sharp changes in their social and economic systems and reside in an earlier era of information systems than the US, the countries of Western Europe, or Japan. The differences among them have less to do with their information needs than with the political, ethnic and social upsets that will retard progress, not just in information systems development, but in many other areas of the economy as well.

Our perspective is managerial rather than technical. We believe that the most important problems for business and industry in these countries involve management, not politics. Our focus is therefore on the development of information systems that can make a difference in management effectiveness and competitiveness — one company, one industry at a time. This is not necessarily a universal view, as noted in the following quote:

The first barrier of information strategies in Czechoslovakia are economic and legislative problems of the Czechoslovak economy connected with the transformation process to a market economy. These problems overshadow the importance of IS/IT in the solution of present company problems. In other words, the current period does not seem to be mature enough to allow for solutions on an IS/IT level. (Vorisek, 1993)

Let us state the perspective in a different way. There are difficult economic and political problems and it is easy to find many reasons why things cannot be done. Yet, from a competitive perspective, even a survival perspective, companies must focus on what must be done and ways to start doing it, and that is our focus as well.

## PRESSURES AND TRENDS

We turn now to a brief summary of the pressures that are creating the need for improved information and management systems: increasingly difficult, price-based competition, spreading intolerance of anticompetitive practices, mergers and concentration, and access to capital. This is clearly a 'business school' rather than an IS perspective, but the biggest problems are economic and managerial, not technical.

Future success in most of the markets that count to most countries will come in large measure to global competitors. No nation will be able to resign from the global marketplace. Companies in many changing economies face tremendous competition, both in their own domestic market and in international markets, from well-established global competitors and world brands. In many instances they must compete without the information systems and information technology that more fully developed international competitors possess.

Established cost and value relationships among countries, and among companies, their customers, and their competitors, are changing very rapidly. Generally this means that price competition will become more severe in most markets, and price compression between markets will be a significant trend.

Good information systems can support the development and execution of competitive pricing policies and can assist with timely decision-making in this area. As price competition becomes more prevalent, as we think it will throughout the global economy, information systems will be an important key for success.

The number of truly global brands, such as Sony, Mercedes Benz, IBM and Benneton, and world products such as Coca-Cola, McDonalds hamburgers and the MS-DOS microcomputer operating system will increase. Competitors in changing economies will have to spend more on brand development and marketing and the information systems to support these functions. Typical information systems, even in the US, are weak in providing 'soft' and 'external' information on markets, competitors and customers. The need for this kind of information is particularly acute in changing economies, since they have very little of it now and future competitive success will depend on it.

Although in many changing economies access to capital will continue to be a major problem, 'technology capital is becoming more important than monetary capital' (Wriston, 1989). Access to capital will not be sufficient; all firms, not just those in the highly developed economies, will have to create, embody and apply advanced technology in their products, processes and systems in order to stay afloat in difficult markets. Information technology can provide significant impact in product improvement and in developing customer-oriented systems. Examples include simplifying payments, customizing products and services to fit individual customer needs, incorporating microchips and sophisticated logic in products, and providing improved warranties, repairs and maintenance services.

Despite the importance of burgeoning technology, and perhaps because of its increasingly rapid dissemination, marketing and service will continue to be very important issues, perhaps more important than technology per se.

Response time, and the ability to manage it, is clearly becoming an important key to improving competitive performance, and information technology is critical here. Yet, in most changing economies, this concept is not well understood.

Globalized competition and the other trends noted here do not imply the need for centralization or hierarchical control. A trend that is just as important as those mentioned above is the elimination of management layers and the flattening of organizational structures — 'downsizing', streamlining and learning to operate leaner and quicker. Bureaucracy is under formidable threat. Communication with and control over far-flung operations with considerable local autonomy is a major management issue.

Indeed, developments in computing and information systems are making downsizing possible. Distributive architecture, end-user computing, telecommunications and networking suggest that more flexible, more responsive, more autonomous organizational structures are possible.

How information technology is applied to meet the competitive pressures cited here is critically important, particularly for companies operating in changing economies. If they are to shorten the development process and squeeze competitive advantage from investments in information technology and systems, they must do it wisely and carefully. Changing processes and the way the company does business is the objective, in other words 're-engineering' the business.

## RE-ENGINEERING

We now have the wherewithal to approach an answer to the first question that we posed at the beginning of this paper: what is the role of information technology (IT) and information systems in economies that are undergoing rapid and difficult economic and social change? In our context, we can say that IT and related systems must assist the development of competitive advantage by lowering costs, by making the company more market-driven, by improving quality and customer service, by shortening response times and (product, systems and other) development cycles, etc. The popular term for all of this is 're-engineering'.

Can all of this be done without information systems and substantial IT investments in the countries that are the focus of this article? We doubt it. Faster, more globalized competition, more price and cost pressure, and all of the other factors that we have mentioned will make it almost imperative for the players in most markets to have good information systems.

Davenport & Short (1990), who use the term 'business process redesign' instead of re-engineering, argue that information technology must be used in the effort and that business process design (the analysis and design of work flows within the between organizations) and information technology (the capabilities offered by computers, software applications and telecommunications) are natural partners. Perhaps, but US evidence of productivity improvement from investments in IT (with the clear exception of manufacturing applications) isn't all that clear (Anonymous, 1987). In changing economies, improvements must come more quickly and be more conclusively documented than has been the case in the US and Western Europe. Thus, the process of developing and implementing information systems must be different.

Michael Hammer (1990) states the US problem with IT investments well: 'Heavy investments in information technology have delivered disappointing results — largely because companies tend to use technology to mechanize old ways of doing business. They leave the existing processes intact and use computers simply to speed them up.' Only by generating and using information to improve flows, to reduce times and overheads, and to improve performance — to change the process — will companies be led to changes that will create improved competitiveness.

The implication here is quite clear: companies operating in changing economies must use information technology and systems as tools in accomplishing fundamental change, real restructuring of the enterprise. Yet their systems typically are not adequate for operating in the old ways under the old system, let alone in the new, more competitive environment.

Perhaps in no other industry is restructuring more important than in banking, since the banking industry can be a real engine for growth and can assist restructuring efforts in all other industries. The following example highlights a system failure in a situation where success was sorely needed.

## Missed opportunities for restructuring: the Investbanka Belgrade example

Investbanka is one of the largest and oldest banks in Belgrade. During most of the company's development its focus was on large corporate clients, disregarding smaller private transactions.

In 1988 a consulting firm was engaged to prepare the general design of an integrated information system for the bank. Individual applications were found to be well developed but generally unconnected. IT utilization was file-oriented and definitely at the data-processing level. An important characteristic was that virtually all applications involved routine internal processes and support activities such as accounting and payroll, with practically no applications for core banking activities or to support decision-making.

As the systems design project progressed, it became clear that there were great differences of opinion between the consultancy team and bank management. At issue was the acceptance of a new role for information systems, the creation of a new organization to support a different decision-making pyramid with decidedly more autonomy at lower levels and a much more active role for (end)-users of the new system. Every organizational unit in the bank was uncertain about its position and role in the new integrated information system.

In 1989, well before the project was finished, the government merged Investbanka with Beobanka, another Belgrade bank with strengths in serving light industry and trading enterprises, personal accounts and international banking. Beobanka had been developing its own information systems and had reasonably efficient transaction processing and foreign-currency management systems.

As might be expected, the two management and IS groups did not integrate smoothly. The combined bank's information system was not powerful enough to accept all the new organizational units and even old operations that had functioned in the separate operations in the past showed signs of stress.

Less than a year after the merger, the two banks were demerged and returned to separate operation. Information systems at both banks have regressed rather than progressed, and an integrated system with substantial MIS or DSS content is still far away for either of the reconstituted entities. Movement towards more activity in money and stock markets, trading in international markets, and providing improved support for private and commercial customers will be delayed. (For more details of examples in this paper see Petkovic & Crnkovic, 1991.)

The major lesson from this case is that a significant opportunity to restructure and use information technology to become more powerful and more integrated was lost. The move from data processing to information systems will most certainly take longer. Here, the solid data-processing foundation that we referred to earlier existed. Movement towards information systems was essential for management decision-making, but it implies the use of much more expensive resources and the use of more, and more expensive software. Moving up is expensive, but it also can provide sizeable returns on earlier-type systems investments, particularly when those early investments were well planned and well executed.

Unfortunately, in this example we see a classic case of management inability to recognize the competitive and productivity improvement potential of restructuring using information technology. A bank without substantial IT to support its core (banking) activities might seem an anomaly, but in changing economies it is not at all unusual. The problem is not IT or systems to use the available technology, and not even people to develop and install systems. Management, discipline, leadership and an unwillingness to see opportunities and push for justification are much larger problems.

Significant changes in management are coming quickly in Eastern and Central Europe, the former Soviet Union and other countries that are changing their economies. Western management ideas and techniques are being adopted and adapted with almost alarming alacrity, faster perhaps than changes in government policy and infrastructure to support new ways of managing and competing. In Hungary, the Czech Republic and Poland, considerable foreign investment is flowing in and significant numbers of state enterprise have been privatized — as many as 70% by the end of 1993. In other countries, the Ukraine is a good example, there is considerable private enterprise activity, but virtually no legal structure to support it. Without a clear framework of laws and regulations to control business activities, foreign investors are understandably reluctant to buy state assets to create joint ventures with existing companies.

In many of the countries that we are considering, contemporary information technology is widely available and the ability to use it is almost up to Western standards. But availability is not the key to success; access and implementation are, and in those areas most countries lag behind Western practice by a significant margin. For example, 'Computerization in Hungary is rather backward in comparison with the industrialized countries — there is a time-lag of a decade in hardware' (Chikan, 1990). Yet a 1989 survey of 78 Hungarian companies indicates that 18 (23%) use computers for forecasting and material accounting (Chikan, 1990), so there is a sizeable technology and experience base to support further development.

We turn next to the appraisal, evaluation and justification of information systems — an important reason why IT implementation and application lags far behind US and West European standards.

## APPRAISAL PROBLEMS WITH THE INTRODUCTION OF NEW INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY

Traditional methods for evaluating investment decisions have involved payback (the number of years to recoup the original investment), return on investment (an attempt to quantify the stream of expected savings and compare it to the investment required to generate the stream) and various applications of discounted cash-flow analysis. Even the newer methods which measure all the cash flows, positive and negative, involved with an investment still focus on the obviously quantifiable financial flows.

The problem with such methods is that they fail to seek out and quantify the real benefits of IT and information systems. They can measure cost reductions, traditional savings areas and profit increases that can be directly attributed to the investment. They cannot handle gains in business that come from less tangible areas such as quality, speed (response time), image or customer satisfaction.

In the light of our earlier summary of 'pressures', we believe that it is precisely in the intangible areas where improvements must be sought. Indeed, if information technology is to have a strategic impact on a firm's ability to compete, investment in IT must assist the firm to accomplish strategic goals; to utilize time more effectively, to deal with cost and price pressures, to consolidate and downsize, to develop broader and larger markets and more significant brands and, most importantly, to improve quality, marketing and service — in short, to re-engineer the business. Note that these are exactly the things that Investbanka and Beobanka needed but failed to do.

The next example deals with appraising and justifying investments to move to a higher level of information systems.

## Necessity for integration, not luxury: the port of Bar example

Bar is a small Mediterranean city in Montenegro. The port of Bar had, owing to its geographical position, excellent prospects after the construction of a railroad connecting Belgrade to Bar in the 1960s. The port's location, together with the rail service, opened substantial opportunities for domestic and international trade.

In the early years things went well. Newspapers headlined 'Initial Huge Success' and 'Making Big Competition For Other Mediterranean Ports'. By the early 1980s, although the port was active, serious organizational and management problems became evident. Management decided that improved information systems were a prerequisite for running the port and for the very survival of the enterprise in an increasingly competitive market. The fundamental idea was that information systems should provide not only internal integration, but connect the port with the business environment — with banks, with the railroad's information systems, and with the systems of large customers and international associations.

The project to design the new information system was given to a consulting company with clear goals: improved efficiency for the port's management; smoother, more efficient port operations; lower administrative costs; improvement in the port's services; and enhanced reliability of operating and information systems. The development of the new system was divided into two phases: office operations and the management of transshipment or 'field operations'. The port secured bank loans at good rates to finance the development effort.

Early efforts went smoothly, in part because the new system was being built to provide services that were not previously automated. The 'clean slate' approach resulted in effective subsystems for accounting, personnel records management, purchasing, sales tracking, inventory management and treasury functions.

The first problems appeared when the port tried to join MEDITEL, an association of Mediterranean ports that organizes and manages transshipments within the region. MEDITEL's goal is to use IT for managing member ports' 'production processes' on a just-in-time basis, from the receipt of goods through the rapid unloading/loading of ships, throughout the entire Mediterranean port system. To join the system, a port must have an information system that can be connected to MEDITEL. It was obvious that the port of Bar's systems would have to be developed further to qualify for admission to MEDITEL. This required improved organization and procedures and a higher level of information systems. A key requirement was an integrated database to connect the various activities of the port and to reconcile charges and expenses with the railroad, shippers and other ports.

Moving up to higher-level systems is proving difficult. Development costs are much more expensive. Software for MIS and decision support system (DSS) modules that are appropriate for the port cannot be purchased off the shelf. Most have to be custom-built by software consultants, adding to the expense and escalating the problems of coordination and control. All of this presents a formidable challenge to justify in a public authority. To a board of directors in a changing economy, higher-level systems, no matter how important for competitive success, may seem a bit too luxurious, elegant and expensive.

There are several interesting conclusions to be drawn from this example. The most visible one is connected with the investment decision-making process.

Broad, but clearly understood, measurement criteria applied early in the systems development process, coupled with more attention to the impact of information and information systems on decision-making, can bring a firm to see the information system as a key ingredient in long-term success. Information and communication within the whole company and with the external environment, in this view, become strategic targets of opportunity. The port of Bar management understands this now — the question is whether it is too late to complete a comprehensive project that will involve considerable investment and risk.

This example highlights the critical relationship between management and information systems. Senior managers decide on the criteria that will be used to justify new investments — what intangibles will 'count', what is important and strategic, what is not. Earlier we cited the importance of using broad criteria, including the consideration of intangibles, to justify investments in information technology to create competitive advantage. The literature suggests that many US managers do not have a broad enough view of IT investment criteria or see re-engineering in a large enough frame to move away from local details (see Devenport & Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990). It is perhaps a lot to ask that managers in changing economies take a more comprehensive view than US managers, but we believe they must.

Finally, the business environment in many countries makes it difficult to 'connect'. In order to move to a higher-level IT phase, the technology in surrounding companies and agencies (banks, shippers, insurance companies, post and telephones, government) must be well-developed and interconnected. An important technology in this area, which frequently lags very far behind, is telecommunication or networking.

## NETWORKING AND TELECOMMUNICATIONS

Two quotes set the stage for our consideration in this area: 'The future is the network. It's a matter of survival . . . Faster movement of data is more important than the amount of data . . . but networks have the effect of generating enormous amounts of data, which is both a "blessing and a curse"' (Clendenin, 1989).

In the US we are witnessing an explosive growth in the use of networked systems using microcomputer platforms based on processors such as the Intel 486 and Pentium, the Motorola

Power PC and the DEC Alpha, or RISC-based workstations. Powerful operating systems such as Microsoft's Windows, NT, IBM's OS/2, Novell's NetWare, and various versions of Unix make microcomputer or workstation platforms a valid competitor of mini-and mainframe computers and proprietary operating systems.

Three outgrowths of this trend deserve mention in the context of changing economies. First, these newer systems are much cheaper and much more flexible than traditional minicomputer-based systems. By flexible we mean that they can be expanded in a modular fashion, that they use standard, widely available and (relatively) inexpensive software, and that the required networking hardware is also relatively inexpensive and can easily be integrated with public telephone systems and networks.

Second, they allow large numbers of users to access the system from regular PCs (which can also be used as stand-alone devices for tasks such as routine word processing) and those users can use standard applications software which is operable in an increasingly broad range of computing environments. Users who are familiar with spreadsheets, database software, word processing and graphics packages can quickly make the transition to new networked systems. These systems support electronic mail, calendaring and scheduling, and easy access to large international networks such as Internet (already widely available at most universities and research institutes in Eastern and Central Europe and Russia).

Third, such systems are, or will soon be, readily available in even the most remote areas of the countries we are discussing.

Davenport & Short (1990) call the application of networks in the workplace 'communication-based work capability'. To the extent that networks and communications technology assist in the development of new organizational approaches, they are important competitive weapons. For countries such as those in Central and Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union, such technology can help to move companies out of the centrally-planned mode into a more flexible, more competitive, organizational form.

Keep in mind that most enterprises in these countries are huge in comparison to Western companies. State enterprises, which still make up the overwhelming majority of companies, tended towards large aggregation and monopolistic behaviour. Even seemingly small establishments, such as restaurants or hotels, were usually part of a large enterprise comprising several hundred individual units. Thus a significant future trend will be to divide large state-owned enterprises into smaller units, some private, some owned by the state but with considerable local autonomy, and others still state-run.

Calling for significant increases in the application of networking and other communications technology is perhaps quite unrealistic in these circumstances. Even in Western Europe infrastructure problems abound. Phone lines in Western Europe at present cost two to five times more than in the US. European systems also experience a much higher loss of data (approaching 25%) because of incompatibilities in the various national phone systems (Berenbaum, 1989). In the countries of Eastern and Central Europe the communications situation is far worse, and costs and waiting times will represent terribly difficult problems for years to come. It takes upwards of three years to obtain a business telephone line in Hungary, perhaps twice as long for a private line. Yet in Poland, Hungary, Russia and several other countries, the hottest new technology in business is the cellular telephone and fax, precisely because it quickly (but very expensively) gets around the problems of the government telecommunications network.

What can be done to overcome the lack of basic telecommunications infrastructure and to develop systems that work, but do not depend on non-existent resources?

## Moving ahead with existing resources: the Kikinda Steel Plant example

Kikinda has been a partner of General Motors for many years, producing the steel for automobile engines as well as their own parts and tools for foreign and domestic markets. Their main plant is located about 90 miles from Belgrade, with other manufacturing facilities in Yugoslavia and sales offices in Europe and the US. In recent years they have entered the market for custom-ordered systems, fittings and castings. The transition to a broader market motivated changes in the company's information system, moving it beyond a focus on factory applications towards more marketing and integrated applications. The system is based on a Honeywell H-8000 mainframe computer, with dozens of terminals and a proprietary operating system. An 'open' operating system based on Unix is not available for this machine. Several stand-alone minicomputers were in use for CAD/CAM and other specialized applications.

A network to connect existing systems is not completed for many of the reasons that are common in changing economies: problems with the communications processor and software, and with securing external lines that can handle data-quality transmission.

To work around external problems, a local area network is being wired only for the main plant's site, using dedicated, internal lines. Other sites will remain with stand-alone PCs. Implementation is difficult, however, because the company is building the network on top of mainframe hardware and software that was not designed for networking. At almost every step, adaptations and adjustments have to be made — to the operating system to adapt to differences in the original Honeywell software and the newer software being supplied by Bull, Honeywell's parent, to communications processors to handle internal lines, to adapt to external phone lines (when they can be obtained) and even to obtain foreign currency to purchase hardware from foreign vendors.

Despite the problems and the budgetary restrictions, management clearly recognizes the need for a more communications-oriented system. The plant is experiencing internal pressures to reduce costs and manufacturing complexity, and external pressures on quality, delivery, flexibility and lead times. Some elements of the production system are computerized but batch-oriented. The R&D department is using CAD/CAM systems, but unconnected with production, planning and marketing, making quick responses to customer queries about alterations or new products very difficult. Bids and tenders are prepared using mostly manual methods. Clearly the effort to develop 'home-grown' networking will help, and the system will be developed, despite the implementation difficulties.

The answers to what can be done to overcome communications problems lie in following paths that are feasible and that place companies and their systems in a position to capitalize on new technologies as they develop. In the Kikinda example we see efforts to move ahead — first building a local area network for the Kikinda plant, then trying to connect with other sites using telephone lines — in spite of formidable obstacles. We also see a willingness to make do with upgraded older technology where newer systems are simply not available or affordable.

One aspect of the Kikinda example is not typical. A problem in many companies is that IS professionals, the 'keepers of the mainframe', do not see the management of networks as important to their work. This is not just a problem in the emerging economies. In the US local-area networks are becoming mission-critical, and many companies are seeking mainframe-strength services and support.

People managing local-area networks today are releasing the data center of 25 to 30 years ago. Companies have bought a lot of local-area network equipment to beat the high cost of the data center. However, they are finding that central information systems management incorporates services and support that local LAN administrators do not provide — such as backup, availability and security (Butler, 1993).

The Gartner Group Inc., a market-research firm in Stamford, Connecticut, estimates that in 1992 eight times more data center resources were devoted to mainframe support than to networks. By 1996 they estimate that the resources devoted to mainframes and networks will be about equal (Butler, 1993).

Why must companies in the emerging economies wait to reallocate mainframe support resources towards networks and PCs? They can begin earlier and move faster than US or West European companies. In doing so, they must guard against a problem observed by the Czech professor quoted earlier, and also found in many US companies:

The priorities of IS development are not based on well-defined company objectives, but on incidental demands of separate users. It is quite typical to buy PC applications or PC application packages without thinking of their integration to consistent IS . . . IS without a long-term strategy causes gradual disintegration of functions, data, software, and hardware. The loss of integrity causes complicated and non-transparent IS architecture, loss of user-friendliness, expensive and long-term maintenance, disintegration of company departments, etc. (Vorisek, 1993)

Significant change cannot be led solely by users or by IS professionals; management must be involved. But senior managers in the emerging economies are largely unaware of the potential or necessity of change in IS and IT. Education and awareness-building is the real key, and here we see the importance of users and IS professionals, not as leaders, but as educators and prodders of the leaders. 'Champions' of new technology and new applications can help, but here we are faced with yet another difficulty. Users frequently see only a small part of the business in a highly restricted fashion. Typical IS professionals do not really understand the business. They are highly educated and well trained in technical and methodological areas (as noted earlier, often close to Western standards), but usually almost totally unaware of business issues.

We turn our attention now to the important question of systems development, which is closely related to these issues.

## SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT METHODOLOGY

Over the past 40 years, US information technology has gone through several 'eras', which can be viewed from different perspectives, each with its own hardware, applications focus and management focus (Table 1).

The evolution of IT in emerging economies cannot go through all of these phases or eras. As noted earlier, there simply isn't time. Payoffs must come much more quickly. Systems cannot be divorced from business and competitive reality, held only in the hands of MIS specialists behind glass windows, serving only bigger and better systems rather than real business needs. Bypassing the mainframe, moving from manual systems directly to PC-based, networked systems that use widely available applications software can save large amounts of time and money, but it is not easy to do. Many other things must be in place.

The applications development cycle is a critical issue. It must be shortened to well below the times required today. For all of the reasons cited above, and many others, information professionals must find better methods and tools to implement effective and efficient systems. The competitive environment simply will not allow the cost and time of traditional applications development methods. But when we look at available resources (IS and systems development professionals) the picture is not encouraging: 'The main priority of many informatics departments' activity [in the Czech Republic] is operation and maintenance of present projects, the usefulness of which is more and more shaken by new economic conditions. The company informatics management is overloaded by operational problems and "there is no time" for conceptual work' (Vorisek, 1993).

Research on systems development methodologies is already voluminous, but much of it has had little impact in Eastern and Central Europe and the former Soviet Union. As a result, development and design methodology tends to be more uniform and homogeneous than in the US and Western Europe, with less customizing to fit specific application situations.

Current system-design methodology in the US is dominated by structured ('hard') methodologies such as the system proposed by Yourdon (1989) or IBM's Business Systems Planning methodology. A good overview of alternative methodological approaches is found in Klein & Hirschheim (1991). In Central and Eastern Europe, 'hard' methodology is the only methodology befitting the strongly technical (rather than managerial) orientation of most systems developers. We believe strongly that, in the emerging economies, companies must use more ‘soft’ methodology for system design and more prototyping for specific systems development and implementation.

The 'hard' approach, with data flow analysis, modelling and diagramming, maintaining descriptions, etc. ensures accuracy but takes too much time. Another obvious problem with the 'hard' approach to information systems development is that it invites the repair and refurbishing of older, obsolete systems rather than real re-engineering.

'Soft' systems methodology, on the other hand, takes a broader view of the entire business, not just its information system specifies, and encourages bolder, more comprehensive systems development efforts. A good example is found in Checkland's (1981) Soft System Methodology.

Checkland gives us a definition of technique: 'a precise, specific program of action which will produce a standard result'. If 'a technique tells you "how" and a philosophy tells you "what", a methodology will contain elements of both "what" and "how"' (Checkland, 1981, chapter 6). Following is a summary of the chronological sequence of the Soft Systems Methodology:

1 The problem situation: unstructured.

2 The problem situation: expressed.

3 Root definitions of relevant systems.

4 Conceptual models.

5 Comparison of 4 with 2.

6 Feasible, desirable changes.

7 Action to improve the problem situation.

A logical sequence could (and will, in most cases) be different, but 'a start can be made anywhere... Backtracking and iteration are also essential; in fact the most effective users of the methodology have been able to use it as a framework, ... rather than as a cookery book recipe.'

Using this framework, we see an immediate difference between the methodology and much of current applications development practice in emerging economies. 'The problem' is usually the focus of development efforts, not 'the current situation', including the competitive necessities and the other factors that we have discussed. 'Initial analysis should be done by recording elements of slow-to-change structure within the situation and elements of continuously-changing process.' Stage 3 will try to define systems in terms of what they 'are', not what they 'do'. Models for root systems prepared at stage 4 are brought to the real world at stages 5 and 6. These models 'will define possible changes which simultaneously meet two criteria: that they are arguably desirable and at the same time feasible'. Stage 7 then involves taking action based on stage 6 to improve the problem situation.

After high-level design and analysis, rapid implementation requires the right computing platform, good tools, and a close connection between users, managers and IS professionals. Prototyping is the method that we recommend. By definition, prototyping draws managers and users into the systems development and implementation process. Obviously, one way to help in this effort is to use accessible technology, hence our emphasis on networked PCs. Use of a well known, well understood, widely available operating system and packaged software (both applications and languages) is also important.

We return to the question of education. Use of the proper development methodology, the right platform (networked PCs), the right languages (fourth generation) and tools (case for developers, spreadsheets and databases for end-users) facilitates communication and education. Use of the wrong platform, languages and tools places responsibility for systems too much in the hands of IS professionals and not enough in the hands of users and management. A quote from two professors in the United Kingdom is highly applicable to the education issue in the emerging economies as well:

Students of information systems [and, we would add, professors and teachers as well] need to gain experience in analysing, designing and implementing information systems of comparable size and complexity to information systems in the 'real world' and to tackle them in project teams. They would also gain from using the information systems and seeing how others use them ... as an agent of change. (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1991)

If soft methodology and prototyping are applied to information systems development, building systems in companies in the emerging economies will not have to pass through all of the eras and the whole, lengthy, development cycle. Thinking in this type of framework will help to bring to the surface managerial and organizational realities and problems at the beginning that will not have to be identified and handled later in the process of systems development.

Had the bank in our earlier case example used this approach, would they have developed a system that the organization would not accept? Would not a better system, clearly based on the organizational realities of the two pieces of the new bank, have helped to make the combined operation more competitive? Wouldn't the port of Bar have had a clearer, firmer commitment to an integrated system if this approach had been used from the beginning?

A brief anecdote from recent Hungarian experience, although not related to information systems development, makes the point about a full, clear understanding at the beginning of the process:

... the (Hungarian) State Property Agency sold [a large Hungarian] brewery to its management and employees. The decision came as a big surprise, since the other bidder was a consortium of three famous foreign breweries... No one knows how long the brewery will remain in the hands of Hungarian owners. The plant needs significant investment and employees and managers do not have the necessary resources. The most likely scenario is that the new owners will invite foreign capital to finance the necessary technology improvements. If the company wants to survive, it may have no choice but to give equity shares to foreign investors. (Anonymous, 1992)

One way to summarize this point, and to advocate softer systems-development methodologies, is that companies operating in economies that are undergoing rapid change need to pay careful attention to the development of management systems, not just to the development of information systems or to the application of information technology. If 'human factors' include 'management factors', this quote is a deft summary: 'Information systems has a rich multidisciplinary nature, where "human factors" are at least as important as "technological factors"' (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1991).

A good management system (organization structure, clear lines of authority and relationships, clearly stated and well-understood goals and objectives, appropriate measurements and controls, and effective operating procedures) is difficult to imagine without good managers. In the countries that are the focus of this article, good managers (trained, aware and knowledgeable about the technological, operational and competitive environment, sensitive to organizational needs) simply do not exist in many enterprises.

If managers and good management systems are developed, the development of good information systems to support them will follow in a natural manner. Of course, we think that supportive information systems and the creative implementation of information technology in general will assist companies to become more competitive in global markets. Stated otherwise, we believe that, without good management systems supported by appropriate information technology, the chances for most companies in changing economies to achieve a competitive position in world markets are slim indeed.

The question that we started with in the title of this paper still remains: but are information systems essential? The answer, finally, seems to us to be an unequivocal 'yes', for those situations where successful competition in world markets (primarily with US, West European and Pacific Rim competitors) will require it. There may be some markets where old methods without significant use of information technology may allow survival, but we think they are as few as situations where old methods and antiquated manufacturing technology will work.

## TO MOVE FORWARD

To summarize our points, we reiterate what we have said with more specific details:

Start with a review of existing computing platforms and systems, and systems development and maintenance functions.

Are existing systems effective? Are they strategic? Are the right platforms and software being used? Can carefully planned, modest investments in 're-aiming' existing systems pay large dividends?

Are software development and maintenance functions well organized and efficient? A recent study indicates that up to 75% of code in commercial applications is redundant and that only 2% to 5% of maintenance activity involves actual coding; the rest is taken up by understanding the program and testing, integration and implementation (Vitalari, 1991).

Investigate the possibility of shifting mainframe and minicomputer applications to more flexible microcomputer platforms. Wherever possible, promote end-user computing and widespread and unrestricted access to information technology. Computing resources in the US and Western Europe are moving quickly from the mainframe to the PC — one study indicates a 21% decrease in mainframes and a 75% increase in PCs by the year 2000 (McLean et al., 1993). In countries moving towards a market economy, the trend cannot (must not) be far behind.

Where possible, connect machines in proximate areas with local-area networks. Although wide-area networks and connection to public systems require considerable wiring and infrastructure, frequently relatively small local-area networks can be set up independent of the telephone system with easy-to-run, inexpensive wiring within the building. If necessary, investigate the use of cellular networks.

Use common, open operating systems and environments and powerful, well-supported development languages. Investigate case tools for systems analysis and design to shorten development cycles or code generators to reduce coding time.

Use common, readily available applications software. The power of these packages may be less than more specialized mainframe (minicomputer) software, but the loss of power will probably be easily made up through much more friendly user interfaces, ease of program development, superior documentation and the ready availability of assistance from dealers, VARs (value added resellers) and consultants. Wherever possible, use software that has been translated into the local language.

Maintenance can be a significant problem — in many countries very little support from the manufacturers or their representatives is available. An important criterion for new equipment might be: who else has it in the country and what kind of support are they receiving?

Work on the development and implementation of standards and agreements on protocols within the company that will guide the development of systems and promote end-user computing in an orderly manner. Such understandings are particularly important in the design of large, centralized databases and in determining who will have access to the data and under what circumstances. With networking, and the relationships that it brings, such standards are important outside the company as well.

Wherever possible, use prototyping as an important step in the systems development process. Prototyping, by definition, must involve users. In our experience, the involvement of managers who will use the system at every stage of the development process is a critical ingredient of success. Prototyping at all stages, even the development of a 'production prototype', a fairly complete system that has all menus and screens and functionality, and lacks only a complete database to support it, is important.

It is clear that most industries in changing economies have to undertake massive investment programs to upgrade their manufacturing, financial and commercial base. Without the development of more effective management systems to go with those upgrades, and without significant attention to investments in information systems and technology to make those systems more productive, much of the new investment will go to waste.

Can this be done more quickly and more efficiently than was the case in the US and Europe? Definitely 'yes'. The new tools and technologies are already known and available, albeit frequently at very high prices. There is no need to reinvent the wheel and to go through the stages of system development as it was done in the US. The technology is transferrable; indeed, much has already been transferred. What is needed is emphasis, attention, managerial understanding and support, and well-trained professionals to make it happen.
