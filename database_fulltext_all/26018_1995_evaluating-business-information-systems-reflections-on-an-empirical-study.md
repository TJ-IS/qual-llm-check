# Evaluating business information systems: reflections on an empirical study

B. FARBEY, D. TARGETT & F. LAND\*

School of Management, University of Bath, Bath, UK and \*Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, London, UK

Abstract. A leading vendor of information technology (IT) systems commissioned the authors to investigate how its clients made their decision to acquire new systems, what benefits they expected to gain, and the kind of evaluation procedures used both before taking the decision to go ahead and subsequently when the systems were in operation. The 16 organizations participating in the study had all purchased major systems and were willing to talk frankly about their experiences. The results of the research have been extensively reported elsewhere. The purpose of this paper is to draw out the implications for IT management. The paper opens with a summary of the findings of the original research. The main part of the paper consists of six case studies from which the management issues are drawn out.

Keywords: decision-making, evaluation, information systems, management of IT, office systems.

## INTRODUCTION

The process of appraising new investment is always a political process in so far as it touches on the diverse interests of many people and groups. Investment in new information systems (IS) projects is no different in this respect — it is an intervention at an organizational, as well as a technical level (see, for example, Hirschheim & Smithson, 1988; Symons, 1991).

This ‘socio-technical’ nature of IS has two immediate consequences. Firstly, in assessing the possible benefits and risks of a major new system, the social impact must carry due weight. Social impacts are pervasive and therefore important. At the same time, because they are often intangible and hard to quantify, they are sometimes ignored. Secondly, it is difficult to justify the investment at the outset because it is almost impossible to predict with any certainty how the intervention will play itself out. Moreover, there has been no steady accumulation of experience or conventional wisdom to fall back on. There are few universally accepted guidelines for evaluating IS projects; there is no agreed language of accounting for IS.

The result is something like a battlefield with a great deal of mud and smoke but very little by way of coherent strategy. The foot-soldiers make do and improvise with what is to hand. Some employ standard accounting techniques and forego much of the rich picture of social and technical change. A few attempt to move to higher ground, incorporating qualitative factors in various ingenious ways. Others give up entirely and proceed on the basis of an 'act of faith'. Analysis of what practitioners do is nevertheless important. By comparing what is done, across a range of organizations, it becomes possible to identify major issues and test out salient features relevant to the problem of justification.

A leading vendor of IT systems commissioned the authors to investigate how its clients made their decision to acquire new systems, what benefits they expected to gain, and the kind of evaluation procedures used, both before taking the decision to go ahead and, subsequently, when the systems were in operation. The 16 organizations participating in the study had all purchased major systems and were willing to talk frankly about their experiences. They varied in size and in their sectors of operation. Some had apparently been successful in implementing beneficial systems; others admitted failure, including one that had returned the equipment to the vendor before the lease ran out. Applications also varied but were heavily biased towards office-type applications.

The research was based on interviews with managers in each of the 16 organizations. Inevitably, there was a danger of faulty memories and post hoc rationalization as well as the general problem that history tends to be written by the winners. By conducting in-depth interviews across different functions and at different levels of management, and by analysing documents and reports, we sought to cross-check the information given.

The results of the research have been extensively reported elsewhere (Farbey et al., 1993). The purpose of this paper is to draw out the management implications from the detail of the interviews. The paper begins with a look at the context in which the decisions were made and at the level of change sought. This is followed by a summary of the differing procedures for performing an evaluation, as they occurred in each organization. Next, the variety of people involved in the decision-making process are considered. Six case studies are then described briefly, and from these the management issues are drawn out.

## ISSUES SURROUNDING EVALUATION

## Was there an IT strategy?

Current thinking in IS theory takes it as axiomatic that IT investment should be made in the context of an IT strategy and that the IT strategy should act to support the overall strategic aims of the organization (Earl, 1989; Berenbaum & Lincoln, 1990; Macdonald, 1991). The research showed that theory and practice were different. Fewer than half the organizations claimed to have an IT strategy and only one spoke of IT in the total business context. For the rest, IT investment decisions were made piecemeal.

## What level of change was sought/expected?

In the course of discussing the key issues for business success in the 1990s, Porter (1988) suggests a hierarchy for IT impacts. The kinds of impact that were sought by the informants, according to justification reports reviewed, before the systems were installed were compared with Porter's 'levels' of hierarchy. Usually the impacts covered more than one level and the most relevant had to be chosen. The results are shown in Table 1.

Table 1 shows that the organizations were moving beyond using IT to automate activities in their current form. Instead, they were planning to use the technology for enhancing and optimizing their activities. Five were using the networking capabilities of the systems to coordinate activities that were geographically spread. However, with one exception, they were not using the technology to reconfigure the way in which work was done, nor, again with one exception, were they using the systems to connect with customers or anyone else outside the firm. None had considered using the technology to support cooperative work, although one or two organizations were edging towards this. For example, they had recognized that collaborative bids were made easier if everyone was using the same, or a compatible technology. None of the organizations had reached the levels of sophistication suggested in the literature either in the way they considered IT (Scott Morton, 1991 McFarlan, 1984; Porter & Millar, 1985) or in the way they went about installing it (McFarlan, 1981) or justifying its use (Parker and Benson, 1988). Again, theory and practice were different.

## TYPES OF PROCEDURE USED

## Was a justification required?

Although some sort of justification had been required in most cases, only about half had followed a standard justification procedure, i.e. a generalizable one, and only half required any quantification of the benefits. As a rule, it was up to the champion (a specific person who had taken up the challenge of driving the system through) to do whatever he or she thought was necessary to gain approval. Figure 1 gives an overview of the procedures.

Nine out of the 16 projects were subject to a formal justification process. Of these, 4 were justified using classical return on investment techniques. Three of these were 'for real', meaning, according to the interviewees, that the process governed the decision to go ahead; the remaining one was a rationalization of the decision which had been taken on other grounds. In five cases the justification was not quantified. Only one of these seemed to be 'for real' — as before the others seemed to be rationalizations.

Of the seven that were not formally justified, two were justified as 'got-to-do' projects, i.e. projects that had to go ahead in order to remain in business. In the first of these, the primary reason was that the company's US clients were accustomed to a better standard of presentation and service than obtained in the UK. The system was necessary if clients were not to be lost. In the second, the investment was considered an essential part of the IT strategy. The other five out of seven went ahead as 'acts of faith', i.e. the organizations believed the projects would be beneficial but chose not to justify in detail.

## Was there an attempt at quantification?

Quantifiable, tangible benefits were identified in 10 out of the 16 projects. In the remaining six even benefits that were tangible, and therefore might have been measured, were not measured.

Only three of the 16 projects attempted to quantify intangible benefits. This did not mean that people were unaware of them. Rather, they considered that intangibles would not be acceptable justification to those in power. However, in seven cases intangibles were cited as extra arguments to support the case for going ahead. In the remaining cases, intangible benefits were entirely excluded.

Often the justification procedure explicitly excluded anything but the most immediate operational benefits. In one case, this was true even though there were much larger strategic benefits that might have been included; see, for example, the pharmaceutical case study later in the paper. In general, the focus was on short-term, quantifiable, tangible benefits. The larger picture was missing.

## At what stage(s) was the evaluation undertaken?

Evaluation is not, or need not be, a one-off activity. Evaluation of IS projects may take place at many different points in the project lifetime. The major points are when:

● strategy is being developed;

● a specific project has been identified and its feasibility is being assessed;

● the project is in the developmental stage;

\- the system has to be 'signed off' (from developer to user);

● directly after implementation;

● the system is being monitored after implementation;

● towards the end of the system's life: new options are being considered.

The research showed that justification, when it was done at all, was done at the 'feasibility' stage. At first sight, this is in contradistinction to the literature, which suggests that most evaluations are done at 'sign-off', that is, when the systems are handed over from supplier to customer (Kumar, 1990). However, in this case it is probable that the differences were due to the nature of the projects being investigated. Virtually all of them were off-the-shelf systems, as opposed to tailor-made ones. For such systems, the agreement to purchase is the key handover point from the supplier to the customer.

In addition, six organizations undertook post-implementation audits, all undertaken at the instigation of the vendor.

The interviews indicated that this apparent lack of enthusiasm for evaluation at other stages might be because of two factors. Firstly, evaluation can be time-consuming and expensive, whatever method is chosen: post-implementation evaluation must itself be justifiable. Secondly, there is a tendency to regard information systems as a 'sunk cost' and therefore to take no follow-up action.

However, the research findings suggested that multi-stage evaluation would be valuable. It has already been argued in principle that the impacts are unpredictable. It was also found that in practice there was a marked difference between the expected outcomes and those which occurred. This point is illustrated in the later case studies. It follows that only an active benefits management policy will enable the benefits to be identified and exploited. Only an active policy will enable disbenefits to be controlled and risks to be minimized.

## WHO TOOK PART? A STAKEHOLDER ANALYSIS

The process of justifying investment in new IS projects involved a wide variety of people in each organization and, on occasion, outside it.

Within the organization management participation varied. Many of the cost-justification procedures had involved management at the most senior level, including the Board or Managing Director. Other general management figures included divisional and group managers. Financial management were involved as were functional managers as appropriate, for example, site managers where the installation required it, as well as administrative or business systems managers or in one instance, the publications manager.

IS practitioners generally took part although, in one case at least, the project managers specific concern was to minimize the participation of the central IT department. Users were generally consulted, including the support staff such as secretaries, as were professionals, such as fund managers or technical authors.

The people from outside the organization most in evidence were employees of the systems vendor. In many cases they were directly involved in the justification process and, as noted elsewhere, it was always the vendor who forced the pace on post-installation audits. The vendor saw these audits as part of the process of maintaining a relationship with the customer and, ultimately, as a way of developing the account.

Not unexpectedly, most systems had relied heavily on a champion. Of the champions, seven were managers, six were IS/IT practitioners, one was a user. In the remaining two cases the initiative came from a combination of a systems person and a manager.

Other findings can be quickly summarized. Very few attempts were made to treat the system differently from any other capital expenditure proposal; there was no consistency in the way in which cost-justification had been approached; no organizations had carried out an ex-post valuation of the system, except where initiated by the supplier of the system. Moreover, the perception of what was needed to be considered was disappointingly narrow, whether it concerned the possible scope and level of use of the system, the range of people who could or should have been involved or the timescale over which the evaluation could profitably have been undertaken.

Some explanation is needed. These people were not stupid, or insensitive to the social impact of new systems, or suffering from a lack of vision in other aspects of their work. Nor were they necessarily lacking in IT/IS expertise. The case studies that follow allow us to search a little deeper for explanation and understanding.

## CASE STUDIES

Although it was not part of the original objectives of the research, it became clear as the investigation progressed that it was not possible to separate, or sometimes even distinguish, evaluation from the general IS decision-making processes and the wide range of other important organizational factors otherwise unconnected to IT. While evaluation is taking place a variety of decisions are being taken and many other issues are influencing events. For example, the exact scope and definition of the project is being decided; the project management teams are being assembled; the roles of the users are changing; the roles of other stakeholders are also changing and the nature of their stakes becoming clear; differences between formal and informal agendas widen, as do those between formal and informal meetings. Organizational politics starts to play its part.

All these are major issues, which are intertwined with the evaluation of the investment. The decisions about the project are not usually one-off: more often they are rolling decisions which emerge over time as experience is gained and new information becomes available. As the project progresses new stakeholders become involved. This may be systematic as when different managers are assigned to different stages in the process, or random as, for example, when key figures leave the company. As more stakeholders enter the decision-making process the dimensions of the project change, sometimes gradually, sometimes explosively.

## Case 1: Engineering industry

A division of a large engineering company produces computerized industrial controls. A typical example would be a system for running an automated manufacturing plant. Sales are made throughout the world including major deals in the former USSR and Central Europe.

The technical documentation associated with the controls is extensive and increasingly complex. Moreover, imminent legal changes now means that documentation has to accompany products rather than following it. The documentation process was therefore going to be critical in meeting delivery dates. In addition, a substantial amount of documentation was needed to support the process of bidding for new contracts and the associated presentations. The management of the Product Documentation Section and that of the central business systems group were aware that the latest publishing technology could make document publishing much more efficient.

There was no standard procedure for justifying new IT investment within the company although there were guidelines, which meant that a rigorous proposal document had to be prepared. This was essentially economic, dealing with cost and workforce reductions and other efficiency benefits. The proposal had to be approved by the Business Systems Group, the Site Director and the Group General Manager. The proposal envisaged the following benefits:

● better performance in meeting product delivery dates;

\- 50% cost reduction through halving the number of clerical staff;

● reduction in external printing costs by bringing all printing in-house;

\- improved control of projects by bringing printing in-house;

● storage cost reductions by printing on demand instead of to store;

● income: doubling price of manuals because of their better presentational quality.

After implementation an IT audit carried out by the IT vendor revealed some unplanned benefits:

● the system was a significant factor in gaining a large contract because of the speed with which the proposal was prepared;

\- cost reductions were achieved even though the throughput was almost doubled;

● the system acted as a catalyst to a re-organization of the management structure.

The general user reaction has been favourable and the system has had a real business impact. 'The Russian contract would probably not have been achieved without it', according to one senior manager.

## Case 2: Pharmaceutical industry

The organization develops, produces and markets products ranging from prescribed drugs to off-the-shelf medicaments for the UK and some Middle Eastern markets. It is a division of a global organization whose Group Head Office, in the USA, exercises close control over UK activities, which are spread across several UK locations.

A critical task of the Medical Department is to create the documentation for registering and licensing new drug products. This involves taking reports on, for example, the product 'recipe', tests and trials, and quality assurance data.

These and other reports are consolidated, bringing together text, tables and graphics from the different parts of the company, into one document. This final report, which can run to thousands of pages, is sent to the relevant government department in London for approval. The drug cannot be sold until this department has registered and licensed it. The process is lengthy (it can take years) and usually involves discussions between civil servants and the company that result in changes to the documentation, requests for additional information and requirements for further research and testing. The process is at the core of the business.

The previous system was to produce most of the documents on a series of stand-alone word processors and then insert the rest via cut-and-paste. Medical Department staff (medical doctors) wrote reports in longhand and gave them to secretaries working on the word processors. The proposed new system was intended to integrate the whole document production process. It had the capability to produce sophisticated tables, charts and graphics very easily. The full system was a series of Local Area Networks (LANs) at the UK locations, all linked together over leased lines.

The Managing Director requested the Management Information Systems Department and the Medical Department carry out a joint cost–benefit study of the new system. The standard justification procedure, which was stipulated by Head Office, only allowed for quantifiable benefits. The resulting report had to be approved at four different levels of UK management before being forwarded to the USA for further appraisal. Justification of the system was based on the achievement of four benefits:

1 Reduction in professional time estimated at 20%.

2 Reduction in clerical time estimated at 20%.

3 Further reduction in clerical time from the use of templates for other formal documents.

4 Reduction in paper used.

Two other benefits known to the justifying team, were excluded from the report. They were:

1 Extra revenues: getting products to market sooner — estimated at £5 million per day.

2 Easier Ministry approval: better information and improved presentational quality.

These benefits, far greater in size and importance than the ones in the report, were excluded because the organization's corporate procedures and culture dictated that only hard quantified benefits could be considered. The team felt that the inclusion of the higher level, but less tangible and measurable benefits, would generate argument and delay because of the difficulties in providing precise quantification. They felt that the lower level efficiency benefits were sufficient by themselves to get through corporate approval procedures. Thus the bigger benefits were omitted and the request was approved, with little opposition. An evaluation of the project after implementation revealed that the following benefits had been achieved:

1 It was thought that products had been 'got to market' faster but since each product is unique it was not possible to prove this.

2 A doctors' database had been created and linked to a 'phone in for information' facility. This provided better service, leading to improved market share.

3 Eighty percent of the Medical Department Staff (previously none) keyed in information themselves. This shortened time and gave improved control.

4 The organization moved further down the management learning curve, making it easier to implement other applications.

5 The project has led to the creation of a database of information required for registration in other countries.

But paper usage was increased, not reduced.

The two most far-reaching and strategic benefits stemming from the project were therefore not included in the justification document: one (getting the project to market faster) for political reasons, the other (a new information product for doctors) because it was not foreseen. The outcome was a happy one — a successful project — but the situation does point to the need for management processes that can search for benefits and face up to their implications. A further question, however, is what would have happened if the quantifiable benefits had not been sufficient to gain approval. Would the project have been delayed, losing valuable competitive advantage? Or would it not have gone ahead at all?

## Case Study 3: Financial services industry

The Asset Management Group at this organization manages investment portfolios for clients ranging from pension funds to individual large investors, located in many areas of the world. Reports on the performance of the portfolio are presented to the clients at regular intervals. Other reports may be proposals to prospective new clients or recommendations for substantial changes in the structure of existing clients' portfolios. These reports are usually put together under extreme time pressures since the month end is the usual reporting date and clients would be suspicious of over-long delays.

The existing system for producing reports was a network of word processors which produced text only. Graphics were produced by hand and integrated via cut-and-paste.

The motivation to change came from the Director of Asset Management who had a 'vision' of the importance of quality, both in terms of information and its presentation, in managing client relationships. There was also the generally recognized and perennial need to report on time. Not everyone in the organization saw the situation this way, with many individuals taking the view that only the performance of the portfolio had any business impact.

The debate was resolved by the group that dealt with US clients. They reported that the organization was well behind US rivals in reporting procedures and that business would be lost unless changes were made. They suggested a system that they knew had been successful in the USA.

No costed economic justification was put to the Board. The decision about the system was taken on a 'got-to-do' basis. Since the Fund Managers who were complaining about the existing system were represented on the Board the decision process was speedy.

This first system to be implemented comprised of just three workstations. It was perceived to be a success and other Asset Management Groups soon asked for similar equipment. However, the additional equipment was subject to more critical justification than the original. A capital investment analysis was carried out and presented to the Board.

There was some opposition from the central IT group, who objected to specialist systems that were not under their control. They preferred a personal computer option. However, they took the view that they should allow the Asset Management Groups to go ahead and then say 'I told you so' when a disaster occurred.

The decision to buy the additional systems was straightforward since growing experience with the US group showed that the times to produce reports could be significantly reduced. In any case, many fund managers had already promised clients tighter deadlines. Consequently, this decision also veered towards 'got to do'.

More workstations were installed in other areas. The local networks were linked together and eventually were linked in turn with the company's financial system, which held market information including share prices and portfolio details. The system is now used to producing integrated client reports, which take financial data, tables and text from fund controllers' secretaries working on the financial network, and add or improve tables and graphics under instructions from the fund controllers. Fig. 2 illustrates the changes in working practices.

The organization did not carry out a post-implementation review and had no intention of doing so until the computer vendor offered to undertake one free of charge. It produced some surprising results, both good and bad:

\- productivity improvements far in excess of expectations: clerical costs reduced by 50%–90%;

● enhanced customer service: deadlines (nearly) always met, reports accurate with no transcription errors;

\- invoices showed more computers had been bought than were in use: 10 were found in a storeroom unopened;

\- some UK clients asked to return to the old reporting style, with a price decrease.

Two management changes were brought about by the system. Firstly, the organization now never requires lengthy justification for IT equipment, which has the same status as the telephone system as far as justification is concerned. Secondly, the central IT group no longer exists. IT expertise is decentralized in all the business groups.

In our research, no organizations chose to audit their systems to see whether they were achieving business benefits: those that did were prompted by the IT vendor. Organizations were much more likely to check on the technical performance only. This case study indicates the value of post-implementation, business audits of IT.

## Case Study 4: Financial services industry

The organization is a firm of chartered accountants whose main business is carrying out financial audits. However, management consultancy has recently been added and this is expanding rapidly. There are offices in several locations, including London and Birmingham, but the head office is in Leeds.

The organization used to have a central computer department but because of its growing computer consultancy work, the department became a subsidiary. This group imposes policies relating to the choice of equipment and of software but there was no long-term plan covering the developing of IT usage within the company.

The Administrative Manager wanted a new system to produce the 3500 annual accounts (75% of the workload of her department), reports relating to consultancy (currently 20% of the workload but increasing) and other documents such as marketing material where high quality output was required. The existing system was a series of stand-alone word processors. They produced text only; accounts and tables had to be input as if text.

The IT committee (several partners plus IT experts from the computer group) agreed with the Administrative Manager that a new system was needed. However, they also wished to support the computer group policy of standardizing on personal computers. Accordingly, the committee recommended that the administrative department be upgraded to the PC system.

The Administrative manager thought a PC system would be inadequate for the task since the software available at the time was not particularly suitable for producing accounts and graphics, or for achieving suitable presentational quality. She wanted some form of specialized Desk Top Publishing (DTP) system.

The IT committee continued to press for PC standardization; the Administrative Manager continued to press the view that a PC system was not right for her department and that the inadequacy of a PC system would result in an explosion in staff numbers. Eventually, a sub-committee was set up to look into the issue. It took the views of a range of people and looked at alternative systems. A DTP system was brought in on trial, on the grounds that its manufacturer was that of the existing successful system. After the system had been demonstrated to the subcommittee, the Administrative Manager produced a review document stating the reasons for acquiring the system. The subcommittee, especially the Director of Finance and the Director of Personnel, became the sponsors of the DTP system and recommended it to the Senior Committee of partners, which eventually approved the DTP system. If the Administrative Manager had not championed the system so persistently it would not have been bought. A DTP network was installed at Leeds. Similar networks were eventually installed at other locations with the capability of communicating with the organization's other regional offices.

The system achieved its cost-reduction benefits but it also produced a large, unexpected benefit. The Wide Area Network (WAN) allowed work to be moved from one part of the country to another according to capacity available and costs. In the main, this amounted to moving a lot of work from London, with its high clerical staff costs, to the North, where costs were lower and staff turnover (and therefore quality) higher.

## Case Study 5: Management and technical consultancy

The organization is a general technical consultancy company serving management and technical functions in many industries, of which the major ones are space and defence. It has a London-based service and support division, called 'X International', supporting national offices in different parts of the world.

An IT strategy was developed, part of which identified two requirements:

\- make the production of documents — consultants' reports, tenders and progress reports — efficient and effective;

\- improve inter-office communication for information and documents, nationally and internationally, for the 3500 employees.

To meet the strategy, a system which would meet these requirements worldwide was to be implemented. Development and implementation of this new system was driven by the Managing Director of X International, who was also a main Board Director of the global organization.

The existing system for report production was locally based. Usually, consultants sent longhand reports to secretaries for word processing. They were then returned in hard copy form for proof-reading. The graphics were prepared separately by hand. There was a growing preference on the part of the consultants to key in their own material but the very limited communication between national offices was by telephone and fax. In essence, local divisions operated in isolation: they were not able to carry out joint projects without extensive travelling, nor were they able to learn from one another's experiences. Sometimes, two divisions would carry out identical assignments in different counties without any liaison.

Another consultancy company was employed to carry out a needs survey based on the strategy. This consultancy produced a report specifying the requirements and a list of major computer vendors was then invited to tender. Of those received, the response of one vendor was clearly the closest to the needs in terms of functionality, although it was by no means the lowest priced. This vendor was then asked to prove in practice that its system would work satisfactorily. As a result, managers attended demonstrations at the vendor's offices and some trial equipment was brought to X International.

The system did seem to be satisfactory for its applications, which included word processing, graphics, communications within and between offices, file storage, laser printing, database management, table generation, business forms and spreadsheets. The Managing Director of X International prepared a Justification Report for the main board in order to obtain final approval to purchase and implement the system.

The main Board mainly comprised of the Managing Directors of the national offices. Unfortunately, there was some opposition to the system at this level because some MDs objected to spending money from their own budgets. Their reasons were that if one of the major benefits was better communication, then the equipment should be paid for centrally. X International's role is to advise rather than mandate and the final outcome was a recommendation that the linking equipment should be supplied to all subsidiaries and paid for centrally. However, the MDs would have to pay for the equipment installed within their own subsidiaries.

Given this agreement and the fact that opting out of the system would exclude a division from electronic mail, document exchange, corporate support for the system and training, it was thought that all the MDs would take it up. The system was installed and achieved many of its objectives in terms of the expected benefits:

\- It improved client service through enhanced report content and presentation.

\- It promoted the exchange of management information.

\- It led to a consistent company style.

\- It took the organization further along the IT learning curve.

\- It improved job satisfaction for consultants and secretaries.

In spite of all this, the system was, overall, a complete disaster. Only a third of the divisions opted in, the others keeping existing systems or adopting new systems that were incompatible. As a result, the number of users never reached a critical mass and its facilities, particularly those relating to communications such as electronic mail, were little used. Even some divisions that had opted in stopped using the system, or indicated that leases would not be renewed, when they ran out. Within 3 years the whole system had collapsed.

X International, the support group, had done many things according to the book: a business-related IT strategy had been developed, the requirements had been analysed objectively and users had been able to influence the system and its implementation. The problem was that X International had misjudged the decision-making culture.

Interestingly, a formal audit of the system after it had collapsed, by a principal consultant of the organization, blamed the disaster on the computer vendor, on the grounds of poor support. Certainly, vendor support could have been better and the vendor did in fact reorganize as a result. It may even have been the 'last straw' for some of the subsidiaries that abandoned the system. But all this happened long after two-thirds of the subsidiaries had already decided not to participate, and vendor support could hardly have contributed to this.

## Case Study 6: Defence Industry

The organization is part of a major aerospace group specializing in the design and production of military aircraft equipment. The Contracting Group is responsible for producing proposals, bids and tenders for the whole company. This involves liaising with engineers doing the development work and accountants doing the costing. It is the Contracting Group's overall responsibility to bring together formal proposals to be sent to client organizations, usually government departments, in the UK and elsewhere. The Group also has to liaise with other defence contractors in the UK and elsewhere since collaboration between companies is playing an increasing role in the defence industry.

The existing production system was based on a central clerical department with word processors for text. Graphics and scale plans were produced manually by the draughting group under the supervision of engineers. The central clerical department used cut-and-paste to incorporate them in the final documents, which were lengthy and included many figures and graphics.

The move to a more up-to-date system originated when the manager of the Contracting Group saw a caravan display of computer-based office equipment in a local car park. His mind had been on other business and technical matters and he had not previously realized how rapidly office systems technology had improved. He felt that the latest equipment could offer substantial benefits in terms of project management and the production of technical documents.

He quickly went to work to outline the type of system that would meet his requirements. The basic requirements were:

● a networked system which could link different functions;

\- his group should have its own system and thus retain control — he did not want to use the company's mainframe;

\- compatibility with other company equipment which included IBM and DEC mainframes and various personal computers.

He contacted the vendor whose hardware he had seen in the caravan and was lent some trial equipment for a month. Benchmarks were set, and passed within the month. The Manager of the Contracting Group put together an Investment Proposal Report and sent it to the Board of Directors, at the same time inviting them to come to see the trial system working. Some of them actually did so and enthusiasm was displayed at all levels. The report specified benefits in terms of cost, labour and time. Even so, the decision took 6 months, but eventually a network was installed and linked to other networks in the company.

Post-implementation, the system users were enthusiastic. The expected efficiency improvements had been achieved, including a massive reduction in the numbers of draughts-men. In addition, some unlooked-for benefits were found:

● deadlines were met more frequently

● there had been a reduction in uncertainty and risk

\- improved presentational quality was thought to have had an impact on bid acceptance.

Perhaps the most interesting benefit was that the system had allowed the organization to take part in collaborative international proposals through the electronic exchange of information. What is interesting about this benefit was that while the users were aware that without the system the organization would have been barred from these international partnerships, other managers, including some senior managers, were not. The need for such a system had not been foreseen at a strategic level. Nor was this major benefit of its existence fully appreciated even after installation. Communication within the organization about the impact of the system was poor.

## Case discussion

Only one of the cases, Case 1, from the engineering industry, could be classified as 'straightforward', in that evaluation went ahead as planned. The expected benefit was cost reduction and the 'return on investment' method captured the economics of the investment accurately. The context of the investment was tightly defined and implementation went down the appointed track.

Case 2, pharmaceuticals, had to be treated as if it were straightforward because organizational procedures and culture required that all benefits and costs be quantified. This created difficulties and turned evaluation into an obstacle to be surmounted in the eyes of the managers concerned. The main benefit was getting a product to market sooner, which would, it was estimated, mean a daily increase in revenue far greater than the capital cost of the system. However, this benefit was so hard to quantify exactly that it was omitted from the evaluation, which relied on minor cost-cutting benefits. The minor benefits were sufficient to get the project past the corporate hurdle, but the only strategic benefit, getting the product to market sooner, was delayed in its realization and it is possible that it could have been missed altogether. Evaluation seemed to be a corporate ritual to be got through as quickly as possible, rather than a true evaluation.

Evaluation in Case 2 was influenced by pressure from inside the organization. Pressure from outside the organization was decisive in Case 3, financial services. Competitive pressures to do 'something' were strong and resulted in fast and limited evaluations: the organizations thought that anything more was unnecessary, since there was no question that something had to be done.

People issues were also highly influential in Case 3: the existence of 'champions' was crucial. The competitive pressures were keenly felt by a particular business group, who forced through a project which they regarded as a business-winning system, but which the rest of the organization regarded as an unnecessary expense. A champion was also important in Case 4, the second financial services case. The champion was a functional manager who fought the Board and IT committee over the adoption of a generic personal computer solution when, she believed, a specific solution was essential. Her championship overturned corporate evaluation and approval procedures and secured the implementation of the system of her choice. The 'ownership' that this success engendered led to the system being further exploited.

People issues were a factor in Case 5, the consultancy, where including an incomplete set of stakeholders in the evaluation process was a major source of difficulty. The IS was undertaken as an 'act of faith', led by senior management. While many studies (for example, Galliers, 1991) have concluded that senior management commitment is necessary for the success of IS projects, if that commitment is not accompanied by the involvement of a full set of stakeholders then, according to this case, it may not be sufficient. This conclusion may be especially relevant if, as here, the excluded stakeholders are mainly professionals with substantial autonomy and control over their working practices. These stakeholders wielded enough corporate power to resist and ultimately block the projects they did not understand and felt threatened by.

In the last case, Case 6, in the defence industry, the set of stakeholders was comprehensive, but communications amongst them poor. The IS had originally been undertaken as a standard cost reduction system. It later emerged as a major strategic system (Runge and Earl, 1988) but the senior management were not aware of this role. Consequently, there were misunderstandings within the organization about the role of IS at a strategic level.

## CONCLUSION

The case-based research findings, although far from rigorous, indicated that a prime contribution made by evaluation was that it produced new information about the project (beyond the financial implications) and about stakeholder perspectives on the project and its implications.

To summarize, the paper has highlighted five major factors contributing to the success or disaster of an IT investment:

1 A comprehensive search for benefits.

2 A post-implementation business evaluation.

3 An understanding of related management processes.

4 Good communications amongst stakeholders.

5 A champion to drive the investment through.

The first two of the factors, the necessity for a comprehensive search for benefits and for a post-implementation business evaluation, are directly concerned with evaluation as an activity. In both cases, the outstanding purpose of evaluation is to put and keep the costs and benefits squarely on the management agenda. The other three factors all relate to people and the necessity for constantly persuading and consulting all the stakeholders.

In general, evaluation has too narrow an interpretation in many organizations, being taken to refer to the quantification of well-defined benefits with the sole objective of getting the project over organizational fences. The inevitable result is that clear-cut projects are evaluated more or less according to the textbook whilst fuzzy, possibly strategic, projects are evaluated in cursory fashion or not evaluated at all, because many of the benefits do not match the requirements of standard accounting techniques such as return on investment. If, as seems probable, evaluation influences implementation success then it may also be inevitable that without evaluation projects are more likely to fail. Information reduces uncertainty and therefore it could be expected that evaluation is linked to project risk.

What is needed is a wide interpretation of evaluation as an overall assessment, by whatever means, encompassing the full impact of the system: financial, strategic, social and so on. This wide interpretation would imply that evaluation should in itself act to tease out any latent benefits and should investigate the full range of impacts within the organization.

As IS cover greater areas of the organization and have an impact at all levels ranging from strategic to tactical, the problems of evaluation and implementation success are going to increase. In particular, as empowered organizations become more common, situations parallel to those involving the management of professionals described in some of the cases will arise more often and the importance of the wide interpretation of evaluation will increase.
