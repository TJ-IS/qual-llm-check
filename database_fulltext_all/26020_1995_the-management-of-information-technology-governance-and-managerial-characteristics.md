# The management of information technology, governance, and managerial characteristics

Z.A. Karake

Department of Economics & Business, The Catholic University of America, MCM 309, 620 Michigan Ave., NE. Washington, DC 20064

Abstract. Using a sample of Fortune 500 industrial firms, this study hypothesizes that various intra-organizational factors, including those commonly associated with agency, governance and managerial characteristics of the firm, that affect the decision to create a top management executive position for a chief information officer (CIO). Statistically meaningful relationships were observed between the creation of a top executive CIO position and (1) top management's equity interests, (2) the number of outside directors on the board, (3) Chief Executive Officer (CEO) age, and (4) CEO Experience.

Keywords: Agency Theory, Information technology, Upper Echelon Theory.

## INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this paper is to isolate those intra-organizational factors associated with the creation of a CIO position on the top level management team. This phenomenon is analysed from two management perspectives; namely, agency and governance views on the one hand, and management characteristics perspective on the other. Since the CIO phenomenon is a relatively new one, few definitions of the term exist. Synnot and Gruber (1987) were among the first to offer a definition for the CIO. According to the two authors, 'a CIO is a senior executive responsible for establishing corporate information policy, standards and management control over all corporate information resources.' Borbely (1989) defines a CIO as '... the overall authority for policy, technology, and performance standards related to information resource management and applications, as well as for the development and management of interdepartmental or corporate systems and resources.' Weiner and Girven (1990) maintain that a CIO is a generic title for an enterprise-level executive who takes part on an equal basis with other corporate officers in drawing the strategic path for the organization. The various definitions have revealed the CIO to be (1) a full participant in the company's strategic planning process; (2) responsible for the adoption of new application development techniques; (3) a manager of client/server architecture and open systems; and, (4) an active partner in the revamping of business functions. On the practical front, over the past few years, the role and responsibilities of the CIO have been examined in a number of large American corporations, such as Otis

Elevator Company (1991), Chrysler Corporation (1992), Eastman Kodak Company (1992), Merve Griffin Resorts (1992), Xerox Corporation (1992), and others. These case studies have revealed that CIOs in these corporations have in fact assumed, at one time or another, responsibility for each of the four functions listed above, with varying degrees of success. The most overlooked function was the participation of CIOs in their corporations' strategic planning. Recently, however, many corporations are emphasising the strategic dimensions of the CIO function (Haley, Fordonski, & Puckett, 1993).

Even though the fever of the CIO phenomenon has been catching on rapidly in corporate America, a large number of the Fortune 500 companies (the 500 largest industrial companies in the United States for a specific year) still refrain from establishing a top executive CIO position. In 1988–1989, for instance, only 108 Fortune 500 companies had a CIO as part of the top management team. $^{1}$

This study attempts to empirically uncover the differential effects of a number of management characteristics and company-specific factors on the creation of a CIO top level executive position. Using Logistic analysis, the paper investigates possible determinants in the creation of a CIO top level position.

## REVIEW OF UPPER ECHELON AND AGENCY THEORIES

In a previous study, Karake (1992) analysed the CIO phenomenon from an Agency Theory/organizational characteristics perspective. Her findings revealed that the creation of a CIO position on the top level management team was positively correlated with the ownership structure of the corporation and board composition. Here the analysis is extended to combine two theories in examining the CIO phenomenon: Agency Theory and Upper Echelon Theory. Agency Theory was developed by Jensen and Meckling in 1976, and it can be summarized as manager and shareholder desire to maximize their own interests. If the managers of the firm do not have significant ownership in that firm, a divergence in the interests of the managers and the owners is expected. This divergence of interests is costly and is referred to as the costs of agency. The costs of agency are said to be constrained and/or attenuated by the markets for corporate control and managerial labour, corporate governance and other internal control mechanisms (Fama, 1980; Fama & Jensen, 1983; Kerr & Bettis, 1987; and Baysiner & Hoskinsson, 1989). A primary application of the agency argument is that a failure to minimize the costs of agency results in a drain of firm's resources, thereby placing the firm at a competitive disadvantage. In addition, this theory's framework is concerned with the contractual relationship between equity holders, debt holders, managers, and employees in the firm. Equity holders and debt holders are treated as principals and managers are treated as agents. Agency Theory re-establishes the importance of incentives and self-interest in organizational thinking (Perrow, 1986). It is a reminder that much of organizational life, whether we like it or not, is based on self-interest.

Hambrick and Mason (1984) and others (Gupta, 1984; Chaganti & Sambharya, 1987; Norburn, 1989; Reed & Reed, 1989) have investigated the idea that characteristics of top level managers make a difference in how the organization performs and how it is run. Hambrick and Mason (1984) developed the Upper Echelon Theory suggesting that characteristics of top level management teams are determinants of strategic choices. Several propositions were developed by Hambrick and Mason suggesting that organizational outcomes can be predicted from the observance of managerial backgrounds. The authors argue that this is a reasonable expectation because (1) the observance of certain managerial backgrounds are expected to result from previous organizational experience, and, (2) managers are often selected because they have a background that is deemed relevant by the selecting body; for example, if as previous managers they were associated with superior firm performance. More recently, the relationship between changes in corporate strategy and the demographic characteristics of a firm's management team has been investigated. Strategic change was operationalized as the absolute percentage change in diversification strategy, and the relevant demographic variables included age, organizational tenure, top management team tenure and educational data. Essentially, the authors found that strategic change was associated with relatively youthful top management teams who were highly educated, with academic training in engineering and science. Other significant characteristics included relatively short organizational tenure and higher team tenure.

Thomas and Ramaswamy (1991) offered additional support of the perspective that a 'fit' should exist between managerial characteristics and strategic orientation. Their empirical analysis suggests that firms with appropriate matches between managerial characteristics and strategies achieve higher performance than those firms with inappropriate matches.

As a result of this discussion, it is reasonable to assume that certain top management characteristics are related to the internal strategic decision of creating a CIO position on the top management team. Specifically, the age, organizational tenure and functional expertise of the CEO appear to capture a significant portion of theoretical relevance of the managerial characteristics perspective. Our emphasis then will be directed toward CEO characteristics, because the CEO is, probably, the most important executive in the top management team for strategic decisions (Hitt & Tyler, 1991). Since creating a CIO position on the top level management team is considered to be a strategic choice, one might infer that those characteristics act as determinants.

## DEVELOPMENT OF HYPOTHESES

## Ownership structure

In Agency Theory, information is regarded as a commodity; It has a cost, and it can be purchased. This gives an important role to both formal and informal informational systems in organizational life. Jensen and Meckling (1976) explored the ownership structure of the corporation, including how equity ownership by managers aligns managers' interests with those of the owners. As managers' equity increases, they tend to exercise tighter managerial control over the operations of their corporations and tend to require more accurate information about the various operations of their companies. Hence, an information agent position on the top management team becomes necessary. This in turn guides us to postulate that managers who have substantial equity positions within their firms (outcome-based contracts) are more likely to appoint a CIO on the top level executive management team. As a consequence, the first hypothesis is formulated:

Hypothesis 1. There is a positive relationship between equity ownership by managers and the creation of a CIO position on the top level management team.

## Composition of the board

Another particularly relevant information control system for monitoring executive behaviour is the Board of Directors. From an Agency perspective, boards can be used as monitoring devices for shareholders' interests (Fama and Jensen, 1983). Kosnik (1987) examined the board of directors as an informational mechanism for managerial opportunism. She studied 110 large U.S. corporations that were greenmail targets between 1979 and 1983. Using both Hegemony and Agency Theories, she related board characteristics to whether greenmail was actually paid.

According to Agency Theory, the primary role of boards is to monitor actions of agents (executives) to ensure their efficiency and to protect principals' (owners) interests. Within this context, boards perform the critical function of monitoring and rewarding top executives to ensure maximization of shareholders' wealth. In essence, the board is seen as the ultimate mechanism of corporate control (Zahra and Pearce, 1989). Following this approach, board contribution to organizational performance occurs by reducing agency cost arising from non-compliance of executives with established goals and procedures; by articulating shareholders' objectives and focusing the attention of key executives on company performance; and finally through strategic decision making and control (Mizruchi, 1983).

The importance of board composition is prominently recognized in Agency Theory. This importance stems from the fact that outside members are considered to be independent of managers and will actively monitor and evaluate managerial activity for the benefit of the stockholder. Although Baysinger and Hoskisson (1990) argue that information requirements and information processing abilities of outside directors may limit their effectiveness, from an agency perspective, outside board members are generally expected to promote those activities that benefit the owners. In addition, composition of the board reflects the extent to which management dominate. This is often judged by the ratio of outside to inside directors. The appointment of outside directors is important in achieving their responsibility in overseeing the performance of the CEO and salaried management teams. Outside directors may represent the traditional interests of stockholders by monitoring the overall effectiveness of management, rather than giving routine approval of management programmes. Outside directors, then, are considered essential for ensuring an effective system of checks and balances in the corporation. Thus, the board is perceived as an agent of control and the more efficient, accurate, and timely information it has, the better it is for members to make sound, rational, and informed decisions. Consequently, as the ratio of outside directors increases, CEO domination of the board becomes more difficult and directors are in a better position to exercise more control, i.e. managers will be squeezed for more timely and accurate information. It is argued here that as the ratio of outside directors increases, the need for a better and more sophisticated informational system will increase. The reason for that is two-fold. Firstly, since there is a high number of outside directors not involved in the internal and routine operations of the firm, these have to be informed in a timely fashion to assist them in making rational, informed decisions; hence the need for an informational agent — the CIO — on the top management team. The second reason is that inside directors, who are mainly officers of the company, would benefit from the presence of such an informational agent, who will be regarded by them as an ‘assurance factor’ in dealing with outside board members. Based on the foregoing analysis, the second hypothesis is postulated:

Hypothesis 2. There is a positive relationship between the ratio of outside directors and the creation of a CIO position on the top management team.

## Size of the corporation

One of the principal assumptions of Agency Theory is that the various parties are rational economic individuals who act in their own self interest. A conflict will occur between agents and principals because of individual self-interest. Agents (managers), for instance, attempt to maximize their own interests at the expense of principals' (equity holders and debt holders) interests. Consequently, 'agency costs' are incurred. There are two important components of agency costs — the agency cost of equity and the agency cost of monitoring and bonding (Morris, 1987). The agency cost of equity is the decline of a firm's value when managers do not pursue the interests of shareholders. The agency cost of monitoring and bonding managers is the cost of control devices such as 'accounting reports, the incorporation of restrictive covenants in debt contracts, the establishment of management bonus plans related to reported profits, and the establishment of sophisticated information systems' (Morris, 1987). With more investment in information technology, the need for a top level executive to manage those investments would increase, as, consequently, would the necessity to create a CIO position on the top level management team.

Information technology and its management is thought of as a monitoring device employed to decrease agency cost. The question that raises itself is why are managers willing to expend resources (spend a fairly respectable portion of their budgets on the acquisition and maintenance of information technology and pay a high salary for an executive CIO) in order to decrease agency cost? According to Fama (1980), the welfare of the individual in the firm (manager, shareholder, or bondholder) depends on the survival of the firm. Therefore, reducing conflict among the members of the firm might result in an increase in the welfare of some without a decrease in the welfare in any of the others. Managers of corporations recognize that 'the managerial labour market uses the performance of the firm to determine each manager's outside opportunity wage' (Fama, 1980, p. 293). Manne (1965) examined the market of corporate control. He acknowledges that with diffuse security ownership, management and risk bearing are mutually separate functions, and that disciplining management is an 'entrepreneurial job' which in the first instance falls on the firm's organizers, i.e. the Board of Directors.

Conflict between managers and shareholders tends to occur more often in large firms. Since the ownership of larger firms is generally more diversified than those of relatively smaller firms, this raises the likelihood of more conflicts among managers and shareholders. In this situation, owners will push the managers for more timely, accurate, and reliable information about the firm's activities. Consequently, the need for better information systems increases and the designation of a CIO as part of the top management team would be looked at favourably by both management and the various shareholders. To the shareholders, even though the CIO is a member of management, he is thought of as an agent that increases the credibility of other managers, and increases shareholders' confidence in the information released. To summarize, then, as the size of the company increases, a more diversified ownership is expected. Consequently more conflicts will arise between principals and agents; this leads, in turn, to agents requesting more accurate, timely, and credible information, which induces the creation of a CIO position on the top level executive team. This is formulated in a hypothesis format as follows:

Hypothesis 3. There is a positive relationship between the size of the firm and the creation of a CIO position on the top level management team.

## Technological level

Capital-intensive production techniques carry with them a more advanced technological level and, hence, the use of information systems or information technology for production, running, and operating the technology. As capital-intensity of the corporation increases, investment in information technology will increase, and this, consequently, will increase the need for a CIO on the top level management team. In general, the ratio of not fixed assets to number of employees is used to measure the degree of capital-intensity. The lower the ratio of fixed assets to the number of employees, the lower the capital-intensity of the firm and, consequently, the smaller the likelihood of creating a CIO position on the top level executive management team.

Companies within certain industries may obtain a substantial portion of their fixed assets through leasing agreements. However, current standards promulgated by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) require presentation of fixed assets acquired with capital leases on the balance sheet. Consequently, such assets will be included in the computation of fixed assets. Based on the above discussion, the following hypothesis is formulated:

Hypothesis 4. There is a positive relationship between capital-intensity and the creation of a CIO position on the top level executive management team.

## Age of the CEO

The age of the CEO is expected to have an impact on the types of strategies that the firm will pursue (Hitt & Tyler, 1991). Older managers are expected to be more risk-averse than younger managers. Thus, it is expected that older managers will resist major organizational changes and attempt to maintain the status quo. Bantal and Jackson (1989), in a study of banking innovation, indicate that the average age of top management team and innovation are relatively related. It is expected that the age of the CEO is related to managerial and personnel restructuring in a similar manner. Since older managers are expected to be more conservative and less willing to pursue new strategies, it is expected that they will resist any change in the structure and composition of the top management team.

Hypothesis 5. The creation of a CIO position on the top level management team is negatively related to the age of the firm's CEO.

## Organizational tenure

The association between organizational tenure and composition and structure change of top management teams is similar to the relationship previously described regarding CEO age and change. The desire to maintain the status quo is expected to prevent any change in the structure and/or composition of top management. The contention to preserve the status quo stems from the fact that senior managers gain firm-specific functional knowledge and experience as their organizational tenure increases. Singh & Harianto (1989) have shown that managers who have high levels of specific skills would have fewer employment opportunities than managers with more general or universal management skills, and hence they tend to be more conservative in their strategic decisions than managers with less organizational tenure. Other researchers have argued that organizational tenure is associated with a higher commitment to the status quo in an organization, which implies that long tenured managers are less willing to introduce any changes in top level management; hence, they will be less willing to support the idea of creating a CIO position on the top level management team.

Hypothesis 6. The creation of a CIO position on the top level management team is negatively related to the organizational tenure of the firm's CEO.

## Experience of the CEO

Functional experience of the CEO is somehow related in a similar way to that of organizational tenure. A manager with functional experience in the core industry will have greater familiarity with the activities and technologies which are relevant to the industry. As core functional skills of the CEO increase, the levels of uncertainties and changes, in both the micro and macro environments, are better anticipated and proactively prepared for. This suggests that managers and CEOs with core functional experience are proactive in maintaining the integrity and importance of existing managerial structures, and consequently may not feel the need, nor have the enthusiasm, to have a CIO acting as a control agent in the face of unforeseen changes and uncertainties.

Hypothesis 7. The creation of a top level CIO position is negatively related to the extent of the CEO's managerial experience with the firm.

## METHODOLOGY AND EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

An empirical examination is conducted to determine the significant organizational and managerial variables which are useful in explaining the phenomenon of creating a CIO position on the top management team. Specifically, an experiment is designed to test the seven hypotheses formulated in the previous section. A cross-sectional design is used to compare corporations who had a CIO on the top level management executive team in 1988 to similar corporations who did not have one for the same year.

## The sample

The sample of firms included in the experimental group in the present study were selected from the 1988 Fortune 500 industrial corporations which satisfied the following two criteria:

1 An examination of the top management executive team of the corporation revealed the existence of a CIO position on the top level executive management team.

2 The firm made the Fortune 500 list in the past three years (1986, 1987, and 1988).

This second criterion was deemed necessary to ensure that our sample is comprised of a set of stable fortune 500 industrial corporations, and to safeguard against periodic fluctuations. Applying this criterion to the list of 1988 Fortune 500 firms resulted in 326 stable companies. Employing the first criterion to this set of companies resulted in a sample of 108 corporations. In other words, our experimental group of corporations with a CIO top management position consists of 108 corporations (refer to Table 1).

The control group is composed of a set of 108 corporations from the population of 1988 Fortune 500 corporations which did not have a CIO on the top level executive management team. The matching process of the experimental corporations was based upon the satisfaction of the following criteria:

1 The company made the list of Fortune 500 industrial corporations in the past three years, i.e. the companies are selected from the stable set of the 326 companies identified above;

2 Comparable primary industry based upon Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes;

3 Comparable size based on the average of 1986, 1987, and 1988 revenue values.

These criteria were designed to identify the most compatible match of control subjects to experimental subjects.

## Data collection

Publicly available data were collected on the operational variables described above for the 108 experimental and 108 control corporations. Because of limitations imposed on us by data availability, 10 companies were eliminated from further analysis. This, however, will in no way affect the viability of the statistical tests, or the stability of the results, since the samples are large enough to be suitable representatives of the populations. All data were collected from the companies' proxies and annual reports. Where possible, these data were cross-validated from the proxies against information provided by the Standard and Poor's Industrial Guide and Moody's Industrial Manual. Data on the age of the CEO were obtained from companies' proxy statements; information on CEO tenure and CEO experience were obtained from companies' 10-K and proxy statements. The variable CEO tenure was measured as the number of years the CEO had been employed by the company. Because 10-Ks and proxy statements provide managerial experience information only for the 5 years prior to the reporting period, the variable

CEO experience was operationalized as a dichotomous variable, having a value of one if the CEO has been serving in a senior executive position in the same industry for 5 years, and a value of 0 otherwise.

## MODEL CONSTRUCTION AND STATISTICAL TESTING

The logistic regression (logit) model was selected because the dependent variable is discrete (binomial), and because logit analysis avoids some of the strong assumptions of other multivariate analysis techniques like discriminant analysis (e.g. the independent variables are normally distributed and equality of the variance-covariance matrix).

The functional equation was constructed as follows

Y = f (EQ, OB, RE, EM, GA, CI, AG, TN, EX) where Y, EQ, OB, RE, EM, GA, CI, AG, TN, and EX are the variables identified in Table 2.

Computing the Pearson Correlation Coefficients revealed that the three indicators of size (GA, RE, and EM) are highly multicollinear with each other and with other variables in the model. Severe multicollinearity in logit regression leads to overestimation of the results. Several remedies exist to correct the problem of multicollinearity, the best of which is to eliminate the variables that are highly multicollinear with each other. Two of the three highly multicollinear variables GA and EM were eliminated from the model. The choice of these two variables is based on the thorough investigation of the literature where most authors utilized revenues as a proxy for size. Multicollinearity diagnostics also revealed that the potential multicollinearity problems arising from the correlations between age of CEO, CEO tenure, and CEO experience are minimal; hence, the three variables were kept in the model. With gross assets (GA) and number of employees (EM) variables eliminated, a stepwise logit regression analysis was run on the remaining seven variables. Table 3 represents the results of the stepwise logit regression. This procedure selected four variables for inclusion in the model. The $\chi^{2}$ value for the overall model was 129.7 with 4 degrees of freedom (significant at the 0.0001 level). In other words, the model as a whole is statistically significant as indicated by the P-value (0.0001). These results indicate that hypotheses H01, H02, H05, and H07 are supported, with management ownership structure, board composition, age of the CEO and the experience of the CEO affecting the decision to create a CIO position on the top level executive management team. Specifically, as the level of management ownership increases, the likelihood of appointing a CIO on the top level executive management team will increase. In the same way, as the number of outside directors on the board increases so would the likelihood of hiring a CIO to manage IT resources. These two variables, which represent agency and governance factors, were found to have the hypothesized relationships with the creation of a CIO. Age of the CEO and experience of the CEO were found to be statistically significant. These two variables represent management characteristics factors. However, contrary to the hypothesized relationship, the results of the study indicate that the variable age of the CEO is positively related to the presence of a CIO on the top management team. From our analysis, the size of the corporation (as measured by revenues), capital intensity, and CEO tenure with the company had no significant direct effect on a top executive CIO presence. In other words, hypotheses H03, H04, and H06 were not supported by the analysis.

## DISCUSSION

The objective of this paper was to identify those management characteristics and company-specific factors that affect the creation of a CIO executive position on top level management team to manage information and information technology in an organization. It is argued that as managerial supervision decreases (less effective control) the need for a well-managed information system increases; hence the necessity of a top level executive position to manage this informational system and act as a liaison between the various informational departments and the CEO and other executives.

The statistical analysis identified two managerial characteristics and two company-specific governance characteristics that are significantly related to the creation of a CIO top executive position where the CIO acts as an 'information control agent'; those characteristics were empirically identified as CEO experience and CEO age, on the one hand, and management equity ownership and the composition of the board of directors, on the other. The findings support the first hypothesis that when management equity ownership is high, managers would, naturally, be more involved in the close monitoring of company's activities and the collection of information; hence, the need for an independent information 'agent' (CIO) increases. Empirically, a statistically significant positive relationship was found between the existence of a top level executive CIO and the level of management stock ownership. This was expected since large equity ownership by managers implies the existence of an effective governance system, and our study suggests that active changes in the composition and structure of top management does not occur primarily in response to weak and ineffective governance system.

The finding that companies with a relatively large number of outside directors tend to have a CIO in an executive position supports the premise of Agency Theory, i.e. that more outside board members means better attention to stockholders' interests. In order for outside board members to better serve their constituents, they need to have access to more timely information. As a result, they will push for a better management of information technology resources and, consequently, the creation of a CIO position on the top level executive management team. The other two hypotheses associated with company's governance and agency, namely hypotheses three and four, failed to receive support because the relevant variable, company size and capital intensity, were not significant predictors of the creation of a CIO position.

The study failed to support the hypothesis of agency cost as exemplified by the size of the corporation. According to the firm size hypothesis, as postulated in this paper, the larger the firm the greater the likelihood of hiring a CIO on the top management executive team. A possible explanation for this unexpected result is the choice of the experimental and control groups. As mentioned in the methodology section, the choice of the corporations was based on a matched-pair design and a matching variable was size, as measured by revenues. In other words, the impact, if any, of the size variable was neutralized through the sample selection process. Another possible reason is that the creation of a CIO position on the top level management team might be related to the structure and complexity of the organization more than its size. The more complex the corporation, the more the need for a high level information manager.

CEO age, CEO tenure and CEO experience, the three variables developed from Upper Echelon Theory, were also hypothesized to related to the creation of a CIO position on the top management team. Two of these variables, CEO age and CEO experience, were found to be statistically significant. CEO age was expected to have a negative direct effect. The results imply support for the statistical significance but not the direction of the hypothesized relationship; a positive direct effect is observed. Although the observed effect is contrary to the anticipated results, they are not totally inconsistent with the previously referenced value-maximizing behaviour. In order to maximize the value of their firms and their own portfolios, older managers may be willing to create a top management position for a CIO.

Support for the significance of CEO tenure as an important determinant of creating a top CIO position was not obtained from the statistical analysis. One explanation for the lack of significant results is that decisions in large corporations are not made by one individual, rather they are made by top management teams. Thus, characteristics of the CEO do not accurately reflect the characteristics of the top decision making team in very large corporations.

This research has isolated four variables which aid in the explanation of the phenomenon of a CIO creation on the top level executive management team. Two variables are related to agency and governance issues, management equity ownership and the composition of the board of directors; and two variables are related to top management characteristics, the age and experience of the CEO. Other variables were postulated to be strong candidates as potential determinants of a CIO top executive position, like company size which was used as a proxy for agency cost, but was not selected by the logit analysis model.

## Biography

Zeinab A. Karake is an associate professor of Management in the Department of Economics and Business, the Catholic University of America (CUA), in Washington, D.C. Prior to joining CUA, she was an Assistant Professor of Management Science in the School of Business Administration, and George Washington University.

Dr. Karake's research interests lie in the areas of information technology, organizational structure, control and governance of organizations, entrepreneurship and intrapreneurship.

She is the author of 18 scholarly published articles, four monographs, and two books. Her book, Technology and Developing Economies: Eastern Europe versus Western Technology Transfer, was published in 1990, and Information Technology and Management Control was published in 1992. Both books received excellent reviews by both researchers and scholars.

In addition to her work as a professor, Dr. Karake has developed and delivered a number of seminars and training programs for mid- and high level American and international managers. She has served as a consultant and resource specialist for a number of national and international companies and institutions.

Dr. Karake holds a Ph.D in Decision Information Systems and International Business Finance from George Washington University (Washington, DC), 1987; an M.A. in Quantitative Management and Economics from Catholic University (Washington, DC), 1982; and a B.A. in Marketing and Research from the College of Business, Beirut, 1978.
