# Ethics and information technology use: a factor analysis of attitudes to computer use

Sue Conger, \*Karen D. Loch & †B. Loerine Helft

Management Information Systems Department, Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275 \*Department of Decision Sciences, College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 †Department of Statistics and Computer Information Systems, Baruch College, CUNY, 17 Lexington Avenue, New York, NY 10010

Abstract. In 1986 Richard O. Mason identified privacy, accuracy, property and access as four ethical issues for the information age. The research reported here sought to answer the questions: are these social issues empirically verifiable constructs? Second, what consensus exists on the factors?

A field survey of 79 business professionals and students identified 12 factors which were grouped into five clusters: ownership, access, motivation, responsibility and privacy. These constructs identify additional dimensions and complexity to extend Mason's definition of key ethical issues. The importance of separating the computer user who experiences the ethical dilemma from the stakeholder(s) who deal with the consequences of the dilemma is identified.

This study also demonstrates some consensus within the survey items. Consensus exists that it is unethical to profit from non-job, computer-related acts. Consensus also exists that personal use of company-owned information technology resources is acceptable. The other items show little consensus, identifying areas of necessary discussion within the computing professions to determine ethically consistent and appropriate computer uses.

Keywords: access, accuracy, computer ethics, ethical attitudes, ownership, privacy, stakeholders.

## INTRODUCTION

Ethics is that branch of philosophy that studies moral judgment and reasoning. Ethical dilemmas are situations in which a decision results in unpleasant consequences. The unpleasant consequences are treated as a zero-sum game in which someone always loses. From one perspective, the individual decision-maker is the loser; making the 'right' decision will cause extra work, discomfort, personal injury, loss of money or physical harm. From the other perspective, the company or some abstract entity — a stockholder, user, or society — might lose (Kahn, 1990; Moore, 1912).

Introducing computers into a situation makes the recognition of a potential loser more abstract and difficult to identify (Sproull & Kiesler, 1991). People interacting via computer seem to feel safe from surveillance and criticism, and lose touch with the work constituents who might otherwise be considered (Sproull & Kiesler, 1991). The computer mediates the human relationship which causes this lost sense of the psychological presence of others at the end of the computer connection (Culnan, 1993; Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994). This lost recognition implies that computer users might not recognize an ethical component because they have lost sight of others who should be considered.

In 1986, Richard O. Mason published an essay identifying privacy, accuracy, property and access (PAPA) as four ethical issues for the information age. He provided anecdotal examples for each issue by describing the injured party's perspective to irrefutably justify selection of each issue. While this article fostered a new branch of research in 'computer ethics', no published research to date has sought to validate Mason's proposed issues.

Two questions underlie this research. First, are Mason's social issues empirically verifiable constructs? Second, what consensus exists on the factors? While this research extends Mason's work, it is at the same time distinctly different. Mason's interest was in defining the issues and an agenda for dealing with them. Our focus is on individuals and attitudes about computer user behaviours. In particular, this study examines the attitudes from the computer user perspective because it is the computer user who experiences the ethical dilemma.

Computer users include analysts, designers and programmers who develop computer-based applications, and anyone who uses applications such as e-mail, group coordination software, word processing and so on in their work.

## BACKGROUND

Ethics relating to information technologies is a relatively new branch of a growing body of work on ethics relating to business. There are two basic bodies of research on ethics, the first relating to deontological or teleological ethical philosophical development, and the second relating to empirical research that seeks to explain (un)ethical behaviour (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994; Trevino & Weaver, 1993). This paper is an exploratory empirical study to define some of the key issues in the ethical use of computers. Ethics is not religion nor does it presuppose religious precepts. Ethics, as conceived here, defines higher order, universal reasoning that applies regardless of culture or nationality (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994). This survey sought to determine whether and the extent to which micro level norms exist about ethical computing behaviour (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994).

The four issues of interest in this research, PAPA, have been the focus of single-issue research (Cespedes & Smith, 1993; Culnan, 1993; DeJoie et al., 1991, Gotterbarn, 1992; Laudon, 1993). Privacy is a legal and social concept referring to the protection of personal effects and information. Privacy is defined as the individual control over disclosure and use of personal information (Culnan, 1993). Electronic eavesdropping, monitoring e-mail and selling electronic customer identification information might all be construed as transgressions against privacy (Miller, 1991; Sipior & Ward, 1994; Straub & Collins, 1990b). Legal liability for such transgressions varies by country (Milberg et al., 1994). The use of computers to screen job applicants from databases of arrested people or those having AIDS screening tests are documented transgressions against the privacy of people whose data is in the databases (Miller, 1991; Straub & Collins, 1990). Privacy issues in this research included database purchase and expert observation.

Information system accuracy is the completeness and correctness of data in a database, processing algorithms in a program, or work papers and documentation relating to an application development project. The extent to which accuracy relates to ethics is subject to discussion. One code of ethics specifies that an ethical software professional should only develop defect-free applications, a statement which implicitly includes all of the things above (ACM, 1992). Other discussions describe how inaccuracy is harmful, for instance, harmful inaccuracy includes incorrect reporting of credit histories, usage of data deliberately omitting the context or development of applications with inadequate controls (Mason, 1986; Parker, 1979; Straub & Collins, 1990). Questions included in this research related to the ethicality of inaccuracy in transaction and intelligent applications. In addition, we sought to identify responsibility for accuracy: the user, the project manager, individual programmers, or all of them or someone else.

According to Webster's Dictionary, property is something which a person has the exclusive right to possess, dispose of and use. Tangible computer-related property includes software, hardware, documentation, physical databases and program code. Intangible computer-related property includes design ideas and methods of application development.

Research relating to property deals with the incidence of software piracy of software products through illegal copying (Weisband & Reinig, 1994). The Software Publishers Association asserts that as much as 90% of software valued at over \$9 billion per year is stolen worldwide (Elmer-Dewitt, 1994). Piracy, in most of the world, has passed from the realm of the unethical into the realm of the illegal and therefore is not included in this research.

There are numerous ethical property issues beyond piracy for which there is no published research to date. Ownership of intangible work products, such as design ideas, is an issue that may be resolved legally before an ethical consensus is formed (Branscomb, 1985; Smith & Parr, 1993). Ownership of computer resources, especially spare computing time on personal computers, can be an ethical issue, depending on the context (Capen & Wondolowski, 1990; Hafner & Markoff, 1991).

Access is a property-related issue. In Webster's Dictionary, access is a means of approaching, entering, exiting or making a passage. Access is an ability, not a right. In the context of the ethical use of computers, unethical access is the unauthorized, unnecessary use of the 'access passage' to read, copy of otherwise make use of databases, programs or other computer resources.

Access research at present is prescriptive, identifying the need for access controls against unauthorized and unwanted use of the 'access passage' (Gotterbarn, 1992; Neumann, 1991). Telecommunications networks, especially Internet, facilitate unethical acts because many nodes do not have adequate controls and are highly vulnerable to access attacks (Hafner &

Markoff, 1991; Neumann, 1991). While there are published calls to action in establishing access controls, there is no published research about the ethicality of types of computer access (Neumann, 1991). Access issues appear to be similar to those of the other constructs: stakeholders, what can they gain and the circumstances of the access transgression. These issues were included in this research.

To summarize, Mason's four factors of privacy, accuracy, property and access generate numerous situations that pose ethical dilemmas for the participants. The issues relating to each factor were used to develop the research instrument and the general research questions discussed in the next section. The lack of theory on ethical computing, while a major shortcoming, is not a key issue in this research because the basic constructs of interest have yet to be identified in a systematic manner (Whetton, 1989). Identification of the basic attitude constructs relating to ethical use of information technology resources was the goal of this research.

## RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Two research questions guided this research. First, are Mason's PAPA issues empirically verifiable constructs? This question sought to determine other PAPA-related issues such as who is involved, contextual variants and motivations. Mason argues the importance of the constructs from the perspective of those who suffer from unethical acts of others. This research includes the stakeholder(s) as those who may suffer or benefit by unethical computer use, but the main focus is on the computer user who actually experiences the ethical dilemma.

The second research question assessed the extent of consensus on ethical behaviour. This was a secondary question although its answer is important to theory development, since theory must be based on stable constructs (Whetton, 1989). Consensus does not mean complete agreement on an issue. Rather, some range of (un)acceptable behaviours should be identifiable by having normally distributed responses which should be related to some universal tenet (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994), and using the area representing plus/minus one standard deviation from the mean as that range.

A consensus might arise that identifies some ethically indefensible behaviour. These become issues for which discussion is needed to change current thinking and develop a socially appropriate ethical consensus. Constructs might also demonstrate a lack of consensus. For these, we hope to initiate a dialogue among computing professionals to discuss the issues and begin to develop an ethical consensus (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994; Randall & Gibson, 1989).

## METHODOLOGY

This study used a survey developed by the authors to identify ethical attitudes relating to computer use. The scenario technique was selected as useful in eliciting attitudes by personalizing the issues (Couger, 1989). The authors developed new scenarios after examination of several other surveys were found to have multiple issues or ambiguity in single scenarios (Capen & Wondolowski, 1990; Parker, 1979). Survey items were designed to respond to criticisms of past research (Bommer et al., 1987; Das, 1991). Instrument development, data collection and data analysis methods are detailed in this section.

## Instrument development

Instrument development was iterative. The survey was given to two pilot groups who commented on wording and survey content. Debriefing followed survey completion to review questions and further validate the instrument (Straub & Collins, 1990). The first questionnaire pilot resulted in a restructuring of several scenarios, deletion of two scenarios and addition of three scenarios. The restructuring was to ensure elicitation of attitude rather than intent (Azjen & Fishbein, 1980). The second pilot also included a debriefing on the study and survey instrument.

The second pilot test resulted in the addition of the following sentence to every scenario asking about a company situation: 'The company has no stated policy about this issue.' This sentence served several purposes: it reduced tendencies to respond based on respondent work context, to assume a code of ethics or to interpret statements as relating to potential rewards or punishments (Josephson Institute, 1992).

The final questionnaire contained 16 scenarios each asking the ethicality of one or more related computer uses (see Appendix). Table 1 classifies questionnaire items according to the expected PAPA characteristics. As the four columns relating to PAPA show, the questionnaire contained 25 property items, 11 access items, 8 privacy items and 7 accuracy items.

In responding to the questionnaire, respondents checked a box for the scale anchor they thought appropriate; anchors ranged from highly unethical to grey area to highly ethical. An option that the question was not an ethical issue was also presented. The responses were numerically coded for statistical analysis.

## Sample characteristics

The revised instrument was administered in Summer 1992, to graduate MBA/MS\* students in business policy classes in two urban universities. Participation was voluntary and completely anonymous with no reward for participation. A total of 83 questionnaires were returned of which 79 were complete and used in this analysis.

Although the sample was of graduate students, 35 (44%) worked full-time, 18 (23%) worked part-time, 10 (13%) were full-time students at the time of the survey; 16 (20%) did not respond to this question. All respondents had business work experience with an average of 7.5 years. Fifty respondents (67%) had over five years' work experience. These people, because they all had work experience, were believed to be representative of the general working population. The greater the respondent maturity and work experience, the greater the likelihood that respondents have experienced ethical dilemmas involving technology in the work place (Josephson Institute, 1992).

Thirty-five respondents (44%) classified themselves as professionals, 11 (14%) were middle managers, 10 (13%) were full-time students and the remaining 13 (16%) divided between first-line managers, executives, clerks and 'other'; 10 (13%) gave no answer. Approximately 24 (30%) respondents worked in MIS jobs, 21 (26%) in management, 15 (19%) in accounting, 12 (15%) in finance, 8 (10%) in 'other' jobs. Computer use experience averaged 7.2 years with 30 respondents having ten or more years of experience.

The sample was heterogeneous, representing a cross-section of nationalities (10 countries), business industries (12 industries) and majors. All seven business-school majors were represented. Fifty-three per cent of the participants were male. The average age of the group was 31.5 years, while the range for the entire sample was 21 to 56 years.

## Statistical methods

The statistical methods used in analysis included t-tests, correlations, factor analysis and Cronbach's alpha. The t-tests were used to perform pairwise comparison between the two university groups. There were no significant differences in mean responses between university groups, or between full-time versus other respondents. Therefore responses were aggregated for the remaining analysis.

Factor analysis was the principal method of analysis used to identify ethical attitudes. The typical cut-off of 0.40 was used for factor loading on an item to be accepted (Nunnally, 1970). Four items loaded on more than one factor; if further statistical analysis were being performed, the items would be assigned to their highest loading factor. In the tables presented in the next section, they are shown with both factors as a caution to future researchers who may want to use the items. Cronbach's alpha was used to confirm the strength of the intra-factor relationships (Nunnally, 1970). These multiple goodness-of-fit tests, including scree plots, Tucker-Lewis reliability coefficient and chi-square, were all consistent with the traditional 1.0 mineigen value criterion for selecting factors for analysis (Bollen, 1989; SAS Institute, 1988). Low inter-factor correlations provided evidence of factor orthogonality.

## Limitations

There are two major limitations to this research. The sample size of 79 is small for the number of factors. In particular, the four factors with Cronbach's reliability scores under 0.70 (Factors 9–12) are highlighted for cautious interpretation and further empirical verification. For that reason, the weak factors are not discussed in the same detail as the other factors. Second, the scenario method, while used consistently for this type of research, is problematic. There is no camouflaging of research intention in ethicality scenarios so respondents might be inclined to give what they perceive to be desired answers. Even though several evaluations for internal and external validity were performed on the scenarios, interpretation across samples may not be consistent.

The solution to these problems is direct questions to measure the same constructs and a larger sample.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

There are three key findings of this research. First, the computer user and other stakeholder(s) must be considered when developing opinions about ethicality of behaviour. Second, 12 factors identified ethical issues relating to computer use, suggesting that subtle and complex issues underlie PAPA. Third, there was some consensus on the 'right way to act' in ethical situations relating to computer use. Items without consensus identify areas for discussion within our profession to develop boundaries for ethical computing actions (Donaldson & Dunfee, 1994). These findings are elaborated in this section.

The factor analysis identified 12 factors, explaining 84% of the total variance in survey item responses. The 12 factors were based on 16 scenarios comprised of the 51 items listed in Table 1. Inter-factor correlations were all under .30; none were significant. The first eight factors have acceptable Cronbach alpha reliability scores of 80% and higher (Nunnally, 1970). The last four factors are somewhat unstable and have weak inter-item correlation reliability as measured by Cronbach's alpha (Nunnally, 1970).

The 12 factors were grouped to simplify discussion. The groupings sought to match Mason's PAPA issues, but, in fact, identified five slightly different subjects: ownership (property), access, motivation, responsibility (accuracy) and privacy, respectively by the strength of the results (Mason's issues are in parentheses where they differ) (see Table 1). Each grouping is discussed in detail, identifying the contributing factors in order of their strength. The right-most five columns in Table 1 show items rated relative to the factor groups. Motivation is a new issue which accounts for all but one of the items which do not match Mason's PAPA issues; the motivation grouping supports Mason's arguments. Three items loaded on factors different from expected. Each is arguably in one or the other categories. For instance, 'help company use computers ... to replace people who are laid off,' which we felt was a privacy issue, loaded on a responsibility factor.

## Ownership

The ownership cluster is comprised of three factors: I did it, it's mine, ether ownership and intellectual ownership.

I did it, it's mine ownership. The first factor, I did it, it's mine, relates to the taking of application development work products as personal property and some use of those products by others. A total of nine items from two scenarios had factor loadings ranging from 0.41 to 0.84 (see Table 3). Cronbach's alpha for the factor was 0.90, indicating strong reliability to the factor (see Table 2). The factor explained $11\%$ of the total item variance. This result led to a change of Mason's 'property' notion to shift attention from property, a thing, to 'ownership', an attribute of people who own property. This shift is necessary because ownership is the ethically disputed issue.

Respondent attitudes on the I did it, it's mine factor are reflected by anecdotes that justify actions on the basis of personal ownership. One person said, 'If the company still has a copy, no one suffers, so it's OK.' Another person said, 'I did it, it's mine as much as the company's.' A third person, who worked for a company with strict ownership policies, said, 'I signed a release to my ideas when I went to work for this company. They own everything I do whether at work or not, while I'm employed there.' When asked how he felt about it, he answered, 'I go along on big things, but I keep little things, like programming techniques. Why should I have to reinvent the wheel?'

The results of the survey indicate some consensus among respondents on the unethicality of taking source code (56%) and application documentation (54%) and on the ethicality of taking work products for personal use with no financial gain (43%) (see Table 4). Twenty-three % of respondents felt taking user documentation home was unethical. The grey area accounted for 22–34% of responses across the nine items, indicating some ambiguity over the ethicality of taking work products.

Ether ownership. Factor 4, explaining 9% of item variance, refers to the notion that many company resources are on the ether and are public property to be used at will (see Table 3). Eight items from three scenarios loaded on this factor. Cronbach's alpha for the factor is 0.89. Items from the first scenario related to personal use of work resources that included paper clips, copy machines and several uses of computers, including 'playing', writing personal programs, using company data and personal e-mail. The loadings for these items ranged from 0.51 to 0.85 (see Table 3). The item from the second scenario, with a factor loading of 0.47, was about doing a sports club newsletter using company resources. The third scenario item, the ethicality of marketing software developed for a previous employer, loaded on this factor with 0.43 loading.

There was strong feeling among respondents that computer resources are on the 'ether', that is, they are intangible, and a 'use it or lose it' resource that is there for the taking. For instance, comments on doing sports club work included, 'It's my time, it's none of their [the company's] business.' When asked how the company might view this attitude, the response was 'unused computer time is lost and does not cost anything, therefore it is free for the taking.' Several people commented that if the company had no policy, 'they [managers in the company] do not care if people use the computer for personal work'. They agreed that this fact might be disputed by company executives.

## Motivation

The four factors relating to motivation identify the ethicality of actions depending on who benefits and who suffers from the computer act. Motivation extends Mason's stakeholder discussion to include beneficiaries of unethical acts, but is consistent with his examples of victims. The title motivation rather than stakeholders reflects scenarios projecting the respondent as application developer, or whistleblower or computer user who does something that affects or requires consideration of others. The shift to motivation is also appropriate because behaviour change must focus on the motivation of the computer user who experiences the dilemma. The factors include: Who suffers, which identifies classes of victims; Personal motivation, which relates to rationales for creating computer viruses; Who benefits financially, which relates to companies and individuals and the resulting financial outcome; and Friends benefit, which relates to using company IT resources for a friend.

Who suffers? The five items on this factor were all from the same scenario which describes the ethicality of whistleblowing when there was no penalty to the whistleblower. This was the third strongest factor with loadings ranging from 0.79 to 0.94 (see Table 3). Who suffers explained 9% of the total item variance; Cronbach's alpha was 0.93.

Insight into motivation for ethical behaviour is valuable for management if they wish to influence the ethical practices of their employees.

## Privacy

Two factors in this group were: computer exploitation and personal privacy.

Computer exploitation. The computer exploitation factor represents three items from three scenarios dealing with different aspects of intellectual ownership. The loadings ranged from 0.48 to 0.82, and the Cronbach's alpha is 0.52 (see Table 3). The items included purchase of information with subject permission, expert observation that was unknown to the expert and marketing software developed for the previous employer. Experts and previous employers respectively were exploited. This factor must be interpreted cautiously because of the very low reliability measure.

Personal privacy. Personal privacy was the ninth factor in loading strength and the only one in this category, with two items from the same scenario about company monitoring of policy dictating business-only use of phone, e-mail, word processing and software. The factor loadings were 0.78 and 0.79, but the Cronbach's alpha was 0.59, indicating weak reliability (see Table 3).

Four % of the total item variance was explained by the factor. This factor casts a selfish perspective on the privacy issues raised by Mason.

Company monitoring, when known by the individual, was clearly an issue that had no consensus as indicated by the 33% unethical, 24% grey area and 37% ethical responses. The most consensus in the entire survey is that unknown company monitoring, even though a policy states the company position, was viewed as unethical, with 83% of respondents in this category.

Consensus does not necessarily indicate an attitude that is ethically consistent with current work mores or legal precedent. Especially for company monitoring, the scenario specifically stated that 'Your company has a policy against personal use of company resources including telephones, computers.' At present, ethicality of monitoring is a grey area in business that is being contested in both the workplace and the courts (Sipior & Ward, 1994). In the US, for instance, all states, except California, currently condone such monitoring (Oz, 1994).

## SUMMARY

This research presented the results of an exploratory survey that sought to determine the issues of interest in ethics relating to the use of information technologies. Three important findings emerge from this study. First, both computer users and other stakeholder(s) in computer use are important research issues. The computer user is important because the computer user actually experiences the ethical dilemma and is forced to make a decision. If the goal is to obtain ethical behaviour, then understanding of computer users and their attitudes is needed. This understanding can then serve as the basis for development of policies and reward and punishment structures to foster ethical use of IT resources.

The motivation group of factors all identify different types of stakeholders. Stakeholders other than the computer user are important because someone may benefit or suffer from the actions of the computer user and because only studying the computer user may lead to selfish and ethically inappropriate decisions. The inability of computer users to identify with other potential stakeholder(s) highlights the need to raise the consciousness of computer users that other(s) may benefit or suffer from their actions.

The survey results showed that people were consistently egocentric and concerned for themselves. They were only tangentially concerned with, or even aware of, other stakeholders. This lack of awareness of the stakeholder appears to stem from the mediation of technology. The technology fosters a psychological separation of the computer user and stakeholder which may explain, in part, the increasing number of incidents of unethical computer usage, such as system break-ins and viruses.

Second, the 12 factors identified five broad areas for future research: ownership, access, motivation, responsibility and privacy. Mason's PAPA issues were validated by this research but found to be more complex then previously thought. Issues for future research include ownership of computing time, access paths, personal information and expertise; rights to privacy and accurate, timely applications; and stakeholder methods of identification, rights and responsibilities.

Finally, there is some consensus on 'correct' ethical actions. The need to develop consensus on all ethical issues is critical but will require extensive and candid dialogue among interested parties. In this case, the dialogue might include practitioners, academics, association members and other computing professions. The more public the debate, the more opinions might be heard and the more robust the resulting consensus.

From a practical aspect, companies wishing to discourage unethical computing behaviour must decide exactly what type of behaviours are acceptable and expected. They should develop policies for each desired behaviour to encourage compliance. Policies are necessary because, as the findings show, employees interpret a lack of policy as licence to do what they wish. By increasing employee awareness of company policy toward proper use of company resources, companies may reduce the incidence of unethical use of information and computer technologies.

Ethics is an issue for our times. As this research showed, the ethics of computer use is a complicated set of issues that need more investigation for better understanding of their complexities. As we aspire to theory development, the factors identified in this study need additional refinement and empirical testing. More research on ethical attitudes and their role in behaviours is warranted to place these attitudes within a theoretical framework.
