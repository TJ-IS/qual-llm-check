# The impact of EDI implementation commitment and implementation success on competitive advantage and firm performance

H. R. Rao, C. C. Pegels, A. F. Salam, K. T. Hwang & V. Seth

Department of Management Science and Systems, School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260

Abstract. Electronic Data Interchange Systems (EDI) are increasingly being used by business firms to improve operations and customer service. One of the major motivations for business organizations using EDI is to gain a strategic advantage in the marketplace. Although EDI has been implemented by many organizations, unfortunately not all have gained the same level of expected advantage or envisioned benefits.

In this study we focus on the impact of EDI implementation commitment and implementation success on competitive advantage and firm performance. We study two categories of companies: companies that initiate the development of EDI and are known as hub companies and those that are their non-hub counterparts. Findings indicate that non-hub firms may not reap the same level of expected benefits resulting from EDI technology adoption and implementation as hub firms.

Keywords: EDI, Inter-organizational systems, IS implementation, LISREL

## INTRODUCTION

Inter-organizational communication and information systems (IOCIS), also frequently referred to as inter-organizational systems (IOS), are beginning to play a vital role in changing industry structure, according to Porter and Millar (1985). A special class of IOS is the Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) system. According to Lagner (1990), about 17,000 US companies used EDI in 1990. The number of users of EDI is estimated to be growing at 40% annually. The automotive, grocery, transportation and chemical industries are extensive users of EDI systems and have even established their own industry-specific standards. Other industries, such as healthcare, insurance, telecommunications and electronics, are beginning to use EDI (Gardner, 1989; Skagen, 1989). While it has been predicted that the use of EDI will have a significant impact on the competitive advantage of organizations, very little is known about the successful deployment of such systems.

EDI systems have the potential to provide competitive advantage to the adopter. According to Hinge (1988), benefits can be direct or indirect. Direct benefits consist of reduced order cycle time, lower inventory costs, less labour and improved cash flow. Indirect benefits include improved customer service, more rapid response to suppliers and improved operating efficiency. Initiators are considered more likely to benefit than followers.

However, it is not clear how many of the benefits are captured by the adopting firms and how long these benefits can be effectively sustained. In addition, it is not yet clearly discernible whether some or all of the attained advantages have been competed away in the form of benefits to the customers, thereby transforming a competitive advantage into competitive necessity. Many strategic applications of information systems (IS), including ATMs and distribution systems for wholesale drug distribution, eventually became strategic necessities (Clemons, 1986; Banker and Kauffman, 1988; Clemons, 1989). Evidently, more research is needed to understand these complex issues.

In this study we shall focus on the impact of EDI implementation commitment and implementation success on competitive advantage and firms' performance. We study two categories of companies: companies that initiate the development of EDI and are known as hub companies and those that are their non-hub counterparts. Hub companies acquire, develop and undertake the implementation of EDI systems both within their own companies and with their trading partners. In this respect, the hub companies have to incur the initial costs of investing in the new technology, training the internal personnel, integrating the technology, transforming the business processes and marketing the system among its trading partners. Although this signifies a greater degree of risk on the part of the hub firms, this accumulated early experience sometimes provides these companies with the leverage needed to exploit the technology to their own advantage. Non-hub companies, on the other hand, are those that have not initiated an EDI system, but have become part of a system either out of proactive strategy or strategic necessity. In either case, these companies had to modify or transform part of their business processes to conform to the requirements of the new system initiated or developed by their trading partners. Findings indicate that non-hub firms may not reap the same level of expected benefits resulting from EDI technology adoption and implementation as hub firms.

## ELECTRONIC DATA INTERCHANGE SYSTEMS

EDI has been defined in numerous but similar ways in the literature: as intercompany, computer-to-computer exchange of business documents in standard formats; as computer-to-computer interchange of documents in a standard digital format; as corporate-to-corporate exchange of business information electronically between business parties in a structured format (Emmelhainz, 1986):

(1) Intercompany communication indicates that data transfer occurs between two separate companies and extensive cooperation between the two is a requirement to make the system effective. A company that initiates the development of an EDI system is known as a hub company.

(2) Standard format communication implies that information to be transmitted must be formatted according to a predefined layout so that a computer can process the information without human intervention. The electronic transmission of a document (purchase order, invoice, etc.) between the companies' computers is described by the term 'transaction set'. Each transaction is composed of a series of segments which are used to convey a logical grouping of information. To create an EDI transmission, the information from internal information systems is converted to the standard format and punctuated with control characters. Four types of EDI standards exist: proprietary, industry-specific, cross-industry and international (Hinge, 1988). A proprietary standard is set by one company which has enough leverage to ensure that all trading partner firms use its standard. An industry trade group sets an industry-specific standard in order to promote industry-wide electronic communication. Examples are the Uniform Communication Standard (UCS) in the grocery industry and the Transportation Data Coordinating Committee (TDCC) standard in transportation. These separate standards issues are now beginning to be resolved by a cross-industry standard, called X 12, which was developed by American National Standards Institute. At the international level, a standard called EDIFACT (EDI for Administration, Commerce and Trade) has been developed by the United Nations.

(3) Computer-to-computer communication refers to the fact that information flows directly from a hub company's application system to the trading partner's application system or vice versa without human intervention. For computer-to-computer transmission to be possible, a communication network must be established to interconnect both companies' computer systems. Three ways to develop an EDI network are: (1) establish a point-to-point connection between partners, both of whom accommodate the same communication protocols and standard formats; (2) establish a value added network that provides the electronic equivalent of mailbox service between partners, accommodating standard formats; and (3) use a third-party value added network that provides mailbox and translation facilities between different standards. Large EDI users are more likely to set up their own communications networks because of the sheer number of communication links involved (Belitsos, 1988). However, for nearly all but the very largest corporations, a third-party network is a more feasible solution (Stix, 1987). This is because point-to-point configurations require a great deal of coordination between the companies to establish communications protocols and translation procedures. The systems can become overly complex and require much technical expertise.

EDI systems can potentially have an impact on a firm's competitive advantage. Anecdotal literature has documented a large number of benefits accruing from EDI implementation: faster and more accurate data exchange; reduction in telephone communications; a shorter lead-time cycle that contributes to inventory reductions; elimination of redundant data entry between manufacturer/distributor/buyer; increased productivity through improvement of staff and space utilization; improved cash flow; fostering of partnership relations; and internal systems integration.

The accelerated growth of EDI can be attributed to three major developments:

1 Cost savings. When major retailers like K-Mart and Sears realized dramatic cost savings using EDI, it piqued the interest of other retail giants. Today many retailers are encouraging their suppliers to engage in the technology. The auto-manufacturing industry has also embraced EDI, exposing thousands of large and small suppliers to the technology.

2 The emergence of a national EDI standard. The American National Standards Institute chartered a committee known as ASC X12 to develop uniform standards for inter-industry electronic interchange of business transactions. The result was a migration from industry-and company-specific proprietary standards to a single standard for electronic interchange, greatly reducing the time and money spent on multiple custom software packages.

3 Off-the-shelf translation and communications packages. Several vendors have developed off-the-shelf EDI translation and communications packages based on the ASC X12 standard. The availability of these packages means that companies no longer need costly software technicians to maintain and enhance EDI software. Software companies that provide EDI packages include IBM, Sterling Software and Supply Technology. In addition, value-added network (VAN) providers have tailored their services for inter-industry ASC X12 communications.

## RESEARCH MODEL

Research relating to EDI systems is scarce. Even though practitioner articles abound, Wrigley (1991) points out that there have been only a handful of research articles in the area and there is a pressing need for more research activity, focused specifically on EDI. Benjamin et al. (1990) have relied on anecdotal information and on information based on a small number of case studies. Kekre and Mukhopadhyay (1991) carried out field studies of EDI in the manufacturing sector. Hanson and Hill (1989) based their research on a survey of a large number of US firms representing 12 standard industrial classification (SIC) codes. Their survey investigated the reasons for using EDI, the factors important for EDI use, and its effect on the control and audit environment. However, to our knowledge there has been no study specifically tying EDI technology implementation with competitive advantage and firms' performance. This study focuses on investigating the implementation characteristics of EDI and its impact on competitive advantage and performance in US manufacturing and service firms.

Past studies in information technology (Runge, 1985; Grover, 1990; Hwang, 1991) have recognized commitment and successful system implementation as factors that are important for a firm's competitive advantage and performance. We argue that these factors to some extent determine or influence the level of competitive advantage resulting from EDI applications that a firm may obtain.

## Implementation commitment

The management of the development of an EDI system is complicated by the inter-organizational nature of the implementation process. EDI being the 'technology of partnerships' superimposes the requirement of effectively involving key people from trading partner organizations in addition to the internal personnel of a hub company. The duality of managing internal personnel as well as fostering the development of effective cooperation among trading partners requires a high degree of implementation commitment on behalf of the hub company management.

Implementation commitment is contingent upon factors affecting successful partnerships, such as shared vision at the top management level, reciprocal skills in information technology, competence in telecommunications (Cash et al., 1992) and involvement in the implementation process. Each of these factors hinges on extensive involvement by internal users and trading partners (Hwang, 1991). Recent research (Runge, 1985; Grover, 1990) has found a positive relationship between the level of customer involvement and the rate of customer acceptance of inter-organizational systems. User involvement in the development of IS is one of the well-tested factors that affect the successful implementation of information systems (Baroudi et al., 1986).

Involvement of internal users and trading partners in system development. A company that initiates the development of an EDI system and prompts its trading partner firms to adopt the system is called a 'hub company' (Lagner, 1990). Hub companies are usually the initiators with a strategic vision to exploit the EDI technology to gain a competitive advantage in the marketplace. Some hub companies encourage their trading partners to participate voluntarily while others mandate them to use it. In the EDI context, users of the system are both the internal personnel of a hub organization and personnel from the hub's trading partners.

## Implementation success

The successful EDI system must not only be blended into internal business applications, it has also to be driven by the user community and must cross functional boundaries of the organization. Past studies (Hwang, 1991) conceptualized implementation success as consisting of factors such as the degree of acceptance of EDI by internal users and trading partners, the extent of usage of EDI and the degree of EDI integration with internal applications.

Degree of acceptance. From the perspective of the hub company the rate of trading partner acceptance (Runge, 1985) is an appropriate measure of system success. It is important to note that a decision by a hub company to invest in EDI does not imply that the trading partners have the same enthusiasm for EDI. For example, Hewlett Packard, a leading hub company in the electronics industry, found that even after seven years of EDI experience, only nine of its trading partners were active participants (Senn, 1992).

From the trading partner's perspective, the appropriate success measure is the acceptance of EDI by its own organization. EDI will be embraced by a trading partner's employees only when it makes it easier for people to do their jobs effectively. For instance, acceptance would result if EDI causes an increase in productivity, a decrease in the routine elements of jobs, or provides employees with the opportunity to use their skills for more creative decision-making tasks.

Extent of usage. Extent of usage is one of the classic measures of system success in the IS implementation literature (Ives & Olson, 1984). The greater the level of system use, the greater the information benefits (Danziger & Kraemer, 1985). This study builds on the extent of EDI usage as a measure of EDI implementation success. Since hub companies need to have a critical mass of trading partners using EDI, it can be argued that, from the perspective of the initiator, the extent of usage of EDI services by its trading partner is an appropriate measure of system success (Lagner, 1990). From the trading partner's perspective, an appropriate success measure is the degree of usage by its own organization.

Degree of integration with internal applications. In order to realize the potential savings from an EDI system, it is necessary that it should be integrated with existing internal application systems, including purchasing, order processing, financial management, manufacturing, transportation, etc. EDI has been found to yield benefits that justify its implementation costs when integrated with internal application systems. Otherwise, it is nothing more than an elaborate e-mail system between the buyer and the seller (Skagen, 1989). Therefore the degree of integration with internal systems is one construct for measuring implementation success. Other literature (Morell & Leemon, 1987) has pointed out that integrated systems result in better organizational performance.

## Competitive advantage

Previous studies have proposed various types of competitive advantage obtainable from the application of information technology (Porter, 1980; Porter & Millar, 1985). The following types of competitive advantage are considered in this study in relation to EDI implementation: comparative efficiency, relationship between trading partners, differentiation and firms' size (annual revenues).

Comparative efficiency. Comparative efficiency refers to whether a firm can produce the same goods or services at lower costs than its competitors, thereby increasing its profit margin. Comparative efficiency can be operationalized, based in Porter's (1985) value-chain activities. It is pertinent to note here that EDI will have an effect on comparative efficiency only to the extent that it supports strategic business objectives. For example, even if EDI results in the speeding up of information flow, and/or the automation of processes, unless the internal systems have been modified to incorporate the faster information transfer to improve the processes involved, EDI may turn out to be counterproductive or the expected benefits may not materialize.

Eight items were identified for customer-linked EDI systems and six items for supplier-linked systems. These included cost of order processing, purchasing, input material handling, sales force management, price quotation, inventory management, manufacturing and general administration.

Relationship with trading partners. One of the significant but intangible benefits of EDI is improved customer–supplier partnership and long-term procurement relationships founded on mutual commitment (Skagen, 1989). This will result in sharing of ideas and human resources. For example, customers may be asked to participate in product definition and testing, and suppliers may be asked to participate in the product design processes (Barber, 1989). Emmelhainz (1986) uses vendor relationship and buyer attitude as dependent variables in studying the impact of EDI on the purchasing process and notes the effect of improved vendor relationships.

Product/service differentiation refers to the value created by a firm through the uniqueness of its products or services as perceived by its customers, thereby increasing a firm's revenue through increased sales or charging a premium price (Neo, 1989). EDI can help a firm maintain its competitive position by differentiating the firm's product or service offerings through reduction of product cost or lead time as well as through the development of strategic partnerships (Skagen, 1989).

Firms' size. The size of the firm can be critical in information technology studies. Lehman's (1985) survey of MIS directors found that the larger the organization, the larger the MIS department, and larger organizations also employed more sophisticated information systems. Larger firms are more likely to have the capital to implement new technologies, but smaller firms may be more adept and effective in implementing new technologies. Size is measured by annual revenues.

## Firms' performance

Business firms continue to invest heavily in information systems. Naturally, it is of interest to everyone concerned how this investment in information technology (IT) relates to firms' performance. Since IT is a relatively new technology, comparatively little is known about its impact on contemporary organizations (Weill, 1992). Roach (1988) points out that there is very little convincing evidence that investments in IT generate positive financial returns. On the other hand, some studies have found a positive relationship between IT investment and firms' performance (Bender, 1986). Cron & Sobol (1983) concluded that organizations that made extensive use of computer technology were either very strong or very weak in terms of financial performance. Weill (1992) concluded that 'a clear picture of the relationship between IT investment and firm performance has not emerged from the previous studies.' Markus & Robey (1988) provided several possible explanations, including variation in the units of analysis, use of different base paradigms and conflicting or unclear definitions of IT.

In this study firms' performance is measured by the size of the firm (same variable as defined under competitive advantage), return on assets (ROA) and long-term debt to equity (LTDE).

Long term debt to equity measures the financial leverage of the firm, but indirectly it is also a good measure of risk. Highly leveraged firms are much more risky for an investor due to the increased proportion of debt in the capital structure. This elevated riskiness of the firm tends to inflate the cost of capital for the firm, and the firm may eventually have less capital available for technology improvements.

Return on assets. Managers often measure the performance of a firm by the ratio of income to total assets. This ratio reflects differences in financial leverage as well as in the operating performance of firms (Brealey & Myers, 1991). It is used here in this study.

Based on the above discussion, we develop an a priori research model as presented in Figure 1. Two latent exogenous constructs, EDI implementation commitment and implementation success, affect the two latent endogenous constructs, competitive advantage and firm performance. Implementation commitment is measured by the manifest variable extent of involvement (EOI). Implementation success is measured by the three variables: degree of acceptance (DOA), extent of usage (EOU) and degree of integration (DOI). The endogenous construct competitive advantage is measured by the variables comparative efficiency (CE), differentiation (DIF), relationship with trading partners (RTP) and firms' size-(SIZE), while firms' performance is measured by size (SIZE), return on assets (ROA) and long-term debt to equity (LTDE). Structural equations analysis was performed using USREL 7 to understand the hypothesized relationships between the exogenous and endogenous variables represented by the model.

## RESULTS

Data for the research was obtained primarily through a mail survey (Hwang, 1991). The study then focused on firms for which financial and operational data were available in the Compustat database. The final sample size consisted of 65 firms. The intent of the survey was to obtain information about general characteristics of EDI systems and the respondents' organization, therefore target respondents of the questionnaire had to have detailed information about the EDI system as well as general business knowledge. The respondents were people in the highest managerial positions, who had been involved in the implementation and were responsible for managing the EDI system.

The sample represented a wide variety of industries with about 70% in manufacturing industries and the remainder in service industries. Companies in industries known to be well established in EDI utilization, such as those in the automotive, grocery, transportation and chemical industries, comprise about 40% of the sample. About 10% of the sample are from industries beginning to use EDI, such as telecommunications and electronics. A discussion of the findings is given:

## Hub firms only

Only the hub firms were selected and the a priori model was applied to test the fit of the model with the data (see Figure 2). Since there is no single recommended measure of model fit, a variety of measures were obtained from the literature (Bentler & Bonett, 1980; Hayduk, 1987; Joreskog & Sorbom, 1989). Chi-square analysis of the hub firms indicates a good fit of the model with the data (chi-square = 31.64, degrees of freedom = 32, p = 0.485). Several researchers have suggested that instead of regarding $\chi^2$ as a test statistic, one should regard it as a measure of goodness (or badness) of model fit in the sense that large $\chi^2$ values correspond to bad fit and small ones to good fit (Dillon & Goldstein, 1984). Another measure of model fit is obtained by dividing the $\chi^2$ value by the degrees of freedom. For hub firms, the value of chi-square divided by degrees of freedom is approximately 0.988 which is below the conservative cut-off point of 2 or 3 suggested by Carmines & McIver (1981), and well below the cut-off point of 5 suggested by Wheaton et al. (1977) as indicative of model fit. The goodness of fit index is 0.860 and the adjusted goodness of fit index is 0.759, indicating a reasonable fit. The root mean square residual (0.113) and the largest standardized residual (1.911) are at acceptable levels (Joreskog & Sorbom, 1989; Hayduk, 1987). All of these measures taken together indicate a reasonable fit for the a priori model with the hub firm data. The total coefficient of determination for Y and X variables are 1.000 and 0.914 respectively, provided further evidence of the reliability of the measurement model.

## All firms

The research model was then applied to all the firms regardless of hub or non-hub status (see Figure 4). The purpose was (1) to determine the strength of the model and (2) to determine whether any other significant paths might be identified. The chi-square value for this data set is 54.52 with 32 degrees of freedom (p=0.008). The chi-square value divided by the degrees of freedom is 1.70, which is well within the recommended conservative values in the literature (Carmine & McIver, 1981; Hayduk, 1987). The goodness of fit index is 0.865 and the adjusted goodness of fit index is 0.767. The root mean square residual (0.100) and the largest standardized residual (2.56) are within acceptable levels (Joreskog & Sorbom, 1989; Hayduk, 1987). Together, these measures indicate a good fit of the model with the data. The total coefficient of determination for Y variables is 1.000 and for the X variables is 0.985, providing further evidence of the overall reliability of the measurement model.

An examination of the path coefficients indicates, similar in the case of hub and non-hub firms, that implementation commitment is the major determinant of competitive advantage ( $\Gamma_{1,1}=0.437$ , t=2.748; $\Gamma_{1,2}=0.627$ , t=1.476). However, the paths represented by implementation commitment and implementation success to firms' performance, and implementation success to competitive advantage are not significant.

The total coefficient of determination of structural equation is 0.866, indicating that the model explains approximately 86% of the variance in competitive advantage resulting from the adoption and use of EDI technology by all the firms.

However, the model fails to explain any significant variance in firm performance. This is not too unlikely, since numerous factors besides the use of EDI technology affect firm performance. The model also provides evidence of the fact that EDI technology, in general has benefited all firms regardless of hub or non-hub status, although not all firms have benefited equally.

## DISCUSSION

The application of the research model to hub firms only, non-hub firms only and all firms regardless of the hub or non-hub status, indicated that implementation commitment is the key determinant of competitive advantage resulting from the adoption and use of EDI technology. No significant relationship could be found between implementation success and competitive advantage, implementation success and firms' performance, and implementation commitment and firms' performance.

Implementation commitment determines the degree to which a firm is able to incorporate EDI within its own organization and/or influence participating firms through extensive involvement in the development process. Involving, educating and training internal personnel about EDI are prerequisites to effective assimilation of the technology in the fabric of the organization. The more users know about the technology, the more advantageous and sustainable benefits can be reaped by the organization. In addition, as EDI is known as 'the technology of partnerships', involving key people from trading partner organizations paves the way for effective incorporation of the system through mutually agreed standards and formats for document interchange. This helps a firm to develop long-term trading relationships based upon mutual commitment and trust. Thus, active involvement of a firm with a proactive strategy aimed at exploiting the EDI technology to its own advantage possibly provides the necessary means for attaining competitive advantage.

It is somewhat surprising that no significant relationship could be found between implementation success and competitive advantage. One possible reason may be that the sample size is not large. Although the paths between implementation success and competitive advantage, in all the cases, are not significant in terms of t-values, the hypothesized positive relationship is supported by the high positive values of path coefficients. The positive relationship indicates, in congruence with popular notions, that implementation success, as measured by the degree of acceptance, the degree of integration and the extent of usage, influences the competitive advantage resulting from the adoption and use of EDI technology. However, the present research failed to provide concrete evidence to support such a hypothesis. Further research is necessary to understand the nature of this relationship. The paths between implementation commitment and firms' performance, and implementation success and firms' performance are not significant. This is not surprising, as numerous factors beside EDI implementation commitment and implementation success affect firms' performance. Perhaps better measurement variables linking EDI investment and firms' performance are needed to effectively relate EDI implementation constructs with firms' performance.

The model had a good fit when applied to hub firms and to all firms regardless of the hub or non-hub status. The model explained approximately more than 91% of the variance in competitive advantage arising from the use of EDI technology by hub firms. This is in congruence with the generally accepted theory that EDI technology is beneficial to business firms, especially to hub firms, as it reduces operational and transactional costs as well as improving customer service and trading partner relationships through faster communication and reduced lead time.

The model had a moderate fit with the non-hub firms, indicating that the measurement variables and postulated relationships between the exogenous and the endogenous constructs do not hold as strongly for non-hub firms as for hub firms. The model explained approximately 82% of the variance in competitive advantage resulting from the use of EDI systems by non-hub firms compared to approximately 91% in the case of hub firms. This to some extent indicates that the use and application of the EDI technology do not benefit hub and non-hub firms equally. This shows that (1) some of the factors underlying the development and implementation of EDI systems are not the same for hub firms as for non-hub firms and (2) some of these factors affect non-hub firms adversely compared to hub firms.

Hub firms are considered to be more proactive than non-hub firms and therefore more involved and advanced in experimenting with the technology. This proactive strategic posture regarding EDI adoption helps a hub firm to learn, transfer and assimilate the technology within its own organization, and to develop early a strategic initiative to effectively incorporate its suppliers and customers within its EDI system. It also helps the hub firm to capture the advantages of an early mover and to avoid the costly penalties of having to adopt a technology solely due to strategic necessity. The latter situation is more pronounced for non-hub firms as they are sometimes forced to adopt EDI systems purely as strategic necessities to stay in business. This is in congruence with the findings in recent research regarding the advantages of EDI systems. Bergeron & Raymond (1992) found that organizations that implement EDI systems because they have to, not because they want to, obtain significantly less advantages overall. Benjamin et al. (1990) argue that the majority of the firms that adopt EDI systems as competitive necessities can derive cost savings from these systems only through 'the painful exercise of redesigning basic organizational structures and work processes'.

Clearly, in the case of EDI technology implementation, hub firms can be considered as innovators. Evidently, the primary motivation to implement EDI systems is to capture strategic advantage in the marketplace (Benjamin et al., 1990). Competitive advantage is usually defined as the ability to earn return on investment persistently above the average for the industry (Porter, 1985). Clemons & Row (1991) argue that 'the ability of any innovation to contribute toward competitive advantage depends on the innovator receiving a larger share of the economic benefits from the innovation than the competitors, or incurring a lower cost (investment) in implementing the innovation than competitors.' They further argue that competitors and potential entrants are motivated to imitate the innovation and thereby the whole process forces down the price of the new good or service. Eventually, a larger portion of the benefits of the innovation is competed away to the customers in the form of lower prices or better services at no additional cost (to the customers). The rapid adoption of ATMs among banking institutions is a case in point.

Reaping the major share of the economic benefits by the customers, in the case of EDI systems, is further aided by their structural position in the marketplace (Benjamin et al., 1990) and by the nature of the EDI system in reducing transaction costs. Buyers are able to reduce costs through higher levels of coordination using EDI systems to reduce inventory, improve materials handling and increase productivity by timely placement of orders to suppliers.

Benjamin et al. (1990) conclude that EDI applications, rather than being a competitive weapon, are increasingly becoming a necessary way of doing business. Clemons & Row (1991) explain this phenomenon as situations where competitors will not want to imitate, or will be at a disadvantage in imitating, but the impacts of the innovation would be so great that failure to duplicate them would threaten the viability of the competitors and the innovation would quickly become a strategic necessity.

Since EDI implementation process requires time and patience, firms with proactive strategy might be able to capture significant first-mover advantages. But to hold on to such advantages would require the continuous addition of new capabilities to the system, deriving cost savings from increased productivity and decreased overheads (Benjamin et al., 1990). In other words, the firm has to continually innovate to appear as a moving target to its competitors, if it is to sustain its competitive advantage through EDI application.

## CONCLUSION

This study used an a priori research model to test relationships between exogenous constructs of implementation commitment and implementation success for EDI systems, and endogenous constructs of competitive advantage and firm performance. The research model was applied to hub firms only, non-hub firms only and to all the firms regardless of the hub or non-hub status.

The purpose was (1) to determine the significant relationships between the exogenous and endogenous variables under various firm specific characteristics and (2) to determine how well the model performed and approximated to the real-world phenomena. The study found that implementation commitment is a key determinant of competitive advantage arising from EDI application. In addition, the model had a moderate fit with non-hub firms' data compared with hub firms' data, indicating that the hypothesized relationships and measurement variables may not hold as strongly for non-hub firms as for hub firms. The results indicate that non-hub firms may not reap the same level of advantage as hub firms.

However, it is to be noted that any generalizations based on this study should be made with caution because the sample size is not very large. The model also failed to account for any significant variation in firms' performance. Perhaps more measurement variables directly tying investments in EDI with firms' performance could be incorporated in future studies.

Additional research should be conducted to understand whether firms in the manufacturing and service sector differ in terms of EDI technology adoption and implementation, specifically, whether the existence of sector-or industry-specific conditions that might make the use and application of EDI more favourable or advantageous for firms in one sector or the other.
