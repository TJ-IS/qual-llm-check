# Methodology as metaphor: the practical basis for multiview methodology (a reply to M.C. Jackson)

Heather Watson & Trevor Wood-Harper

Department of Maths and Computer Science, University of Salford, Salford, M5 4WT, UK

Abstract. Multiview Methodology (MVM) combines a range of hard and soft approaches to developing information systems. We explain the practical basis for MVM's combination. This centres on the gap between theory and practice. This arises because methodology in use is metaphorical since its meaning is both (a) a practitioner's experience and (b) a property of a methodology. In practice, the meaning of a methodology is therefore not necessarily restricted to an espoused theory. Multiple perspectives make this explicit and help to create new interpretations.

Keywords: information systems, metaphor, Multiview Methodology

## INTRODUCTION

Practitioners face an increasing variety of methodologies. This suggests that no single approach is always appropriate. Like many of us, Jackson (1992) therefore favours methodological pluralism. As such, he criticizes Multiview Methodology (MVM) for prescribing a linear sequence to be followed in practice. Jackson refers to early work (e.g. Wood-Harper et al., 1985). However, we have since learned that conventional descriptions of methodology are misleading because they suggest a literal reading. Instead, we have found that practitioners carry out a series of iterations not shown in a methodology's theory (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1991). Now even in theory we do not necessarily advise proceeding through MVM in a sequential or a prescribed manner (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Bell & Wood-Harper, 1992).

Jackson also critiques MVM for combining a variety of hard and soft approaches. He explains that this 'distorts' both rationalities and that it 'does not rest upon a true complementarism at the level of theory' (Jackson, 1992). In making a case for methodological pluralism, we would like briefly to explain the practical basis for MVM's combination. In replying to Jackson, we are not using the term practical strictly in the context of Jurgen Habermas's (1972, 1974) social theory. This is an important matter, but in what follows we hope to address a wider audience who are concerned generally with the following question: why is there a gap between a methodology's theory and its practice?

Practitioners of different methodologies share this concern in having to put any theory into practice. It is, we imagine, also of interest to Jackson in arguing for the practical implications of Habermas's theory (e.g. Jackson, 1993).

## Wood-Harper and Fitzgerald's 1982 taxonomy reconsidered

As Jackson suggests, a self-conscious commitment to methodological pluralism would find taxonomies useful. Therefore, we return to Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald's (1982) taxonomy, since this informed MVM's combination of approaches.

The taxonomy describes the following range of six approaches: (1) General Systems Theory (GST), (2) Human Activity Systems (as in Soft Systems Methodology), (3) Participative (Socio-Technical), (4) Traditional (as in NCC), (5) Data Analysis and (6) Structured Systems (Functional). These are further classified into two paradigms: science and systems. The first is reductionistic and the second is holistic. There are two surprises here. The first is discussed in the taxonomy. The authors place traditional approaches to systems analysis in the science paradigm although they are usually thought to be based in the systems paradigm. They do this because the theory reflects the systems paradigm, but the practice reflects the science paradigm. In practice traditional approaches tend to be reductionistic because systems are broken into component subsystems, which are then optimized and implemented. However, such approaches do not often consider the 'overall' system as intended by the holistic theory of the systems paradigm.

A second surprise is also linked with practice. Here also the two paradigms criss-cross with objectives in use: in the systems paradigm GST shares a concern in achieving a better analysis of the situation, along with Data Analysis, and Structured Systems approaches in the science paradigm. Similarly, Traditional, Participative and Human Activity Systems are used for problem solving, yet in theory they also fall into different paradigms.

Both surprises have the following implication: the meaning of an approach is not necessarily restricted to or guaranteed by the espoused theory. This is not to defame an author's intentions. However, it may go some way towards explaining why we continue to have disagreements over the intended and the actual uses of a methodology (e.g. refer to Checkland, 1982, and Jackson, 1982, concerning Soft Systems Methodology). These arise because a methodology's meaning is always open to further interpretations, and these may exceed an author's original intentions (Watson & Wood-Harper, 1993). This has a potential implication for how practitioners use methodology: they understand methodology as metaphor. We explain this below.

## Methodology as metaphor: meaning is between experience and its description

We think metaphorically when we try to understand one aspect of experience in terms of another (Lakoff & Johnson, 1982). Thus, metaphor works through implicit or explicit statements such as (A) is or is like (B). Morgan (1980) emphasizes that the creative potential of metaphor also depends on a degree of difference between (A) and (B), and that the power of metaphor arises when differences are viewed as significant but not total. This is evidenced in the above taxonomy by the two different paradigms that sometimes overlap in practice.

The taxonomy indicates that finding similarity in difference goes a long way towards explaining what methodology is about. This hinges on what we take to be a methodology's meaning, and this also depends on a degree of difference between (A) and (B). To return to our question, we commonly speak of this difference between (A) and (B) as the gap between a methodology's theory and practice. In this way, a methodology's meaning in use is metaphorical since it is both

● (A) a practitioner's experience and

● (B) a property of a methodology.

Consider this quotation from Checkland & Scholes (1990) about SSM:

If a reader tells the author 'I have used your methodology and it works', the author will have to reply 'How do you know that better results might not have been obtained by an ad hoc approach?' If the assertion is: 'The methodology does not work', the author can reply, ungraciously but with logic, 'How do you know that the poor results were not due simply to your incompetence in using the methodology?'

When faced with this, it may be tempting to side against theory and against the authors by arguing that a practitioner's experience is the methodology's meaning. However, in choosing our own experience, we do not escape the author's situation. If we describe our own experience, we present other practitioners with a situation not of immediate understanding but of deferral. In perceiving this difference, they try to relate (A) their own experience with (B) our previous description. This has an implication for answering our question: it suggests that the gap between a methodology's theory and practice is a special case of a more general gap already admitted within practice. This is the gap or difference between experience and its description.

If we consider more closely what happens in an intervention, we soon realize that our experience is not taken literally merely as something given. Instead, it is actively created. In beginning an intervention we part-imagine the past in the same way as we subsequently part-imagine the real. So, for example, staging debate about change using SSM is part-imagined by comparing models with real situations (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). In this sense, our experience of using a methodology is metaphorical. We interpret a plurality of meaning as we try to understand a situation (A) using models (B).

Our practical experience may end with the production of a real model, a technical artefact, but this too is a description whose meaning is not taken literally as given. Instead, it is further part-imagined in relation to a real situation. So, if we continue to describe aspects of an information system in use, we reiterate our predicament with methodology. The meaning of using an information system is both (A) a user's experience and (B) a property of the information system. Rather than having one source, in practice meaning is divided or pluralized because it is somewhere between experience and its description. Checkland & Scholes (1990) reiterate this point: 'the inseparability of the methodology from the use made of it by a particular user in a particular context will always mean that SSM itself, as a description on paper according to the new Constitutive Rules, will forever remain undecidable.' This divided character of meaning marks the practical basis of MVM. In seeing methodology as metaphor we accept that a practitioner's experience potentially exceeds the stated rationale of any methodology. We try to make this plurality of meaning more explicit with multiple perspectives as explained below.

## Generative metaphor and multiple perspectives

Schon (1979) explains that problem settings are mediated by the stories people tell about 'what is wrong and what needs fixing'. How we frame problems in these stories depends on metaphors that generate how we set and solve problems. As such, the way we view a situation constrains how we will set a range of possible solutions. Failure to reflect on this can lead to the tacit use of metaphors that automatically 'generate' solutions. Schon argues that generative metaphors result in a kind of blinkering in which some matters are emphasized at the expense of other equally important aspects of a situation. Such metaphors become unquestioning in so far as they generate solutions that may seem obvious or natural.

Schon's main point is that an awareness of generative metaphors allows them to become interpretive tools for critical analysis. In identifying such metaphors we can sometimes question the assumptions that arise from them and consider how appropriate they are in a situation. Schon (1979) explains how the Japanese film Rashomon stimulates reflection by presenting several different stories about the 'same' situation. This focuses on the partial truths of metaphors, on the way they highlight certain aspects of a situation while playing down or even ignoring others. Having multiple perspectives in MVM's theory helps to emphasize that we are not dealing with one reality but with many ways of perceiving a situation. This reflects Schon's argument that an awareness of different perspectives often leads to conflict and sometimes to restructuring our perceptions of a situation.

Hence in MVM we have different paths through the methodology even at the level of theory. This helps to avoid there being an immediately obvious or 'natural' way to view a situation. What we hope to achieve with this structure is a methodology that creates rather than protects existing interpretations (Watson & Wood-Harper, 1994). From this premise an advantage arises in combining hard and soft approaches: this gives us a new perspective in which whatever appears obvious to either may suddenly be seen from an unaccustomed angle. In relation to methodological pluralism, one practical lesson we've learned with MVM is that the structure helps to produce a unique method for every project (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1991). This also supports Lyytinen's (1987) view that perceptions of a situation change throughout an intervention.

## An implication for debate

Flood & Jackson (1991) explain that different methodologies arise from alternative theoretical positions and that these alternative positions must be respected. However, in practice the metaphorical status of a methodology's meaning has a potentially interesting implication for debate; that is, one adopts at least some of the language and assumptions of a perspective to make arguments against it. Therefore, one may gradually assume many of its implications in developing an 'alternative' position (MacCormac, 1985). In practice this means that we may end up reiterating a 'mistake' we criticize in others. If we return to why we continue to have disagreements over the intended and actual use of methodologies, we see this pattern recurring. For example, Mingers (1992) critiques Jackson's (1990) use of a system of systems methodologies for contradicting its own premises since it depends on the assumptions of one paradigm — the unitary based on functionalism — and this assumes that problems can be defined correctly in one particular way. Mingers argues that the whole point of pluralist methodologies is that problems do not exist objectively. Instead, they are different people's perspectives. Mingers explains that Jackson now realizes this, and we suggest this as evidence that combining different perspectives can lead to increased critical awareness and to insightful questioning, not simply to 'distortion'.

If we consider that metaphorical truth is partial, then perhaps hard and soft views are already 'distortions' in so far as they do not represent total or complete understandings of a situation. In this sense, MVM is restricted as it combines only a range of hard and soft approaches. However, the practical basis of this multiple perspective structure shares a family resemblance to more recent developments such as Flood & Jackson's (1991) Total Systems Intervention. This combines a range of systems methodologies through the above system of systems methodologies. Additionally, a range of metaphors is used to highlight strengths and weaknesses through critical discussion and debate.

Such research is an important development, and we hope this short explanation takes metaphor to an even more fundamental level. Not only do we use different metaphors in information systems development, but the meaning of a methodology in use is itself metaphorical.

## CONCLUSION

Our commitment to methodological pluralism recognizes that meaning is not to be found entirely in what a theory explicitly asserts but rather in our own continual questioning of meanings that language forces us to compound. Perhaps the power of multiple perspective methodologies is that they sometimes disturb our relations with other approaches. In combining what we previously thought was so unlike, they force us to reflection. This phenomena is perhaps the emancipatory interest that Jackson proposes from Habermas's social theory. Similarly, that's why we understand methodology in use as metaphor, because it allows for the unexpected. Resulting debates may eventually lead to the breakdown of a structure, but this may render unanticipated insights.

To return to our question, perhaps there is a gap between theory and practice because, unlike theory, practical activity does not merely assert, it also creates.

Trevor Wood-Harper is a Professor of Computer Science and Information Systems. He is also Director of a multidisciplinary Information Systems Research Centre at Salford that consists of 12 members.
