# The fetish of technique: methodology as a social defence

David G. Wastell

Department of Computer Science, University of Manchester, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9PL. Tel: 0161-275 5722; Fax: 0161-275-6236, E-mail: was@cs.man.ac.uk

Abstract. Methodology is a central issue in the theory and practise of information systems development. Structured methods, for instance, have been widely championed as providing a way of improving the quality of software systems. In this paper a case study is presented in which a mail order company made an attempt to implement a well-known methodology, Structured Systems Analysis and Design Methodology (SSADM). Far from facilitating the development process, SSADM encouraged a rigid and mechanical approach in which the methodology was applied in a ritualistic way which inhibited creative thinking. The argument is thus, that methodology, although its influence may be benign, has the potential to operate as a 'social defence', i.e. as a set of organizational rituals with the primary function of containing anxiety. The grandiose illusion of an all-powerful method allows practitioners to deny their feelings of impotence in the face of the daunting technical and political challenges of systems development. By withdrawing into this fantasy world the learning processes that are critical to the success of systems development are jeopardized. Methodology, whilst masquerading as the epitome of rationality, may thus operate as an irrational ritual, the enactment of which provides designers with a feeling of security and efficiency at the expense of real engagement with the task at hand.

Keywords: Information Systems, methodology, organizational learning, social defence.

## A CAUTIONARY TALE

Acme Stores is a well-established 'homeshopping' company providing a mail order shopping service. It sells a wide range of household items, which are advertised in a glossy catalogue. Acme is an intensive user of information technology; IT systems underpin all areas of the business (stock management, distribution, marketing, sales order processing, credit control). These 'core systems' are absolutely critical to the efficient operation of the organization.

In the early 1990s, concern was growing within Acme's senior management with regard to the IS function. The function was centrally organized, employing around 60 staff in three groups (logistics, merchandising and customer services). There were a number of concerns within the company which strike a somewhat familiar chord. The growing size of the IS department was a major issue, coupled with general frustration at long lead times for new software developments. An internal study had indicated that over 80% of the IS 'effort' was devoted to maintaining the company's legacy systems. These had been originally developed in Cobol and ran on an Amdahl mainframe. The internal study also highlighted the lack of 'user orientation', the need to adopt a 'standard approach' and the poor documentation being produced as important issues.

In late 1990 a firm of consultants was engaged to help develop a new IS strategy. A key recommendation was that Acme Stores should adopt a structured methodology (and supporting CASE tools) to replace the current in-house 'methodology', which was basically a set of documentation standards. From a short list of methods, the decision was made to adopt the SSADM methodology (Downs, 1992; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995) on the grounds that it was a mature methodology and was the market-leader in the UK (Hares, 1990; Fitzgerald, 1994). It was also well-supported in terms of tools, training and consultancy.

In early 1991, the SSADM project was formally initiated. The plan was to carry out a pilot project in order to develop skills and to allow the effectiveness of the methodology to be evaluated. The development of a new parcel-tracking system was identified as a suitable pilot project and December 1991 was specified as the delivery date. An experienced SSADM practitioner was recruited and appointed as project leader along with several new analyst/programmers. The decision was made to train all the IS staff in SSADM and an intensive 2 week training course at a local consultancy was arranged. Following training, the pilot project got underway in March 1991. Two groups were involved in the project: a steering group (consisting of two IS managers and managers from the three user departments that had a stake in the parcel-tracking system) and a project team, which was responsible for the development work (this team was made up of analysts and of user representatives seconded to work on the project).

The early days of the project were characterized by a number of problems. For example, many of the original IS staff showed considerable diffidence with SSADM techniques (e.g. dataflow diagramming, Entity Relationship (ER) modelling). One symptom of this was excessive worrying about 'getting the notation right' and about the aesthetics of the diagrams (e.g. getting all the 'bubbles' lined up). The idea of 'logicalization' (i.e. inverting physical models to logical ones) was also troublesome to many developers. The CASE tool itself was complex, and learning to use it effectively required considerable effort and commitment.

As the project progressed, further problems developed. One of the claimed strengths of SSADM is its clear prescriptive structure, in which every step in the development process is precisely delineated. Many of the developers appeared to relish the rigour that such an explicit framework provided. However, it became increasingly apparent that the methodology was being followed in a blind, mechanical way and that as a result the project was becoming bogged down. Commenting retrospectively, one of the senior developers (MC) observed that:

People were investing dataflow diagrams with more formality than they justified ... they became more involved with following the methodology and lost sight of what the system was for — they become worried about what task to do next ... task 3.2.1, then 3.2.2 and so on.

With inexperienced people there was a tendency to over-rely on the structure, to let the method think for them ... people got bogged down ... they lacked the confidence to say 'look we're getting into too much detail here'.

Concerns on the user side were growing. SSADM places considerable emphasis on user involvement, but there was increasing evidence that communication between users and designers was becoming obstructed by the methodology. Users complained that they would be presented with large sets of dataflow diagrams, or other SSADM products, which they were asked to check and sign-off. Although these diagrams were intended to promote understanding between developers and users, users felt increasing frustration that they were becoming hopelessly embroiled in the details of the diagrams and were 'losing sight of the broader context'. They felt that they did not know or understand what they were signing off. One user observed that 'we did not know what we were doing or why we were doing it ... we felt ourselves becoming lost in the huge structure of the method'. MC commented:

SSADM is not a replacement for social skills ... but a lot of people wanted to avoid talking to users ... they would send the documentation through the internal post with a request such as 'check these please and tell me by Friday if there are any errors ...'. Who would do that? A lot of people did that though, they liked to rely on the procedure. It was a defensive attitude ... I'll send this out, get on with the next thing, let them see all the work I have been doing. Sign-off was a ritual ... users just did not understand all the documentation that we threw at them.

The disenchantment of the users and general dismay at the speed of progress surfaced at the steering group meeting in October 1991. There were calls at this meeting for the development process to be streamlined, for 'non-value adding activities to be eliminated' as one manager put it. The IS staff continued to insist on the importance of taking a rigorous approach. They argued that the slow progress was due to inexperience with the method and the CASE tool, compounded by growth in the breadth and depth of requirements. Moreover they argued that SSADM represented a new 'culture of quality'; that in the past 'people just jumped in and started coding as soon as possible'.

In June 1992, 6 months after the due delivery date, the project was nearing completion. Stages 4 and 5 (data and process design) of the methodology had been completed, i.e. a logical specification of the system had been produced and signed off. Acceptance testing of some components was underway although a full system test had not yet been carried out. At this point, a systematic review of SSADM was instigated.

The review highlighted a number of issues, some of which we have already touched on. The rigid bureaucratic structure of the methodology was identified as a major concern. The review called for a radical and critical re-evaluation of the methodology to look for ways to improve flexibility and extract the 'high value' aspects. There was also concern that too much time had been taken up in quality assurance. The review commented that:

A major problem was that SSADM produces mountains of paperwork for review. The pressure to sign things off was very strong and the time pressures often meant that products were not reviewed properly, although they were still signed off.... Users felt that sign-offs were sometimes held over them to block changes to their requirement.

The issue of user involvement was a major one. One of the arguments for adopting SSADM had been that the methodology promoted a more active role for users in system development. In the past, systems in Acme had been developed by the IS department with very little reference to users. MC commented that:

In the past, users got what they were given. They expected ISD (the IS department) to do the work, to go away and bring a system back ... they did not see systems development as part of their job. SSADM was an opportunity to break away from this passive role. Users were expected not just to be passive information givers. They were expected to play an active part in specifying the system and ensuring that what they got was what they wanted. SSADM was an opportunity to break from the constraints imposed by departmental boundaries.

In practice, this higher level of involvement had not occurred. Managers in the user departments had sat on the project steering group. However, in the words of the project leader (PG) 'The group was very passive. I just reported back progress. There was no substance to the meetings. No real decisions were taken ... it was basically just a talking shop'. By and large managers had distanced themselves from any serious managerial involvement in the project. Most departments had simply seconded someone to work full-time on the project. Once they were on the project, they were more or less a 'law unto themselves'. The project leader (PG) gave an example of this:

These users were dangerous. They were not really responsible to anyone. When we were doing the acceptance testing, we needed to get the users to supply test cases. However, the user representatives said that they were too busy with other things. We needed to approach their managers to get them to cooperate and of course these managers had no idea of what their people were supposed to be doing.

The review considered in some detail how greater involvement could be promoted. Largely, this discussion focused on the role and responsibilities of users (what tasks should users perform? what products should they see? what skills do they need to participate effectively?). Specifically, the report recommended that, in future, a senior user manager should formally coordinate the activity of users on project teams.

In the following year, two further SSADM projects were initiated which were partially successful, although in practice they paid lip service to the methodology; MC described them euphemistically as 'pseudo-SSADM' projects. There was evidence of growing opposition to the use of SSADM. Two departments (Marketing and Customer Services) declared that they were opposed to its further use. Ironically, nostalgia for the pre-SSADM method was also expressed, although the deficiencies of this method had been the spur to implementing SSADM. The view became widely accepted that SSADM was only appropriate for very large systems. In mid-1993, a second review of Acme's IS strategy was carried out and a new IS manager was appointed. The decision was made to abandon further internal development and to move to a strategy based on packages and the use of outside consultants. The main ground for this decision was that SSADM was too slow, that the business needed much quicker solutions.

## METHODOLOGY: THE LIONIZATION OF TECHNIQUE

It is the Age of Machinery; the age which forwards, teaches and practises the great art of adapting means to ends. Nothing is now done directly, or by hand; all is by rule and calculated contrivance. Our old modes of exertion are discredited, and thrown aside ... the living artisan is driven from his workshop to make room for a speedier inanimate one. Nothing is left to be accomplished by old natural methods. Everything has its cunningly devised implements, its pre-established apparatus (Thomas Carlyle, 'Signs of the times', Edinburgh Review, 1829).

There is great belief today in the power of methodology, especially in the area of information systems (IS). The development of software systems is a complex, sociotechnical process in which demanding technical challenges are confronted within a conflict-laden political context. Methodology has been hailed as the answer to the vicissitudes of the software process and as the solution to the 'software crisis' (Fitzgerald, 1994). For every aspect of IS development there lurks a 'cunningly devised implement' of some kind. For the engineering challenges of building a software system, there are structured methods (Gane & Sarson, 1979). For organizational issues, sociotechnical systems design is the solution (Mumford, 1983). For defining 'unstructured requirements', the elixir of soft systems methodology will provide the cure (Checkland, 1981). Carlyle, speaking over 150 years ago, lamented that even education was 'no longer an indefinable tentative process requiring a perpetual variation of means and ends, but a secure, universal, straightforward business, to be conducted by proper mechanism'. How well his words apply to information systems development today.

Many organizations have turned to a structured methodology when confronted by the sort of critical challenges faced by Acme of low software productivity, high maintenance costs and inflexible systems. Structured methodologies reflect a metaphor of the development process as a rational technical process; they embody a rigorous engineering approach to system development. Organizations such as Acme see them as a universal remedy, 'a magical wand that will solve all problems' as the project leader wryly commented. Yet, this belief in the magical qualities of methodology is a chimera. Real problems require a real engagement with the task; turning to methodology can be an elaborate way of avoiding problems not solving them.

Methodologies such as SSADM are claimed by their votaries to be highly successful. Hares (1990) writes:

SSADM is a structured design method with a seemingly Midas touch — it is proving to be astonishingly successful as regards its acceptance and use in the marketplace ... The merits of SSADM are now recognised internationally. Many governments have adopted SSADM. SSADM is a hit and worthily so.

Yet where is the evidence that methodologies are indeed efficacious? In contrast to the scientific rhetoric of the methods themselves, there is an astonishing paucity of empirical evidence that structured methods (or any IS methodologies for that matter) are any use at all (Wynekoop & Russo, 1993). The methodology literature is a depressing sight for the empirical scientist. By and large it is full of lengthy descriptions of methods by votaries, with carefully elaborated frameworks for comparing methodologies, and with tortuous theoretical deliberations. Claims for the efficacy of methodologies are typically based on scant critical evidence of whether and how these methods work in practice; they seem to have more of the qualities of religious convictions than scientific truths. The little evidence that exists, for instance, on the efficacy of structured methods provides no real support for their effectiveness. An early interview study with project managers by Jenkins et al. (1984) reports favourable attitudes, but this is hardly solid evidence. More recently, Cerveny & Joseph (1988) found that structured methods enjoyed more, not less, maintenance effort than an ad hoc approach. Banker, Datar & Kemerer (1991) also report a similar result in their survey. A recent case study by Dekleva (1992) showed no direct effects on maintenance.

Respected commentators such as Boehm (1981) and DeMarco (DeMarco & Lister, 1987) have pointed out that methodology is less important than the abilities, inventiveness and determination of developers. DeMarco & Lister (1987) have noted that detailed methodologies stifle creativity and reduce, rather than increase, productivity. SSADM, for instance, is perceived by some observers as being 'prescriptive, burdensome and difficult to apply' (Middleton, 1994). Middleton notes that staff often do not fully understand the rationale of the method and simply go through the motions, 'learning it by rote as an excuse not to think'. DeMarco & Lister (1987) describe the post-mortem that they conducted of a failed project. One of the developers is quoted as saying:

By March we had been doing this for nearly two months. I couldn't see how it was helping us in any way, but George kept reassuring us that it was. He said that we should all trust in the methodology and that it would all work out in the end.

## Of course, it didn't!

The present paper arises from a programme of case studies concerned with the impact of structured methods on the dynamics of software development. Acme was one of four organizations studied (in the private and public sectors) which have attempted to implement the SSADM methodology. SSADM was of interest because it is the leading methodology in the UK (Fitzgerald, 1994; Middleton, 1994) and is spreading steadily through Europe and further abroad. It is mandatory for large government projects in the UK. The foregoing description of Acme's experience of implementing SSADM has been reconstructed from interviews with key personnel, such as the project leader (PG) and the senior developer (MC).

Implementing a methodology is a radical step which entails profound changes to working practices and a vast investment in technology, training and staff time. In a large organization, there may be tens or even hundreds of IT staff who will require training in the use of sophisticated new tools and techniques. The organizational effort to implement a methodology is enormous. Of the four companies investigated, only one is still actively pursuing its implementation programme. The three others experienced broadly similar problems to those in Acme and have now abandoned all internal development. After high initial expectations the same sorts of concerns emerged about the pace of development and the bureaucratic complexity of the methodology.

The purpose of this paper is to reflect on the experiences of these organizations, typified by Acme. Psychoanalytic theory, in particular the concept of the 'social defence', will be used to shed light on the problems of implementing new methods. The idea is proposed that methodology, far from being a rational tool to facilitate systems development, in practice often functions as an elaborate device for avoiding the painful challenges posed by IS projects.

## METHODOLOGY AS A SOCIAL DEFENCE

In a recent paper, Kendall & Kendall (1993) have argued that the system development process can be viewed from several angles by bringing to bear different metaphors. The conventional view is to see systems development as a deterministic engineering process — the metaphor of the machine. But other metaphors can be applied; system development can be seen as a 'journey', as 'warfare', as a 'zoo', as a 'family' or as a 'game'. Embedded within many of these metaphors is a methodological prescription. For the 'family' metaphor, we have sociotechnical systems design (Mumford, 1983); for the journey metaphor, prototyping; for the 'zoo', rather obscurely, Kendall & Kendall suggest soft systems methodology (Checkland, 1981). For the machine metaphor, we have the structured methodologies, of course, which embody the very epitome of the notion that system development is a rigorous mechanical process.

In this paper a further metaphor is explored. This is the metaphor which depicts the organization as a 'psychic prison' (Morgan, 1986). Essentially, this is a psychoanalytic perspective in which the fundamental role played by anxiety in shaping individual behaviour and social processes is brought to the fore. Let us begin with a quote from Menzies-Lyth (1988) taken from her seminal psychodynamic study of the practises of the nursing profession:

In developing a structure, culture and mode of functioning, an organization is influenced by a number of interacting factors; crucial among these are the primary task, the technologies available for performing the task, the needs of members for social satisfaction and, above all, the task of dealing with anxiety ... the influence of primary task and technology is often exaggerated

This is indeed a radical inversion of the world of common-sense. In Menzies-Lyth's view, group structures and processes do not reflect rational purposes, but are shaped by the 'struggle against anxiety', i.e. that many aspects of group organization and group dynamics need to be seen first and foremost as an elaborate system of social defences erected to contain anxiety. Their apparent rationality and task orientation is a façade, a rationalization. By 'social defence' Menzies-Lyth means a persistent institutionalised pattern of social activity that primarily serves to reduce feelings of anxiety, and provide a feeling of warmth and security. Schein (1985) also identifies the social defences as central elements of an organization's culture. They reflect ritualistic patterns of thinking and acting 'that were learned as ways of avoiding painful situations'. Bureaucracy, the epitome of rational organization, is all too often a highly sophisticated social defence: bureaucratic systems, whilst 'parading as efficient procedures, actually waste resources. Excessive paper helps contain the anxiety of face-to-face communication; excessive checking and monitoring reduces the anxiety of making difficult decisions by diffusing accountability' (Hirschhorn, 1988).

In a recent paper, Wastell & Newman (1993) have argued that systems development is an intensely stressful process, which imposes exacting technical and political pressures on participants, and which sets up a 'chain of anxiety' amongst these actors. The anxiety chain is argued to play a critical role in shaping the behavioural dynamics of the development process. In a nutshell, the thesis of the present paper is that methodology often functions as a social defence, i.e. as a highly sophisticated social device for containing the acute and potentially overwhelming pressures of systems development.

Hirschhorn (1988), drawing on the work of Wilfred Bion (1961) and Menzies-Lyth (1988) distinguishes a number of forms of social defence: basic assumption behaviour, the covert coalition and the organizational ritual. The most primitive of these mechanisms is the 'basic assumption' defence in which life in a social group becomes dominated by one of three implicit norms; of fight or flight, pairing and of dependency (i.e. that the group has symbolically come together to fight an enemy, to beget a saviour, or to serve a charismatic leader). These assumptions make sense of the life of the group; they provide relief from anxiety by nourishing the survival of the group, membership of which provides an instinctual feeling of warmth and security. But basic assumption behaviour is maladaptive in the sense that it can usurp entirely the practical work-task of the group. Wastell & Newman (1993), for instance, describe a case study where basic assumption behaviour came to paralyse the work of a participative design team. Basic assumption behaviour took the form of group members fighting the group facilitator, who was projected into the fantasy role of an external persecutor. By engaging in this destructive struggle, the group avoided progressing with the design work which they felt ill-equipped and too time-pressured to perform.

Covert coalitions provide a more durable and sophisticated defence against anxiety. They involve the acting out of a more elaborate set of social relationships, often based on the well-trodden roles and rituals of family life. Hirschhorn (1988) describes the case of a public sector agency in which senior executives retreated from their leadership role by enacting a fantasy of sibling rivalry. The internecine struggles within the senior management team, in which key personnel undermined the authority of their colleagues, allowed the team to avoid the challenges of providing decisive leadership. In Acme, the senior managers of the IS function and the user departments could be seen as enacting a fantasy of parental omnipotence and sibling dependence. This allowed them to avoid the risks of genuine collaboration and partnership.

The operation of the third defence, the organizational ritual, is the central theme of this paper. Menzies-Lyth's work provides a thorough illustration of this idea. Her concern was the apparently heartless and impersonal execution of 'nursing protocols' by nurses, in which they treated their patients very much as objects (e.g. the 'leg' in bed 10). She attributed this heartlessness to the functioning of such procedures as defences against the intolerable anxieties of working with sick and often terminally ill patients. Drawing on Melanie Klein's object-relations philosophy of psychoanalysis (Segal, 1989), she described the psychodynamics of the nurses' behaviour in terms of three linked mental processes: splitting, projection and introjection. Splitting refers to the process whereby nurses separated themselves from their personal responsibility for their patients, that this responsibility was no longer 'part of them'. By then projecting this authority onto the social defence, e.g. the rituals of drug administration, they relieved themselves of the acute anxiety of personal involvement. In the third stage, the nurses psychologically 'took in' (introjected) the new authority of the rituals in order to justify their conduct.

The central thesis of this paper is that IS methodologies have the potential to function in a similar way to these nursing rituals, i.e. that their primary function is not the ostensible rational one of providing an efficient and effective medium for building information systems; rather, they function in a more fundamental way as a social defence against the acute anxieties and uncertainties of developing systems. Through the triadic process of splitting ('responsibility for doing this is not part of me'), projection ('the methodology is responsible') and introjection ('the methodology says that things must be done this way') participants in system development can escape from feelings of anxiety, helplessness and inadequacy.

## THE PSYCHODYNAMICS OF LEARNING: TEDDY BEARS AND TRANSITIONAL OBJECTS

In this paper the process of learning to use a new methodology is of central interest. The issues of concern, in particular the phenomenon of rigid over-dependence on methodology, are likely to be much more salient when organizational members encounter a methodology for the first time. MC, the developer in the Acme case, observed astutely that:

when you have people with training but no experience then you get a very mechanistic approach to SSADM... I would say that the technical content took priority over the business issues.

The paper shall now examine the training/learning situation in more depth as the psychodynamic perspective has some valuable insights to offer on the dynamics of the learning process.

At this point it is useful to introduce another psychoanalytic concept, namely the concept of the 'transitional object', borrowed from Winnicott's seminal studies of learning in children (Winnicott, 1987). Winnicott describes how transitional objects are important in the development of independence and self-reliance in childhood. The child's teddy-bear is the classic example of such an object. In normal development the teddy-bear serves to help the child separate from its mother by acting as a surrogate protective figure. Supported by the transitional object in times of stress, the child ultimately makes the transition from dependency to independence.

Learning a methodology can be modelled in terms of a very similar set of dynamics. The archetypical learning situation can be seen as a symbolic configuration of four elements: the teacher (the methodology expert), the methodology itself, the novice and the world (the problem domain). Initially the novice feels helpless and dependent; the teacher is seen as an all-powerful figure, the 'ego-ideal' of the novice. Ideally, methodology should function in this milieu as a transitional object. The initial diffidence of the learner is overcome by first projecting the authority and skill of the teacher onto the technique, and then, by introjecting this power, the novice feels empowered to take action. By using the technique as a psychological support, the novice is emboldened to tackle and to solve problems in the world. As experience develops, the neophyte's self-confidence grows and the technique is used with increasing discretion and flexibility. Ultimately, the methodology is fully internalized and becomes invisible; the novice has become an expert. The transitional object has served its useful purpose and like a crutch is discarded.

Peter Checkland (Checkland & Scholes, 1990) vividly describes this process (with reference to the SSM methodology) using a sporting analogy:

initially the thinking of the apprentices was SSM oriented rather than situation oriented. Everything that was done started from a reference to the methodology.... It was noticeable that by the end of the work ... (they were) using SSM more as a set of internalized guidelines which helped the attack on a complex of problems. The schoolboy batsman learning the craft of cricket thinks consciously about getting his left foot to the pitch of a good length ball, keeping his elbow up and swinging his bat through a vertical arc. Only when he has stopped thinking consciously about these things ... can he begin to be a real batsman

In practice, however, blockage in the learning process can occur and an unhealthy dependency on the transitional object can develop. Methodology becomes a fetish, a procedure used with pathological rigidity for its own sake, not as a means to an end. Used in this way, methodology provides a relief against anxiety; it insulates the practitioner from the risks and uncertainties of real engagement with people and problems. Hirschhorn (1988) observes that positivistic social scientific methodologies often function in this way. He argues that through the use of the apparatus of quantitative techniques (polls, surveys etc.) social scientists distance themselves from the complex meanings and interpersonal conflicts of real-world situations:

they eschew interpreting reality because, to do so, they must take the risks of proposing hypotheses that are not clearly evident in the data. Thus the technique functions as a fetish in two ways: it distances social scientists from real people and helps them to sustain the fantasy that the technique has the answer. In empowering the technique, the social scientists deauthorize themselves.

## ACME REVISITED: THE FAILURE OF RITUAL IN THE POST-INDUSTRIAL MILIEU

The modern organizational environment is a far cry from the well-ordered world of the classical bureaucracy, with its elaborate hierarchical division of labour and highly routinized procedures.

The modern organization, in contrast, is characterized by constant innovation, by flux and fluidity, by decentralization, by the increasing need for integration of roles and tasks and by the imperative for a more external 'customer-oriented' outlook. All these characteristics are well-evidenced in the new management rhetoric epitomised by the current fashion for business process reengineering (Wastell, White & Kawalek, 1993). Hirschhorn describes this environment as 'the post-industrial milieu'. It is a milieu, in essence, in which 'boundaries' have become more permeable:

The culture, politics and technology of the post-industrial milieu integrate once divided roles, tasks and departments so that people outside the organisation, division or unit are psychologically more present and find it easier to press their claims ... it becomes more difficult for people in one unit to deny the claims and experiences of one another. The post-industrial milieu creates greater anxiety by complicating and intensifying relationships across role and organisational boundaries. People work in an increasingly imploded organisational environment.

The post-industrial milieu presents a potent challenge to the social defences that characterize the traditional organization, such as the bureaucratic ritual, which contain anxiety by narrowing attention and by defining rigid roles. The new demands require a broadening of roles, wider boundaries, increased integration and interdependence. Acme was very much an old-style organization; indeed it still is. It was strongly hierarchical with a rigid organizational structure of functional departments with well-defined and tightly policed boundaries. Managers were not entrepreneurs, they were effectively administrators. The company attributed its historical success to the loyalty of its traditional working class clientele; innovation was regarded with great caution. The introduction of new methods was a potent threat to this 'ancien regime', especially methods that were intended to stimulate more interdepartmental cooperation.

Large IS projects are challenging for organizations; they involve daunting technical and organizational issues. Technically, the requirements for the new system must be thoroughly understood and carefully crafted into a working piece of software. Organizationally, there are many people whose work must be efficiently coordinated. Methodology promises to solve both these problems, by providing a set of techniques to support analysis and design, and by providing a 'process model' for the project in which tasks are rigorously defined in an explicit structure.

Acme saw SSADM as providing a ready-made solution in both these areas. The project leader commented that 'they saw it as a magic wand that would solve all their problems'. In practice, this was not what happened. It has been argued that methodology has the potential to function as a powerful social defence, and that this potential is likely to be particularly seductive in the early days when experience with the methodology is low. This certainly appeared to have happened in Acme where many analysts apparently developed a fetishistic dependence on methodology, i.e. the transitional object. They appeared to withdraw from the real job of analysis, of engaging with users in an open and frank debate about the system requirements. Instead, they withdrew into the womb of security provided by the method. They worried about details of notation, of whether the method was being correctly implemented and of the need to press on and fulfil deadlines rather than ensure that they had really understood what users wanted. They distanced themselves from the users. Meetings took on a ritualistic quality; face-to-face contact was avoided. In an extreme manifestation of this some analysts even resorted to sending SSADM products through the post for checking and approval.

Managerially, there was also evidence of a retreat from roles. One of the motives for implementing SSADM was to promote more user involvement. However, authentic collaboration, in which users and designers take an equal share of responsibility for system development, presented a radical challenge to the hierarchical paternalism of Acme and the social defences that were embodied in its bureaucratic organization. In practice user managers made little substantive input to the project. Their typical approach was to assign someone to the project and then withdraw from the scene. This behaviour gives the strong impression that responsibility was being evaded and that challenges were being avoided.

A key feature of the operation of ritual as a social defence is the 'psychological splitting' of vision (substance) and implementation (process). Krantz & Gilmore (1990) have argued that effective problem-solving requires the integration of strategic vision with the organizational mechanics needed to realize the vision. The painful challenges of taking action can be avoided by splitting these two aspects, and then elevating one and devaluing the other. By aggrandizing process, for instance, questions of substance are de-emphasised. However, both vision and implementation are required for success. By splitting these elements and lionizing technique, organizational actors are able to take refuge in a fantasy world of idealized techniques, thus avoid the painful dilemmas that would flow from real engagement with real problems.

There were certainly difficult substantive issues in Acme. The rigid departmental structure of the company was a source of much inefficiency and inflexibility. There was a considerable amount of unnecessary paperwork and duplication of effort. The system developer (MC) commented:

The users just wanted us to go away and bring a system back. This hardly stimulated radical thinking but the managers didn't want change. We had lots of good ideas in the pilot project. We found lots of work that was being done for no apparent reason ... People followed procedures but no-one was responsible for improving the procedures. Managers just ducked these issues. People were not used to making decisions. They protected their departmental bailwicks.

For success to be achieved, the pilot project required a realistic and critical appraisal of current organizational practices and a recognition that a genuine partnership between the IS professionals and users was required in order to confront the difficult substantive and political questions posed by the new system. Both IS staff and users needed to admit that they did not have the answers and that they were dependent on each other. Rather than admitting their helplessness and interdependence, a spurious psychological splitting occurred in which substantive policy issues were divorced from the technicalities of system development. These policy issues were, in theory, the province of the steering group, although it has been seen that little of substance was discussed by this group. By this splitting, the illusion was created that system development could be reduced to mere technical procedure, i.e. the execution of the methodology.

## CODA

Broadly-speaking, methodologies play two main roles in information systems development: managerial and technical. By providing an explicit structure for the development process, they make the process visible and thus amenable to management and external control; this role is primary in the eyes of many managers (Jenkins et al., 1984). By furnishing an organizational framework, a rationale and a set of tools, methodologies are also intended to assist practitioners with the technical challenges of systems work. Academic methodologists often emphasise this role. Jayaratne (1994) contends that methodologies provide a paradigm for problem solving, for structuring thought and action in complex and chaotic problem domains. Wastell (1995) argues that, by providing a shared frame of reference (a common language and cognitive structure), methodologies have the potential to promote learning and cooperation in a community of users and designers.

A strong a priori argument can thus be made in favour of methodology. However, as the Acme case shows, there are serious problems when we move from the normative realm of methodological theory to the messy world of practice. Stolterman has argued that the 'idea of method' (Stolterman 1994) rests on some rather naive and questionable assumptions about the relationship between these two domains. In particular, he attacks the assumption that methodologies act as a simple vehicle for packaging and transferring 'rationality' (skill and knowledge) from a 'method-authority' to the practitioner. The situation in practice is a good deal more complicated than this, as has been seen in Acme.

In this paper, a psychodynamic account of the relationship between methodology and practice has been put forward. Broadly speaking, this relationship can adopt one of two general forms. On the one hand, methods can be successfully assimilated and used by practitioners in a critical and flexible way; evidence suggests that this adaptive reaction is common, especially in experienced designers. Stolterman indicates that, far from accepting methodologies as given, developers characteristically redefine and adapt them in terms of their preconceptions and local exigencies. Bansler & Bodker (1991) have also noted that experienced designers treat methodologies in a highly pragmatic way; Fitzgerald (1994) makes similar observations.

However, the relationship between methodology and method-user may go awry, as was seen in Acme where blockage and morbid dependency on the transitional object occurred. The present findings are based on a single case, a limitation readily acknowledged, and there were local circumstances (e.g. the bureaucratic culture of Acme) which arguably had an important bearing on the behaviour observed. However, there is ample evidence that the behaviour patterns in Acme are not unique but are part of a general syndrome whose symptoms may appear, to a greater or lesser extent, in any IS project, although not always in the 'full-blown' form seen in Acme. Fitzgerald (1994), for instance, alludes to the phenomenon of 'goal displacement', whereby 'developers become engrossed in following the methodology and lose sight of the fact that development of a system is the real goal'. Based on her work in the government sector, Damodaran (1993) has also observed that systems developers have a strong tendency to take a 'checklist' rather than a critical approach. Other writers have made similar observations, as has already been noted (DeMarco & Lister, 1987; Middleton, 1994).

In this paper it has been shown that methodology, whilst masquerading as the epitome of rationality, has the potential to operate as a ritual whose enactment provides individuals with a feeling of effectiveness but which in reality alienates them from their real task. Robey & Markus (1984) have also argued that systems development is a ritual. Their argument is limited, however, to the symbolic role that methodology plays in upholding the myth that organizations are rational entities. This paper goes beyond symbolism in order to examine the way that methodology functions at a deeper, psychodynamic level. From this perspective, it is seen that methodology has the potential to operate as a social defence, to provide a womb-like zone of security insulating users and designers from the anxieties of systems development. It is worth noting that the concept of 'social defence' has much in common with the 'organizational defence routines' so vividly described by Argyris (1990), i.e. the devious and self-defeating stratagems that arise in organizations as ways of avoiding taking action in threatening situations. Both notions embody the idea that phenomena that appear rational and efficient on the surface can disguise an underlying reality constituted by the mirror image of these qualities.

In Giddens terms, methodology provides 'ontological security' (Giddens, 1984). Following the methodology helps analysts and users avoid the risks of real engagement with each other and with the task at hand. They turn away from the exigencies of work and take refuge in a surreal world. The methodology provides them with an illusion of control. Klein (Segal, 1989) has referred to this as a 'manic defence', a defence mechanism in which stressful feelings of weakness and dependence are cloaked by an illusion of control and omnipotence. The grandiose fantasy of an all-powerful method allows practitioners to deny their feelings of helplessness in the face of the formidable challenges of systems development. But by surrendering their authority to the methodology, participants become little more than acolytes fetishistically absorbing themselves in details, ritual observances and proprieties. This propensity to draw back and take refuge in a risk-free, controllable world is a common reaction to stress which has been termed 'encystment' (Wastell & Newman, 1993). The tendency will be particularly insidious when practitioners are lacking in experience and confidence, as they were in Acme.

Systems development critically depends on effective learning processes involving users and designers (Newman & Noble, 1990). This is emphatically true in the 'open systems' world of the post-industrial milieu, a world which puts increasing demands on organizations to innovate in radical ways. A strong theoretical argument can be made that methodology has the potential to operate benignly, promoting learning in a community of users and designers. Wastell & Newman (1993), however, have argued that systems development is an endemically stressful process, entailing change, uncertainty and conflict; it requires a broadening of roles, a dissolving of protective boundaries and a willingness to confront painful questions. Wastell & Newman argue that the acute stresses of systems development will exert a morbid, distorting influence on cognitive and group processes. This paper builds on that earlier work by developing the concept of methodology as a social defence. It has been shown that methodology (which purports to facilitate systems development) has, in an environment permeated by stress and anxiety, the insidious potential to undermine the learning processes that are the essence of effective analysis and design.

All stories have good and bad guys, heroes and villains. Methodology is clearly the villain of this piece. The aim, however, is not to prove that methodology is always bad, but rather to argue that it has the potential to operate malignantly, and to discuss how this corrosive influence is exerted in term of cognitive processes and organizational dynamics. The author admits to some partisanship and readily acknowledges that his position is, on the whole, 'anti-method'. The last word will be left to that scourge of methodology, Paul Feyerabend (1993):

It is clear then that the idea of a fixed method rests on too naive a view of man and his social surroundings. To those who look at the rich material provided by history, it will become clear that there is only one principle that can be defended under all circumstances and in all stages of human development. It is the principle: anything goes.

## Biography

David G. Wastell is a senior lecturer in Information Systems in the Computer Science Department at Manchester University. He holds a PhD in psychology from Durham University, and began his professional research career in 1978 at the Applied Psychology Unit, Cambridge. Moving to Manchester in 1980, he took up a lectureship in Medical Informatics in the Medical School, where he gained extensive practical experience in information systems development in the healthcare environment. In 1988 he transferred to the Computer Science Department. David Wastell has published widely in information systems, applied psychology and clinical physiology. His research interests are in information systems development (the effects of stress on individual and group behaviour), business process reengineering (methodology and case studies) and human factors (the design of complex human-machine systems).
