# CASE tools and organizational action

Stephen F. King

School of Business and Economic Studies, University of Leeds, Leeds, UK. e-mail: S.F.King@bes.leeds.ac.uk

Abstract. CASE research to date has been dominated by positivistic enquiry; particularly tool building, surveys and normative writings. In contrast, there is a growing community of IS researchers developing models of IS practice that highlight the complex relationship between context and process that has to be mastered in order to develop viable information systems. This paper bridges the gap between the two bodies of knowledge by presenting a phenomenological study of CASE tool usage in a large UK manufacturing company over a four year period which shows that organizational context, tool features and usage are inextricably linked. The lessons arising from the work are presented and grounded in interpretive IS theory. The results of the work are clearly in accord with this theory, thus, showing the importance of interpretive CASE research, as a complement to positivist thinking, in bringing to light the human and organizational issues which strongly influence systems development practice.

Keywords: CASE, organizational context, action research, grounded theory, phenomenology, information system development

## INTRODUCTION

'Assessment is not a simple task because the relationship between technical solutions and organizational consequences is not well understood. Successes are frequently sheer luck, and failures are often due to our lack of ability to foresee the organizational consequences of technical solutions and/or because the chosen technical solutions do not have the anticipated results.' (Bjorn-Andersen & Davis, 1988).

The research presented here set out initially to evaluate the impact of Computer-Aided Systems Engineering (CASE) tools on systems development quality and productivity. This was in response to the claims by many proponents of CASE that the automation of some of the development tasks would lead to significant improvements in performance (Price Waterhouse, 1989; Holloway & Bidgood, 1991). The four-year investigation has recently concluded; this paper tells the story of the strengths and limitations of the tools in the face of the complex reality of organizational action.

In 1991 Wynekoop & Conger published a classification of 40 CASE research papers. Two major classes of work were identified: applied research (tool building) and normative writings, typically practitioners' experiences or academic opinions and reviews. Of the four case study papers reviewed, two were based on tools and techniques that were not widely used and the other two were Orlikowski's insightful studies of CASE implementation at Beta Corporation (Orlikowski, 1988, 1990); these constituted the only detailed longitudinal studies of CASE use identified by the authors. Wynekoop and Conger concluded that there was a worrying trend towards inventing new tools without learning from the experience of use of earlier products; in other words, CASE knowledge was not being grounded in the rigorous examination of practice.

A subsequent review of the CASE literature by King (1995) shows that these two themes continue to dominate CASE research, but they have been supplemented recently by a number of surveys of CASE practice (e.g. Wijers & van Dort 1990; Stobart et al., 1991; Aaen et al., 1992; Howard & Rai, 1993). Whilst these surveys have helped to develop a broader picture of current practice, they leave a number of questions unasked. Typically, the surveys focus on IT professionals' perceptions of the usefulness of tool features and of benefits gained from using the tools, however, the surveys do not reveal the context in which the tools are used, and which, no doubt, influences the respondents' perceptions. The assumption underlying this survey research is that tools are discrete entities that 'impact' IS development in a way that can be quantified using, for example, Likert scales. It is, therefore, timely to attempt to develop a deeper understanding of the underlying process of organization change that occurs whenever an information system is developed, addressing issues of development context, such as the relationships between developers and users, the position of the IT function within the organization and the history of IS success within the organization. This paper attempts to deepen CASE enquiry in this respect by presenting the results of a four year longitudinal study of CASE tools used in a major UK car manufacturer, CarMaker $^{1}$ . The adoption of an interpretive research approach has enabled a number of significant contextual issues to be identified which clearly show that CASE tools are not discrete entities that can be used in isolation of context to deliver quality and productivity improvements, but are instead but one element in the complex web of computing that is systems development (Kling & Scacchi, 1982).

## THE IMPORTANCE OF CONTEXT

The preceding analysis is based on the view that context matters in IS development and that CASE tools will not deliver benefits if context is ignored; furthermore, CASE research is of limited value if context is ignored! The view that information systems consist of more than just information technology is well documented. Necco et al. (1987), in their survey of US development practice, identified user involvement and management commitment as the two major critical success factors. Unfortunately, neither is amenable to exhortation, and other researchers have delved deeper into the underlying causes of poor involvement and commitment to develop models of organizational change that help to explain more clearly why systems development can be problematical (e.g. Kling & Scacchi, 1982; Markus, 1983; Orlikowski, 1992, 1993; Sauer, 1993; Walsham, 1993). ## PLURALISM IN IS RESEARCH

The dominance of positivistic research in the CASE field mirrors the situation in IS research in general. Swanson (1987) describes information systems as 'a young field of study' with, as yet, no '… general consensus as to its appropriate foundations....' (p. 27). Nonetheless, a dominant research paradigm has emerged: positivism. Wood-Harper (1992) contrasts positivism with action research (an approach drawn upon in the CarMaker studies):

The appropriateness of positivism for improving understanding of the rich socio-political context of IS practice is questioned by Wood-Harper (1992). Furthermore, the dominance of positivistic research designs in the MIS literature is shown graphically by Orlikowski and Baroudi's (1991) classification of 155 articles appearing in four leading US MIS publications; of which 76 were surveys, 42 were laboratory experiments and only 21 were case studies (plus one action research study). ## RESEARCH APPROACH

The CarMaker CASE research programme lasted four years and consisted of three systems development projects undertaken by the author and his colleagues: the New Model project, the Warranty project and the Recycling project. The same upper CASE tool was used on all three projects, the host organization remained the same, and the author acted as lead analyst in all three studies; thus, there was a degree of consistency between the studies. The research approach adopted was a hybrid of action research (Checkland, 1981; Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990), grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) and phenomenology (Boland, 1987; Boland & Day, 1989) in line with a pluralistic approach to IS research argued for by, for example, Galliers (1991) and Gable (1994). In order to explain the genesis of the work, the events that immediately preceded the start of the programme are described.

## Genesis

The story began when the author joined a university research centre which was funded by the CarMaker group, and was a high-profile organization at the forefront of industry–academia collaboration. The major area of research was in advanced engineering technology. The research centre also provided a home for the CarMaker IT Strategy team, who were tasked with developing and implementing a company-wide IT strategy in conjunction with the business units. Shortly before the author joined, a consultant from a CASE tool vendor, ToolVendor, had approached the director of the Research Centre with a proposal for a programme of collaborative research between ToolVendor, the research centre, and CarMaker. The aim of the programme was to evaluate the impact of the vendor's integrated CASE toolset, consisting of the upper CASE tool Analyser, and the lower CASE tool FourthGen, on system development quality and productivity $^{2}$ .

## Hybrid Approach

At about the same time as Wynekoop & Conger (1991) were undertaking their literature review, the author and the consultant were formulating the tool evaluation programme at the Research Centre. We did not know of Wynekoop & Conger's work (indeed, it had yet to be published), but we were aware of a backlash forming against CASE hyperbole and, therefore, of the need to justify tool purchase by providing objective evidence of its benefits. The Consultant's initial proposal to the research centre represented a timely window of opportunity, enabling academics and practitioners to work together in addressing these issues. The fact that the research was initiated by a tool vendor's marketing concerns rather than by an academic's literature review is unimportant now. The key point is that the work was motivated by a genuine research problem that was of concern to both the practitioner and academic communities.

There was no theory to guide data collection. Instead, we were guided by a shared belief that data on effort, stakeholder participation, activity descriptions and problems/thoughts would form a rich resource for subsequent analysis. In this sense, the work was of the form of grounded theory research (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), the intention being to experience tool usage and systems development first and, through that experience, to allow important issues to emerge. Secondly, the work was action research (Checkland, 1981; Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990), in that the author and the consultant were acting as both researchers and practitioners, using the tools themselves in order to study tool usage in a real organizational setting. Lastly, the work represented a phenomenological study (Boland, 1987), with the researchers recording their thoughts and feelings in a shared diary as the research progressed. The diary provided the major source of material for subsequent analysis and was supplemented by other project documentation (see below). The analysis of the documents was undertaken by the author as an hermeneutic study (Boland, 1987); major events and their consequences were identified and classified then revisited later and refined until the essence of the author's experience of being an analyst and using the tools was uncovered. The essence of experience is presented as a set of lessons in this paper.

## Specifics

The data which has been interpreted in this paper was derived from four sources:

## 1. The Project Diary

This was a set of paper folders kept in the project office and written to by the author and the consultant on an ad hoc basis as the research progressed. Each entry was dated and initialled. Entries were hand-written and sometimes included diagrams. We included both factual statements and personal interpretations of significant events.

## 2. Stakeholder views

The views of the other stakeholders were obtained by structured interviews conducted over a 3-month period commencing some three months after the end of the New Model and the Warranty projects. Four stakeholders were interviewed for the New Model Project and five for the Warranty project. The stakeholders were the managers and users most closely involved with the projects. Approximately half of the interviews were conducted jointly by the author and the consultant; the others were conducted by one of the research team. Interviews were held with the stakeholder alone and typically lasted between one and two hours. Notes were written as the interview progressed, with further thoughts added by the interviewer shortly afterwards. A one or two page transcript was sent to the interviewee for confirmation, and alterations were made where requested. A user information satisfaction (UIS) survey replaced the interviews for the Recycling Project in an attempt to quantify user satisfaction. The survey was conducted, over a period of 3 months, some two thirds of the way through the project with the intention of repeating the survey later to identify changes to UIS. The second survey was not carried out due to the pressure of work on the researchers, brought about by their dual role as developers and researchers!

## 3. Project Documentation

This included minutes of meetings, progress reports, defect reports, change requests, data flow diagrams, data models and the output from the computer systems developed as a result of the work.

## 4. Quantitative Data

IS development productivity was measured in function points per work month. Function points were derived from analysis of the DFDs, and a log of developer effort was maintained to provide the denominator. Development quality was measured by counting defects identified from a post-project analysis of the project diary, documentation and stakeholder views.

Triangulation between the four sources of data was undertaken to verify the interpretation of events presented in this paper.

## THE CARMAKER PROJECTS

The three systems development projects are described in King (1995), here only a brief outline of the flavour of the projects, from the author's perspective, is presented in order to provide a foundation for the lessons learned from the work, which are presented in detail in the next section $^{3}$ .

## Project 1: The New Model Project

The aim of the project was to develop a design problem tracking system for new model development at CarMaker's South Factory and West Factory manufacturing plants. The system was to enable engineers to record problems and track problem resolution, replacing a spreadsheet-based system that was perceived as slow and storing insufficient data. The project started in February 1990; a system was implemented in December 1990 and the system was replaced by an upgrade to an existing problem management system in May 1991. The system was developed by the author and the consultant using Analyser and FourthGen. The New Model project delivered a small-to medium-sized networked system very quickly, in terms of development productivity, prior to implementation. The system had a very low defect density $^{4}$ compared to the other systems cited in the literature, but suffered from several serious defects. Nonetheless, the users were generally satisfied with the system's functionality, but were very dissatisfied with the lack of on-site support and the resulting inflexibility of the system when new reports were required (post-project interviews). These factors, plus the availability of a substitute system at the South Factory meant that the New Model system was phased out after six months of operation.

## Project 2: The Warranty Project

The aim of the second project was to improve the identification and management of post-volume problems (typically warranty claims) at CarMaker's East Factory manufacturing plant. The problem management staff were overwhelmed by the volume of data available to resolve problems; most of the data were presented as paper-based documents and the format of presentation differed greatly. It was, therefore, difficult and time-consuming to establish links between symptoms and problems and to prioritise problems in terms of impact and cost. The project started in May 1990 and finished in January 1991, and ran in parallel with the New Model project. The author and the consultant again acted as lead analysts using the Analyser upper CASE tool, but, as no software was developed, FourthGen was not used.

The Warranty project, although not leading to system implementation, was arguably the most successful of the three projects. The users were very satisfied with the quality of analysis and with the analytical skills transferred $^{5}$ . However, there were also some problems: as with the New Model project, context was largely ignored, which led to a lack of commitment on behalf of Warranty management and a failure to integrate the research centre/ToolVendor team into the local IS development and the company-wide problem management review activities. Analysis gradually ground to a halt, largely due to the above causes and partly due to an over-emphasis on fully populating the tool repository with all available data.

## Project 3: The Recycling Project

The aim of this joint project between CarMaker and the recycling company, Recyclate Ltd., was to develop a cost-effective vehicle disassembly and recycling process at Recyclate's plant. The project started in November 1991 and was still underway when the author left the research centre two years later. Unlike the New Model and Warranty projects, information system development was a sub-project within the main project, which was to develop a viable recycling process. The author led the IS development sub-project, the consultant having left ToolVendor in early 1992. The Recycling information system was to provide information on how to disassemble vehicles in a cost-effective manner by identifying parts which were worth recovering and showing how best to remove them. The IS was to act as both a 'disassembly manual' and a decision support system for comparing vehicle recycling profitability. Again, Analyser was used to support the analysis process, with system development taking place using the tools in place in Recyclate, namely the QuickStore relational database software and the NumberCrunch spreadsheet for graphical output. After the loss of momentum suffered in the Warranty project, the developers decided to adopt an evolutionary approach to systems development for the Recycling project (see Gilb, 1988). This approach was considered appropriate due to the newness of the business process and the exploratory nature of the project.

In contrast to the previous two projects, the Recycling project generated a great deal of conflict between the stakeholders, with the evolutionary IS development approach consuming considerable resources as the developers attempted to keep up with the constantly changing requirements of the conflicting user groups. Interviews with the stakeholders towards the end of the project showed that views of the IS were strikingly polarized: the two stakeholders with the greatest understanding and involvement in IS development were generally very satisfied with the IS, whereas the other stakeholders were dissatisfied; the major complaints concerned usability, training and lack of on-site support. The quality of information provided was highly praised by the two advocates, and was criticized by the rest.

## LESSONS LEARNED

In this section the lessons learned from the CarMaker studies are presented and grounded in previous work which emphasises the importance of organizational context and social interaction in systems development.

## Lesson 1

Certain stakeholders can dominate projects and wield power to divert projects to serve their own interests

For example, the Consultant appeared to pursue his own and ToolVendor's interests in both the New Model and Warranty projects in that he limited the scope of the New Model projects to avoid time-consuming investigation of alternative systems (such as the South Factory Warranty system which eventually replaced the New Model system), and to ensure that a significant amount of effort was devoted to exploring the technical issues relating to the Analyser/ FourthGen interface.

The Recycling project was a minefield of political manoeuvring. At the heart of the problem lay, the different objectives behind Recyclate's and CarMaker's involvement. CarMaker wanted to strip their own, current, vehicles to ensure that future designs were more recyclable. Recyclate Ltd. wanted to strip older popular models from all manufacturers in order to assess the profitability of a new business opportunity: a vehicle recycling network. The CarMaker project manager eventually lost out to the greater persuasive power of the Recyclate project manager, and high selling vehicles from many manufacturers were stripped ahead of the new CarMaker models. A sub-plot was the CarMaker project manager's insistence that his vehicle strip schedule be used as the sole measure of progress. The Recyclate project manager agreed to this, and, despite frequently acrimonious monthly meetings, the rest of the project team were forced to strip cars more quickly than our understanding of the process, or the data, should have allowed. The end result was the collection of a large quantity of flawed data that was eventually discarded $^{6}$ .

## Lesson 2

Past IS experience can influence user behaviour and expectations of new IS projects

For example, the West Factory New Model staff's bad experiences with CarMaker's preferred IT supplier, SystemsHouse, prompted them to develop their own spreadsheet-based New Model system. They were equally prepared to invest in the FourthGen-based replacement system, despite the limited support arrangements and idiosyncratic technology, preferring to trust in fate rather than consider the benefits of a SystemsHouse-supported product.

The Warranty Director confessed in his post-project interview that he had been involved in an unsuccessful IS development in the past and was sceptical of the warranty cost savings claimed by the Consultant for the Warranty project. This was one reason for his limited support for the project.

The vehicle stripping engineer (Recycling project) was a keen Windows user and a vociferous critic of the DOS-based QuickStore Recycling IS. This was not a '20th Century' system (UIS survey response). The engineer's constant criticism helped to maintain the technology-focus of the Recycling project debate and reduce the energy available to address the more deep-rooted organizational problems facing the team.

Users in all three CarMaker projects exhibited powerful 'spreadsheet loyalty' due to their past, and ongoing, use of spreadsheet-based information systems. The FourthGen-and QuickStore-based information systems were less usable than the spreadsheet systems and the users were well aware of the loss of usability.

## Lesson 3

Upper CASE tools (e.g. Analyser) are used by the IT specialist away from the user workplace, generally as a background documentation aid.

For example, the logical models (DFDs and ERMs) of both the New Model and Warranty systems were developed through meetings with users in offices away from the factory floor where the systems were to be implemented. The models were initially developed using pen and paper or whiteboards and were subsequently entered into Analyser by the analysts on returning to the Research Centre. The models were then printed out for use in subsequent discussions with the users $^{7}$ .

Poor integration with downstream tools (e.g. 4GLs and spreadsheets) leads eventually to logical model and repository obsolescence since changes to the IS have to be reflected in the downstream 'code' but not in the upstream 'model'. This problem is exacerbate if the development team is under-resourced since alterations to models (i.e. documentation) provide no immediate benefits.

The two projects where more than one tool was used (New Model and Recycling projects) both showed, clearly, the great difficulty in maintaining two or more separate sets of descriptions of a system. Two tools were used in the New Model project: Analyser and FourthGen. The Analyser/FourthGen interface was 'one-way, one-time' in that changes made to the Analyser model could be exported to FourthGen, but they wiped out any further work undertaken in Fourth-Gen. Conversely, changes made to the FourthGen instantiation could not be imported back into Analyser. Once the FourthGen application was implemented at South Factory, the developers had little time to maintain the Analyser repository in line with the changes being made to the FourthGen application. The repository soon became out of date as new functions were added in FourthGen and existing functions altered.

A similar problem was encountered in the Recycling project. As QuickStore and Analyser were not integrated, there was no incentive to record the evolving, paper-based ERM in Analyser.

## Using CASE tools does not automatically lead to high levels of user participation

One of the original aims of the research was to demonstrate that the use of integrated CASE tools reduces the amount of time spent on coding and, therefore, increases the amount of time spent on analysis and other user-centred activities. The productivity figure for the New Model and Recycling projects show that development productivity was high; but the corollary, that user contact would increase, is not borne out by the effort analysis for the projects. Instead, high productivity tools used in an evolutionary manner enabled the developers to take on more coding work. The focus of effort remained technical, i.e. programming, and user involvement remained sporadic.

## Lesson 4

Tools suffer from usability problems; both from the developer viewpoint and from the end user viewpoint. Documentation is often of poor quality

Analyser was the least difficult tool to use from a developer's perspective. This may have been due to the fact that the tool was largely used as a diagramming package with a repository underneath. In comparison, the 4GLs were used to implement some complex algorithms. Despite this, Analyser had its limitations. There was no word processing facility, so project reports had to be created and stored using separate word processor packages and there was no 'cut and paste' facility so common data elements could not be copied between data flows and data stores; this became particularly laborious when documenting large numbers of similar documents for the Warranty project.

FourthGen presented more serious usability problems. The documentation was full of technical detail and offered few examples. The software was driven by an underlying processing cycle which meant that programming consisted of filling in the options and slotting in small pieces of code at the appropriate point in the cycle. As the cycle was poorly documented and obfuscated by the old-fashioned user interface, the programmers had to resort to programming by trial-and-error. The logic editor was a primitive line-editor with no cut-and-paste facility to enable common chunks of code to be copied between logic routines. The processing cycle also applied to reports; there was no user-friendly query language.

In comparison, QuickStore was far more usable from a developer perspective. The product supported a query language, screen painter, and had no hidden processing cycle. However, there were also a number of usability limitations; the central repository was simply a copy list of fields, which was often overlooked when changes were made to the system and, therefore, soon became obsolete (cf. Lesson 3). There was no mechanism for sharing code between triggers; no auto-repeat flag for field values that should be carried over between successive records; and, finally, no graphing facility, which meant that NumberCrunch had to be used for graphing, with the resulting data maintenance problems described in Lesson 3.

Users may well have different views to developers. Given the above comments, it came as a surprise to the developers to find that QuickStore came in for more criticism from the IS users than FourthGen. The South Factory users' major criticism of FourthGen was in its inflexible reporting facilities, bugs and lack of support. In contrast, QuickStore was severely criticized by the Recyclate Ltd. stripping team. This was partly due to the strong Windows and spreadsheet loyalty of the team (see Lesson 2), partly due to the rapidly changing nature of the recycling activity, and largely due to the very noisy conditions in which the data were collected, with the operator sitting at the PC a few feet away from cars that were being torn apart.

## Lesson 5

The Lower CASE tools (FourthGen, QuickStore and NumberCrunch) provided inflexible reporting facilities that increased developer workload, reduced the opportunity for end-user computing and restricted user ownership of the systems

Most of the changes requested by South Factory for the New Model system were for new reports or modifications to existing reports. Most of the requests were conceptually simple; maybe a change in report format or the addition of some new fields. These changes could have been made by the end users, provided they were given an adequate query language and sufficient training. In contrast, QuickStore provided a usable query language and flexible parameter-driven reporting. Unfortunately, the Recycling IS data structure (the Bill of Materials, or BoM) was far more complicated than the vehicle problem structure of the New Model system. Consequently, many of the reports requested by the users, such as the materials reports and the dependency-based cost reports, entailed development by the programming specialists.

## Lesson 6

User information requirements are often unclear, and are subject to change. Analysts can overlook requirements or assume that they have been agreed. The tendency to undertake analysis away from the workplace exacerbates the problem

For example, the need for the database to support multiple new-model projects was not identified during the initial system analysis with the West Factory staff (New Model project). This resulted in a major restructuring of the database subsequently. Numerous new and enhanced reports were requested by the South Factory users once the system became operational. These had not been identified by the earlier analysis; indeed the sponsor and the South Factory manager both remarked that the system 'suffered from its own success' by stimulating latent demand for information (post-project interviews).

Several examples of unclear requirements analysis arose during the Recycling project. The heated arguments that took place at Recyclate Ltd. after the team member tasked with economic modelling of the recycling process complained of the poor quality of the data being collected stimulated a more close-knit team to form and led to more focused discussions on the activities being undertaken when stripping the cars; which in turn led to a redesign of the database. Furthermore, most of the group had not viewed or taken part in the stripping of a vehicle up to that point. The suggestion to view the next vehicle strip arose after the meeting.

## Lesson 7

Outside events cannot always be predicted and can have a significant impact on the project

For example, the CarMaker re-organization into strategic business units denied West Factory IT support to the New Model project; the far greater volume of problem reports than expected, and the decision to bring the new executive car launch forward, increased pressure on the system users at South Factory and reduced the opportunities to train the users to support the system themselves (New Model project). The Warranty project suffered from a lack of pressure until the corporate re-organization of problem management prompted the Warranty director to introduce the Problem Information Control Centre at East Factory. Finally, new European Community legislation threatened considerably to reduce the need for detailed vehicle disassembly information by allowing 'energy recovery' (incineration of plastics) as an alternative to dismantling and reuse (Recycling project).

## Lesson 8

Alternative (substitute) solutions are often overlooked, ignored or hidden from the project team

This was a very common feature of the CarMaker projects. For example, the Sponsor did not regard the other problem tracking systems already in place at CarMaker as relevant at the start of the New Model project. Ironically, it was one of these systems that finally replaced the Fourth-Gen system.

The Warranty project also faltered due to the presence of alternative, hidden, initiatives. The Warranty Systems Manager developed his own local system solution, whilst he and a CarMaker IT Strategist were also working together on a company-wide re-organization of problem management. Neither initiative was communicated to the development team.

Likewise, many alternative information systems to the QuickStore-based system emerged during the course of the Recycling project. The CarMaker materials expert devoted much of his energy to the parallel AutoStrip project (graphical vehicle strip manuals); the economic modeller developed his own economic model using NumberCrunch, and the engineers briefly flirted with their own recycling IS using NumberCrunch before admitting that IS development was more complicated than they realized.

## Lesson 9

Complex software products are inherently ‘unknowable’ because the capabilities of the product cannot be seen nor touched; they are only revealed during execution. A developer needs time to work with a product in order fully to appreciate the product’s capabilities and limitations. In the meantime, the developer can easily assume too much of a product and can attempt to implement software solutions that are beyond the product’s capabilities

For example, the prototype problem maintenance screen (New Model project), designed using the Analyser prototyper, could not be implemented in FourthGen as it mixed single fields with scrolling windows. The prototype screen mirrored the layout of the paper problem report form in use at the West Factory and the South Factory at the time; the modified FourthGen screen did not, which caused some delay in data input, subsequently. During the support phase of the New Model system at South Factory, the developers attempted to implement some sophisticated reports for the local manager that stretched FourthGen, and the developers' knowledge of FourthGen, to the limits. The reports consumed much effort and were not completed successfully.

Like FourthGen, QuickStore also harboured hidden limitations. The data model for the Recycling project was based on the BoM. Recursive programming techniques are most appropriate for processing this kind of tree data structure. Unfortunately, QuickStore did not support recursive programming, and the developers spent much time attempting to emulate recursion using the QuickStore query language. With hindsight, it may have been more appropriate to have used a recursive programming language, or to have called in an experienced QuickStore programmer to code the routines; but hindsight is not available in the heat of a project.

## Lesson 10

Problem-solving rarely follows a defined development method. Instead, ad hoc problem-solving takes place using familiar tools and techniques. Well-known techniques, such as data flow diagramming and entity-relationship modelling have value, but are not valuable in all situations. Furthermore, different stakeholders develop and use their own ad hoc techniques

For example, the developers attempted to follow ToolVendor's structured systems development method at the start of the New Model project, but this proved time-consuming and unwieldy and an ad hoc problem solving approach soon came about. The project made extensive use of DFDs. ERMs and prototyping with all three techniques being of value $^{8}$ .

In contrast, the Warranty project used DFDs as the primary modelling technique with minimal entity-relationship modelling and no prototyping. This time the use of DFDs was not so successful; analysis soon became immersed in minute, and unnecessary, detail.

An evolutionary development approach was adopted for the Recycling project, again not based on a defined method. The primary modelling technique was ER modelling, which provided a useful foundation for evolutionary database development. However, ER modelling is a technically-oriented technique and has no facility to represent the scope, reporting facilities or personnel involved in the system. It also hides complexity to some extent: for example, the tremendous problems encountered when trying to traverse the parent-child BoM hierarchy were not highlighted by the single relationship line labelled 'parent-of' in the ERM! The inadequacy of the technique for 'selling' project achievements led the developers to turn to DFDs later in the project for management presentations.

## Lesson 11

Pressure on developers increases greatly once the system becomes operational. This is because analysis doesn't directly change peoples work, implementation does. Evolutionary development can catapult developers into the maintenance stage without sufficient analysis, with the tangible IS diverting criticism away from intangible requirements. Supporting and maintaining an evolving IS can be highly stressful and may require considerable resources, as the direction of new requirements is often difficult to foresee

This lesson is derived from the experiences of supporting and maintaining evolving systems with inadequate resources during the New Model and Recycling projects. In contrast, the

Warranty project, which failed to deliver a working system, remained calm and pleasant throughout.

The New Model project was unstressful until implementation, after which point the developers found themselves struggling to cope with FourthGen bugs, demands for new reports and lack of support from CarMaker and ToolVendor. It was not surprising, therefore, that the FourthGen system was eventually discarded in favour of the better-supported SystemsHouse-based Warranty system. Even then support was not as good as expected (South Factory IS user interview).

The Recycling project started in a relaxed manner; a first-cut QuickStore IS was put in place, but was not used by Recyclate Ltd. for some months whilst the Recyclate engineers refined their paper-based data collection procedure. When the database was finally used, both the data collectors at Recyclate Ltd. and the ultimate data customers, the economic modeller and the CarMaker materials expert, found the system did not support the way they wanted to work. The next six months saw the system developers battling with changing requirements, software limitations and algorithmic complexity until some calm was restored by the following summer. By this time the database had settled down and had become a usable data collection and analysis tool. Unfortunately, the project management had begun to turn to other sources for their data.

## Lesson 12

It is often difficult to persuade users to give sufficient time to a systems project. Users are often 'busy' with their on-going work and do not appreciate the commitment demanded of a systems project. Furthermore, the manufacturing culture may well encourage 'reactive' working styles, whereas the systems culture tends to expect a more studied, analytical approach

For example, West Factory IT staff training requirements and availability were barely considered in the New Model project. Similarly, South factory staff were not made available for system support due to the pressure to launch the new executive car ahead of schedule.

Many of the group meetings during the Warranty project were squeezed into the lunch breaks at East Factory. Furthermore, the CarMaker staff each identified the greater importance of day-to-day operational work as a barrier to project progress during the post-project interviews.

Finally, the Recycling project suffered from the general management view that the database work was straightforward. This stemmed, partly, from the superficial data analysis undertaken, by a graduate engineer briefly seconded to the team, at the start of the project which itself ignored complicating factors such as dependencies between the parts being removed and gave management the impression that most calculations were simple. As the project progressed, the developers called, on numerous occasions, for greater input from the data ‘customers’. After the economic modeller’s ‘explosion’, user involvement became easier to obtain and a whole-day database JAD session was held at the Research Centre. Despite this, the project management remained detached from the database efforts, relying on the monthly progress meetings to keep themselves informed.

## GROUNDING THE RESULTS

In the introduction to the paper, the need for context-rich CASE research was noted. The CarMaker research addresses this issue by providing a detailed set of experiences of CASE tool use in a real organization. The work presented so far represents an 'accurate description' (Strauss & Corbin, 1990), from the author's perspective, of the CASE phenomenon. Qualitative research of this nature does not seek to uncover generalizable truths (Wood-Harper, 1992). Instead, the purpose of the research is to delve deeper into the human experience of systems development in a way that brings to the surface issues that are in danger of being overlooked in our haste to embrace new technology. Similar conclusions have been reached in a number of studies in the IS development literature (see Kling & Scacchi, 1982; Land & Hirschheim, 1983; Markus, 1983; Mumford, 1983; Boland & Day, 1989, Orlikowski, 1992; Sauer, 1993, Walsham, 1993; and Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995). Table 3 illustrates how the CarMaker studies build upon previous work by grounding the lessons learned in the IS development literature as represented by the work of Boland & Day (1989) and Sauer (1993). Boland and Day, in a hermeneutic study, distilled three statements which summarized one system designer's experience of the system development process:

1) The experience of moving through organizational space.

'Statement: The concerns of individual managers are experienced as sets of bi-polar opposites and the designer's movement through organizational space is experienced as her location in relation to these bi-polar opposites.'

2) The experience of interfacing with others.

'Statement: Interacting with others is experienced as a problem of interpenetrating with them through language, without intruding on them.'

3) The experience of moral choice.

'Statement: The designer experiences responsibility for the moral choice of what constitutes a system betterment, in addition to a general responsibility for creating a system.'

Boland and Day's work closely resembles the phenomenological approach adopted by the author in that the system designer's experience is recounted as a story of moving through organizational space in which the relationships between the designer and the other stakeholders is a key factor in determining her behaviour. Many of the issues and dilemmas faced by Paula in Boland and Day's study were very similar to those encountered by the author during the CarMaker research. In a similar vein, Sauer (1993) portrays systems development as a rich process of interaction between stakeholders, the information system and the organizational context. Furthermore, Sauer makes explicit a broad range of contextual factors (see Figure 1) which facilitated the mapping of the CarMaker lessons onto Sauer's model. Therefore, both Boland and Day and Sauer provide appropriate and usable models with which to ground the CarMaker work in the extant body of IS knowledge.

## CONCLUSIONS

A new information technology, in the form of a set of CASE tools, has been evaluated in a real organizational context. The results show that the benefits of the technology; high development productivity and a low level of software defects were outweighed by the influence of a number of organizational factors including politics, previous IS experience, initiatives elsewhere in the organization, lack of resources and variably stakeholder commitment. Clearly then, the tools are not a panacea; indeed the use of the tools may well encourage developers to focus more on technology than organizational context because the tools support development techniques, such as data flow diagramming and entity-relationship modelling, that provide an impoverished conceptual vocabulary for exploring organizational issues (Kling, 1987). Returning to the 12 lessons, the impact of an impoverished vocabulary can be seen clearly in the following key problem areas:

Issues of power were not addressed (lesson 1).

User participation did not come automatically (lessons 3, 6 and 12),

nor was end-user computing encouraged, leading to IS inflexibility (lesson 5).

and finally...

the external project environment remained ‘unseen’ (lessons 7 and 8).

Developers of the next generation of CASE tools should bear these lessons in mind, as should their customers, when considering the automation of the IS development process.

## Biography

Stephen King is a lecturer in Management Information Systems in the School of Business and Economic Studies, University of Leeds. He has a PhD in Information Systems from Warwick University Business School and has extensive experience in the development of information systems with major companies including EDS and Unisys. He teaches Information Systems to Leeds MBA students in both Leeds and Singapore, with a particular emphasis on IT-enabled business process re-engineering (BPR). He has published a number of articles on the organizational issues related to the automation of systems development through CASE tools. The CASE research is now being taken a stage further with the investigation of the role of multimedia tools in raising management awareness of human and organizational issues surrounding IS development and BPR. A second research project is looking at the role of object-oriented technology in facilitating organizational flexibility, particularly the relationship between business processes and business objects which encapsulate elements of the process in software.
