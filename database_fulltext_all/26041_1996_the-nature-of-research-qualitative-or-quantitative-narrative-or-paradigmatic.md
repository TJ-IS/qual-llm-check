# The nature of research: qualitative or quantitative, narrative or paradigmatic?

Dan Remenyi $^{1}$ & Brian Williams $^{2}$

$^{1}$ Visiting Professor at Henley Management College in the United Kingdom 100345.427@compuserve.com and Visiting Professor of Information Systems at the University of the Witwatersrand 035dans@mentor.edcm.wits.ac.za & $^{2}$ Director of the Epidemiology Unit of South Africa, Johannesburg brian.williams@williams.pix.za

Abstract. This paper explores the role of qualitative and quantitative approaches in information systems research. The importance of qualitative data and the construction of narratives as a key procedure in the development of theoretical conjectures and empirical generalizations or hypotheses are discussed. This paper also puts the importance of paradigmatic thinking and research into perspective by contrasting this approach with narrative thinking and qualitative research.

A recent PhD dissertation on strategic information systems is used to illustrate some of the concepts discussed in the paper.

Although the paper advocates the importance of qualitative research and argues that in some respects it may be regarded as more creative than quantitative research, it clearly recognizes that the distinguishing feature of modern science is the formulation of laws, very often mathematical, that capture the essential features of a problem and allow us to make predictions both into the future and about other current situations.

Keywords: Narrative thinking, paradigms, qualitative research, quantitative research, research methodology, theoretical conjectures.

## INTRODUCTION

One of the most important aspects of research in the social sciences in general, and information systems in particular, is to decide on an appropriate starting point for the research and on the conceptual framework within which the data will be collected and analysed. It is also important, especially in information systems research, to decide if the data collected will be of an essentially qualitative or quantitative nature. Once these decisions have been made, carrying out the research project may then be largely a matter of routine, using well established methods for analysing and interpreting the data (Remenyi & Williams, 1995). However, deciding on where to start and establishing the basic framework for the research can present considerable difficulties (Boland & Hirschheim, 1987; Galliers & Land, 1987; Galliers, 1992; Gable, 1994), particularly for novice research workers, because these issues are not always well understood and have been poorly articulated by academics in the social sciences in the past.

There can be little doubt that the hallmark of modern science is the assimilation of our observations and experiences into a rational framework. Einstein (1922) has said that;

'the object of all science, whether natural science of psychology, is to co-ordinate our experiences into a logical system'.

But what is the nature of this system or framework, and how are we to arrive at it? In this paper some of the basic issues involved in the early stages of constructing a research project, and, in particular the relationship between the collection of data and the formulation of a theoretical framework or model within which to interpret the results of the study are discussed.

We are especially concerned to contrast two kinds of evidence that may be collected in the social sciences: qualitative and quantitative. We will also be concerned with two kinds of thinking which will be referred to as narrative and paradigmatic. Qualitative evidence uses words to describe situations, individuals or circumstances surrounding a phenomenon, while quantitative evidence uses numbers in the form of counts or measurements to give precision to a set of observations. What we will call narrative thinking involves the construction of a consistent and convincing description of the processes or subject matter under investigation, while what we will call paradigmatic thinking involves the construction of laws, rules or conjectures that one hopes to test against the data or observations. The construction of a narrative depends greatly, but not exclusively, on the qualitative information that is available, while the construction of paradigms generally depend on the quantitative data available. Of course narrative thinking may also draw on quantitative data while paradigmatic thinking, which will be illustrated below with an example from Einstein, may be based on qualitative evidence.

What many authors regard as paradigmatic (Kuhn, 1970; Baker, 1993) would include what we regard as narrative. Our notion of paradigmatic is more specific and therefore more limited than that held by others (Baker, 1993). However, this distinction is felt to be both useful and important. Indeed, we believe the narrative form of thought is important, if not essential, in facilitating the shift from one paradigm to another.

It is possible to regard narrative thought and paradigmatic thought as two poles of a continuum, over which ideas are refined from descriptive generalizations to quite specific statements of relationships. However, it could be argued that there is, in fact, a discontinuity between these two polarities. In the example of the theory of gravity we have the account of the apple falling off the tree as the narrative, while Newton's Law of Gravity provides the paradigm.

It is our contention that creativity in science lies primarily in the narrative mode of thinking, and it is mostly here that new discoveries are made and new ideas are developed. By reformulating narrative accounts of the world in terms of paradigmatic laws and theories, we are able to do several important things. First of all, the probably loose narrative description can be replaced by a tight paradigmatic framework, which enables the consistency of the narrative to be tested more rigorously. Secondly, by a process of measuring and quantifying our observations of the world we can begin to make precise predictions which can then be tested. Finally, we can use our paradigms to make predictions about what will happen in other situations, and thereby hope to discover both the extent of the general applicability of our paradigmatic theory and the areas in which it breaks down and requires further elaboration.

## THE POINT OF DEPARTURE

The aim of research is to add to the body of knowledge in a chosen subject area. The problem is where to begin? Generally, one will be working in a discipline within which there are clear, unambiguous and agreed observations, concepts and theories which may be identified by reference to the published literature. The first step is therefore to review the literature. However, it is possible that a researcher may wish to investigate an entirely new aspect of a subject on which little has been published, perhaps based on ideas or thoughts that arise from the research worker's own experience. In such cases various empirical techniques such as grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) or concept discovery (Martin & Turner, 1986) may be used to establish the point of departure. Whichever technique is used, the information generated in this way will form what we will refer to as the primary narrative. This was done in an unpublished dissertation on Strategic Information Systems: Current Practices and Guidelines (Remenyi, 1990) where a grounded theory approach was applied to 55 interviews in order to develop a primary narrative. The research problem in this instance was how to establish a set of viable guidelines for the formulation and implementation of strategic information systems. Research in this field, where there is little established theory, required the use of an induction based, grounded theory approach. This collected evidence from practitioners, supplemented the literature review and was the first step in establishing the primary narrative. The evidence collected in this way is then used to discover useful concepts as part of the process of developing a theoretical conjecture. An example of this process is discussed later in this paper.

## QUALITATIVE VERSUS QUANTITATIVE RESEARCH

Once the starting point has been established and the questions that are to be addressed have been identified, it is necessary to decide on the kind of data to be collected and how it should be analysed. The data collected may be essentially qualitative, for example in the form of loosely structured interviews, or it may be essentially quantitative, for example in the form of precise measurements of the properties or performance of the subject of interest. Clearly these two approaches are not mutually exclusive and research scientists will often work with both, so that qualitative and quantitative research techniques are sometimes viewed as the ends of a continuum (Gable, 1994). Researchers should be ready to draw on both kinds of evidence in order to address different aspects of a research problem. In the unpublished dissertation referred to above (Remenyi, 1990) approximately equal effort was expended on the collection and analysis of qualitative data through structured interviews and on quantitative data collected through the use of self completion, postal questionnaires. Much of the discussion in this paper will be concerned with the relationship between these two sorts of data and the way in which it may be used to construct theoretical models.

## DATA COLLECTION

The collection of qualitative data usually involves interviews, which may be structured to a greater or lesser degree, in order to collect complex information about a particular aspect of the subject, for example about how firms formulate and implement strategic information systems. Because research into issues such as strategic information systems requires the collection of complex evidence concerning 'why', 'how', and 'who', simple survey techniques are not appropriate, and the researcher has to engage in the use of a sophisticated research strategy. One of the most frequently used strategies in the information systems research arena is the case study approach. Case study research has been defined by Yin (1989) as follows;

A case study from a research strategy point of view may be defined as an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon within its real life context, when the boundaries between the phenomenon and the context are not clearly evident, and in which multiple sources of evidence are used. It is particularly valuable in answering who, why and how questions in management research.

Of course case study research is a broad concept and evidence may be collected in a variety of ways ranging from structured interviews to active participation with the subjects being studied. However, in the context of information systems research, structured interviews frequently constitute a major part of the research protocol (Bell, 1987; Kasanen & Suomi, 1987; Yin, 1989, 1993). During such an interview the informants will typically provide a large amount of information that will be recorded. Care must be taken to recognize and acknowledge bias. Bias is omnipresent both in the researcher (Vitalari, 1985) and the researched, and thus permeates the whole research process. Bias may be in the form of perceptual distortions or deceptions (Brittain White, 1985). Although it cannot be totally eradicated, bias may be minimized by the use of such techniques as triangulation. The issue of bias is especially important if the research is based on a single case study and any attempt to generalize from such a study must be treated cautiously.

During the course of a research project a very large amount of information may be collected and incorporated into the primary narrative. The problem is then how to use this information to construct a higher order narrative which both captures the essential aspects of the information represented in the primary narrative and provides a more parsimonious conceptual framework. This framework will then form the basis of the theoretical conjecture which can eventually be presented, reduced to hypotheses or empirical generalization, and rigorously tested.

Collecting quantitative evidence either involves the gathering of statistics, such as published financial reports, or making measurements to establish facts such as the size of the information system or the amount of funds invested in information technology. Quantitative evidence, because it deals with numbers, appears to be both precise and hard. However, it is important to remember that the value of the numbers depends upon both the assumptions under which they were produced or calculated and the way in which they are interpreted. Because of these limitations, frequently quantitative evidence is neither more precise nor robust that qualitative evidence.

## NARRATIVE THINKING

The purpose of constructing a higher order narrative is to capture the essential features of the primary narrative in an ordered context which will allow its relevance to be easily perceived and understood. This is essential if one is to establish a clear theoretical conjecture or set of conjectures that one hopes to test against the collected data. Indeed, the social sciences have often attempted to develop methodologies which take the natural sciences as their role model. Since the natural sciences have developed, over many centuries, precise, quantitative, well-defined paradigmatic laws and theories, the role of the narrative is played down in the attempt to formulate equivalent paradigmatic theories based on quantitative data and evidence. The importance of narrative thinking — the construction of a consistent story that describes the essential features of the problem under investigation — is frequently not recognized, or at least not openly acknowledged, in academic research. However, the relationship between narrative thinking and paradigmatic thinking lies at the heart of modern research into information systems and social science in general, and it will be useful to examine these in relation to other sciences.

When we examine the grand theories which have been developed in the natural sciences over the last few hundred years, Newton's theory of gravity, Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism, Einstein's theory of space and time (special relativity) and his theory of gravity (general relativity), the atomic theory of quantum mechanics or Darwin's theory of evolution in its modern formulation, we are presented with theories which, provided we grant their premises, enable us to make precise and testable deductions as to the way in which the world should behave. Having developed and tested our theories we can then apply them to the real world. The difficulty, however, lies in the reverse process. Starting from observation on the world around us, how can we discover or infer the laws from which, by a process of deduction, we can use to explain the world around us? Einstein (1954) states the problem quite explicitly:

'I am convinced that ... the concepts which arise in our thought and in our linguistic expression are all, when viewed logically, the free creations of thought which cannot inductively be gained from sense experiences.'

Nevertheless, he acknowledges that such freely invented theories must be constrained in some way if we are not to be swamped in a multitude of competing theories, and argues (Einstein, 1936):

'The justification (truth content) of the system rests in the proof of usefulness of the resulting theorems on the basis of the sense experiences, where the relations of the latter to the former can only be comprehended intuitively.'

Therefore the challenge to modern science is that, if we agree with Einstein that there is no strict, well-defined inductive method that can lead us to the formulation of our laws and theories, but rather that these are ‘the free invention of the human mind’ (Einstein, 1936) how then shall we go about inventing our theories and discovering our paradigms?

The distinctive feature of Homo sapiens lies in our ability to develop, change and, where necessary, discard, mental models of the way in which the world functions. Indeed, given this flexibility and power, it is almost inevitable that we have, perhaps, in our genes, not only the ability to construct such models but a compulsion to do so. This is seen directly and clearly not only in the Book of Genesis, for example, but in the myths and fables of all pre-literate societies. Very young children delight in stories (some would say narrative models) and in imaginative play which enables them to construct their own models of the world. An extreme example of this sort of behaviour is reported by Sachs (1986) who discusses the case of a woman in whom the ability to think paradigmatically was wholly lacking, while she was still able to fully comprehend the world when presented in the form of narratives. He describes her condition as follows:

'Rebecca made clear, by concrete illustrations, by her own self, the two wholly different, wholly separate forms of thought and mind, 'paradigmatic' and 'narrative'... And though equally natural and native to the expanding human mind, the narrative comes first, has spiritual priority. Very young children love and demand stories, and can understand complex matters presented as stories, when their powers of comprehending general concepts, paradigms, are almost non-existent. It is this narrative or symbolic power which gives a sense of the world — a concrete reality in the imaginative form of symbol or story — when abstract thought can provide nothing at all. A child follows the Bible before he follows Euclid. Not because the Bible is simpler (the reverse might be said), but because it is cast in a symbolic and narrative mode.'

From the above it may be seen that there seems to be at least two different mind processes involved, one of which facilitates narrative thinking and the other paradigmatic thinking. It is interesting to note how Einstein, as quoted by Holton (1978), makes reference to different types of thinking when he describes the drive to understand the world;

Man seeks to form for himself in whatever manner is suitable for him, a simplified and lucid image of the world, and so to overcome the world of experience by striving to replace it to some extent by this image. This is what the painter does, and the poet, the speculative philosopher, the natural scientist, each in his own way. Into this image and its formation, he places the centre of gravity of his emotional life, in order to attain the peace and serenity that he cannot find within the narrow confines of swirling personal experience.'

Popper (1975) is also quite explicit about the importance of narrative thinking in the development of scientific theories:

'It is one of the novelties of human language that it encourages story telling and thus creative imagination. Scientific discovery is akin to explanatory story telling, to myth making and to poetic imagination.'

Moszkowski (1970) quotes Einstein as describing the process of scientific discovery as:

'In every true searcher of Nature there is a kind of religious reverence; for he finds it impossible to imagine that he is the first to have thought out the exceedingly delicate threads that connect his perceptions. The aspect of knowledge which has not yet been laid bare gives the investigator a feeling akin to that experienced by a child who seeks to grasp the masterly way in which elders manipulate things.'

When we attempt to develop models of the world, these start as narrative descriptions within which our imagination is allowed to range freely and widely over many possibilities. This can be illustrated more precisely with reference to Darwin's theory of evolution. When Darwin sailed on the Beagle the idea that the natural world had evolved was widely accepted (Gould, 1980). The problem was in providing a theory to explain this process of evolution. In the tropical world the fauna and flora were quite different from those in the temperate regions and this impressed itself greatly on Darwin, reinforcing in his mind the extraordinary diversity and flexibility of the natural world. Visiting the Galapagos Islands he discovered many new species of finches and believing, correctly, that in spite of their diversity in form and function they must have originally evolved from the same species which colonised the island in remote past times, he began to develop a narrative theory to account for the process by which they evolved. After very many years he arrived at his now celebrated theory in which a combination of random variation and survival of the fittest adapted particular traits, and eventually led to the appearance of new species. Darwin developed his theory entirely narratively without developing any formal paradigms. Although Darwin's theory may be thought of as presenting a new paradigm for evolution, its essentially narrative casting left it open to criticism for lack of falsifiability for it was argued that a sufficiently imaginative person could always find a Darwinian explanation for any observed trait. During his life time Darwin's theory remained firmly rooted in its narrative structure.

As a second example we might consider Einstein's General Theory of Relativity. The theory seeks to explain the nature of the force of gravity. This mathematical formulation, in the hands of Newton, was one of the corner stones of 19th century physics. The problem was that Newton's theory, while providing a set of mathematical laws with solutions which could be used to describe the motion of planets around the sun or the speed at which an apple falls to the ground, gave no hint as to the essential nature of the gravitational force. It did not tell us what this 'thing' which bound the earth to the sun, the apple to the earth was. The key to the development of Einstein's theory lay in the realization that gravitational forces and the forces produced when a body is accelerated are in a specific, but rather profound sense, equivalent. For our present discussion, however, it is sufficient to note how Einstein, as described by Ishiwara (1977), came to this realization:

'I was sitting in a chair in the patent office at Bern when all of a sudden a thought occurred to me: "If a person falls freely he will not feel his own weight". I was startled. This simple thought made a deep impression on me. It impelled me toward a theory of gravitation.'

The theory was borne not out of the solution of complex mathematical equations but simply out of a reflection on what would happen if one was falling freely into space.

The strength of narrative thinking is that it encourages the free play of the imagination. The narratives are of course constrained by the currently accepted knowledge and theories. When Einstein imagined what would happen to him if he were falling in free space he was assuming the validity of Galileo's observation that bodies of different weights fall at the same speeds. Nevertheless, he might equally had wondered what would happen if Galileo was wrong and this would have led him to develop an alternative narrative.

It is interesting to note that some individuals have much greater skill at narrative thinking than others and it is perhaps this skill which attracts them to qualitative than to quantitative research. For others narrative thinking on its own may be too difficult or there may not be sufficient structure to allow them to proceed with confidence. Various quantitative techniques, such as content analysis (Berelson, 1980) and correspondence analysis (Greenacre, 1984), may be used to help develop a higher order narrative based on the primary narrative, before this in turn is developed into theoretical conjectures. However, content analysis at the end of the day is essentially, in fact, quintessentially quantitative in character, as it counts the number of occurrences of different concepts which have been mentioned during the evidence collection process. Some researchers argue that a purely narrative description is just as good a groundwork for a theoretical conjecture. In the unpublished dissertation referred to above (Remenyi, 1990) content analysis was used as an aid to establishing the critical variables which were needed to formulate the theoretical conjecture, and correspondence analysis was used, subsequently, to corroborate the basic theoretical framework which was postulated. Correspondence analysis is a statistical technique using multivariate analysis which produces a perceptual map, indicating the association between different notions or concepts. ## THEORETICAL CONJECTURE

On its own, narrative thinking does not generally yield sufficient rigour for what is now regarded as modern science and usually we need to progress beyond the purely narrative presentation. Darwin's theory illustrates this well. Although his Origin of Species may be regarded as having led to the formulation of a paradigm to describe the process of evolution, it was still firmly rooted in its essentially narrative structure. Following the rediscovery of Mendel's work and the development of modern genetics, we are now able to quantify in measurable terms, the various features of Darwin's theory such as fitness, inheritability, rates of mutation and population growth rates. We can now say that if a certain trait in an individual has a certain inheritability and produces individuals with a certain degree of fitness relative to others, it will in a predictable period of time become the dominant trait in that population. In other words, we can subject Darwin's theory to much more stringent tests than were previously possible. We can now do more than simply argue (as Darwin did) that the validity of his theory follows from its consistent explanation of a very large class of facts and can now make precise and testable predictions based on quantitative analysis of the theory.

It is equally true that many years elapsed between Einstein's reflections in the patent office, the development of his field equations and, eventually, the general theory of relativity which explains the nature of gravity, in terms of the curvature of space-time brought about by the presence of massive bodies. However, in both the Darwin and Einstein examples, the narrative description can be seen as a necessary prelude to the formulation of the fully paradigmatic quantitative theory. Medewar (1984) expresses this connection between the two modes of thought as follows:

'Scientific theories . . . begin as imaginative constructions. They begin, if you like, as stories, and the purpose of the critical or rectifying episode in scientific reasoning is precisely to find out whether or not these stories are stories about real life.'

The role then of paradigms is to take our diverse narratives and to identify the common features that run through them, to synthesis our many thoughts into a few more powerful explanations. This is expressed by Whitehead (1928) as follows.

'To see what is general in what is particular and what is permanent in what is transitory is the aim of scientific thought. In the eye of science, the fall of an apple, the motion of a planet round a sun, and the clinging of the atmosphere to the earth are all seen as examples of the law of gravity. This possibility of disentangling the most complex evanescent circumstances into various examples of permanent laws is the controlling idea of modern thought.'

The next step in the research process is to use the higher order narrative to develop a theoretical conjecture. If the narrative has been constructed with this in mind then the theoretical conjecture will simply be the formalization of the conclusions of the higher order narrative presented in such a way that it will be relatively easy to produce empirical generalizations or hypotheses for the purposes of further testing. The following is the theoretical conjecture developed in the unpublished dissertation previously referred to (Remenyi, 1990).

Strategic information systems occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers. The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its critical success factors, and these issues determine the formulation of the strategic information systems. The decision to attempt to take advantage of strategic information systems is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and vendor selection, but with more attention to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

At this stage a substantial amount of research has been done and it is clearly the case that if this has been conducted well a major contribution could have been added to the body of knowledge and, in some cases, this may be sufficient for a research degree.

However a theoretical conjecture alone will not be enough to allow the theory to be operationalized in a practical and useful way. For this to happen paradigmatic thinking is needed which requires that the theoretical conjecture be developed into one or more hypotheses or empirical generalizations. Once this has been done quantitative evidence needs to be collected which may allow the hypotheses to be rigorously tested using appropriate statistical techniques.

In the unpublished research previously referred to (Remenyi, 1990) the theoretical conjecture was broken down into four empirical generalizations. From these generalizations a comprehensive questionnaire was developed in order to collect evidence. The following are the four empirical generalizations developed for the dissertation on strategic information systems.

1 Strategic information systems occur as a result of pressure or opportunities directly related to industry drivers.

2 The firm's response to this pressure or opportunity is influenced by its strategy and by its critical success factors, and these issues determine the formulation of the strategic information systems.

3 The decision to attempt to take advantage of strategic information systems is made with little attention to detail concerning cost-justification and vendor selection.

4 More attention is given to communicating with the staff, training appropriate people and setting up support facilities.

## THE RANGE OF EVIDENCE

In the process of conducting a research project one must decide on the range of evidence that will be collected; for example, it is necessary to decide on the number of case studies or structured interviews that are required to ensure that the findings are valid and significant. Research workers who espouse the qualitative or narrative approach to research sometimes argue that a single case study is enough to enable the researcher to add to the body of knowledge. This single case study approach has interesting implications. Clearly the discovery of a phenomenon as a result of a single case study may add significantly to the body of knowledge simply because it has established that this phenomenon exists. However, in most cases, the object of our research is, inter alia, to be able to comment on what we expect to find under a variety of different circumstances. If the single case study was comprehensive enough and especially if it had a longitudinal dimension then it could satisfy all our requirements. However, in most instances, the findings of a single case study are usually only regarded as suggestive, and are thus regarded as only a mechanism to lead us to further investigate a new phenomenon under a variety of conditions. A broader exercise, including multiple case studies or evidence from a variety of sources, is more likely to lead us to interesting generalizations about the phenomenon under investigation. Nevertheless, it may be that in certain cases the construction of a significant narrative from a single case study source is as much as can be done, and the development of a rigorous generalizable paradigm is either unimportant or irrelevant.

## QUANTITATIVE RESEARCH AND PARADIGMS

The qualitative approach to research and especially the construction of narrative descriptions of the problem under investigation needs to be contrasted with quantitative data collection and analysis techniques, which will generally be treated paradigmatically. In the latter case, very often, one is working within a well defined theoretical framework, derived from the literature or from previous research, and one starts with a clear underlying expectation of how a particular phenomenon is likely to behave. This understanding will have been formalized into a model or paradigm. If the model has been obtained from previous research then it is likely to have been developed by the qualitative research process described above and labelled narrative thinking.

For quantitative research it is usually obvious what data are required and this data may usually be collected within a tight structure. Thus in the social sciences in general, and information systems research in particular, data collection often involves the use of a questionnaire. Data collected in this way may sometimes be far less hard, i.e. less precise or less certain to be a measurement of what the researcher is actually seeking, than appears and thus may undermine the validity and reliability of the quantitative research approach being used. Information systems research, especially information systems management research, which relies exclusively on data obtained from techniques such as questionnaires, should be regarded with particular circumspection.

Quantitative research is so well established in the psyche of the research community that many researchers do not accept the validity of any other kind of research. It is then claimed that qualitative research is soft research, and can therefore add little to the real body of knowledge, except in as far as it suggests new directions for quantitative or hard research. This view is no longer acceptable. It is important to remember, as Darwin shows, that qualitative research and the construction of narratives that embrace the essential features of the problem can contribute substantially to the body of knowledge, even if one hopes to eventually go beyond this with the use of quantitative techniques.

It is important to understand the part played by models in paradigmatic research. Baran and Sweezy (1970) describe the role of models as follows:

'Scientific understanding proceeds by way of constructing and analysing “models” of the segments or aspects of reality under study. The purpose of these models is not to give a mirror image of reality, not to include all its elements in their exact sizes and proportions, but rather to single out and make available for intensive investigation those elements which are decisive. We abstract from non-essentials, we blot out the unimportant to get an unobstructed view of the important, we magnify in order to improve the range and accuracy of our observation. A model is, and must be, unrealistic in the sense in which the word is most commonly used. Nevertheless, and in a sense paradoxically, if it is a good model it provides the key to understanding reality.'

This provides an excellent description of the nature and role of models in helping to understand the world. What it perhaps omits to say is exactly what the process of deciding what to include and what to exclude is, and which aspects of the situation being modelled are important and which are not.

Figure 3 shows the steps required in quantitative research. Note that it follows a very similar pattern to the steps required in qualitative research shown in Figure 2, except that it omits the development of the primary and the higher order narratives as these techniques are not necessary given that the researcher will already have a model or paradigm with which to work.

However, it is essential to understand that the model or the paradigm with which the quantitative researcher is working is constructed by reference to the narrative account of what is regarded as the essential features of the process that is under investigation. Thus, even in quantitative research, the narrative is an indispensable part of the formulation of the model or paradigm, as well as the development of any hypotheses from which conclusions may be drawn. If the tests conducted on the model by means of the hypotheses fail, the researcher will refer back to the narrative which may be retained, and a new model and hypotheses created for further testing. Alternatively, if the tests have failed catastrophically the researcher may discard the higher order narrative and re-use the primary narrative to try to reconstruct a new higher order narrative.

In the unpublished research referred to above (Remenyi, 1990) the data collected as a result of the questionnaire was used to test and validate the empirical generalizations using a variety of standard statistical techniques, and as a result of these tests minor modifications were made to the empirical generalizations and the underlying theoretic conjecture. These modifications brought the theory closer into line with the evidence supplied by the questionnaires distributed to the larger sample. Consequently the changes made as a result of these tests made a contribution to generalizing the theoretical conjecture.

## CONCLUSIONS

Although this paper has argued strongly that the non-paradigmatic aspect of research encourages creativity, and is, indeed, very important and ultimately underpins all research, it is not our intention to minimize the power, or critical nature, of quantitative research. In as far as models need to be generalized to cover many situations or individuals, the role of quantitative research is paramount. This is the case even when relatively qualitative techniques for data collection are being used. Thus, even if a case study protocol has been used to collect data, if the researcher wishes to claim that the results have a degree of generality then quantitative techniques for hypothesis testing will be essential.

A great deal has been written by others concerning various aspects of quantitative research (Whalen, 1994; Miller, 1994; Snee, 1995), including the testing and validation of paradigmatic models and theories. This aspect of research is well understood and extensively utilized in the social sciences. Issues such as the design of experiments and the use of statistical techniques, both univariate and multivariate, allow us to ensure that effects that we observe and measure can be attributed to what we believe are their causes. Much of the work associated with the quantitative research process and paradigmatic thinking is well established and can be routinely applied. Although this requires considerable skill and expertise it does not rely on any great degree of creativity. In short, because quantitative research has been employed by the academic research community for hundreds of years it is well understood and enjoys a high degree of acceptance and status.

Qualitative research has often been regarded as of secondary importance and of relatively less value (Gable, 1994). The purpose of this paper is to suggest that both approaches to research are necessary, and that each depends upon the other if significant and generalizable additions are to be made to the body of knowledge. It is hoped that this paper will help to clarify the debate concerning these issues.

Finally, we have stressed the importance of qualitative data and the construction of narratives. It has been suggested that in some respects qualitative research is more creative than quantitative research, and perhaps this is so, because the latter is much more well established and its techniques and procedures are better understood. They have been developed and refined to the extent that they may be relatively routinely applied. However this view should not be seen as an attempt to minimize the importance of paradigmatic thinking, of theory construction, of quantitative data collection and of hypothesis testing in science. Without quantitative research smallpox would not have been eradicated, food production would have required an enormous percentage of the population to have become farmers, and a man would not have walked on the moon. Indeed, the distinguishing feature of modern science is the formulation of laws, very often mathematical, which capture the essential features of a problem and allow us to make predictions into the future and about other, different situations. Joseph Needham (1988) goes so far as to say that:

'Modern [as opposed to mediaeval or ancient] science is the mathematization of hypotheses about nature ... combined with rigorous experimentation'.

Many of the challenges which the information systems researcher faces are formidable and it is not always clear which research approach is the most appropriate. The above discussion suggests that multiple approaches to a research problem may lead to very satisfactory results. In fact it is essential that the information systems community fully embrace both the traditions of quantitative and qualitative research and encourage their use in creative ways to answer the many interesting questions generated by the discipline.
