# A framework for linking Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) and Jackson System Development (JSD)

A. Savage & J. Mingers

Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, United Kingdom, Tel: 0203 523523; Fax: 0203 523719

Abstract. This paper describes a proposed framework for linking soft systems methodology (SSM), a problem structuring technique for use in messy, ill-defined problem situations, with Jackson System Development (JSD), an information system development methodology which has object-oriented characteristics. The approach taken has been to embed the modelling phase of JSD within SSM after the conceptual modelling stage, but before the debate on desirable and feasible change. The JSD modelling phase is carried out at a conceptual rather than a real world level. The framework comprises of a series of steps which enable JSD entity structure diagrams to be derived to a large extent from the activities in an SSM conceptual model. A data model may then be built up by exploding the activities in the conceptual model or by using 'conceptual hows' to identify more entities and actions. One of the main benefits expected to arise from the use of such a framework is an improved user requirement definition, which is important to successful systems implementation. The difficulties in actually achieving a link relate to the methodological and philosophical differences between SSM and JSD.

Keywords: Information systems, Jackson System Development (JSD), methodology, soft systems methodology

## INTRODUCTION

The proliferation of computerized information systems in both public and private organizations has brought both benefits and problems. Ideally, such systems should make a positive contribution to the achievement of organizational objectives and prove useful to those both within, and connected to, the organization. Unfortunately, despite the development and use of a number of information system development methodologies, the benefits have proved elusive all too often.

Several reasons have been cited for the failure of information systems to deliver what is required, with many reasons relating to organizational and human aspects of information systems development. This paper discusses these problems and the ways in which they might be alleviated by the use of a framework to link Checkland's soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Checkland and Holwell, 1993) and Jackson System Development (JSD) (Jackson, 1982; Cameron, 1985; 1986; 1988; 1989), an information system development methodology (ISDM).

This paper starts by discussing the main problems which have been identified in relation to information systems development. This is followed by an explanation of why it is argued that SSM could alleviate some of these problems, and a discussion of philosophical and methodological issues involved in trying to link SSM with an ISDM. The framework that has been developed is then described and illustrated by a case study. The final sections of the paper discuss the benefits of such a framework, the limitations of the framework and some unresolved problems.

## PROBLEMS RELATED TO THE USE OF INFORMATION SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT METHODOLOGIES

The common aim of ISDMs, whether process-oriented or data-focused, is to assist developers in successfully implementing information systems. The latter activity involves intervening in a human activity system. However, despite the greater importance of the human, rather than the technical aspects of an information system, the former receive far less attention from ISDMs than the latter (Galliers, 1987). It is this failure to address human and organizational issues which is considered to be part of the reason why so many computerized information systems fail (Jones, 1992). The importance of such issues is evident when the nature of an information system is considered.

An information system is more than a data processing system. Information has been defined as interpreted data, that is, data to which meaning has been attributed from a particular viewpoint (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). An information system should provide relevant and useful information about the organization and its environment to the users who can be defined as anyone who has an interest in the input and output of the information system. This information ought also to meet management needs for information to support organizational objectives. User dissatisfaction is an aspect of many computerized information systems, sometimes leading to users failing to make any use of the system, or even to the cancellation of the whole project (Mingers, 1995). One of the reasons for user dissatisfaction is the extreme difficulty which can be experienced in trying to identify user's key information requirements. A reliance by many ISDMs on interviews, comments and documentation is not necessarily sufficient for identification of these needs. The lack of mechanisms or techniques within ISDMs for enabling users to express their information needs can prove serious because there is evidence that the more thoroughly information needs are defined at the outset, the more likely implementation is to be successful (Galliers, 1987). In addition, choices made at the beginning of a project are critical in determining the final version of a system (Stowell, 1985). However, insufficient time is often spent on the investigation stage of the systems life cycle.

At the heart of the failure of ISDMs to perform effective analysis is the assumption that the '...determination of a single agreed model of the real world is a straight-forward activity' (Wood & Doyle, 1989, p. 4) involving no disagreement among users. The focus is on trying to extract a single set of compatible user requirements rather than to explore, and understand, users' requirements within a problem situation which might benefit from computerized support. The failure to identify fully user requirements may also lead to an unambitious system design which reflects the developer's viewpoint rather than the users' viewpoints. This situation is exacerbated because most ISDMs expect user needs to be decided early on in the development process and then not to change. This combination of limited user input and early commitment to needs definition often has the unfortunate consequence of changes to the design having to be made later on in the development process, and/or a high maintenance workload after implementation when the users find that the system delivered is inadequate for their needs. Such failures are very expensive.

Other problems with ISDMs relate to the modelling technique employed. For example, process-oriented modelling techniques may produce unstable data models because real world processes have a tendency to change over time. Techniques which begin with functional specifications may also lead to problems because the outputs required are subject to change, and this may lead to an unsatisfactory system design. An additional difficulty is that ISDMs focus on routine processes and find it hard to cope with exceptions.

## USING SOFT SYSTEMS METHODOLOGY (SSM) IN IS DEVELOPMENT

## Benefits of SSM

A number of writers have argued that these difficulties could be alleviated if a problem structuring methodology such as SSM were in some way combined with more traditional ISDMs (Paton, 1992; Jones, 1992; Fitzgerald, 1992; Prior, 1992; Lewis, 1993; Mingers, 1995; Stowell, 1995). Checkland himself has argued that SSM (Checkland 1981, Checkland & Scholes, 1990) would be ideally suited to redress the imbalance in, or neglect by, ISDMs of the human aspects of information system development. This is because SSM recognises the importance of the culture and social values of individuals and groups in the organization, which can lead to a multiplicity of viewpoints concerning the purpose of an information system. There are also psychological issues related, for example, to job design and the degree of end-user involvement in the development process that could be explored using SSM (Jones, 1992). In other words, the place of an information system in a human activity system and its potential impact may be explicitly addressed.

SSM could also be extremely useful in addressing the failure of ISDMs to explore user requirements fully, as it is designed to enable users to articulate their viewpoints, which are then each expressed in a separate conceptual model. If these conceptual models could then be used as the basis for a data model, the definition of information needs ought to be improved. This is particularly important given the tendency of developers to concentrate on computerizing routine operational activities rather than supporting the information needs of management.

It has also been suggested by Miles that using SSM with data modelling techniques would enable a 'sustained collaborative relationship' to be established between the developers and the users (Miles, 1988, p. 59). It would also promote shared understanding of the data model. A further benefit might be to help to redress the imbalance which often arises in terms of control over the data model which is built. This is because the non-technical users would be able to make a more effective contribution to its development. As Stowell has pointed out, the use of an ISDM alone allows the analyst '... to impose preconceptions about the problem upon the 'solution" (Stowell et al., 1990, p. 66) which may lead to a system defined on the basis of ill-founded assumptions.

## Philosophical and methodological issues

In trying to establish links between SSM and ISMDs, there are two broad sets of issues to be faced. One is the extent of the differences between SSM and ISMDs in terms of underlying philosophy, particularly epistemology. The other concerns whether or not SSM should be a precursor to an ISDM or the metalevel methodology within which an ISDM is employed.

SSM clearly embodies a subjectivist, that is phenomenological or hermeneutic epistemology (Mingers, 1984). For example, in SSM the social world of human activity systems are always conceptualised through people's Weltanschauungen (worldviews) and these inevitably differ among different actors within a situation. Furthermore, SSM uses the concept of 'system' (or 'holori') as a construct to explore different views rather than as an independent model of some aspect of the social world. This is a key difference between subjectivist and objectivist systems methodologies — is the data model considered to be a construction based on subjective meanings, or an objective representation of some part of reality? In most ISDMs, the data model is an account of a reality which is considered to exist. Different views about the data model are thought to arise from '… human error and in principle can be eliminated.' (Klein & Hirschheim, 1987, p. 9).

Mingers (1988) has compared data flow diagrams (DFDs) and SSM conceptual models and identified a number of differences arising from their separate philosophies. For example, the conceptual model of SSM is not a model of what exists in any sense, whereas the DFD is clearly based on what is already considered to exist. However, both Prior (1992), for data flow diagrams, and Lewis (1993), for object oriented approaches, have claimed that data analysis techniques are not purely objective but inevitably involve subjectivity. Lewis argues that even if the entities of object-oriented approaches are considered to exist in the real world, there has to be a process of selection in order to define the data model. He suggests that there is a recognition that the data model is an epistemological device. However, this does not alter the fact that object-oriented approaches use the concept of a system ontologically rather than epistemologically, unlike SSM. Differences between SSM and structured systems analysis methods in terms of information theory have also been pointed out by Gregory (1993).

How SSM and ISMDs are to be linked is a major issue. Three approaches have been suggested (Wood & Doyle, 1989). Firstly, by incorporating within an SSM framework, tools and techniques from ISDMs. Secondly, by linking SSM to ISDMs, for example, using SSM as a front-end. And finally, by using elements of both methodologies to build a new framework. The first and second options are known respectively as 'embedding' and 'grafting'.

'Embedding' involves using SSM as a metalevel methodology within which 'hard' techniques may be employed when appropriate, for example, alongside the conceptual modelling stage of SSM. Thus whatever data models are built at this stage, whether conceptual or real world based, they would form part of the SSM debate on desirable and feasible change. This approach allows for continued iteration of SSM, a result of which could be changes to any information systems requirements which have already been defined in a previous iteration (Miles 1988). To preserve the essence of SSM, its recognition and exploration of multiple perspectives, the data modelling would need to be linked to more than one conceptual model given that each embodies a particular Weltanschauung. This could lead to a high degree of complexity in that a number of conceptual and data models would have to be considered simultaneously.

One of the alternatives to ‘embedding’ is ‘grafting’, the use of SSM as a precursor to an ISDM. In this approach SSM is used as a front-end feasibility study and overlaps with the initial stages of the traditional information systems life cycle. The approach is dependant on the ability to agree a single primary task conceptual model, which is then used as the basis for a data model, as in Wilson’s methodology (Wilson, 1990).

The other option of linking SSM and ISDMs is to develop a new framework which uses elements of both. However, no new framework for defining information systems requirements has been developed which maintains the philosophy and richness of SSM. Wilson's approach has been criticized by Holwell (1992) because of its use of a primary task conceptual model. The Multiview approach Avison and Wood-Harper (1990) has also been criticized because it tries to mould a range of different IS development approaches into the traditional systems life cycle (Wood and Doyle, 1989).

The research reported here investigates one of the possible ways of achieving a link between SSM and ISDM, that of embedding JSD within SSM in order to preserve the philosophy and richness of the latter methodology.

## LINKING SSM TO JACKSON SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT (JSD)

In trying to establish links between SSM and ISDMs, efforts have concentrated on three types of ISDM modelling: information flow identification; data flow diagramming; and JSD entity modelling (Mingers, 1995). Approaches based on information flow identification use SSM as a precursor to traditional information systems analysis (Wilson, 1990; Checkland & Scholes, 1990). A primary task conceptual model provides the basis for the information flows which are determined by identifying what information is needed as an input to enable an activity to happen; what information output is produced as a consequence of an activity taking place; and what information is needed to control the activity. There is, however, no direct movement from the conceptual model to the information flow; this relies on a process of interpretation by the analyst. A process of interpretation is also evident in the use of SSM with data flow diagramming. Prior (1992), for example, has described how moving from a conceptual model to a 'logical' DFD involved looking at the activities of the conceptual model and then seeing if they could be converted to data flows or processes. Additional information such as data flows and sources of data are then added to the DFD (Stowell et al., 1990).

One of the attractions of using JSD object-oriented type of approach with SSM is that the data model in JSD is based on entities and the actions they perform or suffer. Wood and Doyle have advocated the use of JSD rather than other modelling techniques because entity models give '... an extended perspective of what the root definition implies the relevant notional system to be, by specifying what it must model in terms of what is done (at the level of real world actions)' (Wood and Doyle, 1989, p. 8). In his action research, Doyle (1990) used SSM to provide the what and JSD to provide the how of the information system development process. The main difference between SSM and JSD in terms of the construction of the model is that in SSM the basis is activities, whereas in JSD it is entities. Actions, however, are integral to the JSD data model in that a life history of actions in a time-ordered sequence is specified for each entity. This, of course, reflects the JSDs objectivist philosophy which means that its data models are considered to be representations of reality as opposed to the conceptual models of SSM.

The main purpose of developing the framework to link SSM with JSD was to be able to derive the JSD entities from the SSM conceptual model and to use them to model the activities of the conceptual model.

## DEVELOPING THE FRAMEWORK

## Background to SSM and JSD

SSM is an organized set of principles used to guide action, in an attempt to 'manage' problem situations which exist in the real world. It is a cyclic methodology which operates on an iterative basis as a continual process of enquiry and learning. The traditional (mode 1) seven stages of SSM are shown in Figure 1. They involve exploring the problem situation and its context, expressing the situation visually in a rich picture, taking forward some problem themes, and expressing them as human activity systems which are then modelled at a conceptual level. The conceptual models are then compared with reality and desirable feasible changes are identified, which will improve the problem situation. Participation and the exploration of the diversity of viewpoints are crucial elements in SSM. Recently, a mode 2 use of SSM has been identified and this will be discussed later in the paper.

JSD is intended to guide the IS developer in the composition of the system. It is based on principles of modelling real-world entities, and separating the specification from the implementation of the system. There are three main phases in Jackson System Development: the modelling phase in which model processes, entities and their associated actions and attributes, are selected and defined; the network phase in which the rest of the specification is developed, the input, the output functions and communication processes; and, the implementation phase in which the processes and data are fitted onto the available computing facilities. The starting point for the phases of JSD is a general idea of the purpose of the subject matter of the system. The aim of the modelling phase is to define this subject area more explicitly.

## Approach to developing a framework

The arguments against using SSM simply as the front-end to an ISDM, and against working with a single primary task conceptual model seem valid: the loss of recognition of the multiplicity of perspectives which is central to the SSM philosophy; the consequent loss of the richness of the SSM process and the limited influence SSM thinking would have on the subsequent development and design of an information system. 'Embedding' the modelling phase of JSD within SSM rather than ‘grafting’, using SSM as a precursor to JSD (Miles, 1988, 1992), was therefore adopted as the approach in developing the framework described in this paper.

Figure 2 shows the relationship of the framework developed with the stages of SSM. It is intended that there be a number of iterations of SSM in order to identify several potentially relevant conceptual models. For selected conceptual models, the framework would be used to derive JSD entity structure diagrams at a conceptual level. In other words, the JSD data model is to be based on the SSM conceptual models and real world considerations are to be excluded at this stage. Many of the rules of JSD modelling are thus suspended. The conceptual JSD entity structure diagrams would then be considered alongside the SSM conceptual models during the debate on desirable and feasible change. Depending on the outcome of this debate, particular JSD entity structure diagrams could form the basis for the network and implementation stages of JSD.

## The framework

It should be clearly noted that in this framework JSD is used within SSM to augment the conceptual model. This leaves open the question of who actually carries out the analysis, for example, analysts or users. SSM's philosophy would clearly suggest that the users should be heavily involved and since this phase is concerned with business activities rather than computer technicalities this seems entirely feasible. We would recommend, therefore, that it should be user driven with analyst facilitation.

The stages in the framework that were developed to derive JSD entity structure diagrams from SSM conceptual models are:

Each of the stages will now be explained in detail. The examples used in the explanations are drawn from its application to a case study on the activities of an insurance agent. An insurance agent purchases business in a specific geographical area from an insurance company. He/she then services the insurance needs, for example, mortgage or motor insurance, of the existing and prospective customers within that area.

## (1) Draw up an action list

The purpose of this stage is to identify the entities associated with the activities of the SSM conceptual model, the problem being that the latter is based on actions, the what, not the actors performing or suffering the actions, the how. Thus for each action in the conceptual model, the following questions are asked:

## Who/what performs the action?

Who/what suffers the action?

It may not be clear at this stage who or what the entities are performing or suffering the actions. This is often more obviously a problem with performing entities but may also occur for suffering entities. When it is unclear who or what the performing or suffering entities are, there are two approaches which may be adopted.

The first approach involves assigning all the performing actions without an entity to an unnamed, non-specific entity PERFORMING ENTITIES and all the suffering actions without a specific entity to another unnamed entity SUFFERING ENTITIES. The plural has been used to avoid the assumption that only one entity may perform this particular group of actions. The alternative is to explore conceptual hows (Mingers, 1990). This involves introducing into the what of a particular root definition one of a number of conceptual ways in which it might be done. For example, a system to 'invest spare cash' might have hows such as 'invest spare cash by buying paintings' or 'invest spare cash by taking out an endowment policy' (Mingers, 1990, p.27). In other words, the root definition is restated with a particular how. This is then used to build another conceptual model. It should be noted that using conceptual hows is not the same as moving to a higher level of resolution, as it changes the original root definition and conceptual model. In effect, a new what is being defined. Looking at the Customer Actor Transformation Weltanschauung Owner Environment (CATWOE) of the root definition could help in the consideration of different conceptual hows, particularly the entries for 'customer' and 'actor'.

Not only may the entities be unclear, but there may also be confusion about which entities ought to be identified. For example, in Table 1, the action DECIDES HOW TO IDENTIFY NEW BUSINESS is concerned with the method(s) which might be used for the identification. However, there may be a temptation to cite two suffering entities, METHOD and NEW BUSINESS. In fact the appropriate suffering entity for DECIDES HOW is METHOD because the decision action relates to choosing a METHOD and not to the identification of NEW BUSINESS. This may not become clear until the later stages of the framework which is why iteration is sometimes necessary.

In some cases, of course, there may genuinely be two suffering entities as with the action DECIDE WHETHER OR NOT TO ALLOCATE STOCK TO ORDER. In this instance, a decision is being made which affects both STOCK and ORDER. If it is decided that STOCK should be allocated to ORDER, the STOCK will no longer be available to fulfil any other ORDER and the ORDER itself will have been met. Thus the state of both entities will be changed by the decision. It is thus appropriate to have both as the suffering entities.

Having identified the entities, it is necessary then to derive the actions which will be used in the JSD entity structure diagrams and to ensure that they reflect whether the entity is a performing or a suffering one. The starting point for this is the activities of the conceptual model. To derive the appropriate form of the verb for the performing and suffering entities, the questions asked are:

## What does the performing entity do?

## What is done to the suffering entity?

Thus for a performing entity, the activity of the conceptual model OBTAIN becomes OBTAINS and it is this latter form which will be used in the JSD entity structure diagram. For a suffering entity such as PRODUCT INFORMATION, the appropriate form of OBTAIN is OBTAINED. This is arrived at by putting OBTAIN into the form of a proposition: 'is obtained' (Gregory, 1993). Although his approach may seem laborious, the benefit to be gained is the JSD entity structure diagrams will contain actions which reflect the active or passive nature of the entities with which they are associated.

## 5 Produce an entity attribute list

This stage is optional as it strays somewhat into the real-world. It involves listing all the actions from the conceptual model and then asking the question: what information is needed to carry out this activity? Being able to answer the question may mean bringing in real-world knowledge. It is important, therefore, to consider again alternative conceptual hows in order not to lose the multiple perspective of SSM.

From specifying the different conceptual forms of information that might support the activity, particular attributes may be listed as in Table 3 which shows the entity attribute list for the PERFORMING ENTITIES of the system presented in Figure 3. As shown, the data attributes are then assigned to the entities identified in the earlier stages of the framework. These attributes may then be attached to an entity structure diagram.

Whether or not this stage is carried out depends on how much detail the users wish to explore prior to the debate on desirable and feasible change. It would also be possible to carry out this stage after the debate on change if it was decided that further exploration was appropriate. In order, however, to proceed with JSD and to move from modelling into the network and then the implementation phases, a stage such as this has at some point to be undertaken.

## EVALUATION OF THE FRAMEWORK

## The benefits of using the framework

The use of JSD, together with user participation, may be considered to avoid the dangers of an unstable data model or an output driven data model. This is because the specification of model and function are separated in JSD. The data model is based on actions and entities and not processes or outputs. This in itself makes modification to the model easier and offers the opportunity of reusable software code.

It has been claimed that to describe what happens and who/what does it is more natural for users (Cameron 1988). However, JSD lacks guidance on how to identify actions and entities that are relevant or on how to define the boundary of the model. By using the framework within SSM, there could be a more imaginative and radical approach to identifying the components of the JSD data model as opposed to relying solely on interviews, discussions and documentation. In this way a more ambitious system definition might be achieved which would be more likely to meet users needs.

Through using the framework at the conceptual modelling stage of SSM, and particularly by maintaining more than one possible model, there is also the opportunity to explore the purpose the scope of any information system which is to be developed. Decisions about the final model can be reached via the debate on change which takes place in SSM. The whole process of requirements definition may thus be done with a consciousness of alternatives as opposed to the approach of traditional ISDMs which attempt to define a single agreed model right from the beginning.

The other attraction of JSD is that documentation may be used which does not include technicalities such as files and the modelling of interrelationships between entities. However, it must be admitted that the JSD notation does take some getting used to. The basis of the model, actions and entities, means that more information may be derived from an SSM conceptual activity model rather than through asking questions.

## The limitations of the framework

The framework has been developed so far using only case study material. It is obviously necessary to test it in a real-life problem situation through action research. Using case study material to assist in this is a reasonable way of bringing in some of the richness of the real world. However, it only takes the development of the framework so far. At some point, such a framework has to be used within an SSM exploration of an actual problem situation in order to assess its worth, for example, its appropriateness for use within SSM and its effectiveness in helping to identify information systems requirements. It may well be as Doyle found that users experience considerable difficulty in trying to cope with both SSM and JSD (Doyle 1990). However, this may have been because Doyle did not use JSD models at a conceptual but a real world level.

There is a limitation in terms of the scope of the framework itself. Although it covers the whole of the modelling phase of JSD, it does not include that part of the network phase during which functions, outputs from the system, are defined. The last stage of the framework, in which the information required to do an activity and the associated data attributes are identified, is limited because it does not explore functional output which may be required by users. This is important because specification of such functions may lead to the identification of further data attributes and even highlight the need for additional entities.

As in the last stage of the framework, there would seem to be no means of obtaining functional outputs from the conceptual model other than by asking the users what output would be expected from a particular activity. Further research on this aspect is required. The control and monitoring activities do, however, offer some assistance in this matter by defining performance measures for which data has to be collected and for which output must be produced. This aspect is not covered in the framework.

The other limitation of the framework relates to the degree of difficulty involved in drawing JSD entity structure diagrams. Even seemingly straightforward activities such as might be suffered by an ORDER, for example, PLACED — AMENDED — CANCELLED — DELAYED — ALLOCATED, raise questions such as whether an order may be amended or cancelled after it has been delayed. In trying to answer such questions there is a danger of moving into real world thinking. It is necessary to consider such questions, however, as implicit in the JSD entity structure diagrams are the constraints on the action sequences. It is not acceptable to try to delay answering these questions until the debate stage of SSM as they also have implications for the scope of the functions which the model would be able to support.

## Issues for further research

The use of SSM to help identify information systems requirements assumes that the methods and philosophy of SSM are appropriate for this purpose. There appears to be a belief that if only the appropriate questions could be found and a compatible data modelling technique employed, somehow or other these information systems requirements could be teased out of an SSM conceptual model using varying degrees of interpretation and transformation, in order to produce a data model. It is one thing, however, to use SSM to help to understand the human activity systems which may require information systems support and another to attempt to use SSM for detailed requirements definition.

If SSM is to be used to explore a problem situation with a view to identifying information systems requirements, then either the data modelling has to be done for every conceptual model which is built or at some point in the process it has to be decided that it is appropriate to use a data modelling technique at a conceptual level. The former could involve a great deal of additional effort at the modelling stage of SSM which might detract from the use of the methodology by focusing so closely on IS issues. Furthermore, it means that from the outset there may well be two objectives. First, exploring the problem situation with a view to defining desirable and feasible changes which will improve the problem situation. Second, by capturing the information systems requirements which are assumed to be implicit in the conceptual models. There is a danger that having this second objective in mind could distort the use of SSM, for example, by encouraging users to write very specific root definitions embodying conceptual hows.

The alternative approach involves making a conscious decision during the iterations of SSM that it is appropriate to begin data modelling. Whilst possibly avoiding the danger of being overly conscious of defining information systems requirements during the SSM process, it would require some judgement to decide that a particular conceptual model had activities which might be supported by a computerized information system. In making such a decision, it is possible that not all human activity systems which might benefit from such support would be considered.

In using data modelling at a conceptual level, it is implicit that at some point the conceptual data model has to be transformed into an actual, 'real world' data model with a defined boundary. It is not clear whether this should be done during the debate on desirable and feasible changes or whether the conceptual models should first be modified in the light of the changes defined, and then the consequences for the data model explored at a conceptual level before transformation to a real world model. In making this transformation, it is necessary to move to the objectivist stance of JSD which would seem inappropriate for the SSM process. It would also require a degree of expertise in the use of JSD which might not be available among those involved in the problem situation, thus possibly leading to a loss of client control. In moving to an actual JSD model, there is the need to define the model boundary. This requires a decision on what ought and ought not be computerized and may in itself require exploration through SSM. Such decisions are crucial to the scope of the final computer system.

This leads into consideration of the general relationship between SSM and the technical system designs. We have already made clear that we favour embedding within SSM rather than grafting SSM onto an ISDM. However, there is still a danger with our proposal that user involvement will be lost when the exploration and debate of SSM gives way to technical design. Stowell and West (1994) are concerned with this in their discussion of 'client-led design'. They favour the use of SSM in 'mode 2' (Checkland and Scholes, 1990) rather than the traditional, seven-stage, 'mode 1'. This involves consciously using the underlying concepts of SSM to make sense of and learn about a situation without methodically following the different stages in order to achieve some change to the situation and to use SSM as a tool for reflection rather than for design. In general, we agree with this perspective. Client participation should be maintained right to the end of the intervention; learning and exploration should carry on through and even beyond implementation; and SSM should not be undertaken in a mechanistic, goal-driven manner. However, in most situations some form of computer-supported information system will be an appropriate end result and therefore an efficient and effective conversion of client requirements into technical design will be necessary. We argue that a formal link between SSM and an ISDM will help this process provided that it is employed with due awareness of the philosophy of SSM.

Related to this issue is how the viewpoint which has been included in the conceptual data model would be preserved when it is transformed into a real world model. If the JSD criteria are applied directly to a conceptual data model, very little of that model remains. This leads on to the question of how the iteration of SSM might continue to feed into a data model which has moved into the later stages of the design process, unless it is accepted that the system development is a prototype or evolutionary in nature.

It is obvious that the framework has implications for JSD itself. In order to derive entity structure diagrams from the activities of the conceptual model, it is necessary to set aside most of the rules of JSD and simply to use its notation. The rules that have to be set aside are those requiring that an action exists and is detectable in the real world. Thus difficulties arise when trying to model activities such as KNOW and APPRECIATE because they tend to endure over a period of time and take place alongside other actions. JSD does not support the concept of concurrent actions which means that problems of notation arise.

How far conceptual JSD models would be able to contribute to the debate on desirable and feasible change cannot be assessed without user involvement. In terms of the performing entities, however, it is clear that the JSD entity structure diagrams so closely model the activities of the SSM conceptual model that it is questionable as to whether it is worth modelling them at all. In addition, the implication of using JSD as the data modelling technique means that the SSM users would have to be familiar with and understand its notation and be able to adapt it for use at a conceptual level.

The degree of effort and the amount of time which would be involved in using the framework are also significant. It has been found that the difficulty in identifying suffering entities relates more to choosing the appropriate ones for the data model. However, for performing entities the difficulty largely concerns the fact that none may be apparent. This probably arises from the fact that conceptual models are meant to represent a what and not a how, although it is the latter which is more likely to include conceptual actors in the human activity system. Use of the actor in the CATWOE as the performing entity would only provide one view. In order to explore a range of conceptual actors, it is necessary to build conceptual hows into a root definition and thus its conceptual model.

In addition, if the conceptual model is at a high level of resolution it may not provide a very detailed data model when the framework is applied. In order to uncover more entities and actions, it is necessary to expand the individual activities into more specific root definitions and thus conceptual models. It is hard to know how far this should be taken. At the same time, adding in conceptual hows at different levels of resolution can help to build a richer data model.

## CONCLUSION

SSM was not designed as a methodology specifically for defining information systems requirements. To use it overtly for this purpose runs the risk of compromising it by imposing an additional objective which is akin to a solution. JSD is not designed for conceptual data modelling and thus activities which do not accord with its criteria are difficult to model using its notation.

There has to be some question as to whether it is worth modelling performing entities in JSD at all. This is not simply because of the difficulty in defining them but also because their activities inevitably mirror those of the conceptual model itself. If the modelling were limited to suffering entities only then use of the framework might be less onerous for the users.

In the final analysis, the use of a framework such as the one which has been developed has to be assessed in terms of the benefits which are expected to accrue from trying to link SSM with an ISDM. The extent to which the framework presented here might achieve those benefits would have to be explored by its use in an actual problem situation.
