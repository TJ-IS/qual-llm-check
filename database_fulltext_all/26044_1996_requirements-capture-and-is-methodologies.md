# Requirements capture and IS methodologies

P.D. Chatzoglou & L.A. Macaulay

Department of Computation, UMIST, Sackville Street, Manchester, M60 1QD, UK, email: lindam@sna.co.umist.ac.uk

Abstract. The problem of not using a methodological approach during the development process is well known and its implications for organizations clearly identified. This paper reports on a survey of information systems practice carried out to provide recent evidence about the use of development methodologies by different organizations. Data from 72 projects was obtained through a mail survey within the UK. The focus of the analysis is on the discovery of potential relationships between the use of methodologies for requirements capture and analysis and the characteristics of the projects.

Keywords: Requirements, methodologies, surveys, current practice

## INTRODUCTION

In recent years, we have witnessed a huge increase in the number of commercially available development methodologies. More specifically, reviews reveal that there are at least 100 commercially available methodologies quite apart from the many hundreds that are in use within companies as in-house aids (Longworth, 1985; Bubenko, 1986). We have also seen the development and evolution of methodologies which are not based on a single underlying principle, but which depend upon a clearly defined set of procedures that make use of a range of techniques.

However, despite this increase in both the quality and the number of the development methodologies, the number of projects where a methodology is used during the development process has remained low (Lubars et al., 1992; Southwell, 1993; Hopker, 1994).

This paper presents the findings of an in-depth survey of current practice associated with the requirements stage of a project (Chatzoglou, 1992). The survey covers 72 development projects across 54 different organizations within the UK. The respondents are experienced project managers, systems analysts and consultants. This survey includes the use of methodologies and project planning tools as part of the requirements capture and analysis (RCA) process.

The focus of the analysis is on the discovery of potential relationships between the use of RCA methodologies and project characteristics.

The Research Procedure section describes the survey method, the sampling approach, questionnaire design, the types of respondents and the types of projects we worked with. Our findings are presented in the Results section which is divided into seven sub-sections. In the first one, the use of methodologies is examined, while in the second one, the use of methodologies in projects with different characteristics is analysed. The third sub-section focuses on the use of specific methodologies by different projects, while the fourth attempts to give an answer to the question of which factors affect the choice of a methodology. The fifth sub-section deals with the quality of the requirements gathered and the use of methodologies, and the final sub-section deals with the use of other tools (such as planning tools) in projects and briefly presents the results of some statistical tests. Finally, we summarized our results and draw general conclusions.

## RESEARCH PROCEDURE

## Objectives

The main objective of the present survey was to obtain information from practitioners about the development process, and about requirements in particular. Our aim was not only to find out whether methodologies are used, but also to discover if the use of methodologies depends on the characteristics of the project. To be more specific, the project characteristics that were taken into account were as follows:

a) Project type — (software, hardware, systems, others);

b) Developers — (software houses, consultants, academics, industry);

c) Target user — (in house use, use by an external client);

d) Structure of the problem domain it refers to — (well-defined, moderately-defined, poorly-defined); and

e) Applicability – (bespoke, generic)

The survey contained questions about the requirement process. The importance of this survey is that both general information, about the project development process, and specific information, about the requirements capture process, was gathered. However, not only factual data, but also the respondents' personal opinions about some aspects of the RCA process, were acquired. Briefly, these questions refer to their experience, their position in the development team, characteristics of the projects, techniques, tools or methodologies used, and finally assessment of some factors that might be of importance and affect the project development and the RCA process in particular. Despite the highly subjective assessment of these aspects, the opinion of the respondents is of great significance as they help us to understand what actually happens in real life projects.

## Survey method and sampling approach

Considering all the theoretical aspects (Moser & Kalton, 1971; Sudman, 1976. Namboordiri, 1978; Fowler, 1984; Fink & Kosecoff, 1985) and practical aspects (the nature of the research and the actual size of the population) a decision was made to use a mail questionnaire as the survey device. It must be stressed, though, that the criteria of cost and time were considered along with the nature of the survey.

Mail surveys are widely condemned for not being able to secure an adequate rate of response. However, in this case, it was selected because the sample population had a high educational level, and was likely to be highly interested in the subject of the research. In such cases (Fowler, 1984), mail surveys become more attractive than other survey methods and produce a better response rate.

After careful consideration of all the probability sampling methods, it was decided that the best one for this survey is the proportional stratification sampling method. It is selected for two reasons. Firstly, it can provide a better representation of the population within the sample, and secondly, the answers of different groups within the population can be examined separately and compared with each other.

The sample population was divided into three different strata or groups according to the way people look at, or deal with, the developmental process. These three strata are: academics; software houses and consultancies; and industry.

Four sources were used to locate the survey population. Firstly, a list of names with people who are involved in the system development process, and who had also participated in previous surveys conducted by members of staff of the Computation Department of UMIST. Secondly, journals (Information Systems Journal, IEEE Software etc.) and books (Hellander, 1988; Diaper & Winder, 1987; Carroll & Tanier, 1987; etc.), where the authors of some articles about the system development, and specifically the RCA process, were used. Thirdly, some editions of the ALVEY Programme (Annual Reports 1989, 1990 etc.) were used to find individuals or companies that are involved in development processes. Lastly, magazines (Computing Decisions 1991, 1992; The Software Users Year Book, 1990 etc.) were used, and the names of some companies (software houses, consultants) were taken. The criteria used in selecting these companies were their size (number of employees), their products and their specialized services.

## Questionnaire design

The questionnaire design and implementation followed a structural approach. Before design began, a study was undertaken to ascertain the objectives of the survey. Therefore, there was a rather good idea of what information should be obtained well before the construction of the questionnaire began.

Almost all the main theories about questionnaire construction were considered (Miller, 1956; Fowler, 1984; Weisberg & Bowen, 1977; Belson, 1981; Schuman & Press, 1981; Sudman and Bradburn, 1982; Fowler, 1984; Fink and Kosecoff, 1985; Converse and Presser, 1986).

The results of the questionnaire construction process was a 12 page questionnaire (a brief description is given in Appendix D). During the construction process, which took about 8 weeks, many different versions of the same questionnaire were designed and each of them was tested by a team led by members of staff of the Computation Department of UMIST. Finally, in order to test it in real life, a pilot test was carried out.

The final number of questionnaires sent out was 185. A stamped addressed envelope was enclosed with each questionnaire to encourage replies and ensure a good response rate. They were sent to four different categories of people according to the way they are involved in the systems development process. Thus, 65 were sent to academics, 86 to software houses and consultants, and 34 to industry.

A reminder was sent 5 weeks after the release of the questionnaire to the people who had not replied by that time. Almost 3 months later, 82 completed questionnaires (response rate 44.3%) had been received. However, after a preliminary examination of these questionnaires was conducted, it was decided to discard 10 of them as not appropriate. This was decided because there were too many questions not answered, or because some answers contradicted each other. Therefore, the final response was 72 (38.9%) questionnaires.

Although the response rate of 38.9% is not considered as high, it cannot be considered as low either. The actual number of valid responses, 72, was good enough to allow the survey to proceed and for the statistical results to be theoretically valid (Appendix B). What is also very important, and must be stressed, is that there was a very big deviation in the response rate between the different group categories. From the 65 academics who received a questionnaire, only 12 (18.5%) replied, while, from the 86 people from consultancies and software Houses 36 replied (41.8%), and finally, 24 out of 34 (70.5%) replied from industry.

As can be seen, the people who have replied are mainly involved in the development process as part of their every day work, and also, are interested in exploring this area further. Although the low response rate of the academics was a surprise, many academics returned their questionnaires without answering the questions, indicating that the questions cannot describe the kind of projects they are involved in (mainly research projects).

Statistical analysis is mainly used in order to draw some meaningful conclusions about different aspects of the development as well as the RCA processes, and also, to examine the association that might exist between them. Considering the nature of the format of the data gained from this survey, the first is done by examining the frequency distribution of each aspect (factor), while the second is achieved by using the cross-tabulation technique and the chi-square test.

Although some social scientists believe the correlation analysis and regression analysis can be used in surveys where data has the same format, as in this survey (Hawkes, 1971; Smith, 1974; Somers, 1974), we have not used them to avoid potentially misleading results.

## RESULTS

## Use of methodologies

Although the importance of employing a methodological approach during the system development process is widely documented (Finkelstein, 1990; Ashworth & Slater, 1993; McDermid, 1993), it is also recognised that in practice a methodological approach is often not followed. This is supported here; nearly half of the projects in this survey (47%) use no methodology (Table 2). What seems to be used instead are a number of unrelated techniques and tools. These findings support the findings of other similar surveys (Lubars et al., 1992; Thompson, 1993; Southwell, 1993; Hopker, 1994), but seems to contradict the results of a survey conducted by Hardy et al. (1994), where only 18% of the projects used no methodology.

On the other hand, it is encouraging the projects which use a methodology choose ones that cover the RCA process as well. An indication of the importance of the RCA process, and the significant role it plays for the successful completion of a project, is that 16% of the projects use methodology for RCA only.

It is important to make the distinction here between using a methodology, using a contingency-based strategy, and the random use of non-related techniques. In this research, reference to the use of methodologies includes both the use of specific methodologies and also the use of techniques adopting a contingency-based strategy. Thus, use of methodology has, rather, the meaning of the use of a methodological approach than the simple use of methodologies.

## Use of methodologies in different projects

The picture dramatically changes when the developers of the projects are taken into consideration (Table 3). More specifically, it is revealed that 83% of the projects that are developed by academics use a methodology during the development processes which also cover the RCA process. Furthermore, 70% of the projects developed by software houses or consultancy companies use methodologies in the development process, and 80% of the projects developed by them use a methodology in the RCA process. It is a great relief to see that the many methodological approaches published by academics and researchers during the last few years are actually used by them.

However, there appears to be less success in getting the message through to people working in industry. This is illustrated by the fact that only 34% of the projects developed by industry use a methodology, and also, that only 28% use a methodology in the RCA process.

Why are projects in industry less likely to use a methodology? The answer to this question is not easy, and one can only speculate on the possible reasons that cause it. However, a possible explanation is that people who work for the industry are not well enough informed, or that the particular methodologies are difficult to use in practice. This survey found that in six out of eight projects developed by industry where a methodology is used, use either prototyping or in-house methodologies. Another possible explanation is that projects developed by industry are usually small-sized projects, which are developed for internal use. This is in turn, supported by the finding that, in general, a methodology is used in only 39% of the projects developed for internal use (compared to 69% of the projects developed for an external client. This difference, in the number of projects where a methodology is used between these two different types of project, can possibly be explained by the fact that developers of projects for internal use may think that the problem that they are dealing with is well known to them, and, therefore, that they do not need a specific methodology to assist them during the development process.

## Usage of specific methodologies

It is not surprising that the methodologies most frequently named were SSADM and in-house methodologies. It is also very interesting to see OOA, Prototyping and SSM being used in a few projects. All the other methodologies named in this survey were used by just one project. These results support findings of other surveys such as Hardy et al. (1994), where it was found that in house and SSADM are by far the most used methodologies, and that OOA are used by just 4% of the projects. Hopker (1994) also found that 11% of the projects she examined used SSADM and 6% of them used prototyping. An explanation of the acronyms and relative use of each methodology is given in Appendix A.

However, it is worth mentioning that specific developers usually choose specific methodologies. As can be seen in Table 4, Industry has a clear preference for Prototyping and in house methodologies, while most academics prefer one of the OOA, IEM, BIS, SSA or COMMANDER methodologies. In contrast, 55% of the projects developed by software houses and consultants choose one methodology from SSADM, LSDM, SSAD, SRCA-CORE and PRINCE. A large number of consultants also use PROTOTYPING and IN HOUSE as well as ETHICS, MULTI-VIEW, SUMMIT-D, HTA and EXCELERATOR.

## Important factors affecting the choice for a methodology

There is great confusion in the literature on the way methodologies should be classified. Almost every paper includes a new set of criteria. However, it is important to understand that there is a difference between the criteria used to assess a methodology for theoretical purposes and criteria used in practice by system developers.

In an attempt to understand why specific methodologies are used by specific developers in specific projects, six criteria were selected from the literature (Macaulay et al., 1990; Macaulay et al., 1991). The informants were asked to specify which of those criteria are taken into account when they decide which methodology to choose $^{1}$ . An open option was also given to them to specify any other criterion of significance $^{2}$ .

The six criteria are:

a) Ease of Use (use of standard procedures to guide users — flexible to adapt to individual styles of working);

b) Support (manuals, training, computer support);

c) Design (based on sound principles and valid assumptions — robust and transferable results);

d) Management of development process and team (support planning and help management activities such as cost estimation, scheduling, team organisation-automated aid);

e) Quality of techniques employed (appropriate for the specific environment); and

f) Quality of outputs (steps to improve quality-verification, validation).

The results show that all of these six criteria are equally significant and are considered when choosing a methodology. More specifically, 21% of the methodologies were chosen because ease of use and adequate support to the user is provided (manuals, training, auxiliary tools etc.); 23% of them because of the design of the project and the way it manages the development process; and 26% of them because of the techniques employed by the specific methodology and the quality of the outputs given by the methodology. Finally, in 30% of the projects other reasons were given for the selection of specific methodologies.

The most important of them are the following:

a) users are familiar with the methodology;

b) no other alternative available;

c) policy of the development company; and

d) fit best with the requirements and the characteristics of the specific project.

These results are also supported by findings of a survey by Hopker (1994), where it was found that many developers choose a methodology because of:

a) head office policy;

b) historical;

c) reading literature and

d) supplier.

What this really suggests is that, in many cases, a methodology is chosen not because of its characteristics or advantages over other methodologies, but simply for reasons of history or familiarity.

A question that arises now is why projects developed by different developers choose different methodologies? The majority of the academics and the people who work for industry mainly consider the design, management, techniques employed and outputs of a specific methodology as the most important criteria one must look at. On the other hand, for both consultants and the people who work for software houses, the most important aspects that are seriously taken into consideration are ease of use, support, techniques and outputs. Furthermore, specific methodologies are used by software houses because 'there is no alternative available', and also, because 'it is the policy of the company', while consultants mentioned that, from the other reasons, the most important reason is ‘the familiarity of the users with the use of a specific methodology’, and also, ‘the way it fits with the requirements and the characteristics of the project’.

Examination of the reasons each specific methodology is chosen reveal that SSADM, LSDM, SSAD, PRINCE, SSM and SRCA-CORE are mainly chosen because they are easy to use, they support users and they use specific techniques that can give quality outputs. On the other hand, prototyping and different in house methodologies are mainly chosen for other reasons (i.e. no alternative available, familiarity of the users) and also, because of their design and management. Finally, for OOA, IEM, BIS, SSM and COMMANDER all the criteria are equally important.

## Quality of requirements gathered and use of methodologies

The result of this study indicate that although the use of methodologies in the whole development process, and in the RCA process in particular, improve the situation by capturing more information, this improvement is not a significant one. However, when the effectiveness of a specific group of methodologies is considered, the use SSADM, LSDM, SSAD, CORE, PRINCE, SSM, OOA, IEM, COMMANDER and BIS does not increase the amount of information captured significantly, while the use of a methodology from IN HOUSE, PROTOTYPING, MULTIVIEW, HTA, SUMMIT-D and EXCELERATOR results in the capture of significantly more information.

On the other hand, when we turn our discussion to the quality aspect of the information gathered, the picture is completely different. It emerges that people involved in the development process are significantly more confident about the quality of the information-requirements captured when a methodology is used in the whole development and the RCA process, compared to the situation where no methodology is used. Finally, while the use of SSADM, LSDM, SSAD, CORE and PRINCE, does not significantly improve the degree of confidence, the use of SSM, OOA, IEM, COMMANDER, BIS, PROTOTYPING, IN HOUSE, ETHICS, MULTIVIEW, HTA, SUMMIT-D and EXCELERATOR does significantly increase the degree of confidence in the quality of the information gathered.

## Use of other tools

A final aspect that is examined is the use of a planning and control tool. It is found (Table 6) that while such a tool is used in 62% of the projects for the planning and control of the later stages of the system development process, few of them cover the early stages as well. It emerged that only 29% of the projects use such a tool during the RCA process.

Furthermore, it is found that in 84% of the projects an explicit review part is used at the end of the RCA process, but in only 15% of the projects a tool is employed to determine when enough information or requirements is gathered. Of course, it must be stressed that many people expressed their concern about this, and expressed their doubts that any such tool could be designed. They regard the RCA process as too informal and unstructured a process to be planned never mind controlled. Appendix C contains a list of the main tools used within the projects.

## Statistical tests

As has been mentioned earlier, some statistical analysis techniques were also used to prove the validity of the results. It is also important to note that the findings presented in the previous sections are taken from classical frequency distribution and cross-tabulation analysis. However, most of these findings are also supported by the results of the chi-square analysis and t-test.

More specifically, it was found that the use of methodologies in general and the use of specific methodologies are related to developers, project type and the criteria used for choosing them.

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

## Summary of findings

## This survey showed:

i) More projects use methodologies in the RCA stage than in the rest of the development process (62% vs. 53%, Table 2);

ii) Respondents were more confident about the quality of the information gathered when a methodology was used;

iii) Industry (e.g. banks, utilities, insurance companies) fares badly in the use of RCA methodologies when compared to consultancies and software houses (29% vs. 80%, Table 3);

iv) Projects which were being developed for external clients were much more likely to make use of a methodology than those for in-house ‘clients’ (64% vs. 30%, Table 3);

v) RCA methodologies are more widely used when bespoke systems are being developed than when generic products are being developed (56% vs. 39%, Table 3);

vi) Very few projects used project planning tools to cover the RCA stage of development in comparison to the rest of the development process (29% vs. 62%, Table 6); and

vii) Most projects have an explicit review point at the end of the RCA stage (83%, Table 6).

The number of projects that make use of a methodology in the whole development process or in the RCA process is rather disappointing, as just half of the projects do use a methodology. To be specific, while the great majority of the projects developed by software houses, consultancy companies and academics use a methodology during the RCA process as well as the whole development process, very few of the projects developed by industry use a methodology in either process.

It is also interesting that specific developers usually choose specific methodologies. The most used frequently used methodologies are in house and SSADM followed by OOA and Prototyping. The choice of methodology is based on several criteria. The six most important criteria were: ease of use, support, design, management, techniques and outputs. Although all of them were considered to be very important by all developers, different developers seem to place emphasis on some criteria more than others. However, in many cases, mainly in projects developed by software houses, specific methodologies are used either because 'there is no alternative available', or because 'it is the policy of the developing company'. Similarly, in many projects developed by consultants, specific methodologies are used either because of 'the familiarity of the users with them', or of 'the way it fits with the requirements and the characteristics of the project'.

A methodology is used by most of the projects developed for an external client, with a moderately-or poorly-defined problem domain. On the other hand, most projects that are developed for use within the company that develops it, or projects which have a well-defined problem domain do not use a methodology. Finally, while most of the bespoke projects use a methodology, most of the generic projects do not.

It is important to stress that the use of a methodology increases the managers' confidence about the outputs of the development process, because it is perceived that with the use of methodologies more and better information is obtained. The most effective methodologies, in this respect, appear to be in house methodologies and prototyping.

Finally, most of the above findings which are based on the cross-tabulation analysis, are also supported by the results of the chi-square and t-test statistics.

## General conclusions

Despite the fact that all academics, project managers and systems analysts appear to accept that developers are usually more confident when a methodology is used, the number of real projects where a methodology is used is low. The fact that they also claim that by using methodologies more and better information is gathered does not appear to alter the situation. This position is supported by the results of this survey, which has focused on medium-to-small sized commercial projects, and it contradicts the results of other recent surveys about commercial projects (Hardy et al., 1994), or large military or government oriented projects (Lubars et al., 1992).

Whether a methodology, and which specific methodology, will be used depends on all developers, projects type and project characteristics. However, the selection of a methodology itself is based on whether it satisfies some criteria.

However, in many cases, developers do not use one specific methodology because of its advantages over other methodologies, nor because it can deal with the problems of the specific project in the best way, but, rather, because of familiarity or because they have no alternative.

Finally, it is clear that the problem of low usage methodologies is not caused by the lack of methodologies or the lack of appropriate methodologies, or even the lack of methodologies that can assure developers about the outcome of the development process. The cause appears to be attributable either to the attitude of the people involved in the development process or to the organisational constraints under which they operate.

## Appendix D: Questionnaire structure

The questionnaire is divided into six sections. Each section deals with different aspects of the development process and the RCA process as well.

Section A acquires background information about the respondents, the characteristics of the projects they are involved in, the size of them in terms of elapsed time, cost and people involved, the use of methodologies and tools, and finally, the phases of the RCA process.

Section B has questions that are concerned with the project team members. Questions focus on identifying the level and significance of qualitative factors such as project team members' experience with project development and with establishing requirements, knowledge of the problem domain of similar projects, anxiety and stress about the success or completion of the project, and finally, commitment to and persistence in the development of the specific project.

Section C includes questions that refer to the sources of information used by project team members for gathering information. Questions are concerned with the type of sources used, the major constraints on the quantity and quality of information gathered, and the confidence of the project team members about the adequacy, quality and amount of information gathered. There are also questions which deal with the attitude of users and user participation in the development process and, in particular, the RCA process.

Section D deals with the management of the project development process. It focuses on questions about the level and significance of the resources available for the completion of the process, quality and type of tools and techniques employed, and finally, management style and the quality of management techniques adopted.

Section E provides respondents with the opportunity to make comments about any other aspect of the development and the RCA process they think is worth mentioning.

Section F provides respondents with the opportunity to make comments about the questionnaire itself.
