# Case study research: a multi-faceted research approach for IS

A.L.M. Cavaye

School of Systems Engineering, Policy Analysis and Management, Delft University of Technology, Jaffalaan 5, PO Box 5015, 2600 GA DELFT, The Netherlands, email: acavaye@sepa.tudelft.nl

Abstract. A wide variety of approaches can be applied under the heading of case study research; this paper explicitly discusses the range of the alternatives. Many papers discussing case study research emphasize one particular variation of case study research only. The current paper provides an overview of the various uses of case study research in the information systems field by describing the different ways in which case study research can be used, using examples from published IS literature for illustration, and providing references to other method papers for more detailed discussion of each alternative. Researchers are reminded that case study research can be used in the positivist and interpretivist traditions, for testing or building theory, with a single or multiple case study design, using qualitative or mixed methods. The range of case study research alternatives makes it a highly versatile research strategy for IS.

Keywords: case study research, research methods, pluralism

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Case study research (hereafter referred to as case research) is an accepted research strategy in IS $^{1}$ . A number of publications identify case research as one of a range of IS research approaches (e.g. Galliers & Land, 1987; Cash et al., 1989; Galliers, 1992). However, the term 'case research' is not a monolithic one: case study methods can be applied and used in many different ways and, as such, case research is open to a lot of variation. Case research can be carried out taking a positivist or an interpretivist stance, can take a deductive or an inductive approach, can use qualitative and quantitative methods, can investigate one or multiple cases. Case research can be a highly structured, positivist, deductive investigation of multiple cases; it can also be an unstructured, interpretative, inductive investigation of one case; lastly, it can be anything in between these two extremes in almost any combination. Case research can be employed in a number of ways and can lead to different types of research output. The versatile and pluralistic nature of case research as a method for IS investigation has not previously been made explicit in the IS literature.

Confusion can be created as most authors present one particular variation of case research only. Some authors acknowledge the existence of other types of case research before delineating one particular area, or one type of case research, as the topic for discussion (e.g. Lee, 1989a; Walsham, 1995); others do not make reference to other types of case research and present their case research version as if it were the only one (e.g. Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1989a). By discussing one case research variation only, the impression may be created that that variation is the only, or the preferred, type of case research. In fact, although there may be individual preferences, there is no one accepted way of conducting case research.

There is a noticeable, dominant research approach in IS. Authors have demonstrated the dominance of positivist (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991) and contingency (Weill & Olson, 1989) approaches. This dominance has also affected the discussion and use of case research in IS: Benbasat et al. (1987), Lee (1989a) and Yin (1989a) all present positivist approaches to case research and present this as a desirable manner for carrying out case research in IS. In spite of the dominance of positivist style case research, there is a noticeable increase in the use of interpretative case research to study IS issues and papers discussing the interpretative case research strategy are beginning to appear in major IS journals (Walsham, 1995).

The objective of this paper is to provide a non-critical view of case research as a multi-faceted research approach for IS. It does not focus on any one particular type of case research, but aims to provide an overview of the various possibilities of case research highlighting its richness as a research approach. It is argued that case research is a pluralistic research approach that can be applied in many different ways. Major contributions to the IS methodological debate (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Weill & Olson, 1989) as well as papers discussing particular types of case research for IS (e.g. Lee, 1989a; Walsham 1995) are drawn upon.

The paper is structured as follows. First, case research is described and differentiated from other, related research strategies. Next, the varied nature of case research is discussed and a framework is presented which summarizes the various case research alternatives. Then the various case alternatives are described in some detail. Lastly, the main argument is summarized.

## 2 DEFINITION OF CASE RESEARCH

There is no generally accepted definition of case research. It is, however, possible to describe case research by listing its characteristics, and its strengths and weaknesses. A listing of characteristics is not enough to specifically identify case research strategy, as individual attributes may be shared with other strategies. For that reason, it is important to differentiate between case research and other strategies. This is achieved by listing research strategies similar to case research and then highlighting the differences between such strategies and case research.

## 2.1 Describing case research

Case research is a research approach which uses the case method $^{2}$ . Case method is a term which describes ‘a way to systemise observation’ (Weick, 1984). The case method:

— does not explicitly control or manipulate variables;

— studies a phenomenon in its natural context;

— studies the phenomenon at one of a few sites;

— makes use of qualitative tools and techniques for data collection and analysis $^{4}$ .

Use of the case method is one characteristic of case research. In addition, case research aims for in-depth understanding of the context of a phenomenon. Case research investigates a predefined phenomenon, but does not (though may) define a priori constructs and relationships. Case research aims to contribute to knowledge by relating findings to generalizable theory.

Just like all research strategies, case research has its strengths and weaknesses (e.g. Galliers, 1985; Yin, 1989b). In terms of strengths, case research enables the capturing of 'reality' and detail by studying a phenomenon in its natural context. It allows for the study of a large number of variables and different aspects of a phenomenon, while these need not have been previously determined. Case research is valuable in developing and refining concepts for further study. Multiple case studies enable the researcher to relate differences in context to constants in process and outcome.

Case research also has weaknesses. It is not possible to generalize case research findings statistically to a population. During case research one has no control over independent variables and this may limit the internal validity of any conclusions. Also, case research may establish relationships between variables, but cannot always indicate the direction of causation.

It is widely accepted that the selection of a research strategy entails a trade-off: the strengths of the one approach overcome the weaknesses in another approach and vice versa. This in itself is a powerful argument for pluralism and for the use of multiple research approaches during any investigation (e.g. Gable, 1994; Kaplan & Duchon, 1989). When a researcher selects case research as an appropriate research strategy for a study, the strengths of case research are considered of importance and the weaknesses are accepted as method-related limitations of the research.

## 2.2 Comparing case research with related strategies

Several research strategies make use of the case method; these strategies also share one or more of the other characteristics of case research. Apart from the shared characteristics, each of these strategies is quite distinct from case research. Each of the related strategies are briefly described.

## Field study

Field studies take place in the natural environment of the phenomenon. The researcher enters the field with a priori definition of constructs and relationships and uses systematic techniques (when administering questionnaires and conducting interviews) for the collection and recording of data. The researcher is an observer and has no intent of controlling or manipulating variables (Stone, 1978).

## Action research

Action research combines pure research (observing) with action (participation). The researcher does not define a research problem and its constructs beforehand but allows the problem to be defined by the site. There is no control over any of the variables. However, the researcher enters the field with the intention not only to observe and record, but also to actively take part in attempting to solve the problem at the site (Susman & Evered, 1978; Mansell, 1991).

## Application descriptions

Case application descriptions are accounts of actual events surrounding a phenomenon. They may be written by practitioners or by researchers. The authors do not enter the site to investigate a particular research question, but use the case description to inform readers of successful applications and developments or to illustrate a conceptual issue (Bonoma, 1985).

## Ethnographic research

Ethnographics seeks to understand the meaning of phenomena that participants at a site assign to the phenomenon (Sanday, 1979; Van Maanen, 1979a). The researcher does not enter a site with pre-defined constructs and, on observing and collecting data, does not assume the data to be factual. Instead of interpreting the data from a theoretical viewpoint or from the researcher's point of view, the data is interpreted through the eyes of the participants of the phenomenon. Distinguishing features of ethnography include a longitudinal approach (i.e. a presence in the field of at least a year) and the use of participant observation (i.e. to identify with participants in a process and at the same time remain distant from the process being studied).

## 3 PLURALITY OF CASE RESEARCH AS A RESEARCH APPROACH

Describing case research, and distinguishing it from related strategies, does not immediately imply that case research is a straightforward and monistic concept. The case research strategy allows for a great deal of flexibility and individual variation.

Many authors acknowledge the existence of other types of case research. Walsham (1995) mentions the positivist approach to case studies and then presents a lengthy discussion of interpretative case studies. Lee (1989a) acknowledge the traditional function of case research for 'suggesting hunches and generating theories for later testing'. He then mentions that in addition to this traditional approach, he puts forward his scientific methodology to enable case research to take a place among the research strategies that use natural science methods for testing theory. Eisenhardt (1989) acknowledges that case research can be used for description and testing theory, and then explains that she is focusing on the use of case research to generate theory. Eisenhardt balances the implicit favouring of a multiple case approach (Dyer & Wilkins, 1991) by discussing the value and use of single case research in a later article (Eisenhardt, 1991). These examples show that authors who discuss case methodology are usually aware of the multi-faceted nature of case research and of the possible bias their work may represent.

## 4.1 Case research epistemology

Epistemology refers to beliefs about the way in which knowledge is construed. Interpretivism and positivism rely on quite different assumptions about the nature of knowledge and demand different approaches to research. Case research, in its versatility, can be used in both traditions (Lee, 1989b; Galliers, 1992; Walsham, 1995). Case research can be used in an interpretative manner trying to understand the nature of a phenomenon and trying to elicit meaning from seemingly irrational behaviour in the social setting. Case research can also be employed in a positivist study: to measure pre-defined variables, according to pre-defined measures using pre-defined research instruments.

## Case research in the interpretivist tradition

The interpretative stance aims to understand phenomena from the point of view of participants directly involved with the phenomenon under study. Interpretivism seeks relevance of the research (Keen, 1991) by explicitly including investigation of the context of the phenomenon under study. Interpretative research does not enter a social setting with a priori constructs, but allows constructs to emerge whilst the researcher is in the field learning about and trying to understand the phenomenon.

Interpretative case studies are widely used in the social sciences, but have only recently gained prominence in the IS arena. A good and very readable reference concerning case research in the interpretative tradition is Walsham (1995).

Evaluation of interpretivist studies concerns the evaluation of the researcher's interpretation (Chua, 1986; Lee, 1991). A good interpretation is logically consistent, subjective and adequate. Logical consistency implies that the explanation of an event of behaviour must be compatible with the principles of logic. The subjectivity of the interpretation refers to the fact that the study must reflect meaning and understanding according to the actors in the research setting (c.f. phenomenology, Boland, 1985). Adequacy of an interpretivist study implies the researcher must show evidence of grasping and explaining the rationale behind actions/processes, however irrational they initially might appear (Lee, 1989b).

## Positivist case research

Positivist epistemology tries to understand a social setting by identifying individual components of a phenomenon and explains the phenomenon in terms of constructs and relationships between constructs. The theoretical constructs describing the phenomenon are considered to be distinct from empirical reality. Hence, empirical observations can be used to test theory. Positivism emphasises rigour in research (Keen, 1991) by focusing on theoretical grounding, on evidence and on persuasiveness of logical argument.

Though not traditionally used in this way, case research can be carried out under a positivist banner. Yin (1989a) discusses in detail the quasi-experimental use of case research; Lee (1989b) elaborates on the use of case research designed as natural experiments.

Both Yin (1989a,b) and Lee (1989a,b) pay much attention to positivist criteria for evaluating research, and they explain how case research can be designed to fulfil these criteria. Positivism employs four major research evaluation criteria: good research should make controlled observations, should be able to be replicated, should be generalizable and should use formal logic.

Case research cannot manipulate variables in order to make controlled observations, but use can be made of naturally occurring controls (e.g. by studying different instances of the phenomenon within one firm or within one industry). Case research cannot offer an exact replication of the case setting and circumstances, but it is possible to subject case research findings to the logic of replication. Employing logical and theoretical replication, findings can be replicated using either a similar setting to find similar results (for logical replication) and using a different setting to find different outcomes (for theoretical replication). Case research findings are not statistically generalizable to a population, as the case or cases cannot be considered representative of a population, however, case research can claim theoretical generalizability. Case results can be used to develop theoretical statements on which theory is built; that theory may then be tested for statistical generalizability if that is considered important or necessary. Case research does not have to employ mathematics and the statistical manipulation of data, as case data are predominantly qualitative. However, logic does not require the use of quantitative evidence and can be applied to verbal statements and qualitative evidence. In this way case research can use formal logic in analysis.

## Combining positivist and interpretivist approaches

Lee (1991) presents a thought-provoking framework that integrates positivist and interpretivist approaches. The basis of such integration is the recognition of three distinct levels of understanding concerning social reality. First level understanding refers to the understanding of reality as it appears to the human actors in their natural environment. Second level understanding refers to the understanding of the researcher who enters the field trying to interpret what is happening in the field. This concerns the interpretation or the researcher's understanding of the original, first level understanding (c.f. interpretivism). Third level understanding refers to the researcher's conceptualization and abstraction of second level understanding (c.f. positivism). The three levels constitute increasing degrees of abstraction or distancing from social reality, but ideally should all converge and, in essence, correspond. The positivist and interpretivist approaches need, therefore, not be viewed as irreconcilable and can be accepted as providing different views of the same phenomenon.

Case research can combine both schools of thought (Lee, 1989b). Interpretivist research in one study can support positivist research in another, or vice versa. Positivist and interpretivist methods can also both be present simultaneously and mutually supportive within the one study (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988; Gable, 1994).

## 4.2 Case research objective — description, discovery and testing of theory

A research study can describe phenomena, can build theory, or can test existing theoretical concepts and relationships. Case research can be used for all three: case research has a strong tradition of description and theory building; major proponents of case research advocate a deductive logic to case research emphasising the use of case research for testing (e.g. Yin, 1989b).

## Discovery and inductive use of case research

Discovery is concerned with description and conceptualization of phenomena. Conceptualization is achieved by generating hypotheses and developing explanations for observed relationships (Benbasat et al., 1988). Statements about relationships provide the basis for the building of theory.

There are a number of ways (some more and others less structured) of using case research for theory building. In their book The discovery of grounded theory, Glaser & Strauss (1967) describe a formalized approach to inductive case research; this approach has subsequently been refined (e.g. Glasser, 1978; Strauss, 1987; Corbin & Strauss, 1991). Grounded theory suggests that theory emerges as the researcher collects data surrounding a phenomenon. In order to facilitate theory emergence the researcher enters the field without a priori hypotheses, but with a good background knowledge of the literature. As the researcher collects data, the data are coded and categorized in order to facilitate abstraction and theory construction. Analysis takes place whilst collecting data, because analysis itself may suggest constructs or patterns which require further data collection. Data collection method, coding rationale, integration of categories, abstracting from the data, and construction of theory are, thus, guided by theory as it emerges.

Mintzberg (1979) describes his form of inductive research (which he calls 'direct research') as consisting of two parts: detective work and the creative leap. Detective work refers not only to collecting data, but also to the analysis taking place whilst collecting data: looking for patterns, commonalities, consistencies. The creative leap has analytical meaning: generalization from and beyond the data to draw theoretical conclusion.

Checkland's ‘soft systems methodology’ (1981) is a detailed method of carrying out research in unstructured situations. He proposes to first describe a situation or a problem, to identify major concepts (the ‘root definitions’) and then to take the important step towards conceptualization. The remainder of his methodology is particularly designed for problem solving: it validates the conceptual model with the real world, discusses possible changes to the problem situation and, lastly, takes action and implements desirable changes.

Eisenhardt (1989) presents yet another methodology for building theory from case research. She combines elements of grounded theory (opportunistic data collection, absence of a priori hypotheses) with structured methods (e.g. use of case protocol, a priori constructs and crafted research). Eight distinct phases of research are presented. Eisenhardt emphasizes the use of literature towards the end of the project, as an inductively derived theory can be compared to the existing literature (Miles & Huberman, 1984; Eisenhardt, 1989).

## Testing and the deductive use of case research

Testing is concerned with validating or disconfirming existing theory. Deduction is a means of testing theories according to the natural science model. The researcher uses theory to phrase propositions which are logical conclusions or predictions derived from the theory. The researcher then collects data pertaining to the propositions. The propositions are tested by comparing findings from observed reality (the collected data) with the expected outcome (theoretical propositions). The theory can thus be verified or confirmed. The more often and the more conclusively a theory is confirmed, the greater the researcher's confidence that the theory indeed may be a reasonable reflection of reality.

Yin (1989b) is a strong proponent of the deductive use of case research. His case research methodology starts with the construction of specific theoretical propositions derived from a theoretical model. During data collection, information is gathered on the individual variables indicated by the model. At the analysis stage, the factual conditions and relationships are compared to the theoretical one. Using the principles of analytical generalization, the early model is tested and may be modified according to case findings. This deductive approach is strongly advocated by Yin (1989a), Lee (1989a), Benbasat et al. (1987) and others as a valid use of case research in IS.

The use of case research for deductive purposes represents a non-traditional way of testing theory. The fact that case research has not traditionally been used for testing theory, does not make the case strategy any less appropriate for this purpose than, for instance, survey strategies. A research strategy aiming at validation or theory testing should be able to confirm or disconfirm existing theory. Since deductive logic can be used in both case and survey research, and appropriateness of employing a case research strategy for theory validation is unquestionable.

## Combining discovery and testing in case research

Deductive and inductive approaches can both be used in the same study. Deduction can follow theory generation, or inductive use of data may follow theory testing. Many inductive case research publications indicate a need for some testing of the theoretical model as part of the case research project (Miles & Huberman, 1984; Bonoma, 1985; Eisenhardt, 1989). By selecting cases which are different from, but conceptually similar to, the ones used in the early stages of the project, it is possible to ascertain whether the theory has predictive, generalizable qualities or whether the theory is the result of idiosyncrasy within the original case research settings. By selecting cases which are conceptually different from the original cases, the researcher can check for limitations of generalizations.

A second way of using discovery and testing within one research project concerns inductive investigation of data after the use of case research for theory testing. When the testing of a theory fails to provide confirmation of the original propositions, case research data can be used inductively to derive a theory that is grounded in empirical data. The data and the context can be re-examined, not to investigate 'fit' with a pre-determined mode, but to look for patterns within the data set. The aim of the second round of analysis is to theorize from the existing data.

The purposes of discovery and testing may also be achieved by combining case research with another research strategy in the same study (Gable, 1994). Building on the traditionally accepted strengths of case research in theory building, the first phase of a research project may rely on case research to define constructs, to identify relationships and to formulate hypotheses. Hypotheses can then be tested in a second stage of the research project using a research strategy traditionally accepted for its deductive capabilities, e.g. statistical survey research.

## 4.3 Single and multiple case design

Research design provides the basic framework for the investigation. A major decision in case research design is the decision to study either one single case or a set of cases. One cannot combine single and multiple case designs, though many studies do use elements of both designs. Single case studies with various units of analysis embedded within them are comparable to multiple case research. Multiple case research often uses a single case as a pilot case for the whole study.

## Single case design

Study of a single case enables the researcher to investigate a phenomenon in depth, getting close to the phenomenon, enabling a rich description and revealing its deep structure. The purpose of a single case study can be description, discovery or testing. A full and rich description of a rare phenomenon contributes to knowledge (Yin, 1989a). Single case studies enable theory building by developing and refining concepts (Pettigrew, 1985). Single cases can also be used for theory testing by confirming or disconfirming theory (Markus, 1989).

The investigation of a single case may be designed so that more than one unit of analysis is embedded within the case. The case itself (e.g. an organization) may provide the setting, but within that setting several instances of the phenomenon may be present. Contrasting observations from several units of analysis within one case can create and highlight theoretical constructs (Yin, 1989b; Eisenhardt, 1991).

## Multiple cases

Studying multiple cases may not enable the same rich descriptions as studies of single cases, but multiple cases enable analysis of data across cases. This enables the researcher to verify that findings are not merely the result of idiosyncrasies of the research setting (Miles & Huberman, 1984).

The number of cases to be studied in multiple case design is not pre-determined. The appropriate number of cases depends, firstly, on how much is known about the phenomenon after studying a case and, secondly, on how much new information is likely to emerge from studying further cases (Eisenhardt, 1991). The research questions and the data collected, together, determine at what point the researcher has collected data from sufficient cases to enable appropriate analysis. Most authors are vague when it comes to suggesting how many actual cases to study, but Eisenhardt (1989) suggests that multiple case design requires the study of at least four, but no more than 10 cases.

Cross-case analysis in the 1970s often took a case survey approach. Case surveys isolated factors and tabulated data from a large number of cases. By treating cases as data points, yielding observations for tabulation and extracting single factors from cases, case surveys simplify the phenomenon under study and defeat the purpose of studying the phenomenon in context (Yin, 1981).

Cross-case comparison constitutes a different approach to cross-case analysis. Comparison across cases relies on relating variability in context to constancy in process and outcomes (Pettigrew, 1985) using the logic of literal and theoretical replication (Lee, 1989a; Yin, 1989b). Literal replication of a case expects a second case, which is similar to the first in terms of context, to yield similar findings for process and outcome. Theoretical replication compares cases which are different in context, expecting to find different outcomes.

## 4.4 Case research methods — qualitative and quantitative data

Method indicates the type of research tools and techniques which may be used to collect empirical evidence. There are two basic types of data collection methods: nomothetic or quantitative methods (based on numerical data) and ideographic or qualitative methods (based primarily on verbal data). Some case research uses only qualitative data; the majority of case combines qualitative and quantitative methods.

## Qualitative methods

A qualitative investigation is interested in distilling meaning and understand from a phenomenon and is not primarily concerned with measuring and quantification of the phenomenon. Qualitative researchers consider that it is not possible to assign meaning to a phenomenon (or behaviour) without describing the context and without understanding the position of the people who affect or are affected by the phenomenon (or behaviour) (e.g. Van Maanen, 1979b; Boland, 1985). Direct and in-depth knowledge of a research setting are necessary to achieve contextual understanding. Hence, qualitative methods are associated with face-to-face contact with persons in the research setting, with verbal data and with observations (Van Mannen, 1989b).

Qualitative data can be collected in a number of forms. One major form of qualitative evidence is interviews, which may be recorded and later transcribed. Another source of qualitative data are field notes describing observed events. Qualitative data can also be derived from written documents, papers, archives. Qualitative data are ‘rich, full, holistic, “real”; their face validity seems unimpeachable; they preserve chronological flow where that is important…’ (Miles, 1979).

In spite of strengths in terms of relevance and richness, qualitative data have weaknesses (Miles, 1979; Miles & Huberman, 1984). Collecting and analysing data is time-consuming and demanding. As data of many types are collected, and in many forms, the researcher can be overwhelmed by their volume. The sheer amount and variety of data may inhibit data analysis. In addition, data analysis is not easy, as qualitative data analysis methods are not well-established. The relative difficulty of analysing qualitative data does not invalidate the date or the conclusions ultimately drawn from them. Recognised rules of logic can be applied to verbal data in order to make sense of the evidence and to formally analyse the data.

## Combining qualitative and quantitative methods

Representatives of both the quantitative and the qualitative methods camps have acknowledged the value of the other method. Campbell (1975), previously a major advocate for quantitative methods, displays a remarkable turnaround in his thinking by arguing the value of qualitative methods. A decade later Campbell (1989) wrote the foreword to a major publication on case research (Yin, 1989b). Glaser and Strauss (1967), major proponents of qualitative methods, consider there to be 'no fundamental clash between the purposes and capacities of qualitative and quantitative methods or data'.

Accepting that there is nothing fundamentally wrong with either of the two methods, it can next be argued that both methods might be fruitfully combined (Reichardt & Cook, 1989). A number of reasons have been put forward for combining methods: it enables the building of a fuller, richer picture surrounding a phenomenon than either method on its own would be able; it enables cross-validation of findings through triangulation (Jick, 1979); it helps find explanations for diverging results (Trend, 1979).

Qualitative and quantitative data can be combined and used together, at the same time or one after the other, in the same setting or in different settings. Firstly, qualitative and quantitative data on a phenomenon can be collected at the same time. Case researchers tend to collect both qualitative and quantitative data from sites. Analysis of quantitative data is then often restricted to frequency counts and ordinal ranking. Case research can also have a strong quantitative flavour. McClintock et al. (1979) introduce a case research approach which relies on enumeration, sampling and the creation of a quantitative data set. Projects which include multiple researchers can employ researchers using different methods alongside one another (e.g. Kaplan & Duchon, 1988).

A second way of combining qualitative and quantitative methods within a study is to collect both types of data one after another from the same site. Qualitative initial observations can precede quantitative measurement in order to help formulate the theoretical structure underlying a survey study. Conversely, qualitative probing may be called for after quantitative methods, particularly when quantitative analysis has produced unusual or unexpected findings (Sieber, 1973).

A third way of combining the methods is to collect different data one after another from different sites. Qualitative methods could be used to collect data in an initial, exploratory, formulating stage of a project; quantitative methods could be employed to gather data on specified variables from a large number of respondents from different sites in a subsequent testing phase (Gable, 1994).

## 5 CONCLUDING COMMENTS

This paper has emphasized the fact that case research can be used in a number of ways and that all such variations are valid, legitimate uses of the case research strategy. This means that case research is a highly versatile strategy for use in IS.

Traditionally, a contingency approach is advocated for the selection of research strategy. Case research is considered to be particularly appropriate when theoretical knowledge on a phenomenon is limited or when the need for capturing context is important. However, it is clear that case research can be used not only for description and discovery, but also for the testing of theory. Case research is, thus, an appropriate research strategy at any stage of knowledge on a phenomenon. It can be argued that the capturing of context is always important when discussing people-related and organisational phenomena and that, therefore, case research is always an appropriate research strategy in IS.

From a pluralistic standpoint, any research approach may be appropriate at any one time for the study of IS phenomena, as each strategy throws light on the same phenomenon in a different way (Lee, 1991; Klein et al., 1991). IS is by nature a pluralistic field (Banville & Landry, 1989) which implies that the use of different approaches and methods is appropriate and valid. Pluralism argues that it is the combined knowledge gained from using a variety of research strategies that enables a truly full and rich body of knowledge on a phenomena to emerge.

Whether approaching selection of a research strategy from a contingency or from a pluralistic viewpoint, there are few, if any, research situations where case research would be inappropriate (Galliers & Land, 1987). The multi-faceted nature of case research means that case research is an appropriate strategy to investigate many issues, from different epistemological standpoints, at various stages of knowledge, using various methods.

This paper has demonstrated that case research can be carried out validly in a number of ways. It has not been the author's intention to favour one type over another, but merely to point out the pluralism of case research as an IS strategy. It is hoped that discussion of case research in this way may encourage a wider recognition of the multi-faceted nature of the strategy and, also, that it may stimulate the publication of a wider range of case-based empirical research in IS journals.
