# A cross-cultural comparison of 'excellent' systems analysts

M. Gordon Hunter & \*John E. Beck

School of Business, Georgetown University, 37th and '0' Street NW, Washington, DC 20057, USA, \*Division of Human Resource and Quality Management, School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 2263

Abstract. As business becomes established with a more international focus, there is a growing globalization of the information systems (IS) function. The need for compatible standards and procedures within these international networks means that the systems analyst will play a key role in this globalization process. It is, therefore, considered timely that there is a developing body of research into the effects of cultural differences on the design and development of IS. This article reports research into the way in which 'excellent' systems analysts are perceived in Canada and Singapore. The research method adopted the RepGrid technique from Kelly's Theory of Personal Constructs, which served as the vehicle to facilitate the elicitation of participant comments. Seventeen interviews were conducted in Singapore compared with 53 in Canada. The results suggest that, in terms of the overall approach and priorities that systems analysts place on their work, there is evidence of a commonality, which unites the profession in these two countries. This may be evidence of convergence, which occurs as the result of common education, training and socialization of new entrants into the international occupational community of systems analysts. However, there is also strong evidence of divergence between these two countries based on the different emphasis on the way in which systems analysts will play their roles in each country. As a reflection of the culture of each society, the Singaporean systems analysts are more likely to rely on expertise to influence clients, whereas the Canadian systems analysts rely more on encouraging the client to participate in the design effort.

Keywords: Systems analysts, cross-cultural research, qualitative research methods

## INTRODUCTION

As business becomes more international and as multinational corporations (MNCs) have established themselves globally, technologies and techniques that were developed in the advanced economies of the West have been transferred to less advanced countries. How portable are these technologies and techniques? Do they survive more or less intact as they cross international and cultural boundaries? Or are they changed, perhaps improved, when they pass into a different culture? Is there a difference between the way in which 'excellent' systems analysts are interpreted from one culture to another? These issues are not merely of academic interest, but also have valuable practical implications. Can a systems analyst who has successfully interacted with users in one culture adopt the same approach in another culture? In an increasingly interdependent world the needs for well-integrated and coherent information systems (IS), which will co-ordinate the activities of MNCs, international agencies and governments, will require compatible standards of professionalism of systems analysts in different cultures. Is it likely that such common standards will become established worldwide? This article responds to these questions by reporting upon research conducted regarding cultural influences on systems analysts by comparing the results of research conducted in two different cultures, Singapore and Canada. More specifically, are the interpretations of what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst in Singapore different from the interpretations in Canada?

There are two competing hypotheses about what will happen to different cultures as the world becomes more industrialized and modernized. These hypotheses are discussed by Webber (1969), Yang (1986) and Ronen (1986). The first hypothesis suggests that, as a result of modernization and global industrialization, societies throughout the world will converge and become more similar to one another. A number of forces create such convergence; for example, the development of a universal technology and the consequent creation of jobs, which are essentially the same no matter which culture they are performed in. A second force is education and training, influencing not only skills but also attitudes and values towards employment. These two forces seem to be particularly relevant to the development of the global IS profession.

In contrast, the divergence hypothesis suggests that cultures tend to be resilient and resistant to external interculture assimilation. In particular, it is the way in which people relate to each other that will be resistant to integration as societies strive to retain their cultural distinctiveness. This hypothesis has implications for systems analysts for it is their interactions with users within a specific culture, and the development of an appropriate IS, which will determine the success and utility of the IS.

At one and the same time, therefore, there seem to be forces at work that will bring uniformity and standardization to the skills and activities of the systems analysts working in different cultures, and also forces that would encourage and reinforce differences in their approach to work. The focus of the exploratory study described here is to investigate whether there is convergence or divergence in the perception of 'excellent' system analysts between samples of business and IS professionals in Singapore and Canada. The field of comparative IS research is relatively new and, as far as the authors are aware, no other similar study has been conducted. In view of this, no formal hypotheses about the extent or type of differences between the samples are proposed. Instead, an exploratory analysis of the data using non-parametric methods will be performed.

Over the past two decades, there has been a growing body of literature, which suggests that managerial practices are affected by the cultural influences of the society in which they are practised. This study investigates whether similar effects are seen in the behaviour of systems analysts working in different cultures.

The nature and extent of cultural differences has been researched in a landmark study by Hofstede (1980). He conceptualizes 'culture' as the 'collective programming of the mind'. As individuals grow up in any culture, their assumptions about the way a society should be organized are instilled into them. These taken-for-granted assumptions are rarely questioned, unless perhaps there is contact with another culture that operates on different or incompatible assumptions. In pursuing the implications of this research, Hofstede (1980, 1983, 1993), Hofstede and Bond (1988) and Hofstede et al. (1990) have shown that certain management practices can be compatible, and others incompatible, with the culture of a society, and has gone on to suggest that cultural incompatibility undermines the successful transfer of accepted Western managerial practices to developing countries. In an interesting case study on the acceptance of an IS designed in the US for a subsidiary in Latin America, Robey and Rodriguez-Diaz (1989) have shown how organizational culture incompatibility between corporate headquarters and the subsidiary can lead to problems in implementation. These problems were subsequently overcome when, at another site, the team implementing the same technological change behaved with greater cultural sensitivity.

Hofstede (1980) identified four major dimensions along which it was possible to distinguish one society from another. These dimensions, along which all societal cultures in the world can be located, are presented in Table 1. These dimensions can be illustrated by identifying those countries, which exemplify the poles of each dimension. Individualistic societies in which people are expected to be independent and look after themselves are found typically in North America and Western Europe. Collectivist societies, those with tightly knit mutual social obligations, are found in East Asia and Latin America. Small power distance cultures, which aim for power equalization, are found in Scandinavia, whereas large power distance cultures are found in South Asia and Latin America. Strong uncertainty avoidance cultures control uncertainty by strict rules and codes of behaviour and include Greece, Guatemala and Uruguay. Weak uncertainty avoidance cultures are less control oriented and include Singapore, Jamaica and Denmark. Masculine societies emphasize achievement, success and assertiveness and are exemplified by Japan, Austria and Venezuela. The most feminine nations are Sweden, Norway and the Netherlands.

These are the general dimensions along which all cultures in the world can be located. However, there has been speculation that the neo-Confucian culture of East Asia might be one reason for the rapid economic growth in the region over the past four decades (Kahn, 1979; Bond & Hofstede, 1988; Redding, 1990). Perhaps it is the neo-Confuscan culture of Singapore that shows the greatest contrast with the more individualistic cultures of Canada and the rest of the Western world. While it is not possible to capture the nuances of Confucius' teachings in this article, it is possible to identify simple themes or values that act as guidelines for behaviour in neo-Confucian societies.

At the core of Confucian philosophy is the central role of the family in creating a stable society. The family acts as a model for all relationships within that society. There is a strong emphasis on 'harmony', not only within the family, but in society as a whole. People are expected to control their individualistic needs and subsume them for the collective good. Interpersonal conflict or disagreeable behaviour are seen as disrupting relationships and are frowned upon. This value is clearly similar to the Collectivism pole identified by Hofstede (1980).

Stability in Confucian society is not only based on harmony but also on unequal power relationships between people. Every relationship is seen as a partnership between a senior and junior partner, which places mutual obligations on both partners. The junior partner owes the senior respect and obedience, the senior owes the junior protection and consideration. This value is very similar to Hofstede's concept of 'High' Power Distance.

Another principle in dealing with others in Confucian society is that you should afford them the same respect and courtesy that you would expect from them. This principle of reciprocity not only promotes harmony, but is particularly important in giving 'face' or dignity to another. In showing respect for a person, one is enhancing their social standing. Disrespect, or causing a person to lose face, will never be forgotten nor forgiven. This principle does not seem to be covered directly in Hofstede's work, but it might be related to Collectivism.

The value of work is another important principle from Confucianism. A major task in life is to acquire skills and education, to work hard, be thrifty, patient and persevere at tasks until successful. This principle does not seem to be consistent with Hofstede's dimensions. However, in later work on a survey of Chinese values Hofstede and Bond (1988) report a factor, which they call 'Confucian Work Dynamism'. This factor comprises items measuring persistence, status order, thrift and a sense of shame. Hofstede and Bond (1988) have correlated scores on this factor with the growth in Gross Domestic Product of a number of nations, and suggest that it is this factor that has contributed to the recent spectacular economic growth of some nations in East Asia.

For the IS profession, the implications of these cultural differences might be dramatic. The successful development, evaluation and maintenance of information systems will in part be dependent upon user acceptance. That acceptance may in turn be heavily dependent upon the relationship that systems analysts establish with the user. In order to establish a good relationship with users, systems analysts will have to behave in ways that are compatible with the culture in which they are working. If the cultural pressures to behave in particular ways are sufficiently strong, then we may observe cultural differences in the way in which systems analysts construe their roles. These differences might also be reflected in differences in the way in which users construe the behaviour of systems analysts, since their expectations of what is considered ‘appropriate’ behaviour might vary from one cultural context to another.

Ein-Dor et al. (1993) suggest, 'the consideration of cultural differences at the individual level and their effects on IS construction and use is an extremely important issue in the research of international IS'. They surveyed a number of research articles relating to cultural variables affecting information systems. From a list of ten findings, the top two relate directly to systems analysts. The first finding states, '... cultural factors have a greater impact on technical aspects of IS than on behavioral aspects'. The second findings states, '... while ... analysts had similar individual user models, their organizational user models differed'. This seems to suggest there may be technological divergence, but behavioural convergence between systems analysts in different cultures in the way in which they interact with their customers. The technological divergence is perhaps not surprising, since heavy investment in technology may create and maintain exit barriers preventing the adoption of newer technology. However, the evidence for behavioural consistency between different cultures is an interesting result, since it would indicate that cultural differences do not influence the role that systems analysts play in relation to the user. If the Ein-Dor et al. (1993) results are generally applicable, then one would predict that there is consistency in the way in which the role of systems analysts was construed across different cultures.

The research described in this article compares the differing perceptions of the role of 'excellent' systems analysts between two samples, one in Canada and another in Singapore. In terms of Hofstede's dimensions, Singapore and Canada are societies that are different in a number of important ways. Canada is a highly individualistic society, whereas Singapore is much more collectivist in social relationships. In terms of Power Distance, Canada is a small Power Distance country, whereas Singaporeans are more comfortable with a relatively unequal distribution of power. In Hofstede's (1980) sample, Singapore was the society that had the weakest Uncertainty Avoidance score; however, Canada is also a society that is relatively comfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity. Finally, there is very little difference between the two cultures on the masculinity – femininity dimension. Therefore, these two cultures show quite marked differences on two of Hofstede's dimensions, but lesser differences on the other two dimensions.

Over and above the differences described by Hofstede are the differences between a Confucian and a Western society. The way in which these cultural differences might affect the behaviour of systems analysts in Singapore and Canada will be dependent on how the systems analysts construe their role and how their role is construed by other stakeholders. As a consultant, the systems analyst relies more on expert power than formal authority to influence his/her clients (French & Raven, 1959). Markus and Bjorn-Andersen (1987) suggest that, if both IS professionals and business professionals, in general, have an understanding of different types of power involved in their interpersonal relationships, the resulting IS may be more acceptable to both parties. In Confucian societies, knowledge, skills and expertise are revered, and the systems analyst may expect, and may be shown, greater compliance (Kelman, 1970) than a Western counterpart. This has both advantages and disadvantages. The systems analyst in Singapore might experience less user resistance in implementing the system, but also may have less user participation in the design. This leads to difficulty in forging a partnership in which there is joint ownership and commitment to the system.

Similarly, since overt expressions of conflict and disagreement are avoided in Confucian societies, the systems analyst needs to be very sensitive and skilful in determining when the user does not feel comfortable with a system, especially when the user believes that the systems analyst has got it wrong. In contrast, a Canadian systems analyst might expect to receive fairly forthright expressions of disagreement about their design. While it is true that all systems analysts need some political or diplomatic skills, the need to maintain others' 'face' in a Confucian society probably demands greater political awareness than in the West. Time will need to be spent on building relationships with users, and while this may be true in both societies, the significance of relationship building in Singapore is likely to be greater than in Canada.

The rationale for the research described here is that, if there are cultural differences in the way in which systems analysts perform their roles, then these differences are most likely to be observable in the behaviour of 'excellent' systems analysts. There may be many reasons for these differences. The concept of an 'excellent' systems analyst might vary from one culture to another or there may be different expectations of the behaviour, skills and abilities of the 'excellent' systems analyst in different societies. The assumption is that 'excellent' systems analysts will be those who not only develop excellent technical systems, but in doing so fulfil their roles in ways that accommodate the local culture. They are likely to experience fewer interpersonal problems and find that their systems have a greater level of acceptance from the users.

Because the functions performed by systems analysts are based on technologies and techniques that have originated mainly in developed economies (i.e. those of the Western cultures), problems concerning interpersonal relationships may arise, if systems analysts in Eastern countries attempt to perform their duties by following a 'Western' role model of how to behave. This article contributes to the growing body of knowledge on cultural influences on systems analyst behaviour by proposing a methodology to obtain rich qualitative data on the perceptions of 'excellent' systems analysts in different cultures. It presents some preliminary findings that will form the basis of a more comprehensive research programme.

## RELATED RESEARCH

A number of research projects have been conducted into IS personnel, which have focused on the aspects of motivation and satisfaction, personality, basic skills and specific skills. Because of the relative youth of the field, most of these studies have been conducted within a single culture, usually a Western society.

A number of research projects have investigated aspects relating to systems analyst motivation and satisfaction. Goldstein and Rockart (1984) suggested that training in management and interpersonal skills would improve job satisfaction by reducing role conflict and ambiguity. Ein-Dor and Segev (1986) found a high correlation between attitudes, association and the resulting success of IS. Garden investigated IS professionals' propensity to change jobs (Garden, 1988a) and excitement levels (Garden, 1988b). A series of research projects (Couger et al., 1979; Couger, 1988; Couger & Adelsberger, 1988; Couger et al., 1989) used the Job Diagnostic Survey technique to measure motivation of IS professionals. These studies investigated factors, such as satisfaction levels and need for personal growth. The results indicated that IS professionals' social needs were very low (lower than any occupation previously measured), while growth needs were very high (higher than all previously measured occupations).

Buie (1988), Smith (1988) and Lyons (1985), using the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) as a research tool, all found that the distribution of systems analyst personality types differed from the general population. Their research determined that there was a disparity between the inherent proclivity of these personality types and the required characteristics of the tasks to be performed by systems analysts. Further studies have suggested that there are differences between IS and non-IS workers in terms of the level of their social and achievement needs (Bartol & Martin, 1982); however, Ferratt and Short (1988) suggest that IS and non-IS workers are not and should not be managed differently. These research results suggest that there may exist divergence within one culture (i.e. systems analysts vs. the rest) and could support the contention that IS represents an emerging occupational community.

Some time ago, Arvey and Hoyle (1973, 1974) outlined the major components of the systems analysis function. Crocker (1984) subsequently surveyed systems analysts regarding tasks performed and their duration. Vitalari (1985) investigated the knowledge base of systems analysts. Cheney and Lyons (1980) developed a list of basic knowledge requirements, and Cheney (1988) conducted a follow-up study, which determined that human relations seemed more important as a result of increased user involvement. Cheney et al. (1989) further investigated skills requirements and reached the conclusion that, 'the trend for systems analysts/designers is toward increased knowledge of people and problem-solving skills, and away from developing application software'. McCubbrey and Scudder (1988) determined that communication and organizational skills are important for systems analysts of the future. These research projects indicate the changing nature of the systems analyst function and the increasing importance of interpersonal skills.

Some research projects have taken a more focused approached to identifying the skill requirements of systems analysts. Benbasat et al. (1980) found that systems analysts perceived generalist skills to be more useful than specialist skills. Vitalari and Dickson (1983) found more similarities than differences between problem-solving thought processes of high- and low-rated systems analysts. Other studies found that important skills for systems analysts related to personnel management and communications (Barrett & Davis, 1986) and interviewing (Litecky, 1985). Maxwell (1986) investigated the personality characteristics of successful systems analysts. The results of his research emphasize the importance of interpersonal skills. However, his definition of 'successful' did not support any differentiation based on data emerging from the research, which inhibits the subsequent application of the results. Guinan (1988) found that those involved in IS development (managers, systems analysts and users) would benefit from knowledge about effective communication. This may result in improved quality of interaction during development. Leitheiser and Fouad (1993) have proposed research into the communication problems, which might occur when systems analysts and users are from different subcultures.

In a recent article, Watson et al. (1994) describe research into the impact of Group Support Systems (GSS) on decision-making behaviour of groups in Singapore and the US. They conclude that cultural differences do moderate the influence of GSS, in particular Singapore groups showed greater equality of influence than their US counterparts, but that in Singapore groups with a high level of premeeting consensus, GSS led to unequal influence. One interesting result was that, although cultural differences were apparent in the level of premeeting consensus, these differences had disappeared in the consensus achieved at the end of the meeting.

This body of research aims for a better understanding of the characteristics of systems analysts and the nature of their work. The trend in this research seems to indicate a changing emphasis in the role of systems analysts away from application development towards general problem-solving ability and interpersonal skills. This changing emphasis makes cross-cultural research on these issues an imperative, since the way in which systems analysts perform their roles might vary from one culture to another. Recall that this research investigates how each culture interprets what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst within each respective culture. On the one hand, similarity would suggest an emerging occupational community, above and beyond the specific culture. On the other hand, a difference would suggest that culture plays an important role in how this function is performed. So much so that to be considered 'excellent' in one culture may not lead to the same conclusion in another culture.

## RESEARCH METHOD

When asked what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst, peers may reflect upon past experience with those systems analysts they have held in high regard. Users work directly with the information system (e.g. hands-on payroll clerks). Clients interact directly with the information system like users and are also responsible for budget considerations like sponsors. These three audiences represent another stakeholder group commonly referred to as 'Business Professional'.

The methodology used in this study is based on Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), which suggests that categories and their properties should emerge from the data, rather than being influenced by the a priori adoption of a theoretical framework. The approach allows for both similarities and differences to emerge between the samples. The extent to which there are similarities or differences between the two samples will determine the extent to which there seems to be convergence or divergence in the perceptions of 'excellent' systems analysts. The research used the RepGrid technique from Kelly's Theory of Personal Constructs (Kelly, 1955, 1963), which brings structure to the interview, while allowing flexibility and reducing researcher bias. RepGrids have been used in this case to generate a large amount of rich, detailed, qualitative and narrative data relating to a research participant's explanation of an elicited construct.

The research reported here represents the next step in an ongoing project regarding the qualities of 'excellent' systems analysts. The original research, conducted by Hunter (1992, 1993, 1994) attempted to identify how audiences differentiated 'excellent' systems analysts. The project included 53 participants from two companies in Canada representing five groups of individuals, or audiences, who interact with systems analysts. The interviews were conducted at both companies' head offices in western Canada. One company is a large member-owned cooperative organization specializing in providing services related to the production and sale of various grain crops. The other company is a diversified group of companies, which provide insurance and financial services. Overall, in the original research, the more important aspects that emerged included communication, attitude, knowledge, investigation and experience as the key characteristics of an 'excellent' systems analyst. The detailed definitions of these aspects varied among the audiences. For instance, user audiences emphasized content-related aspects, such as knowledge. Systems analysts and their supervisors differentiated on process-related aspects, such as investigation. Each audience had its own interpretation of the relative importance of what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst. Based upon these research findings, the companies involved in the project have revised their approach to hiring and training systems analysts. The fieldwork for the current project was conducted at a large Singapore-based airline. The company is an international air carrier providing air passenger and air freight services throughout the world from its base in Singapore. Interviews were conducted with 17 participants. While the selection of these three specific fieldwork sites was more a reflection of convenience and availability, it is worth noting that they are, more importantly, considered to be representative of organizations from two rather different cultures.

The first step in the development of the RepGrid is the identification of elements. In this project, the participants were asked to list six systems analysts with whom they had worked. The level of interaction should be sufficient to allow the participant to have formed an opinion of the performance of the systems analyst. Elements 1–6 represent specific systems analysts. The participant was also asked to list two further elements, 'ideal' and 'incompetent', which are represented in the RepGrid by elements 7 and 8. It is not necessary for these titles to represent specific individuals. It is sufficient only that these titles represent the participant's conception of either an 'ideal' or 'incompetent' systems analyst. The participant was not asked for a definition of these two titles.

Indeed, the process used here provides a tool for the documentation of the participant's interpretation of these titles, used as a comparison 'anchors' in relation to the six actual systems analysts. The names of the actual systems analysts plus the two added elements were transferred to individual recipe cards. With one name or title per card, the participant was presented with a series of randomly determined recipe cards (referred to as a 'triad') and asked to identify, with regard to the skills of an 'excellent' systems analyst, how two elements within the triad were alike, yet different from the third. So, for example, the participant may suggest that two elements had 'good communication skills', while the third had 'poor communication skills'. Thus, a construct was elicited based specifically on the participant's interpretation resulting from their experience in working with systems analysts. The contents of Figure 2 represent an example of an elicited construct and subsequent descriptive comments. Thus, 'good communication skills' represents a positive aspect, while 'poor communication skills' represents the negative component of the elicited construct. It should be noted that the elicited construct and the detailed comments represent what the research participant said at this time during the interview. For each administration of a triad, a bipolar construct was elicited, entered into the RepGrid and extensive interview notes were taken in support of the construct. The participant was also asked to rate each element in relation to the elicited construct by assigning a number to each element based upon a nine-point rating scale. The numbers in the cells of the RepGrid represent the participant's rating with '1' representing the best and '9' representing the worst. Invariably, the 'ideal' element was assigned a rating of 1 and the 'incompetent' element was assigned a 9.

As part of the RepGrid triadic elicitation process, the participant would offer a general comment about how two elements (systems analysts) differed from a third. This general comment would be pursued in order to obtain from the participant detailed statements about what was meant by the general statement. This 'laddering' process led to 985 individual constructs (detailed statements) documented in the detailed interview notes for the 17 participants in Singapore and 2727 individual constructs for the 53 participants in Canada.

Therefore, as indicated in Figure 3, each path has been associated with a theme, titled 'Knowledge' or 'Communication' in this specific example. For instance, the path.

understands problem well

... leads to ...

understands system well

... leads to ...

good communication skills

suggests that understanding the problem assists in understanding the system; and in conjunction with the other paths, leads to good communication. This series of statements has been organized, as shown in Figure 2, with the most general construct at the top and the most detailed at the bottom, thus indicating progressively more detailed statements about each elicited construct. It is further suggested, then, that this series (or path) of statements may be associated with the theme 'knowledge'. In this manner, a theme was identified for each path that existed for all of the COPE models involved in this research project. Note that some concepts, then, may appear in more than one theme. Thus, the label for the path (i.e. the theme) has been identified in relation to the content of all of the concepts that form the path.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Table 4, which presents the occurrence of themes by audience for Singapore, represents a summary of the number of times audience members mentioned a specific theme. This count of the number of mentions may be used to represent the relative importance of each theme regarding an interpretation of the skills and personal characteristics of ‘excellent’ systems analysts. Thus, a theme is considered to be relatively more important the more times it emerges from the data.

Table 5 presents the results obtained from the research conducted in Canada. Overall, the first 11 themes from each source are the same as the Singapore sample, with some differences in the relative levels of importance. The last theme in the Singapore data and the last three themes in the Canada data are unique to each source. The top three themes (attitude, knowledge and communicate) are the same for both studies.

In order to analyse the data, the users, sponsors and clients audiences were combined to form a business professional stakeholder group. Also, the systems analysts and business systems managers audiences were combined to form the IS professional stakeholder group. This pooling was carried out for both the Canada and Singapore data. A Spearman rank correlation between the Singapore and Canada IS professional stakeholders was performed. The data indicates $[rho = +0.791 (P < 0.01)]$ that IS professionals in Canada and Singapore perceive the characteristics of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst in a similar way. This provides evidence that between these two countries there is a shared conception of the role and professional qualities of a systems analyst. This is an interesting result and supports the conclusion of Ein-Dor et al. (1993) that there is evidence of behavioural convergence in the way in which systems analysts construe their role. The implication is that this might indicate the development of a global IS profession. The characteristics of a global profession, identified by Von Glinow (1988), are a common body of knowledge and expertise, the application of professional judgement to a bounded series of problems, commitment and identification with the profession, the maintenance of shared values, ethics and standards by peer consensus. In related work, Van Maanen and Barley (1984) defined an ‘occupational community’ as becoming established when a group of people see themselves engaged in work, which transcends the organization in which they are employed. The research results raise the possibility that IS professionals might be members of a global occupational community whose work not only transcends the organization, but also the society, in which they are working.

Similarly, a Spearman rank correlation between the Singapore and Canada business professional stakeholder groups was performed. The data indicates $[rho = +0.681 (P < 0.01)]$ that the business professional stakeholder groups in Singapore and Canada also perceive the characteristics of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst in a similar way. One explanation for this result is that the systems analysts have managed to create a recognizable ‘professional identity’ with the business professional stakeholder group in their countries. Again, this may provide evidence of the development of an international occupational community, since the systems analysts in both countries may have educated their customers to have similar expectations about their professional behaviour.

However, a chi-square analysis of the frequencies with which the business professionals in Canada and Singapore report the various themes does show a significant difference between the samples. The analysis was completed after those cells having an expected value of less than 5 were removed. The calculated value of chi-square is 57.148 with d.f. = 10 and P = 0.000. This result indicates that there are significant differences in emphasis in the way in which 'excellent' systems analysts are seen by business professionals in Canada and Singapore. The Singapore sample places greater emphasis on the themes of 'attitude' and 'knowledge' than their Canadian counterparts. The Canadians, on the other hand, place greater emphasis on 'communication', 'plan', 'investigation', 'flexible', 'experience' and 'involve user'.

Similarly, a chi-square analysis of the frequencies of themes identified by IS professionals also shows a significant difference between the Canadian and Singapore samples. The calculated value of chi-square is 65.956 with d.f.=11 and P=0.000. Again, the Singapore sample placed greater emphasis on themes of 'attitude', 'knowledge' and 'design' in describing the qualities of an 'excellent' systems analyst compared with their Canadian counterparts. The Canadians, on the other hand, emphasize 'communication', 'investigation', 'experience', 'thorough', 'involve user' and 'delegation' more than the Singapore sample.

Taken as a whole, these results suggest a very different picture of the way in which an 'excellent' systems analyst is perceived in each society. The emphasis in Singapore is much more on the professional attitude and technical skills that a systems analyst can bring to bear on IS problems. This is perhaps not surprising in a society in which education is revered and which has a well-developed meritocratic tradition. This might suggest that systems analysts become the dominant partners in determining the way in which a project should proceed with their clients. Their influence is based largely on expert power, and the success of the project will depend largely on their technical input. As a thumbnail sketch the picture of the 'excellent' Singaporean systems analyst is that of 'technocrat'.

The Canadian view of the ‘excellent’ systems analyst produces a different picture. The emphasis is much more on the development of a participative relationship between the systems analyst and the user. The interaction between the systems analyst and the user in a thorough investigation of the project is seen as being crucial to success. While the technical expertise of the systems analyst is still important, what is also important is the way in which the client is managed. Good client management skills could improve the technical quality and the implementation of the resulting IS. The thumbnail sketch of the 'excellent' Canadian systems analyst is closer to that of 'consultant'.

The evidence presented here of behavioural divergence in the way in which 'excellent' systems analysts are construed would contradict the conclusion of Ein-Dor et al. (1993) that culture has a greater impact on technical rather than behavioural aspects of IS. If, as seems possible from these preliminary results, the 'excellent' systems analyst is behaving differently in those two societies, then clearly culture touches all aspects of IS development throughout the world.

## CONCLUSION

The introduction to this article outlined the convergence and divergence hypotheses on the effect of modernization and industrialization on the societal cultures of the world. From the data presented here, there seems to be evidence to support both convergence and divergence hypotheses. This is not as paradoxical as it may seem. From the ranking of the themes, there seems to be concordance between the Singapore and Canadian samples about the importance of various activities to the perception of ‘excellence’ as a systems analyst.

In terms of the overall approach and priorities that the systems analyst places on his/her work, there is evidence of a commonality, which unites the profession in these two countries. This may be evidence of convergence that occurs as a result of common education, training and the socialization of new entrants into the international occupational community of systems analysts.

However, there is also strong evidence of divergence between these two countries. These are differences in emphasis in the way in which the systems analysts will play out their roles in each country. The Singaporean systems analysts are more likely to rely on their professional attitudes and expertise to influence clients, whereas the Canadian systems analysts rely more on encouraging the client to participate in the design process. These differences can be seen to reflect the culture of each society. Singapore, with its strong Confucian values towards education and learning and its acceptance of high power distance, is likely to encourage the development of a technocratic style among its systems analysts. Canada, on the other hand, as a relatively low power distance society, is likely to encourage behaviour in which the client is expected and allowed to participate in the design process. The systems analyst in this situation becomes the consultant in a ‘partnership’ relationship with the client.

Therefore, if there is evidence from this study that a global IS profession might be developing, there is also evidence of societal variation in the way in which the IS professionals play out their roles. The reasons for these differences may be the differences in the cultural values of the societies in which the IS professionals pursue their practice.

To return to the questions that were raised in the introduction to this article, there is evidence that IS techniques and technologies are portable across international boundaries. But they may be modified and mutated by local practice, mediated by the culture of the society in which they are implemented. This should serve as a warning to those involved in international IS. Practices do seem to change as they are implemented in other societies. It is, therefore, important that those involved in international IS familiarize themselves with what these differences are. One problem is that the development of a shared IS professional language might serve to mask these differences rather than reveal them.

This study has been limited to considering data from two countries and should be repeated in a variety of locations around the world to see whether similar results emerge. One of the practical applications of this work is that it begins to offer a clearer definition of what 'excellence' means in a systems analyst. If the core competencies, skills and abilities of systems analysts can be identified, then the work has considerable potential in improving the training, development and deployment of systems analysts not only within a company, or a country, but internationally.
