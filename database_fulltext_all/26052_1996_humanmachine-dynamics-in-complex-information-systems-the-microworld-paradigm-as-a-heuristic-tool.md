# Human-machine dynamics in complex information systems: the 'microworld' paradigm as a heuristic tool for developing theory and exploring design issues

David Wastell

Department of Computer Science, University of Manchester, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9PL, UK, Tel: +44-275-5722; Fax: +44-275-6236; email: was@cs.man.ac.uk

Abstract. The term ‘complex informed domain’ refers to a work environment characterized by high complexity and onerous cognitive demands, in which the management of a remote process is mediated by an information system (IS). Such work environments are typical of highly technical domains, such as industrial process control, but as computer-based information systems penetrate ever deeper into modern businesses, all organizations are taking on the same ‘informed’ characteristics. This paper reports an experiment using a rich simulation (a ‘microworld’), which attempted to throw light on human–machine dynamics in such complex environments. Two issues were investigated: the state of the subject (fatigue induced by sleep loss) and the degree of operator control (empowerment) afforded by the IS interface. Results showed that sleep deprivation led to no degradation in overt performance but, contrary to expectations, lower performance was associated with high control operation. A detailed qualitative analysis showed the high control interface to encourage a more proactive control style, whereas fatigue intensified primitive, reactive strategies. The findings emphasize that cognition is a goal-directed, adaptive system not simply an information-processing mechanism. A model of cognitive dynamics is outlined that distinguishes a hierarchy of control levels: anxiety defences, reactive control and strategic control. In general, the experiment shows the potential value to IS research of the microworld paradigm as a tool for developing theory and exploring key design issues.

Keywords: Empowerment, human cognition, information systems, simulation, sleep deprivation

## INTRODUCTION

Information technology (IT) increasingly pervades modern organizations, bringing about profound changes in the nature of human work. Zuboff (1988) has argued that the transformative capacity of IT has two aspects. Information technology automates: it replaces human agency by taking over activities that were once carried out by people. Information technology also informates: it enables the creation of 'panoptical' information systems that 'translate and make visible' all aspects of work in organizations: administrative and professional tasks, production processes, and so on (Zuboff, 1988). These information systems (IS) enable more of the world to be brought into the attentional horizon of IS users (e.g. managers, process operators). But the mediated nature of this experience means that their relationship with reality is increasingly indirect; it has been described as a hermeneutical relationship, likening the information system to a text interposed between the perceiver and the external world (Hollnagel, 1991).

The transforming power of information systems can be strikingly seen in complex technical domains, such as manufacturing and industrial process control; several of the key case studies in Zuboff's book involve process control systems. Information technology has steadily taken over more and more tasks hitherto performed by people in these environments. Humans remain, but the nature of their work has changed dramatically. No longer are they in direct engagement with the processes under their control; their role is essentially a supervisory one (Sheridan, 1988), involving the remote monitoring of a largely self-regulating automatic system. Intervention is only required exceptionally, e.g. to diagnose fault conditions. This shift from manual to supervisory control is referred to by Zuboff (1988) as a shift from action-centred to intellective skill. While supervisory control liberates the operator from the drudgery of manual control, cognitive work at a higher level (e.g. planning, problem-solving) is typically intensified (Woods, 1988).

The design of information systems for managing remote, complex processes in highly technical work environments is the subject of this paper. This is an important area, as the consequences of poor design in such domains could be catastrophic, especially in safety-critical applications such as nuclear power. Although such ‘complex informed domains’ are typified by areas such as industrial process control, the business environment in general is taking on an increasingly informed character as information systems permeate more and more deeply into the fabric of the modern enterprise, playing an increasingly important role in organizational regulation and decision-making (Brehmer, 1991; Zuboff, 1988).

Woods argues that the design of such complex systems needs to be founded on a systematic understanding of human cognition in complex, dynamic domains (Woods, 1988). However, our knowledge in this area is limited. The research literature on decision-making, for instance, deals largely with static not dynamic problems (Rasmussen et al., 1994). The information-processing model of cognition, the dominant paradigm in cognitive psychology, is an essentially architectural view, concerning itself with the different components of the 'cognitive machine' (sensory buffers, short-term memory, etc.) and how they fit together (Barber, 1988). Beyond contributing some rather broad principles (such as the inverted U curve relating arousal to performance), research in this tradition has told us very little of the dynamics of cognition, i.e. the strategies used to cope with complexity and uncertainty in real-world conditions.

How may our knowledge in this critical area be advanced? Field studies of human problem-solving offer one avenue, but field research poses daunting methodological problems (e.g. lack of reliable information, the infrequency of critical incidents). An alternative approach is the high-fidelity laboratory simulation, the so-called 'microworld' paradigm (Dorner, 1980; Brehmer et al., 1991). Such simulations enable the dynamics of human problem-solving to be investigated under relatively controlled conditions. Microworlds also have the potential to allow system design issues to be addressed empirically. The aim of this paper is to assess what may be learned using the microworld approach about human cognition and IS design in complex, informed work domains.

The research described is part of a larger programme of work commissioned by the European Space Agency (ESA) concerned with human–machine dynamics in highly automated systems. A microworld environment (CAMS) has been developed for this purpose, and the paper discusses a substantial experiment using CAMS. Laboratory studies are normally associated with the positivist tradition in science, in which theories are formally evaluated by testing the empirical validity of hypotheses derived from them. Although the present study has the patina of a conventional laboratory experiment, it is more appropriate to see it as a heuristic mechanism for ‘producing phenomena’ relevant to key issues (Dorner & Pfeifer, 1993) than fulfilling a positivist, hypothesis-testing role.

The interplay of two main ‘independent variables’ was investigated. Woods (1988) has argued that complex environments may be titrated into three core dimensions: the dynamic behaviour and complexity of the system to be acted upon; factors inherent to the agent (the agent’s capabilities and current psychological state); and the design of the human–machine interface. Woods refers to this tripartite arrangement as the ‘cognitive triad’. The experiment addressed two of these apices: the influence of operator state (fatigue) and the relative merits of a user-centred vs. machine-centred interface (the issue of ‘locus of control’). The interaction of these factors with task complexity was also addressed by including a mixture of simple and complex problems.

Locus of control is a key issue in systems design. It refers to the role and responsibility of the human operator vis-à-vis the computer system. The dominant IS design paradigm is machine-centred and Tayloristic (Clegg et al., 1994), emphasizing automation of the human role. There is, however, accumulating evidence suggesting fundamental problems with this approach. There are many situations in which the benefits of automation have not been realized, indeed in which it has been counterproductive (Wickens, 1992). Zuboff (1988) comments that lodging intelligence in the 'smart machine' emasculates human operators; they become dependent and docile, failing to develop the mental models and skills that are necessary to deal with problem situations (Wickens, 1992). In aviation, studies have implicated over-reliance on automatic systems as contributing to many accidents (Norman, 1990). Moray (1986) reports evidence that supervisory controllers handle breakdowns less effectively than manually trained counterparts. Similar untoward outcomes of automation have been noted in the office environment (Wastell et al., 1994).

These lines of evidence suggest that, rather than reducing the prerogative of the human operator, design should aim to 'empower' users (e.g. by giving them more information and discretion). Having more control is known to reduce stress and to increase motivation (Glass & Singer, 1972; Wastell & Newman, 1993). By playing a more active role, operators will be more in touch with the state of the system ('in the loop') and therefore in a better position to detect problem situations and to effect remedial action. There would thus seem to be a strong case for designing systems to empower the user. Authors in the sociotechnical school have traditionally advocated this position (e.g. Mumford, 1983; Rasmussen et al., 1994), and there is cogent evidence that a 'human-centred' approach, accentuating the potential of technology to support and augment the human role, can be highly effective. Wastell & Newman (1996) provide an example of a command-and-control system designed on such precepts, which led to improvements in both job performance and operator well-being.

The second main issue addressed was psychological state. Experimental studies in IS research typically test performance in optimal conditions. However, to generalize to the operational situation, a much broader range of subjective conditions needs to be considered. In particular, negative ‘strain states’ (such as anxiety and fatigue) are of high prima facie relevance to work in complex domains, and their impact on human performance is therefore an important concern. One would not expect people to perform in the same way in adverse conditions as they do when fully fit!

Studies of sleep deprivation reveal a complex and sometimes contradictory pattern of findings; however, the majority of studies do show a worsening of performance with sleep loss (Dinges & Kribbs, 1991). The effects of sleep loss are most prominent on vigilance-type tasks, which involve long periods of sustained attention. However, with suitably sensitive tests, degradation in other aspects of performance can be found, e.g. reduced working memory capacity, cognitive 'slowing' (Dinges & Kribbs, 1991). It should be commented that most sleep deprivation studies use simple laboratory tasks. Few have used demanding tasks of the microworld character, although an exceptional study did suggest that performance on more challenging tasks was less vulnerable to sleep loss (Wilkinson, 1964).

In summary, the present paper describes an experiment using the CAMS microworld to investigate the effects of sleep loss and locus of control on human–machine performance. There was a strong expectation that sleep loss would degrade performance and that enhanced control would improve it. As noted above, this work is part of a broader research programme using CAMS. Earlier technical reports have focused on skill maintenance and the methodology of mental workload assessment (Hockey et al., 1993); more recent studies have investigated issues, such as the impact of noise levels and training procedures. Here, the focus is on cognitive theory and IS design. The underlying concern is epistemological, i.e. with evaluating the effectiveness of the microworld paradigm as a device for developing theory regarding human performance and system design in complex informed domains.

## METHOD

## The Cabin Air Management System

The design rationale of CAMS (Cabin Air Management System) is to enable human interaction with complex systems to be studied under controlled conditions. It was thus designed to embody the two key features of complex informed work domains: a cognitively challenging task (namely the management of a dynamic process made up of many interacting components) and the mediation of this task by a computer-based information system, which automates much of the routine management. CAMS is illustrated by the 'mimic' in Figure 1. In brief, CAMS simulates the control system regulating air quality in the living quarters of a sealed environment. It consists of two subsystems: for air supply and for air purification. The former subsystem supplies nitrogen and oxygen from storage tanks to the cabin via a mixer valve, which regulates the total flow and the relative proportion of oxygen to nitrogen. The air purifier selectively filters carbon dioxide from the cabin atmosphere and lowers pressure.

CAMS incorporates an automatic control system, which, under normal circumstances, automatically manages the quality of cabin air, i.e. maintains oxygen, carbon dioxide and pressure levels within prescribed ranges. For oxygen, the level must be kept above 18%; for carbon dioxide, below 3%; and for pressure, a level in the range 960–1050 millibars is specified. A number of control panels are provided, which enable the two subsystems to be switched on and off, and key parameters to be changed manually. Manual control of the system is, by design, not a trivial undertaking. The two subsystems are tightly coupled and their use has to be carefully synchronized. Great care must be exercised with the use of the purifier in particular, which rapidly lowers pressure and should only be engaged when there is a sufficient surplus of pressure. Serious problems will be encountered if the dependencies between the two subsystems and their dynamic interaction are not properly appreciated.

In order to study problem-solving performance, CAMS allows a range of fault conditions of varying complexity to be scheduled to occur at various times during an experimental session. To alert the operator to the possible presence of a failure, CAMS incorporates an alarm subsystem: whenever one of the key parameters moves out of its normative range, an annunciator is flashed onto the screen and a bleep is sounded. Two faults were of particular interest in the present paper. The first involved the failure of the oxygen controller. In this situation, the oxygen supply simply fails to respond to the on-off triggers (i.e. the upper and lower thresholds of the target range), and the operator must take the air supply system under manual control, switching it on and off as required. The second fault involved the escape of air from the purifier. Control of this problem was somewhat more demanding, requiring carefully co-ordinated manual control of both subsystems: the air supply to restore pressure without oversaturating the cabin with oxygen, and the purifier to minimize the amount of leaked air.

Interaction with CAMS is based on the direct manipulation paradigm. To inspect the current status of a component, the user simply clicks its iconic representation and an appropriate panel is displayed (e.g. clicking the cabin indicates current oxygen, carbon dioxide and pressure levels). It should be noted that these panels remain open only for a short period (10 s), which allows the operators' information sampling strategies to be monitored. The control panels for air supply and purification are obtained in a similar way, allowing settings and trigger points to be changed.

## Experimental manipulations

To investigate locus of control, CAMS was designed to be operated in two possible modes: user-centred (high operator control) and machine-centred (low control). In the low-control mode, there was little scope for manual intervention. While all was normal, the system ran itself. Only during abnormal conditions (i.e. the triggering of an alarm) was the subject allowed to operate the subsystem controls manually. System status could only be inspected when alarms occurred and at specific times (every 5 min) when it was necessary to update the system log. In the high-control version, operators were free to examine all aspects of system status and to intervene whenever they wanted.

To enable fatigue to be investigated, operators were deprived of one night's sleep. The sleep deprivation sessions required the subjects to come into the laboratory the evening before testing. They were then free to use the time as they wished (games, TV, reading) but, of course, they were obliged to stay awake. The vigil was supervised.

## Subjects and training

Fifteen subjects participated in the experiment (14 men and one woman; age range 23–34 years). Subjects were reimbursed for their participation at the rate of £5 per hour. Operation of the system, especially fault diagnosis and management, required considerable understanding of system dynamics and practical operational experience. Subjects were thus given a comprehensive training programme involving 8–10 sessions, each lasting around 1 h. In the initial sessions, they were instructed in the theory of CAMS and given practice at controlling the system in 'fault-free' mode. They were then taught how to recognize each of the fault conditions and given practice in fault-handling procedures. Training was concluded when subjects were judged to be competent in handling the full range of faults.

## Experimental design, test sessions and subject instructions

Following training, the test phase of the experiment began. Subjects carried out four two-hour test sessions, one session for each combination of control and sleep loss. The sequence of treatment combinations was arranged according to a Latin-square design to ensure the counterbalancing of any order effects. Test sessions lasted for two hours and were always carried out at the same time of day (beginning at 09.00 h); they were separated by one week on average.

Subjects were instructed to operate as supervisory controllers. Their primary job was to monitor the system, to detect and diagnose fault conditions, and to intervene manually as necessary in order to keep the system within its prescribed bounds. Six fault conditions were programmed to occur during each work session according to a pseudo-random distribution. Faults lasted 10 min, after which they were corrected. The system was completely reset after every second fault. Every subject received at least one oxygen control failure (simple fault) and one leak (complex fault) during each work session. Their ability to handle these 'probe faults' is the focus of this paper.

## RESULTS

## Statistical procedures

All the statistical analyses were carried out using multifactorial, repeated measures analysis of variance. Control (high vs. low), subjective state (normal sleep vs. sleep deprived) and problem difficulty (the simple vs. the complex fault) were the three main factors. In order to investigate any direct and interactive effects of ability levels, a fourth post hoc factor was formed by dividing subjects into two groups: those above the median group performance level (the high performers) and those below (the low performers). Table 1 summarizes the results of the analysis for the main dependent and independent variables.

## Subjective state

At the end of each session, 'subjective state' was determined on two dimensions of strain (anxiety and fatigue) using visual analogue scales (range 0–100). The level of effort put into task performance was also determined. Fatigue was found to be dramatically increased by sleep deprivation (from an average of 33 in the rested state to 59, F=8.15, P<0.01). Neither the main effect of control on fatigue, nor the interaction between control and sleep were significant. Anxiety showed no significant effects. For effort, the sleep by control interaction was significant (F=4.9, P<0.05), indicating that subjects increased their level of effort when sleep deprived, but only for the high-control condition.

## Task performance

Task performance was scored as the average time the system was out of its target range (TOR) in respect of any of the three parameters during the occurrence of faults. Faults, it will be recalled, lasted for 10 min. The average TOR for high-performing subjects on the simple fault was only 7 s (i.e. their performance was virtually error-free); for the complex fault, the figure was 1.1 min. For the low performers, the corresponding figures were 1.4 min and 2.7 min. The main effects of ability and problem complexity were significant (see Table 1).

No effects of sleep loss on performance were obtained. However, control did exert a significant effect, although this was not in the direction that had been predicted. With low control, the TOR was 1.2 min; high control increased this figure to 1.5 min. Neither the interaction between problem complexity and sleep loss, nor with control were significant. No other two-way or higher order interactions were significant.

## Intervention levels

Interaction with CAMS involved two types of operation: information sampling (reading a parameter level or a flow rate) and control actions (interventions that change the status of a system component, e.g. changing a setting). Information sampling was significantly higher in the high-control condition with 43% more viewing operations made overall (F=18.1, P<0.001). The main effect of sleep loss on information sampling was not significant, nor was the interaction between sleep and control.

Concerning control activity, it was found that the complex problem evoked 25% more intervention than the simple fault and that high-performing subjects produced 20% more interventions (1.0 vs. 0.8 per minute) than the low-performing subjects. Interestingly, the interaction between problem complexity and ability was significant; whereas high performers responded to the complex fault with 35% more interventions than for the simple fault, the low performers exhibited a much smaller increase (9%).

The main effect of sleep loss on control activity was not significant, nor was that of control. A significant interaction between control and fatigue was, however, present. Figure 2 shows that, in the normally rested state, intervention levels were significantly higher in the high-control condition, whereas with sleep deprivation activity levels were the same. A more detailed analysis was carried out dichotomizing control actions into on/off operations and operations to change system settings. It was reasoned that the former, in general, betokened a less sophisticated type of intervention, typically constituting an immediate reaction to a simple event (e.g. a parameter crossing a threshold). In contrast, changing a setting suggests a deeper level of analysis and a longer time horizon.

This analysis revealed a general tendency for more setting changes in the high-control condition, suggesting that the user-centred interface generally prompted an increased level of 'knowledge-based' intervention. In contrast, the similar rates of intervention for high and low control in the fatigued state appeared to reflect a relative increase in the rate of on/off interactions for low control. This suggests that the effect of sleep loss (in the low-control condition) strengthened the tendency to adopt a less sophisticated mode of control.

## Idiographic analysis of coping strategies

Complementing this rather sterile statistical analysis, a thorough idiographic examination of coping strategies was also performed, rendered possible because CAMS generates detailed transcripts regarding system status and operator interaction. This will now be briefly summarized concentrating on the performance of the weaker subjects. The stronger subjects were of much less interest. They made very few errors and their behaviour largely conformed with training procedures.

Let us begin by making an important distinction between two general approaches to control, namely, closed- and open-loop control strategies (Wickens, 1992). In closed-loop control, parameters are simply monitored, with the operator behaving reactively when they go out of range. Open-loop control, on the other hand, involves the planning and execution of a more sophisticated set of manoeuvres in anticipation of a developing situation. Optimal manual control of CAMS required carefully co-ordinated open-loop control, especially in fault conditions.

In general, the weaker subjects exhibited a greater degree of closed-loop control, which was a particular handicap when dealing with difficult problems (such as the leak) in which a concerted, holistic response was required. Two broad maladaptive styles were apparent in their ‘control strategies’. Some subjects intervened less than they should have and on too narrow a basis. One consistently responded to the leak, for instance, with a belated series of small incremental increases in air flow and failed to take any action involving the air purifier. Another showed a tendency to focus on a single aspect of the system, manipulating oxygen richness as his response to every problem situation.

The performance of the second group of weak subjects, in contrast, reflected a pattern of indiscriminate manual intervention. They exhibited a tendency to take over manual control of both subsystems whatever the fault condition, even though this was not always necessary (e.g. for the oxygen failure). Manual control, as has been said, requires carefully co-ordinated intervention based on a sound knowledge of system dynamics. These subjects typically attempted to manage the system by making short-term, unilateral interventions to correct movements in individual parameters, moving from one to the next without really getting on top of the problem.

## DISCUSSION

A firm base of empirically substantiated theory is vital if the design of complex information systems is to be placed on a more rigorous footing. The CAMS microworld has been developed to help establish such a foundation. It provides a generic task environment (a 'sandtable') for exploring theoretical and practical design issues under controlled conditions. The experiment reported here investigated the influence of two such issues, subjective state and locus of control, on human-machine dynamics. Interpretation of the substantive findings of the experiment will now be addressed before proceeding to a more general discussion.

As expected, enhanced control significantly affected the way that subjects performed their work. There was clear evidence that they monitored more closely and intervened more often in the high-control condition; there was also suggestive evidence of a shift to a more sophisticated style of system management. This increase in operator engagement confirms the importance of empowerment as a design issue. By giving users more control, they manifestly felt a greater prerogative to engage actively with the task at hand. In part, this increased intervention may be attributed to subjects' intellectual desire to learn more of the internal dynamics of CAMS by experimenting with it. Another important factor was the low reliability of the automatic systems. The importance of trust in human-machine dynamics is well recognized (Muir, 1987; Lee & Moray, 1992); the less reliable a system, the less it will be trusted and the stronger will be the propensity to intervene manually.

Although they played a more active role in the high-control condition, the expectation that this would lead to the adoption of more effective intervention strategies was not borne out. The increase in intervention was unproductive; indeed, there was, on average, an increase in errors. Manual control of CAMS, as has been stressed, is a demanding undertaking. The worsened error rate indicates that much of the increased interaction, either to gain knowledge or reduce uncertainty, was unhelpful and inappropriate. By doing more, subjects increased their workload and the potential for making mistakes.

An important role for CAMS is to help evaluate key design issues empirically. The control manipulation was introduced for just this purpose, as we have argued that 'empowerment' is a critical issue in IS design. The results of the experiment were salutary. They showed our assumptions regarding the advantages of high control to be somewhat naive: enhanced control was not associated with greater effectiveness, indeed it led to degradation of performance. This 'cautionary tale' shows the general danger of simplistic, 'armchair' theorizing about design. Specifically, it reinforces Norman's truism that there are no absolute principles regarding the relative merits of human vs. automatic control: the issue is one of appropriate design (Norman, 1990). This is not to discount the ethical and practical case for empowering users. In the longer term, we might well have found clear advantages for high control (e.g. in dealing with novel faults), but in routine circumstances (normal operation, familiar faults) our results argue for the benefits of a more restrictive interface.

Let us now consider the effects of sleep loss. Sleep loss, as noted in the Introduction, is known to degrade the functioning of the elementary components of the cognitive system: attention, working memory, etc. However, although our sleep-deprived subjects were significantly fatigued, it is striking that no deterioration in overt task performance was found. For the high-control condition, this maintenance of performance was associated with higher levels of subjective effort, although no changes in intervention levels were found. On the contrary, for the low control condition, there was a discernible intensification of interaction, although effort was unchanged.

What does this pattern of results mean? In general, it shows the poverty of conventional information-processing models of cognition (Barber, 1988). Such models, in essence, deal only with the low-level components of cognition (memory, sensory buffers, etc.); they do not address the regulatory processes that govern the dynamic deployment of these elements. The present results are not incompatible with such models, rather they stress the need for a broader perspective on human cognition incorporating control dynamics as well as architectural components. Figure 3 outlines such a model.

The experimental findings emphasize that cognition is an adaptive system, in which goals and negative feedback processes play a crucial role in maintaining performance, despite changes in the internal and external environment (Hacker, 1987; Frese, 1987). By placing subjects in adverse conditions, we should not necessarily expect to see an overt performance decrement, as the cybernetic properties of the 'system' will inherently attempt to correct any mismatch between goals and performance (see Fig.3). Possible compensatory adjustments include mobilizing extra effort, goal rationalization (focusing resources on top priority activities), deploying more efficient strategies (e.g. non-compensatory decision algorithms; Payne et al., 1988). Hockey (1986) uses the term 'performance protection' to refer to these self-stabilizing qualities of human cognition. Only if subjects are really pressed to their limits will decrements be seen. In the present experiment, enough capacity was apparently available to allow subjects to overcome the effects of fatigue.

Figure 3. A cybernetic model of cognition. Drawing on Beer (1994), two subsystems are distinguished: an operational subsystem (which does the real work) and a management subsystem (which regulates the operational system, setting goals, determining strategies and monitoring performance). Note that the model is generic and can be applied to 'cognitive entities' at any level of abstraction: the individual, the work group or the organization as a whole. The component labelled 'monitor performance' is the heart of the control mechanism. It acts as a comparator, signalling to higher levels the presence of a 'mismatch' between goals and performance, leading either to a change of strategy or a reprioritization of the goal hierarchy. Beer (1994) refers to this 'error signal' as the algedonic signal; psychologically, it is experienced as anxiety.

Different processes appeared to underlie the maintenance of performance in the high- and low-control regimes. In the former, subjects appeared to mobilize more effort in the fatigued state, which is consistent with the higher level of motivation expected in this condition. In the low-control condition, they responded with an increased rate of on/off intervention. There are two ways in which this strategy would help overcome their tiredness. First, by artificially raising their arousal levels (keeping themselves awake), and second by reducing cognitive load. Manual control of CAMS imposed quite severe mental demands, on memory and attention in particular, especially if subjects attempted to control the different subsystems in parallel. The increase in reactive intervention reflected a tendency to adopt a serial mode of operation, in which one subsystem was switched on and then off, before attending to the other. Through these means, operators could reduce the load on memory and attention.

The idiographic analysis of coping strategies yielded some fascinating insights into the difficulties of controlling a system that was complex enough to present a serious challenge. As found in other simulation studies (Sterman, 1989), human decision-makers have considerable difficulty in managing complex, dynamic processes, especially those involving lags, such as the infamous Beer Game (Senge, 1990). Some of our subjects did well but others manifestly struggled, despite having received extensive training.

For the most part, the vicissitudes of the weaker subjects highlighted the vital importance of a good 'mental model' as the basis for effective control. Many of the weaker subjects appeared to have an inadequate understanding of system dynamics; their behaviour in the operational situation was often characterized by fragmented, reactive responses. They were unable to deploy the strong open-loop actions that were necessary to handle the system efficiently, especially during difficult fault states. There was an interesting polarization in their coping strategies. Some subjects reacted by withdrawing, adopting too narrow a focus; other responded by engaging in more manual control than was required, behaving in an indiscriminate, erratic fashion.

This dichotomy recalls the categories of pathological behaviour that have been observed in other studies of behaviour in complex situations. Dorner's early microworld studies (Dorner, 1980) revealed two general dysfunctional coping strategies: encystment (preoccupation with the details of minor issues while ignoring the overall situation) and thematic vagabonding (dodging erratically from one issue to another without ever really coming to grips with the main problem). Dorner interprets both as escape behaviours; by engaging in these activities, subjects create a feeling, albeit an illusory one, of control, which enables them to avoid facing up to their helplessness and inadequacy. These two behaviour patterns show strong affinity with Janis' (1982) concepts of hyper- and hypovigilance.

The ethos of the present research, it will be remembered, was theory generating, not theory testing. The aim was to use the rich but controllable environment of the microworld to advance our understanding of human-machine dynamics in complex, informed environments. It is now time to address what has been learned and to synthesize some general principles. Essentially, the simulation study has demonstrated that human cognition is an active, adaptive process and that there is considerable variation between people (IS users) in the coping strategies they deploy to deal with complexity in dynamic, uncertain environments. Moreover, these strategies are malleable; they can be influenced by such factors as the state of the user and by the design of the information system.

More specifically, the results suggest that there is a hierarchy of generic coping strategies. At the bottom of the hierarchy are the ‘anxiety defences’, represented by two general maladaptive processes: encystment and vagabonding. Both involve goal displacement; they allay anxiety (operators feel that they are ‘doing something’) but the response is maladaptive in the sense that subjects avoid properly coming to terms with objective task demands. At the next level, we have control that is task oriented, but which is essentially closed-loop, reactive and short term. At the top level, we move to open-loop strategic control, characterized by the systematic execution of longer-term plans. This form of control draws on both ‘procedural knowledge’ (what to do in given circumstances) and an in-depth understanding of system dynamics (Sanderson, 1990). Hollnagel (1993) has distinguished a similar control hierarchy.

These control modes are not fixed; they are subject to a range of influences. Hollnagel (1993) refers to these influences as 'performance-shaping factors', and we have observed several at work in this study. The design of the IS interface is one critical factor. We have seen how a restrictive, 'machine-centred' interface encouraged a reactive, closed-loop style, and how a 'user-centred' interface stimulated a more active level of engagement, even though this was ultimately unproductive. The state of the subject is another key factor. In general, we might expect negative states (such as fatigue) to lower the level of control, and we have seen here how sleep loss led to an intensification of the reactive management style. A range of other factors (some transient, some more permanent) will also affect the mode of control, e.g. the experience and self-confidence of the user, the trustworthiness of the IS, time pressure, stress levels, competing goals and so on.

In many ways, the prime purpose of the present paper is epistemological. The aim was to see what could be learned about human performance in complex systems using rich simulations. Microworlds fall midway between traditional laboratory research and field research. While preserving the rigour of the former, they give something of the richness of the latter, although this is not to pretend that they have the same meaning for participants as the real thing. We have seen microworlds to be highly effective as a device for generating a rich set of behaviour patterns that appear to have some external validity; the anxiety defences observed have a strong resonance with those seen in the real IT projects (Wastell & Newman, 1993)! In terms of theory development, the findings have advanced our understanding of cognitive processes in complex, dynamic environments, especially control strategies and the factors that influence the level of control. Microworld research would thus seem to have considerable potential in IS research as a way of exploring complex behavioural phenomena under quasi-controlled conditions, for generating and refining models/theories, and as a way of exploring design issues under ecologically realistic conditions.

He holds a PhD in psychology from Durham University, and began his professional research career in 1978 at the Applied Psychology Unit, Cambridge. Moving to Manchester in 1980, he took up a lectureship in Medical Informatics in the Medical School, where he gained extensive practical experience in information systems development in the healthcare environment. In 1988 he transferred to the Computer Science Department. David Wastell has published widely in information systems, applied psychology and clinical physiology.
