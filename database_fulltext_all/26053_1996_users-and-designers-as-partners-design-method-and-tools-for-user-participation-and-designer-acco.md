# Users and designers as partners – design method and tools for user participation and designer accountability within the design process

Irene S.Y. Koh & \*Michael S.H. Heng

Department of Work and Organization Psychology, Faculty of the Social Sciences, University of Tilburg, The Netherlands, \*Economics Faculty, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Abstract. We have found two main causes of inadequacies in a decision support system designed by us. They are, in general, the lack of direct user participation and the lack of designer accountability in the design process. In an attempt to solve the problem, we develop a new design method, the User Participation and Designer Accountability (UPDA) method. It involves users' direct manipulation of colour cards, which contain the contents of simple design charts. These cards are also made accessible to everyone involved in the design process and form the basis upon which the designer can be questioned. We have applied the UPDA method and so far the reactions are very positive.

Keywords: Design method, designer accountability, direct user participation, interactive interface, set of requirements, paper prototyping

## INTRODUCTION

The importance of the design process for developing an artifact, be it a building, a machine or an information system, is too obvious to need labouring. Concerns about the design methods and methodology arise in many cases as a result of difficulties and problems encountered in the development, implementation and application process of the artifact (Rittel, 1984). Traditional approaches to solve these problems (such as the systems approach) are not satisfactory. Hence, there is a need for a radical look at the existing structure and perspective (thinking) of design methodology and methods. In addition, the search for new design processes is imperative (Thackara, 1988).

This paper describes a novel design method for developing a software computer system that supports active user participation and designer accountability. The evolution of the design method is included in order to give the background of its development. Some experience of using the method is reported.

## Participation

The importance of user participation in the design of artifacts has been argued with gusto in literature on design methods. See for example papers in Lucas (1980), Cross (1984) and Thackara (1988). Indeed, information systems development methodologies of different philosophical persuasions (namely functionalism and neohumanism) recognize the need for user participation in the analysis, design and implementation of information systems (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994). According to Meer and Roodink (1991), two main reasons why users should be involved in the design process are that they possess specific knowledge on the processes to be automated and that their acceptance of the system will be enhanced by this participation. To these, we would like to make an observation. Having user participation in the design process is more easily advocated than followed. This is, in part, because design methods generally are not so easy for users without a technical background to learn and use. By only half understanding what the systems analysts/designers say, users (no matter how sincere the designers' effort may be) cannot really genuinely participate in the design process.

Rittel (1984) sees the design process as an argumentative process, which is not a well-defined sequence of activities that are planned and systematically carried out. It is argumentative because statements and solutions made and found are exposed (or made transparent) and are subjected to the scrutiny of the participants. Different viewpoints are considered and debated. This process is iterative; solutions are generated from the arguments and these are further subjected to discussion and debate until most participants are satisfied or some sort of consensus is reached. Decisions evolve out of this social, communicative and dialectic process among the participants and the designers. The design generated is not only more authentic to the needs of the participants, but will be more receptive and liked by the users involved.

## Accountability

Another important dimension in the whole process of designing is the accountability factor. The ultimate responsibility of the functionality and useability of the system lies with the designer. The users may be invited to participate in the process of design, but without transparency of the process, that is, all aspects are visible and understandable from the beginning to the end, the users will not be effective and rightful partners in the design process. The designers cannot be made accountable for their suggestions, plans and design, if the users do not see and understand these and do not know their implications. The designer should be made answerable to every aspect concerning the design of the artifact. Furthermore, it should be feasible for the users to question or even veto suggestions, plans and decisions of the designer at any moment of the design process. The designers' accountability to the users is at stake here.

We are interested in the design of information systems with user participation and designer accountability. In the world of computer science in general, and within the community of human-computer interaction in particular, the design process of computer systems involving user participation and designer accountability has become particularly important. Methodologies, especially those proposed from the viewpoint of the social-technical approach, e.g. Mumford's ETHICS method (Mumford, 1983), echo the principles of user participation in the design process. Other relevant literature includes studies by Damodaran et al. (1980) and Galer et al. (1992). Far too little attention is being paid to designing computer-based systems with the participation of users. Moreover, there is not a range of design methods to support user participation and designer accountability, which are easy to learn and easy to use. In addition, very little consideration is being given to making the process of designer accountability acceptable to users. Our own experience of designing and developing an experimental computer system has taught us the importance of these two factors. Our conclusion is that users should not just be consulted in the design process, but should also be partners in the process.

In the course of building a decision support system to support the work of selection psychologists, we have developed a new method for designing computer-based systems called the User Participation and Designer Accountability Method (the UPDA method). This paper describes the UPDA method, which is a participative design tool for the designer who has to design systems for users with real-life tasks. We shall briefly describe the research project, which provides the background to the evolution of the method. The project aims to develop a decision support system for the personnel selection process and the system is developed using an expert systems shell, called GURU. We will concentrate on how and why UPDA is developed during the course of the project. This deals with the problems encountered during the design and development stages of the first version of the decision support system and evaluates briefly our experience in using conventional design methods (the Structured Analysis and Design Technique, SADT method). We also describe the various stages of the new UPDA method. Finally, we discuss the advantages of such a method and our experience with its application.

## BACKGROUND TO THE DEVELOPMENT OF UPDA

Six years ago, we started a project to develop an experimental decision support system, called SOS (System for Supporting the Selection Process) for professional psychologists working as personnel selectors in consultancy bureaux. A conceptual model, called the semi-clinical model and developed by Roe (1983), was used as the basic model for this system.

We approached four consultancy bureaux and discussed the project with the management and some of the psychologists in the field. Nominated representatives from the four bureaux formed a supervisory group for the project. This group met six times to discuss the requirements and specifications of the system. The result was a formal written set of requirements and specifications. The design process of the first version of the system, SOS-1, was an iterative process. The methodology used was an evolutionary development approach. This approach calls for the rapid development of a small, but useful, system that reflects some basic parts of the overall system. This was the prototype of the system and was based on the semi-clinical model reported above. This prototype was used as a starting point for the supervisory group's discussions on the requirements. Consensus was the main decision-making procedure. Based on this set of requirements, and using some aspects of the SADT method (De Marco, 1978), the prototype was changed, expanded and refined after every session of demonstration and discussion with the supervisory group. Through iteration of these demonstrations and discussions, the prototype was corrected and expanded until, finally, when the set of requirements was fully drawn up and final modifications were made to the last version of the prototype, SOS-1 was born.

SOS-1 was demonstrated to the group of representatives and, although the overall functionality of the system was confirmed, it was generally agreed that the system was not useable. We carried out a pilot test with five voluntary psychologists and received the same answer. Although the system was experimental and was not intended to be applied straight away within an organization, it should be useable for individual users. What had gone wrong?

## Evaluation of SOS-1

We found that there were certain defects in SOS-1, which could be traced back to the methodology of the design process itself. Two major problems found were:

1 Despite the fact that the set of requirements was clearly set out and that a prototype was developed to help in the design process, not all the requirements have been implemented as was originally agreed; some have been left out purposely, some have just been overlooked and some have been misinterpreted by the designer, since the translation of the requirements from words to the design flow charts involves further interpretation and assessment.

2 Despite the fact that the set of requirements was deemed adequate before the development of the system, with hindsight and after viewing the system, the users and the designers see many flaws and inadequacies in the system. Requirements have not been adequately formulated; some requirements cannot actually be formulated in words; some are actually redundant, not required or unnecessary. Some we have not even thought of, but they loomed large when the system was tested.

We identified two main causes for the defects in the methods used in designing and developing SOS-1. They are:

1 Not enough direct user participation in the design process. Previously (as in most design processes), future users are only asked to specify requirements or make decisions on various aspects of the systems. Making decisions and formulating specifications for the system is important, but it is not enough. The users should be co-designers.

2 No feasible accountability, since users are not familiar with the predominantly technical jargon and, hence, cannot refer to the design process and ask questions or point out inconsistencies and misinterpretations. This leads to inadequate transparency in the whole design process. By this we mean that all verbal discussions and documents specifying how the system should be configured are not connected to the various flow charts and programming design charts in a way that is understandable to users as well as designers. The result is that the final system to be developed cannot be envisaged or imagined by both parties. Hence, when the final product is presented, validated and tested, the discrepancies between the imagined system and the real system are large. This reflects the lack of control users have in the design of the system (Frese, 1987).

Since these problems arise from the design process itself, we started out by re-examining the SADT method of De Marco (1978).

## Difficulties with the SADT method

The SADT method was developed in the 1970s because of the perceived need for a structured, systematic and logical design methodology whereby communication was seen as necessary between designers and users. In the early days of computing, many design processes were ad hoc based. Systems built under such processes were difficult to maintain and modify. Communication between the users and the designer was minimal. In comparison, the SADT method provided a better design analysis process. It introduced concepts and practices for getting requirements and specifications from the users in a systematic way and generally encouraged some degree of meaningful communication between the designer and the users. One specific tool of SADT is the data flow diagram (DFD). We have used DFDs in the creative part of our first design process and have found them useful, but DFDs were not easily understood. Even designers have to have substantial experience to be able to use them effectively. This means that ordinary users would not be able to understand DFDs easily and, as a consequence, would not be able to participate fully in the creative design process based on DFD. We list some other disadvantages of the SADT method:

## 1 It has no temporal sequence.

2 It is still not an adequately powerful method for communication between the designers and users, especially for users without a technical background. Communication between the users and the designer is more a consultation process; real use participation remains an ideal. At most, it can be used for consultation purposes between the designers and users; participation is not possible because of the more technical nature of the method.

3 Maintenance of the system is not easy and evaluation of the system is also difficult to carry out.

4 It is not easy to use data flow diagrams to help users make explicit their requirements and specifications for the user interface.

The conclusion from our evaluation of both SOS-1 and the SADT method was that SOS-1 should be redesigned and redeveloped to correct structural and functional faults. This conclusion called for a different design method.

We set out to find solutions to such problems, and the UPDA method is the result of our endeavour. Although this method is not an ideal design method, we argue it is a better method, which we hope will make our new version of the system more accountable and acceptable to the users. Last but not least, we hope to have a system, which the users can enjoy and have fun with.

## FIVE STAGES OF THE UPDA METHOD

The UPDA method was used in the design of SOS-2 (second version of Computer Support System for Selection psychologists). The UPDA method has five stages (Figure 1).

The five stages are as follows:

1 Conducting a survey and study on the necessity and feasibility of the proposed system.

2 Making a study and analysis on the various aspects of the users' work and their work environment (individual as well as organizational aspects).

3 Formulating a set of requirements and specifications (SOR) and making it into a document.

4 Translating the SOR from a document into flow charts:

a Making a coding system, which enables checking to be carried out to see if every requirement has been taken up in the flow charts.

b Dividing the flow charts into four types, namely, the system-action flow chart, the user-action flow chart, the interface interaction chart and the screen design chart.

c Using different colours for each type of cards (for paper prototyping the four types of charts). This will facilitate the participation of users' in determining the contents and organization of the system.

5 Translating the above flow charts into the GURU programming flow charts.

In the next section we elaborate on the five stages using the design of the knowledge-based decision support system for selection psychologists as an illustration.

## Study on the necessity and feasibility of the project

The first step of the analysis phase was a survey on the necessity and feasibility of developing a knowledge-based decision support system (KB-DSS) for the selection psychologists. The survey was done in parallel with the study on the work activities of the selection psychologists (second stage of the UPDA method). The following question was posed to the psychologists during the survey: 'Do selection psychologists like you really need a decision support system to assist you in your work?'

One important aspect for the project is the management decision to support the project. Without such support, no matter how much goodwill we had from the psychologists, the project could not be carried out successfully. More crucial to the project were the opinions of the individual psychologists, not just those who were already involved in the project, but their colleagues who might be asked to co-operate in the study or who might be asked to test and evaluate the system later on.

The majority (80%) of the selection psychologists interviewed was not adverse to the idea of using a KB-DSS to support their work. However, when the project had begun in early 1990, some negative remarks and feedback were received, particularly from ‘veteran’ selection psychologists in the field. It seemed that accepting new techniques and methods in their work was not easy after having spent so many years using the same techniques and methods. Of course, this does not apply to everyone in this category. The acceptance or rejection of using a KB-DSS depends on the attitude and personality characteristics of the psychologists. In addition, it also depends on the attitude of the director or manager, as well as on his ability to persuade the psychologists to use the KB-DSS and his ability to change structures and processes within the organization to usher in the successful implementation of the system.

## Analysis of tasks and work environment

After studying the feasibility of the project, we went on to examine the tasks and work environment of the selection psychologists. Some months have been spent on observing, studying and analysing their professional work in the field. The study had been carried out with the cooperation of three consultant bureaux involved in personnel selection. The aim of this study is threefold. First, it is to be an updated, empirical study on the work procedures and working conditions of the selection psychologists, as well as their thinking process during judgemental moments. Secondly, this study is to look into the degree of application of science and technology in the field of selection practice. Thirdly, the results of this study would determine the design of the KB-DSS to be developed.

This empirical study has been carried out in two parts. The first part of this study comprised more than four weeks of studying the selection process in one consultancy. The second part was made up of a two-month in-depth study of the judgement process of six selection psychologists in three consultancies. Altogether, more than 20 candidate cases of the assessment process have been observed and analysed. There were 16 cases for the first part of the study and six cases for the second part.

The detailed results of the study are found in Koh (1994). Here, we will only make a short summary of the type of work activities carried out by the selection psychologists. First, we describe the nature of the work of the selection psychologists in their bureaux. The selection psychologists work under contract with client organizations to carry out the selection process for them. Candidates are referred to them by these client organizations. Specifications are provided about what sort of assessment programme is required and, in consultation with the client organization on what are the criteria of the job, the selection psychologist conducts a selection process with the candidate. At the end of the assessment programme, he will give advice and make a report to his client.

After understanding how the selection psychologists work, we went on to define a set of requirements for the system.

## General definition of a set of requirements (SOR)

Obtaining a set of requirements (SOR) is widely recognized nowadays as an important step in the design process of computer-based systems and other tools. A SOR is a list containing requirements, constraints and specifications for the system to be developed. These are basically the wishes and demands of users, made into a textual form, thereby making explicit many implicit requirements. The SOR is developed by a team of representative potential users together with the designer(s), and is meant to be a fundamental starting point for concrete design actions later on. Apart from being used as the basis for design actions, the SOR functions in the design process as a foundation for the success of the system to be developed. It should help to answer questions such as: Does the system function as it is meant to function? Does it fulfil all the verbal requirements set for it? Does it look like what it should look like, and so forth? When the SOR is being properly used, it will also function as a test tool in an iterative way and at the same time as an important source of ideas.

We will now consider how the SOR for designing the KB-DSS for personnel selection was formulated. A prototype was developed rapidly and demonstrated to the supervisory group. As the potential users evaluated and criticized the prototype, a list of specifications, requirements, wishes and needs became explicit. The resulting discussion on the prototype was analysed by us and a first draft of the SOR was drawn up from the list. The first prototype was then modified to include the wishes of the users and subsequently demonstrated to the users again. Their reactions to the second prototype helped to clarify the requirements and specifications of the system and induced implicit and non-verbalizable requirements. In response to the users' reactions to the system, this prototype was again refined and modified and was once more demonstrated to the users. These processes were repeated and the SOR was changed according to the evaluations and conclusions of the group. After the SOR was finalized, the various requirements and conditions were reviewed and grades were accorded to them under the following aspects: essential (E), desirable (D) and not necessary (NN). There are 65 requirements altogether in the SOR, out of which 37 are listed under essential, and 24 under desirable and the rest (4) as not necessary.

## Translation of SOR onto charts

The SOR is an important and essential document for design purposes. Its usefulness, however, greatly depends on the ability of the designer to implement the SOR in a systematic way within the actual design process itself, and not leaving it out as a strange 'document of relevance'. What the designer needs is a systematic way of analysing, reformulating and coding these texts. A technique to do this is reported in Koh et al. (1992). This technique involves the translation of the SOR onto charts. It is actually a graphic representation of the SOR and first consists of the codification of all the requirements under the categories of input, process and output. The codification will ensure that the chart or representation containing all the information of the SOR is not lost or overlooked. It will also enable the designers to check and countercheck them with the four types of flow charts named above. Figure 2 illustrates this function of the SOR.

## Four flow charts

Following the translation of the SOR into a chart using codes, four types of flow charts are used to represent the actions and procedures of the system as required by the SOR. These various types of flow charts depict the actions and procedures of the system, as well as the actions of the users while reacting to and interacting with the system. They encompass the various aspects of the functionality of the system. The four charts are:

1 System-action flow chart

2 User-action flow chart

3 Interactive interface flow chart

4 Screen-display flow chart.

The design of the four flow charts involves active and creative processes and is most useful to the designers, since they can systematically transform the requirements of the system into graphic representations for the direct development of the system. When they are translating all the requirements into the flow charts, they can also check whether all the requirements have been taken up, whether some of them are redundant and so on. It is also easy to translate these flow charts into the data flow diagrams or technical programming charts of GURU, and developing the system will just become a matter of programming. We now describe the four charts.

## The system-action flow charts

The system–action flow charts show, in a chronological and hierarchical order, the actions and procedures that the system has to carry out. They have operators such as: shows, retrieves, asks, gets, saves and so on. For example, the system can begin by retrieving information from the data or knowledge base and then show it to the users. This is followed by asking the users for a certain response, either to get something from the users or to get answers indicating the choice of certain actions. The response of the users, if it is the provision of informative data, can then be saved or stored in the data base. The temporal and sequential nature of the flow charts centres upon actions instead of data (as a contrast to the data flow diagrams). With logical sequences and simple operators, the user can follow the steps that the system has to go through, and hence, point out which steps or actions have been left out or which are undesirable, and so forth. In addition, each requirement from the SOR, translated into number codes, can be placed at the identified action of the system so that checking can be done by the users as well as the designer (Figure 3).

## The user-action flow charts

The user-action flow charts concentrate on the actions, which the users have to carry out with the system in order to obtain certain results or goals. They indicate the number and type of actions that the users have to perform before they can get some results. They have operators such as choose, give/type in, confirm, decide and read. By making explicit the actions of the users at this stage, the users can decide on their reactions and interactions with the system. They can choose, from among alternatives, actions offered by the system. They can decide to take actions, not to take actions or to stop interacting with the system at certain moments, and so on (Figure 4).

## The interactive interface charts

The interactive interface charts give an overall view of the interaction between the system and the users. They are a derivation of the first two types of charts and provide visual and informative details of what the users and the systems have to do, as well as how they should interact with one another. They elaborate on the actions and interactions of the three 'actors' involved in the interactive process, facilitating proper functioning of the system. The three actors are the users, the interface and the knowledge bases of the system. These charts show individual actions of the users and the system, and also the links between actions and the necessary steps to be taken before a certain action can produce a result. It is from these types of charts that technical programming charts can easily be derived (Figure 5).

## The screen display charts

The screen display charts give in detail the exact words and the organization of the various procedures that will appear on the screen. Making the screen display explicit on paper in a systematic way allows the designer to test out his imagined or implicit designs on the screen. In addition, they allow other people, especially the users, to have a say in how the screen should look before the system is being developed. We give an example of a screen display chart (Figure 6).

A whole series of such screen displays designed for the system are drawn onto charts as illustrated above and these charts will be used during the dialogue between designer and potential users.

Drawing all these four types of simple-to-understand charts and making explicit the various phases and aspects of the systems not only allows outside people to scrutinize and criticize them, it also forces the designer to set down designs, which he can defend or can explain explicitly. He has to account for his designs and his work. Setting all these charts on paper is just the beginning of inviting user participation in the design process. These should be made convenient and easy for the users to comment on or to change. We came up with the idea of using colour cards to let users actively change the contents of the charts. ## Colour cards for user participation

One of the most important aspects of the UPDA method is the transfer of the charts described above onto coloured file cards, one type of charts in one colour. Each card is given a unique number based on any numbering system, which the users/designers find convenient. For example, the interactive interface chart might be yellow, the screen display chart green and so on. The different colours facilitate easy recognition of the types of cards and, thereby, the types of activities represented by the charts. These file cards are shown to the users under their respective headings and in the chronological order planned for the system. Not more than three actions or parts can be shown on one card. Too many actions or parts crowded onto one card will defeat the purpose of trying to make the actions clear to the users so that the cards can be evaluated, thrown out or new ones added. The advantage of using cards is that they are modular and, hence, can then be removed or added to or manipulated easily.

There is more than one way to use these cards to invite user participation in the design process. Based on our experience, it is advisable to show novice computer users only the interactive interface and the screen display cards. The four types of cards would be too overwhelming and complicated for them. To expert users, or those already familiar with the design, the design method and the four types of cards can be presented. All of the charts, as well as the GURU programming charts, can be shown to them if they are computer literate and technically competent enough to understand programming charts. The users are told to look at the cards and then to criticize them, correct them, or even to throw them away if necessary, to add new ones when appropriate, to shuffle them, to reverse their order and so on. This gives the users control over what the system should do, how it should be done and what they themselves want to do and how they should interact with the system. They can also determine the exact content and organization of what should appear on the screen. After the users are satisfied with a particular design, it can be captured on photograph. Both the users and designers can bring home the cards to experiment with various design options, capturing them in photographic forms. They can compare their options when they meet again.

In short, the form, the structure and the content of what the system should be can be decided directly by the users using this design method. The designer will first determine the basic design as he deems fit, but the users can point out the necessary changes in the various charts until they are satisfied with the design through manipulating the cards. Subsequently, it is the designer's job to modify the design accordingly and then transform the design into programming charts and develop a prototype of the system based on these. It is not intended that the colour card paper prototyping should replace the prototype of the system. The prototype should also be developed and shown to the users and, if changes are needed, the colour cards can be used again.

Advantages of this colour card method in involving and facilitating user participation in the design process seem to be as follows:

1 It is flexible.

2 It is fast, one can shuffle the cards, add or remove some of them quickly.

3 It is easy to learn and easy to use; the users can, with a short training session, participate in the design process. Ease of learning is essential for the users since they are usually strangers to design techniques.

4 It provides a good overview of the system to be developed, thereby making explicit the designs and plans for the system.

5 It is not 'frightening' to the computer illiterate.

6 It facilitates the communication process between the designer and the users, and between the designer and the developer (if they are not the same person).

## SOME RESULTS OF THE APPLICATION

In Spring 1992, the second version of the system based on the UPDA design method, SOS-2, was ready. A small verification field test (actual cases when the psychologists are making selection decisions) and a group evaluation were carried out to evaluate SOS-2. These tests lasted for three months. Owing to lack of time and resources and much to our regret, more thorough evaluation and validation tests cannot be carried out.

The field test examined the applicability of SOS-2 in the field in terms of structural compatibility, useability, functionality and the amount of support (or added value), which the system would give to the selection process (Koh, 1994). During this field test, the seven psychologists who participated were required to use the system in their work, and 15 real cases were used in the test. At the end of each case, the psychologists were asked to fill in an evaluation form. From the evaluation, a general picture of how successful the system was had been obtained. About 55% of the seven participants said that the quality of the advice (conclusion given by the second knowledge base) given by the system was good, while the rest said that it was not so good. About 80% of the psychologists affirmed that the system had given positive support to their work and 26% rated the support as very good. About 70% claimed that they had learned something from the system. For instance, they said that they had learned to work and think more systematically. From these results, we concluded that many of the goals of the system had been achieved.

After the field test was completed, the representatives of the bureaux and others who had participated in the test were invited to come for a group evaluation session. Four of them were present and all the bureaux were represented by at least by one psychologist. The group evaluation concentrated on the verification of the system, and the SOR was used as the basis. In addition, a general discussion and evaluation of the system was also carried out.

The results of the group evaluation on the SOR were disappointingly low. There are altogether 65 requirements in the SOR, out of which 37 are listed as essential and 24 as desirable and the rest (4) as not necessary. According to the psychologists who came for the group evaluation session, only 32% of the group of essential points in the SOR were fulfilled in the system. Some 54% of the essential points fell in the minus category. Under the group of desirable points, 21% were deemed as fulfilled and 33% unfulfilled. The psychologists involved in the group evaluation were also asked to assess the system in general on its functionality, helping users to learn and useability. The system scored relatively highly on helping the psychologists to learn. It scored average on the functionality aspect, although this was the most important factor upon which the usefulness of the system should be evaluated. Nevertheless, no negative point was given for this aspect.

## CONCLUSION

The UPDA method has been developed specifically to eliminate weaknesses found in SOS-1. It encompasses techniques, which we see as lacking in most conventional design methods, such as coding the SOR and checking them in the flow charts, using colour charts to enable potential users to help decide the design of the system and so on. We have used UPDA in redesigning and redeveloping the KB-DSS. The potential of this method is not yet fully realized owing to the short time and limited experience in applying it; nevertheless, the outcomes of this application are promising. More research and applications should be carried out to test its viability and to discover its shortcomings.
