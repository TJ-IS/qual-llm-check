# An application of the Delta Model and BPR in transforming electronic business – the case of a food ingredients company in UK

Nelson K. H. Tang\*, Prabhaker R. Yasa\* & Paul L. Forrester<sup>†</sup>

\*Technology and Operations Management Research Group, Aston Business School, Aston University, UK, and <sup>†</sup>Birmingham Business School, University of Birmingham, UK, email: p.l.forrester@bham.ac.uk.

Abstract. Companies are leveraging existing resources and the internet to come up with new or modified business models to respond to the demands of ebusiness. Companies in the food processing industry are also waking to the reality that e-business technology can make a big difference to the bottom line. This paper presents the result of an action research project which applied a new approach labelled as the Delta Model developed by Hax and Wilde (2001) of Sloan School of Management to strategy development for brick-n-mortar companies launching e-business initiatives. The case company is called Whetstone Food Ingredients (WFI), located in UK. The company is firmly embedded in the agriproduct industry and operates in the ‘egg by-product’ subsector. This paper is based upon action research supplemented by a programme of in-depth interviews with managers at WFI for various aspects of their supply chain and e-business initiatives. On the basis of these interviews and the companies also made a range of documents available throughout. These included internal memos, strategy plans, operational control documents and minutes of meetings. The Delta Model and Davenport’s methodology of business process reengineering were adopted to structure this analysis. Used in conjunction, they helped to develop a vision, analyse the business processes, identify critical business processes, benchmark the critical processes, and then develop the information technology infrastructure. The infrastructure thus supported the critical business processes and leveraged the ebusiness supply chain to enable the company to gain competitive edge.

Keywords: BPR, Delta Model, e-business, food industry, supply chain management, web-based order management system

## INTRODUCTION

New web-based systems and internet business processes are emerging to change the foundations of supply chain management. There are no clear guidelines for companies to adopt a strategy for their e-business and this has led Hax and Wilde of Sloan School of Management to develop a new approach to strategy development for brick-n-mortar companies launching ebusiness initiatives, which they labelled as the Delta Model. The Delta Model encompasses a set of frameworks and methodologies for articulation and implementation of effective corporate and business strategies. This model makes four contributions to strategic management (Hax & Wilde, 2001):

• The triangle, a set of three distinct strategic options for the company to achieve customer bonding.

• The adaptive processes, which link strategy and execution.

• The metrics, which align aggregate and granular metrics to strategy.

• The experimentation and feedback process, for business transformation and monitoring performance, respectively.

This paper presents the findings of the research project and, in so doing, illustrates how the Delta Model, combined with a business process reengineering (BPR) approach, can provide an awareness of information technology (IT) capabilities in launching e-business for a brick-nmortar company in the food processing industry. However, before discussing the Delta Mode and the findings of the case analysis, the research approach adopted by the researchers wil be presented, followed by some background to the organization.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Gunasekaran et al. (2000) suggested that a good case study ought to contain information that readers can use in replicating the experiences gained and lessons learnt in future endeavours under similar settings. The authors believe that there are valuable lessons that readers can gain from the experience of Whetstone Food Ingredients’ (WFI) project. Because the authors needed to populate the Delta Model with data and consequently apply the BPR approach, a series of in-depth interviews for investigating the various aspects of their e-business initiatives were conducted with managers at WFI, together with access to a range of company documents available throughout.

Case study action research has consistently been one of the most powerful research methods in operations management, particularly in the development of new theory (Voss et al., 2002). Stuart et al. (2002) suggested that case studies should not be seen as a methodology appropriate only for understanding and the preliminary stages of theory development. Because of their observational richness and participation, they also provide a means of refutation of, or extensions to, existing concepts.

The methodological approach used in this project is case study action research. This method is characterized by active participation in analysing and planning organizational change. The researchers were keen to triangulate the data sources from the case in order to validate and support the data collected for subsequent analysis. One of the authors was based within WFI and contributed to e-business systems developments, therefore, participation and action research formed the foundation of data collection. In addition, it was necessary to supplement this with a programme of semi-structured management interviews in order that the action researcher’s observations and perceptions could be checked against the interpretations of other actors. Finally the researchers had full and comprehensive access to documentary sources and information within WFI to provide facts, figures and recorded decision data to sup plement their data set.

## ORGANIZATIONAL BACKGROUND

Whetstone Food Ingredients, located in Rotherham, S. Yorkshire is a £10 m subsidiary of Stonegate, a £70 m integrated egg business. The company is firmly embedded in the agri product industry and operates in the ‘egg by-product’ subsector. They produce different products using ‘second grade eggs’ (i.e. eggs rejected because of wrong size, colour, etc.) returned by retailers to suppliers as raw material. Consumers include all the leading high street retailers and the major food processing companies. Customer loyalty levels are high with most of the customers having a history of long relationships with the company.

The company used to maintain a low profile and lacked a strategic direction until the present new managing director took over who brought a new strategic perspective and many best practices to the company. The company has a fairly simple business model and has no major unique selling point, because of the fact that all the players have similar cost structures and the end product is a commodity.

The management believes that consolidation in the industry is inevitable in foreseeable future and is considering horizontal integration to gain size and scale advantages. The company has begun merger talks with a similar sized company called Thames Valley. If the merger goes through, the present capacity will increase from 200 t a week to 500 t a week. The management believes that this move will help them in better competing with their larger competitors.

A look at the company’s cost structure reveals that 65–70% is contributed by the cost of raw materials, i.e. the cost of eggs. Lowering costs is the main concern of the company and they are specifically looking to reduce the fixed costs. At present the fixed costs stand at £1.6 m and management feels this is extremely high for the size of the company. The key challenge for the company is to gain competitive edge in a highly commoditized product market. Another challenge arises from their desire to double their capacity keeping the headcount at the same levels. After thoroughly assessing the competitive environment in which it operates, the following business goals were identified for the company in the medium to long term:

• To improve overall communication within the company.

To establish more effective customer relationships and improve customer service, customer convenience and customer satisfaction.

• To reduce paperwork, transaction costs, head count and day-to-day operating costs.

• To shorten cycle times, time-to-market and delivery times.

• To improve order management, forecasting and demand planning.

• To improve decision-making across the company.

• To allocate resources to demand and improve warehouse/distribution activities.

To improve perception of the company and products.

According to Boey et al. (1999), e-business is about making the key business processes of an organization available over the internet. By implementing these processes on the web, customers can do more than placing an order and paying for it. They can also track the status of an order from placement to delivery, receive prompt support once it has arrived, and easily place related orders. Organization can also make the web more than just another sales channel by implementing processes online. Inventory can be optimized by directly interfacing with suppliers, improve collections by tracking customers’ payment history, and build brand loyalty by providing customers with the quickest, most efficient, most reliable buying experience. Merely selling over the web is no longer a differentiator for most companies engaged in ecommerce (Mogollon & Raisinghani, 2003). It’s therefore, the authors propose to WFI that they need to recognize their e-potential and adopt the e-business implementation in order to maintain competitive advantage. ## THE DELTA MODEL OF BUSINESS TRANSFORMATION

Hax & Wilde (2001) have developed a new approach to strategy development for brick-nmortar companies launching e-business initiatives, which they labelled as the Delta Model. The Delta Model encompasses a set of frameworks and methodologies for articulation and implementation of effective corporate and business strategies. This model makes four contributions to strategic management:

• The triangle, a set of three distinct strategic options for the company to achieve customer bonding.

• The adaptive processes, which link strategy and execution.

• The metrics, which align aggregate and granular metrics to strategy.

• The experimentation and feedback process, for business transformation and monitoring performance, respectively (Table 2).

## The triangle of the Delta Model

Hax & Wilde (2001) point out that the first reflection to take place in the process of defining the strategy of the firm is to decide on the relevant strategic positioning. According to them, this is an attempt to capture the essence of how the firm chooses to compete in its relevant market place, or how the firm decides to attract, to satisfy, and to retain the customer.

The best product positioning as shown in Figure 1 builds upon the classical form of com petition. The customer is attracted by the inherent characteristics of the product itself – either through low cost, which provides a price advantage that can be partly passed to the custome – or through differentiation, which introduce unique features that the customer values and for which they are willing to pay a premium. But with this strategy, the product tends to be stan dardized and massive, the customer is generic, massive, faceless and the central focus of attention is the main competition. The best product strategic option has as a primary objective the development of a lean internal infrastructure and supply chain that gives the low cost sup port so essential for this positioning to succeed.

The total customer solution is a complete reversal from the best product approach. Instead of commoditizing the customer, an intimate and deep customer understanding and relationship to develop value propositions that bond to each individual customer is sought. Instead of concentrating inwardly on their own supply chains, companies start to develop an integrated supply chain with key suppliers and customers. Instead of focusing on competitors and imitating them, companies redefine the ways to capture and serve the customer by putting together the overall set of corporate capabilities complemented by proper external parties that enhance their product offering.

The system lock-in strategic option has the widest scope; it includes the extended enterprise – the firm, the customers, the suppliers, and most importantly, the key complementors. A complementor, according to Hax & Wilde (2001), is a firm that engages in the delivery of products and services that enhances the product and service portfolio of a company. The key to this strategic option is to identify, attract, and nurture the complementors. The complementors are often external, but may also be internal to the corporation, particularly in large and diversified organizations. The richness and depth of complementors supporting the company’s product or service lock the company’s product into the system and lock-out the competition. A company that achieves system lock-in can exercise an enormous amount of power.

## Application of the triangle of the Delta Model to WFI

WFI has been following the ‘best product’ strategy through its choice of competition based upon product economics. It has a low cost product positioning, as do most of its competitors, because the product is highly commoditized. In line with their goals of reducing transaction costs both for themselves and their customers, WFI will have to move on to adopt the ‘total customer solutions' strategy. This will allow them to concentrate not only on their internal operations, but also on developing an integrated supply network that links with its key suppliers and customers. The views of the managing director are useful to consider here, as they illustrate the needs of the business and areas for improvement:

I would expect communication to improve within the company as well as that with our customers and suppliers. Any ideas that improve our operational efficiency are highly welcome at this stage of our business . . . In operational terms, optimal resource allocation, improved demand management, reduction of transaction costs, and lead-time reduction are the specific goals. It goes without saying that any initiative should improve the customers’ perception about us. Any solution we look into should meet as many of the above goals as possible. Unlike most projects which can be defined specifically with clear cut objectives, this one can’t be. We expect this project to bring out more clear-cut objectives. Basically we are searching for new ways in which the internal operations of the business can be run as well as the more widely broadcast opportunities for external liaisons through B2B and e-commerce.

The role of IT changes from a set of internalized, set of discrete bespoke systems to that of a network of interconnected and interdependent business processes between channel partners. The company can eventually move to the ‘system lock-in’ strategic position over the next 3–5 years, once it consolidates its position through multiple channels and identifies key complementors whom can extend and add value to their products. The managing director explained in the interview, ‘In the long term, we want to emerge as a nimble and agile com pany that plays a crucial role in the group’s portfolio of products. My personal vision I want to sell to the employees of the company is that of a smart business which leverages IT to gain operational efficiencies and competitive edge over other competition.’ Table 3 contrasts the three strategic options and spells out the impact on relevant organizational dimensions for WFI.

## The adaptive processes of the Delta Model

The adaptive processes reflect the changing nature of tasks involve, provide a path to carry the strategic position to a high level of execution excellence. The Delta Model proposes operationa effectiveness, which involves production and delivery of goods and services as a key business processes to achieve the desired strategic positioning and business transformation. Most production and delivery processes will have to meet one or more of four distinct reguirements

• timeliness;

• quality;

• low cost; and

• flexibility.

Each of the above imposes different demands on the system and every competitive situation will require differing levels of performance against these criteria. Achieving operational effectiveness will require the firm to make some tradeoffs between these criteria.

## Application of the adaptive processes to WFI

The operations manager at WFI has identified quality and low cost as the key performance criteria, followed by timeliness and flexibility in that order. Quality is very important to satisfy the regulatory requirements of the food industry. Any slippage in ensuring stringent quality measures might lead to serious health-related consequences and the company runs the risk of provoking a backlash from customers. Low cost is also being considered an important criterion as the company operates in a highly commoditized product market. In the internet age, timeliness has become a weapon for achieving competitive advantage with companies waging fierce battles to win customers with promises of quick fulfilment times. ## Application of the metrics of the Delta Model to WF

A primary purposes of measurement is to assess performance levels and to analyse what is happening and where. The most beneficial aspect of performance measurement, however, is pinpointing problem areas and focusing attention on actions that will have the best impact on overall business performance. The Delta Model aligns performance metrics to the strategic options selected, and recognizes that these metrics are fundamentally different depending on the strategic position they intend to support. The model suggests that metrics to be collected at the aggregate level to aid in execution of a strategic option and at the granular level to understand the variability inherent in the behaviour of some critical parameters such as product costs., customer profitability, worker productivity and so on.

Apart from the categorization at the aggregate and granular levels, measures are usually separated into two more categories: direct physical measures and qualitative measures. The evolution of logistics toward a cross-functional and cross-organizational focus has prompted the need for more integrative and boundary spanning metrics.

## Application of experimentation and feedback of the Delta Model to WF

The final stage of the Delta Model addresses the issues of experimentation and feedback. It is here where we can identify the benefit of linking the Delta Model to BPR analysis. Hax & Wilde (2001) use the term experimentation to denote the process of carrying out business transformation in a responsible manner. This step can be viewed as an all-encompassing process for redesign initiatives, which has been variously described as the most crucial step by pioneers of BPR (Hammer, 1990; Davenport 1994). Experimentation and feedback facilitates constructive criticisms of the overriding strategic approach and, where appropriate, enables a new strategic agenda and course of action to be set to address changes in circumstances that have subsequently become apparent.

Within WFI we have applied Davenport & Short’s (1990) five-step approach to BPR. In what follows, each of the steps will be described and related to the WFI case.

## 2. Identify the processes to be redesigned

Most firms use the high-impact approach which focuses on the most important processes or those that conflict most with the business vision. Fewer firms use the exhaustive approach, which attempts to identify all the processes within an organization and then prioritize them in order of redesign urgency.

It emerged that order processing is the most crucial business process from the analysis. Order processing deals with those activities that collect and prepare sales information for a firm that typically has many warehouses throughout a geographic region. At WFI, the key decisions of order processing involve specification of the order entry procedures, selection of order transmission methods, setting order processing priorities and procedures, and deciding on the most appropriate internal methods for handling regular, split, and consolidated orders.

## 3. Understand and measure the existing processes

Process timing of the business process can be quantitatively analysed using the durations incurred by each activity in the process. This helps to determine the steps in the process where the greatest amount of time is consumed. Also, these detailed data reveal whether the business process’s time is incurred as transit time, in-queue time, out-queue time, or productive process time. The critical subprocesses in order processing are complete order, generate invoice, schedule order, issue production note and issue delivery note as shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3 reveals that almost 95% of time in ‘complete order’ activity is consumed in the inqueue. In other words, almost nothing happens for 3 h after a customer places an order. ‘generate invoice’ activity, which involves using another accounting system, consumes another 5 min. ## 4. Identify IT levers

Awareness of IT capabilities can and should influence process design. Any e-business initiative should start with establishing an internal company intranet to enable business processes to be streamlined and automated, as shown in Figure 4. The proposed web-based e-business system architecture for WFI consists of an intranet for automating different internal business processes and an extranet system which integrates suppliers and customers into WFI’s business processes.

At the core of the system is a centralized data-warehouse (DW), which draws together the discrete pieces of data spread all over the company together. The current accounting system SAGE, the production system SCADA and barcode system will all be integrated into the intranet system through the data-warehouse. The manual process of operators recording parameter values on pieces of paper is currently error prone and has been blamed for some problems reported. Connecting the SCADA system directly to the intranet system and storing all the runs electronically can eliminate this manual process. The purchasing & payment processing module and the order processing & fulfilment module will be integrated into the company’s website, which will be available on a 24/7 basis on a high bandwidth internet infrastructure. Electronic invoicing will reduce the workload on administrative staff, as an invoice is automatically sent to the customer on confirmation of shipment.

The purchasing module will automate the buying process by checking the raw material availability on receipt of a sales order and sending purchase orders to the concerned suppliers. It will also have the ability to despatch payments electronically by using third party payment gateways. The order processing and fulfilment module will have complete order management functionality, including online order placement, order validation, order tracking, order history, order scheduling and shipment notification functionality. It will also generate invoices, production notes, delivery notes and vehicle run sheets. This can eventually be shaped into a Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI) system, where suppliers can monitor the availability of eggs and, depending on the shared production schedule and sales forecast, they can send shipments automatically.

The production planning module will conduct advanced planning, scheduling and resource planning in order to support the due diligence module that will read production parameters from the existing SCADA system and perform different validations and verifications on the param eters to ensure conformance to the quality standards. All the product specifications and quality standards can also be placed on an extended portion of the intranet (the extranet), so that customers can view them and also customize them according to their needs. This will greatly reduce the amount of time the technical manager is spending on manually writing and re writing quality standards and specifications. The Inventory Tracking module will use the existing barcode system and track the inventory right from arrival of shipments from suppliers through to keeping status of availability of finished product in the warehouse. Stocks are currently recorded in physical documents, which are then keyed into excel sheets. If the stock sheets are created in electronic form in the first place, this information can be used in generating KPI documents and also for arriving at costing information by finance. Such a database can also be useful in monitoring the stock levels by week/month/year and also in calculating stock turnover periods. The Laboratory Management System (LMS) can eliminate all the exce sheets being used in the lab. The current lab testing involves filling up a high volume of paper work, some in physical form and some in electronic form and then retrieving the documents later for verification/auditing purposes. If all the data are stored in a database, different anal yses can be performed easily by using automated formulas.

## 5. Design and build a prototype of the new process

Order processing is an integral link in the order cycle and therefore it is a key logistics activity. The salient features of the new business process will be:

1 Customers will place orders through WFI’s order entry website using a unique customer id and password.

2 The order processing system will validate the order for completeness, make credit checks against the customer and then approve the order.

3 The order details are stored in the order database for future reference and tracking purposes.

4 The order database will have unique customer profiles for each customer to offer differ entiated pricing structures. This functionality will also provide the capability for volume based discounting where customers are given discounts for buying higher volumes.

5 Customers can request and view the shipment status of their orders through the website which also provides past purchase history.

6 Once the order is approved, electronic invoices are generated and dispatched immediatel through email. Electronic funds transfer is then initiated by the finance function.

## CONCLUSIONS

To conclude, the applications of the Delta Model and Davenport’s methodology of BPR in WFI have demonstrated the value of these methods in developing an e-business supply chain roadmap. Internet and web-based applications facilitate a new means of delivering products and services to customers. As demonstrated, they also assist the effective implementation of streamlined business processes for inter- and intra-organizational collaboration, enabling in the case WFI to face the challenge of doubling its capacity while keeping the headcount at the same level.

The way orders are being handled at WFI has been transformed by the web-based ordering processing and fulfillment system. One of the key impacts on the sales and logistics functions is the total tracking of orders by the complete integration of sales through to delivery, leading to increased customer satisfaction. The streamlining of processes, automation of quality assur ance and the issue of COC processes become imperative when facing challenges of fast growth in volumes. This is especially important as multisite coordination will be required to ensure opti mal quality and consistency in the product. The new system is highly scalable. It can handle any amount of production data. It will reduce the possibility of errors when production volumes increase, and also reduce the need for extensive training for new recruits on analysis and inter pretation of results. One of the key e-business initiatives is to establish cost effective collaborative partnerships with customers and suppliers. This paper has demonstrated how this can be achieved through the planning and development of an e-business infrastructure to enhance the performance of critical business processes, thus gaining competitive edge in this digital era.
