# Systems development approach: transcending methodology

Markos Goulielmos

Digital Systems & Technology Teaching Department, University of Piraeus, 80 Karaoli & Dimitriou St 185 34, Greece, email: mgoul@acn.gr

Abstract. This paper presents the findings of an empirical study investigating the nature of the process of information systems development. These findings are used to identify systems development approach, which extends understanding of the information systems development process beyond the levels of methodology and technical expertise. The term approach expresses the rich, varied, and complex ways in which professionals deal with development situations. The term transcends methodological components – which can be combined, tailored, or omitted altogether. Within approach, a framework for method choice, a key diagnostic scheme, and the use of a central repository reveal the significant background activity that goes into developing information systems. It is suggested that the information systems development process ultimately involves a professional using an approach to intervene in a problematic situation for developing the organization’s information and, if needed, other systems. This understanding has implications for examining the field’s ineffectiveness in relation to the approach adopted – rather than the methodology used.

Keywords: information systems development process, systems developmen approach, methodology, grounded theory

## INTRODUCTION

There is an ongoing concern that there continues to be a significant failure rate in information systems development (ISD). Although some estimates have suggested that half of all systems fail (Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Galloway & Whyte, 1989), others have argued that more systems fail than succeed (Whyte & Bytheway, 1996). This picture is made even grimmer by reports that still more failures go unnoticed because there is no public reaction to them (Oz, 1994). Although not all projects are destined to fail, it is increasingly difficult to ensure success using the traditional paradigm of ISD thinking. It has long been argued that the mainstream IS paradigm is limited in providing methodological and technological solutions to problems (Kumar & Welke, 1984). Alternative ways of thinking have therefore been put forward to address this problem (Checkland, 1981; Mumford, 1981; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). There is a growing argument that IS development should be seen as more than a technical activity. Rather, it should be understood as a complex social activity that is influenced by the organizational context in which it takes place (Lederer & Nath, 1991; Cannon, 1994; Willcocks & Margetts, 1994). Although this is a powerful argument for transformation in the field, widespread adoption of alternative paradigms has not yet occurred in practice, and accounts of IS failure continue to appear.

To explore these issues, the present study examines the process of developing information systems from a holistic perspective – looking not only at what goes into the construction of systems. but also at any additional activities needed by ISD professionals if they are to make the process operate effectively and successfully within the organizational situation. Such a perspective begins from the point at which an ISD professional is invited to enter into a client relationship to the point at which he or she leaves that relationship. A main proposition of the present study is that ISD professionals should be able to recognize these additional holistic prerequisites, and should have developed ways for dealing with them as they arise. The processes surrounding the traditional limited view of ISD can contribute towards a better understanding of IS failure.

ISD professionals with experience across the whole spectrum of consulting and development activities were selected for the present study. These professionals occupied top management positions in a number of large well-established consultancy firms and were in charge of internal ISD departments of large organizations. It was felt that only such senior professionals were capable of providing a holistic understanding of the problem – rather than a limited technical or methodological perspective. They were asked to talk about the ISD process and the role that ISD professionals have in this process.

In practice a variety of practitioners are involved in ISD. These people are described by different terms – including analysts, programmers, developers, consultants, IT practitioners, and ISD professionals. However, professionals who manage the IT teams are responsible for the whole process and for interfacing with clients. These people have advised internal and externa clients about development issues, have managed teams, and have experience in the construction of systems. Furthermore, they also participate in articulating and formulating ISD policy for their organizations. To distinguish these people from the more specialized practitioners who make up the teams, these team leaders will be referred to as ‘ISD professionals’ in the remainder of this paper.

The structure of the paper is as follows. The next section presents the research methodology for data collection and analysis. A case for using grounded theory is made out, and key concepts are explained. The paper then presents the results of the study – beginning with a presentation of the ISD process theory that emerged from the developed categories and links, and then moving on to present key findings and indicative data from selected cases. The results section ends with a summary of the developed concepts. The paper then proceeds to discuss the findings, including links with existing formal theory and the implications for ISD in general. Finally, the paper concludes with a summary of the contribution made by the study.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

Several studies have discussed the question of methodology in investigating ISD (Fitzgerald, 1997; 1998a; Hughes & Wood-Harper, 1999). The present study selected grounded theory as the research methodology because it was considered suitable for rigorous theory development (Gill & Johnson, 1991) and for the study of context in relation to process (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Turner, 1981; Martin & Turner, 1986). Grounded theory has been previously used in IS research for developing context-based, process-oriented descriptions and explanations of the phenomena under study (Eisenhardt, 1989; Ancona, 1990; Elsbach & Sutton, 1992; PriesHeje, 1992; Orlikowski, 1993; Bowker et al., 1995; Myers, 1997; Allen et al., 2000).

A sample of 50 large consultancy firms was chosen from the greater London area. A twopage request letter was sent summarizing the main issues concerning IS failure and inviting participants to assist in the research. Six firms responded positively. These made up the ‘consultancy sample’. The same process was repeated for large organizations with in-house development departments, and five firms agreed to assist. These made up the ‘in-house sample’. Data from both samples were collected through in-depth semi-structured interviewing, obser vation, and documentary analysis. Secondary data were collected in the form of in-house reports, project reports, manuals on methods, and company brochures.

In addition to the interviews presented in Table 1. several informal discussions were held with other professionals assigned by the original interviewee in each interview. The purpose of these informal discussions was to acquaint the researchers with the methods and tools used by the company in question. After the completion of data collection and analysis, follow-up telephone conversations were carried out with participants for clarification and confirmation of the concepts developed.

Open explorative and historical questions were asked at the beginning of each interview to identify assumptions and generate conversation (Schein, 1987). Ambiguities, contradictions, and inconsistencies that revealed in-house values and assumptions were noted (Argyris & Schon, 1978).

Observation was directed to cultural elements and reflection was focused on changes in personal feelings (Rice, 1963). Data from personal feelings and observation were contrasted with interview and documentary data, thus enabling triangulation (Orlikowski, 1993).

Analysis began with the ‘consultancy sample’ and proceeded to the ‘in-house sample’.

With the ‘consultancy sample’, a dynamic constant comparative cycle (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) was not fully achieved because of difficulties in scheduling interviews with senior people in large organizations. This unpredictability in arranging interviews meant that a new case had to be examined before analysis of the preceding one had been completed. Strauss & Corbin’s (1990) framework was used to manage the research process and its pressures.

Until core categories were firmly identified, open coding was used to generate data. This gave an initial impression of generating large volumes of apparently unrelated data. However, through subsequent cycles, patterns began to emerge and concepts were confirmed. Axia coding helped to develop links among categories. With the identification of core categories, analysis shifted from a process of initial exploration to a process of elaborating key concepts and their properties. Selective coding was used to relate the substantive approach category (see below for explanation) to other categories.

To organize the surviving categories into an emerging process theory, it was necessary to assess the roles of these categories in the overall sequence of changing events and conditions (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). An additional level of complexity involved identifying which concepts were static and which concepts were dynamic.

Scheduling meetings was easier with the ‘in-house sample’, but lower volumes of data were generated because of the more specialized projects undertaken by the companies in this sample. However, rich data were generated concerning the complexity of the organizational situation and context. No new concepts were developed with the completion of data collection.

The consultancies were also asked to provide access to their clients and to allow the researchers to follow through on projects. Two firms responded positively, but it ultimately proved impossible to schedule additional research time. This was responsible for the absence of a ‘client perspective’ on the emerging concepts.

Finally, a conditional matrix was used to place the findings in a wider context (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

## The client phase

As can be seen in Figure 1, organizational concerns within the client’s organization create pressure. This leads to the client taking action (referred to as client’s action taking in Figure 1) and embarking on a project. At this point, initial views about what is needed are formulated, and ISD professionals (either internal or external) are invited to provide advice. The formulation of initial expectations and the invitation to ISD professionals are influenced by such categories in Figure 1 as the client’s ISD tradition, the values and language of internal IT staff (represented by IT rationalities in Figure 1), and the organization’s capacity for ISD. The ISD professiona enters the client’s particular situation, meets the client, and employs a key diagnostic scheme (which is part of the approach in Figure 1). The ISD professional also utilizes his or her skills and professional roles.

## The ISD professional phase

## Approach

In Figure 1, approach is the core category employed by the ISD professional and guides practical action. It expresses the rich, varied, and complex ways in which ISD professionals deal with development situations. A highly saturated cluster of data in the present study characterizes approach as a ‘meta-methodological concept’ that is more abstract than mere methodology. Approach emerges from a number of other key elements – which are examined below.

Values Values within the approach (see Figure 1) include assumptions and metaphors about the nature of ISD, the various actors, the development process, and the role of the firm in the market. The values of each particular organization are unique. As a result, each approach is unique. The approach thus transcends any particular methodology and relates to what is considered essential and best practice in every project. For example, the company designated as VLC1 (see Table 1) utilized structured methodologies to ensure that every project had adequate involvement – even from parts of the organization that had been excluded initially. Similarly, MC2 (see Table 1) always identified actual needs (as opposed to wishes).

In terms of shared values, the firms in the ‘consultancy sample’ exhibited strong pragmatic values (doing what is necessary to get the work done) and a market orientation (operating successfully in the marketplace by offering expertise, tools and services). ISD professionals in the ‘in-house sample’ also exhibited pragmatic values (doing what is necessary to get the work done). In addition, they exhibited IT rationality (a preference for solving problems using infor mation technology).

Framework Within the overall approach, a framework exists for making methodological choices (see Figure 1). This is consistent with the overall values of the organization and employs a number of criteria for making choices among different possible paths to develop ment (see Table 2).

Within the framework, all firms reported that methodologies were used for consistency, coordination, communication, and documentation. For example, VLC1 saw methodology devel opment as ensuring a common communication system among involved parties. LC2 also examined a methodology in terms of its documentation merits, and decided whether its communication system required tailoring.

Guided by the framework, each firm utilized a rich variety of methodological components within its approach (see Table 3). These components originate not only from ISD methods bu also from related areas (such as business process re-engineering and quality improvement).

A diversity of components was employed, with some components appearing in more than one approach in various combinations. For example, a project management and an ISD methodology was used in the same project (VLC1, VLC2) whereas custom methods were used within existing methods in other cases (VLC3, INS1). INS1 employed unconventional combi nations – such as an RAD method within an overall BPR methodology. LF1 and CHE1 claimed that they always use a collection of methods, techniques, and tools rather than a single method. This diversity demonstrates the pragmatic values of ISD professionals and shows that a combination of components is desirable, rather than problematic – despite what the meth odology creators have intended.

Key processes Another element in the approach is represented in Figure 1 as key processes. This category includes problem-solving processes represented by the categories of method ology tailoring, project management, and matching needs with wants.

Methodology tailoring is used within the ISD process during adaptation to situation (see Figure 1). Through methodology tailoring, ISD professionals meet the unique needs of a project by omitting, modifying, or adjusting various steps of chosen components as appropri ate. A form of tailoring is built-in our methodology which allows the consultant to make a choice from a range of components to just the right ones for his project in order to provide creative and innovative solutions which not only meet the immediate needs but also enhance the client’s ability to manage future change and growth. Such solutions are always individual, based on a particular situation and a specific response.

All firms reported that tailoring of methodologies and techniques occurred in more than 90% of their projects. Several criteria were used in making decisions on tailoring (see Table 4)

Project management is another key process for resolving management issues before any formal development starts. VLC2’s IT manager explained the concept of a ‘project’ as being more abstract and wider than the concept of an ‘information system’. The concept of a ‘project allows contextual issues to be discussed and resolved. All firms reported that project management helps balance changes, assists in focusing on a good start to the project, manages inherent risks, manages expectations, and allows control. It also facilitates a consideration of change management issues pertaining to the project by addressing, from the outset, how the project will be structured and resourced.

Project management becomes the focal point of the various parties involved in development while allowing an interface with the organization. As part of approach, project management is the means for managing the intervention of ISD professionals and for managing working rela tionships during the formal ISD activities.

Matching needs with wants refers to the facts all ISD professionals reported, that the client largely controlled the initiation of projects and that projects did not proceed until clients were convinced that priority issues would be resolved at an acceptable cost. Because the ISD professional might have had different ideas about the nature of these priorities, the project could not start until there was agreement about the client’s wants and his or her actual needs. Despite potential conflict at this stage, the ISD professional’s pragmatic values mean that he or she will always help the client achieve his or her wants. However, because the project’s success might be threatened, ISD professionals employ the key process of matching needs with wants. This process utilizes a number of strategies to achieve a match (Table 5):

Matching client’s wants and needs requires time, a great deal of political intervention, and the use of interpersonal skills in areas of ISD complexity. Apart from two firms (MC1 and CHE1), which employed process consultation within their methodological arsenal, most firms presented a limited view of political matters and change-management intervention. Process consultation aims to develop the client’s understanding such that the client is able to deliver the solution (Schein, 1988). This form of intervention requires a deep understanding of relationship building and change-management issues. Although matching wants with needs sometimes means that ISD must go beyond the resolution of technical issues, ISD professionals appear somewhat limited in their ability to deal with these wider issues – resorting to reactive strategies based more on trial and error than considered management techniques. There is a strong argument for bringing in theories and tools from the fields of the behavioural sciences and change management to improve the ISD intervention process.

Key diagnostic scheme Another element in the approach (see Figure 1) is the key diagnostic scheme. The ISD professional, having been invited into a client relationship, utilizes a key diagnostic scheme to assess the client, the project, and the ISD professional’s own abilities before committing to the project. As the VLC1 IT department manager observed: ‘. . . the ISD professional needs to look out for a potentially sick project – a project where everything goes wrong – as early as possible’. Beyond the feasibility of the project, the ability of the organization to undertake the project is assessed in terms of the scheme’s various dimensions (Table 6).

Assessment continues throughout the project. ISD professionals reported that they might even abandon a project if they realize that the client’s attitude has become inappropriate (VLC1). For in-house projects, the rejection of a client can cause contention and raise ‘barriers between IT people and line managers’ (LF2’s IT development manager).

Components repository The components registry is the last subcomponent of the approach category (see Figure 1). Firms reflect on their experiences and can create central repositories of methodological, project support, and process components. These repositories facilitate knowledge and expertise being available for future projects (Table 7). Firms in the second sample also recorded and reused material, but their libraries were not in the form of advanced electronic collections. In the first sample, most of the repositories had evolved into automated libraries offering easy access to ISD professionals involved in projects.

Using the repositories, the approach’s framework, advice, and methods and techniques are available for ISD professionals to use. As MC1’s IT manager observed: ‘… in our templates library there is a lot of material at the periphery of methodologies’. This ‘periphery’ ensures consistency of performance and approach among different ISD professionals and projects. Such repositories provide physical evidence and documentation of an approach. The initia motivation for creating such repositories is to reuse code fragments and thus speed up development in new projects. Eventually more diverse components are added.

## Professional roles

Some of the projects undertaken by the firms in the present study were oriented towards ‘expert content’, whereas others were oriented towards ‘client process’. ‘Expert content’ refers to various degrees of technical expertise being provided or being directly involved in development. ‘Client process’ projects include IT staff and user training projects, in-house developer training on methods, package selection, IT strategy development, and improving organizational performance. As LC2’s director noted: ‘these may be delivered either prescriptively or facilitatively’. This means that either a solution is prescribed by the ISD professional (and implemented subsequently) or that the client is assisted in developing the solution. As reported by all firms, projects often have requirements that are both prescriptive and facilitative.

Professional roles confirm the plurality and flexibility of the approach. All firms had expertise in (more or less) the same methods. ISD professionals are differentiated by their individual values, their conduct, and their approach – rather than by methodological expertise or market specialization.

## Skills

In addition to approach and roles, a number of skills were reported as being characteristics of successful ISD professionals:

A fundamental knowledge of ISD. Such knowledge ensures that issues concerning the challenges of change, sharing of visions, understanding culture, pursuing learning, and assuming responsibility are addressed. As VLC2’s senior consultant noted:

[This] acts as an implicit frame of reference. Without it the consultant is unable to interpret what is going on and what he is required to do.

Political and interpersonal skills. Interpersonal skills relate to an ‘ability to influence customers’ and a willingness to ‘upset people’ if needed (LC2). In terms of politics, the main task is to raise political support for the project even if this has not been ensured from the outset. The ISD professional must be able to ‘access and involve parts of the organization that have been shutout of the process of development’ (MC1).

Technical skills refer to ISD outcome factors related to assessing the technology involved in the project (LC2).

Managerial skills. The ISD professional is required to have strong management skills, an ‘ability to make decisions’ and an ability to ‘manage the quality of his personal work and the quality of the project’ (VLC1).

Process skills are also needed to establish good working relationships with clients that ensure future business. As noted by MC2’s director:

A significant aspect of the ISD professional’s work involves managing his relationship with the client, which requires the ISD professional to manage his process of consultation.

These various skills are closely interrelated. The best ISD professionals are distinguished by their ability to maintain successful relationships with clients (LC2, VLC1, MC2), to solve problems in practical ways (LC2, VLC1, MC2), to listen to clients (LC2, MC2), and to maintain tight project management (VLC2, VLC1). Unsuccessful ISD professionals lack a funda mental understanding of ISD and their role, and are ineffective in building effective relationships with their clients. These skills are necessary if an approach is to be effectively employed.

## The situation entry phase

So far we have examined the client and the ISD professional phases in the process. Now we turn into the phases of the ISD process where approach’s elements come into action.

As we saw in the previous phase, when entering a particular client situation, the ISD professional first employs the key diagnostic scheme to diagnose issues and this is indicated by the client assessment category. A range of criteria is assessed, and if certain conditions for abandoning the project are met, the involvement comes to an end. Once the client is accepted, client assessment continues and the project enters project start.

## Project start

According to LF2’s IT development manager, this stage involves the ‘creation of space’ for the project. It proceeds iteratively until the client and the ISD professional are confident that development should start. The ISD professional collaborates with the client to produce ‘value propositions’ concerning the systems needed (VLC2). The client’s infrastructure is examined to identify actual needs and resources. This activity reveals the systemic nature of approach as client assessment continues. For projects less extensively defined, the ISD professional migh produce a list of diagnostic issues, which the client prioritizes, before deciding which ones to confront (MC2). This links with the professional roles discussed earlier, whereby the ISD pro fessionals could act in an advisory role. Problem-solving activities, training, and improvement thus precede an ISD project.

Although early estimates of cost and time scales are often too low, a good project start ensures that management comprehends problems and that critical issues are dealt with early on when costs are smaller. ISD professionals in both of the present samples expressed the importance of an effective project start, and were of the opinion that successful projects are characterized by it.

## Situational complexity

When the ISD professional enters a particular situation, he or she is confronted with a series of problems – not simply ISD problems. Although many ISD professionals consider these to be outside the scope of ISD, they do regard them as being important in determining a successfu outcome. Some reported that ISD is part of a larger problematic situation in two-thirds of their projects (LC2, VLC1, MC2). Others reported that most projects are disrupted by the wider organizational situation (LF1, LF2, INS1). All firms reported a variety of these ‘out-of-scope’ problems (Table 8).

Dealing with issues was done in a similar fashion to the manner in which ISD professionals match client’s needs with wants. ISD professionals tried to work with clients to resolve such issues. If that were not possible, the main strategy was to try to understand the nature of the problems and to facilitate a solution with or without the client’s help. Less-effective strategies were providing information to the client and letting the client deal with the problems on his or her own. ISD professionals in the second sample were located closer to the organizational context (because of their position in internal departments). Both samples confirmed dimensions of complexity concerning out-of-scope problems (Table 9).

ISD professionals do not find that all clients are easy to work with. In both samples, approximately 25% of the clients were reported as being difficult (or even impossible) to work with – thus producing additional difficulties in managing the relationship. Furthermore, half of all the clients were reported as not being ready for the IT-related change implied by the projects they initiate.

During this process, the client is prepared for formal ISD development. For example, the client’s internal IT staff and management might be trained in using a particular methodology. The project now continues, employing the project management process to guide project run ning (Figure 1). Formal ISD activities commence guided by the appropriate approach.

## The post-ISD project phase

Once the formal ISD activities conclude a number of outcomes are reported in the post-project period.

## Organizational change

All firms emphasized that ISD ultimately leads to organizational change. In the experience of INS1, LF2, and LF1 projects have introduced far-reaching cultural and organizational change.

However, such organizational change is usually considered to be ‘out of scope’ for ISD professionals, who appear to ignore the fact that change might be occurring from the point of their entry into the client situation (and not merely as a result of ISD).

## ISD outcome factors

At the end of a project, ISD professionals evaluate formal ISD activities to record ISD outcome factors in terms of success and failure. These are integrated into components repositories and provide criteria for the key diagnostic scheme. ISD professionals in both samples were asked to identify the most critical outcome factors (Table 10). Given the small number of firms in both samples these are by no means exhaustive and cannot be taken to indicate causal conditions to IS failure. However, they can inform further studies into the phenomenon.

## Withdrawal

Having recorded the ISD outcome factors, the project comes to its end represented by the withdrawal category. The ISD professional has to ensure that his relationship with the client comes to an appropriate closure, leaving the door open for future work. Repeat business is evidence of successful and lasting ISD professional–client relationships.

## DISCUSSION

## The concept of approach

According to the emerging ISD process theory proposed in this paper, the ISD process is more complex than has been assumed in the past and ISD professionals employ complex structures (or approaches) in dealing with the process. These structures and their function can be linked to the existing formal theory, as previously suggested by other studies that have proposed generalizing theoretical concepts and patterns (Orlikowski, 1993). The emerging ISD process the ory organizes static and dynamic elements that can be linked to the notions of structure and interaction that are part of the structuration theory (Giddens, 1984). Approach is a structure that is produced by learning from situations, projects, and clients. It is reproduced from interaction, as long as it remains appropriate and effective.

## Approach vs. method

The centrality of approach in the emerging process theory has serious implications for ISD methodologies. It is clear that there is no ‘pure’ use of methodologies – at least as their creators intended. ISD methodologies assist in development. However, they cannot ensure success or directly lead to failure. Too much emphasis has been placed on methodologies by the ISD discipline (Ciborra, 1997). Nearly half of all ISD projects use no methodology (Hardy et al., 1995; Chatzoglou & Macaulay, 1996; Fitzgerald, 1998b) and a return to the early days of ad hoc development might be occurring (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003). The present data reveal that systems do not fail because of methodologies, but because of the way people use them and conduct themselves. Tailoring is performed in methodologies that were not meant to be tailorable without this being problematic. This process takes place even when an ISD methodology is predetermined – whereby the ISD professional ‘optimizes’ its use. The implication of this is that pursuing the goal of producing an all-encompassing methodology is not only unrealistic but also undesirable in practice. In contrast, the concept of approach expresses the systemic nature of IS development and supports the systems property of equifinality – that is, reaching the same end goal from many alternative routes and initial conditions (Cummings & Huse, 1987). Key processes and frameworks ensure this. Therefore, effort and resources should be directed towards a better understanding of frameworks and how methodological components are evaluated, extracted, mixed, and matched in practice. Methodology development should continue, but it should adopt a new perspective – that of placing methodologies within frame works to facilitate diagnosis, choice, and tailoring.

Pragmatism appears to cause problems in two areas: (1) ‘out-of-scope’ organizationa issues; and (2) dealing with difficult clients or clients who have other ideas about what should be done. Both areas are regarded as unavoidable side effects of the ISD process. However, the present study did not reveal an understanding of these issues or a proactive attitude towards them. The complexity and sophistication of the approach deals with such issues – but only at a superficial level. When the situation or working relationship exceeds certain limits. ISD professionals abandon the project. The emerging ISD process remains shallow and does not drive intervention at a deeper level for addressing needed change into the client system. A direct result of this is an absence of ‘organizational’ components within approaches. The development of an organizational tool kit. as well as an infusion of a theoretical basis. would offer better support to ISD professionals in these areas.

Another aspect of the dichotomy between approach and method is the fact that approach operates from a flexible, open, and diagnostic viewpoint in which key processes guide problem solving. In contrast, most methodologies appear to be employed quite late in the ISD process – depending on a number of issues to be resolved. Methodologies lack access to problem-solving techniques for dealing with a changing context. An example of this is involvement. All firms ensure through their approach that adequate involvement is ensured despite the methodologies used. An important implication is the need for development of flexible, open diagnostic models that can be applied through approaches. Work in this area has focused on holistic sets of factors (Chu & Bannister, 1992; Whyte & Bytheway, 1996). It is clear that more work is needed to turn these into diagnostic models.

Furthermore, a significant number of activities appear to take place outside the traditiona notion of the systems development life cycle and are ignored by methodologies. This needs to be further explored to determine how phases of client assessment, project start, adaptation to situation, and withdrawal relate to a successful outcome.

## The client’s role

ISD professionals complain that clients do not fully appreciate what is entailed in commissioning an ISD project. Poor client involvement or understanding of the implications of the proposed development introduces risks into the project and places pressures on ISD professionals (which sometimes cannot be resisted). There is therefore an emphasis on project start and the diagnostic scheme within the approach. Lacking a client perspective in the study, the present paper cannot contrast this point with data from clients. However, similar arguments are made in the IT/ISD sourcing literature. The client is confirmed to be unprepared for outsourcing projects (Willcocks & Lacity, 1998). Carefully choosing between selective (rather than total) outsourcing and insourcing has been proposed as the optimal strategy for the client (Hir schheim & Lacity, 2000; Lacity & Willcocks, 2001). Another study has confirmed the client’s bargaining power and responsibility for vendor screening (Snir & Hitt, 1999). The notions of establishing a psychological contract and an incomplete contract theory have been proposec to describe the significant initial collaboration period termed in the present study as ‘project start' (Koh et al.. 1999). Furthermore. the significant activity that goes into achieving and main: taining a successful relationship between a vendor and a client has also been noted in terms of building trust (Sabherwal, 1999). All of these studies have supported the arguments for a more involved and educated client who diagnoses the needs and the situation before making appropriate choices.

## CONCLUSION

This study confirms that ISD is a complex social and organizational process. The ISD profes sional is concerned not only with developing a system but also with a number of process issues that determine how he or she manages activities, relationships, and the ISD professional’s own intervention into the client system. Frameworks, choice of methods, tailoring of components, and use of repositories all represent significant background work in ISD. This paper presents the ISD process as a systems development exercise in which ISD professionals use an approach to intervene in a problematic situation to develop an organization’s information and, if needed, other systems (Figure 2).
