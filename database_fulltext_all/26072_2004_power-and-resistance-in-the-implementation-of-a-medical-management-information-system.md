# Power and resistance in the implementation of a medical management information system

Bill Doolin

Auckland University of Technology, New Zealand, email: bill.doolin@aut.ac.nz

Abstract. This paper presents an analysis of the implementation of a large information system in a New Zealand hospital. The system was intended to monitor and scrutinize clinical activity, and management hoped to influence clinical behaviour through the increased visibility afforded by the system. However, doctors were not the passive subjects of a computerized control system, and some were able to resist the application of the information produced by challenging its validity or using it to argue for more resources. Nevertheless, the interrelatedness of the information system with the organizational forms and practices within which it was used and which it helped shape reinforced the concepts, norms and values associated with the new management and economic discourse prevalent in the hospital, and helped to produce more defined accountabilities for doctors. With time, the role of the information system was reinterpreted, and in the face of continued resistance by doctors, it was relegated to a less significant role. The potential for engendering self-disciplining behaviour in organizational participants through the pervasive, everyday use of the information the system produced likewise diminished.

Keywords: information systems implementation, management control, power, health care

## INTRODUCTION

Early work in the information systems field has shown that while the introduction of information systems in organizations may be articulated in relation to concepts of efficiency and rationality, in practice they function in diverse ways related to the social and political processes that exist in the organization (e.g. Bariff & Galbraith, 1978; Burchell et al., 1980; Kling & Scacchi, 1980; Markus, 1981). In particular, information systems can influence which organizational actions and their consequences become relatively more visible. They become ‘mechanisms around which interests are negotiated, counter claims articulated and political processes explicated (Burchell et al., 1980, p. 17). More recently, attention has focused on the role of information systems in facilitating power and control in various organizational settings (e.g. Attewell, 1987; Zuboff, 1988; Gray, 2001).

Much of this work is underpinned by conventional notions of power, which treat information as a resource to be deployed in the balance of power between organizational actors (cf. Jasperson et al., 2002). Such a notion of power underplays the constitutive effects of information systems and the way in which they mediate and reinforce particular understandings of organizational reality. A different understanding of power, influenced by the work of Michel Foucault, has been proposed as a basis for explaining the role of information systems in facilitating a calculative form of control through computer-based surveillance and monitoring by authors such as Orlikowski (1991), Bloomfield & Coombs (1992) and Ball & Wilson (2000).

This paper presents a case study of the implementation of a computer-based information system intended to monitor the activity and performance of hospital doctors. It uses the Foucauldian perspective on power and information systems mentioned above to provide a localized account of power and control in a New Zealand public health setting. In doing so, the paper builds on previous studies of this nature in three ways. First, it provides a ‘fine-grained’ analysis (cf. Ball & Wilson, 2000) capable of exploring the detailed implications of computer-based surveillance systems as a means of management control. Second, it draws on longitudinal data spanning a 6-year period, providing a valuable opportunity to evaluate the long-term power effects of such information systems. Third, it provides an empirical example of the usefulness of the Foucauldian perspective on power in understanding the deployment and operation of a particular type of management information system.

The structure of the paper is as follows. The first part of the paper reviews the literature on power and information systems, and articulates an understanding of these based on the relational conception of power drawn from Foucault. The method used in conducting the research presented here is then discussed. The main part of the paper applies the theoretical understanding developed earlier to the implementation of a ‘casemix’ information system in a New Zealand public hospital. This information system was intended by hospital management to provide a window on clinical activity, explicitly linking clinical resource usage with its financial implications in an attempt to control the clinical behaviour of hospital doctors. The paper ends with some conclusions about the contribution of this work and possibilities for future research.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS, POWER AND CONTROL

Conventional conceptions of power assume that power exists as a capacity that can be possessed, and exercised over others in a mechanical or causal manner. Power is seen primarily as something that denies, represses or coerces (Lukes, 1974; Clegg, 1989; Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992). Such a zero-sum notion of power implies that shifts in organizational power are the result of corresponding changes in the organizational distribution of resources, such as information, which confer power on their possessors. This understanding of power can be seen in early studies of information systems in organizations (e.g. Pettigrew, 1972; Bariff & Galbraith, 1978; Markus, 1981), and continues to provide the dominant view of power in this field (e.g. Pfeffer, 1994; Gray, 2001; Jasperson et al., 2002).

The weakness of this approach is that it fails to consider that power is relational (Clegg, 1989). That is, power is not possessed but is a capacity for action that resides in social relations. It exists only when it is exercised, when it is put into action. Such a relational conception of power is provided by Foucault (1977; 1980). According to Foucault, power is exercised from within the social body, rather than from above it. In their daily lives, individuals are faced with a field of possible responses and reactions. Power operates by structuring this field of possible actions (Foucault, 1982). It is manifested in the numerous knowledges, practices and technol ogies that are brought to bear on the actions of others (Hindess, 1996).

The Foucauldian conception of power has received some attention in the information sys tems field. For example, Zuboff (1988), in highlighting the ‘informating’ capacity of information technology applied to work processes, acknowledges the potential for new forms of surveil lance through information systems. Zuboff’s work, together with subsequent studies (e.g. Orlikowski, 1991; Sewell & Wilkinson, 1992; Ball & Wilson, 2000), invokes the notion of an infor mational or electronic ‘panopticon’, in which organizational participants are subject to a disci plinary gaze through computerized monitoring and surveillance. Such references reflect a direct appeal to Foucault’s (1977) use of Bentham’s central, elevated watchtower, the Panopticon, as a metaphor for the operation of power in the rise of a disciplinary society.

Foucault (1977) argues that the impossibility of avoiding the supervisory gaze of an all seeing, but unseen, observer in the tower engenders a realization in the occupants of the sur rounding cells that they are always subject to surveillance. Even in the absence of the observer, the apparatus of power still operates – continuous and anonymous. This constitutes a new internalized discipline of norms and behaviour in which the occupant becomes his or her own guardian (Clegg, 1989). It is important to note that Foucault’s use of the Panopticon is as an ideal type, an analogy with which to describe the exercise of power (Ball & Wilson, 2000).

The disciplinary power that Foucault describes operates by enhancing the calculability of individuals. It is constantly exercised by surveillance, observation and comparative measures that reference the norm (Foucault, 1977). Various technologies of evaluation and calculation make visible the activities of individuals in organisations and calculate the extent to which they depart from a norm of performance (Johnson, 1993; Miller, 1994). Examples include the comparative application of performance information, or other forms of surveillance (such as super vision, routinization, rationalization, formalization and mechanization) that seek to increase control of organizational members’ behaviour (Clegg, 1989). Increasingly, information technol ogy mediates this process.

Calculative practices such as those facilitated by information systems render social phenomena visible in a particular way, with two potential effects. First, representational, inscriptiona and computational techniques associated with information systems render individuals calculable, and thus knowable and governable. Some activities are given existence and attention, while others remain unrecognized, enabling managerial knowledge to make stronger truth claims (Boland & Schultze, 1996) and engendering compliance in those subject to such scrutiny:

Calculative technologies make it possible to render visible both the near and the distant activities of individuals, to calculate the extent to which they depart from a norm of performance, and to accumulate such calculations in computers and files and compare them. (Miller, 1994, p. 246)

Second, the development of information systems to monitor and scrutinize particular organizational activities facilitates control by making individuals more calculating with respect to their own actions. Individuals learn to survey themselves and discipline themselves (Clegg, 1989). Their actions are influenced through a mechanism of self-monitoring, rather than direct control and supervision. Power operates here through the normalization of individuals’ judgements about what is legitimate and non-legitimate behaviour, rather than as the object of some mechanistically derived power (Bloomfield et al., 1994):

Why power often does generate consent is because it draws upon knowledge that is plausible or legitimate and this helps to transform individuals, whether exercising or being exercised by power, into subjects who secure meaning and identity through the practices it invokes. (Knights & Murray, 1994, p. 36)

In the fabrication of information systems, the constitutive concepts of the dominant discourses and knowledges instituted in organizational practices are defined and organizationa phenomena reconciled with them. The embedding of these constitutive concepts in organizational information systems helps the rules, values and assumptions they represent become reified and taken for granted. Thus, information systems are implicated in mobilizing particular representations of organizational reality. They underpin the framework of meaning within which organizational participants regulate their own behaviour (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992; Bloomfield et al., 1994, 1997).

However, the use of information systems to monitor and control organizational activity does not constitute organizational participants as passive victims of computerized surveillance. Those over whom power is exercised are recognized and maintained as people who act and could do otherwise (Foucault, 1982; Knights & Morgan, 1991). There is a general tendency among those subject to power and control to resist by means of challenging or diverting the systems and rules imposed on them (Clegg, 1989; Covaleski et al., 1993). Disciplinary technologies such as comparative surveillance information systems are not exclusively constraining. Indeed, such systems are ‘double-edged’, in that they also empower by providing a legitimate space for action (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992).

This theoretical understanding of power and information systems was used to inform a case study of the implementation of a casemix information system in a New Zealand hospital. The following section outlines the method used in conducting the case study.

## METHOD

The case study presented in this paper is based on a series of interviews conducted by the author in a large New Zealand hospital in 1996 and 2001. The hospital is a regional health provider, which provides a comprehensive range of health services for a geographically dispersed population. As a site of research, the hospital is a complex organization with strong internal cultures. Organizational activity is dependent on a negotiated order between diverse occupational and professional groups (Reed & Anthony, 1993). The research investigated the way in which information systems and management structures were mutually implicated in the process of organizational change in a hospital subject to extensive public sector health reform.

The organizational terrain surrounding information systems implementation is intimately bound up in power relations, and any specific outcome is the result of a contested and negotiated process. To confront issues of power in technological and organizational change, information systems research needs to open up the ‘black box’ of information technology (Knights & Murray, 1994). Accordingly, the research presented here is informed by a perspective of critical interpretivism (Doolin, 1998). It reflects the problematic and socially constructed nature of organizations by demonstrating how information systems are implicated in the definition of par ticular representations of organizational reality (Knights, 1995).

In 1996, the hospital had just been reorganized from a divisional and general management structure into clinical units based on clinical specialties and headed by senior doctors. Interviews with clinical directors, operations managers, medical consultants and charge nurses were conducted in a number of the new clinical units. Corporate managers, support managers and information systems personnel were also interviewed. The emphasis of each interview varied according to the interviewee’s function and his or her interests. The major topics covered in relation to the research presented here were the operation of clinical units after the recen organizational restructuring, the ongoing development of major information systems for contracting and patient management, and the use of computerized information by clinical unit staff. A wide range of additional topics also arose. In 46 interviews, 37 people were interviewed for about an hour on the average (Table 1). Interviews were taped with permission, and transcripts were prepared. In 2001, the author revisited the hospital and conducted a further six interviews with a range of hospital personnel in order to derive an understanding of how the casemix information system studied had been used in the intervening 5 years (Table 1). Again, interviews were taped with permission, and transcripts were prepared.

The data in the interview transcripts were thematically analysed. Simple coding procedures were used to conceptualize the data. This involved developing initial categories, grouping data using a ‘cut and paste’ technique, identifying patterns and then making comparisons to generate commonalities of shared properties and dimensions (Agar, 1980; Strauss, 1987). Interview transcripts were read, and re-read, to identify statements that reflected assumptions, knowledge or expectations of information technology and organizational change, and the impli cations for work practices within the hospital.

Informed by the theoretical understanding of power and information systems discussed earlier, a number of themes emerged from the qualitative data analysis. These were based on notions of power and resistance, surveillance and the rationalization of clinical activity, man agement control and professional autonomy, and the mutuality of organization and information technology. These themes form the basis of the case study analysis that follows. Where appropriate, quotes from interviewees are used to illustrate the discussion and to provide the reader with a sense of empirical plausibility.

## THE CASEMIX INFORMATION SYSTEM AS A CALCULATIVE TECHNOLOGY

In the New Zealand public health context in the late 1980s, the deployment of information systems was ostensibly concerned with improving operational efficiency and the allocation of limited economic resources in hospitals. The intention was to generate and disseminate information on medical activity and its cost among clinical staff and management. The problem of health care was translated into one of a lack of the appropriate information required to contro costs and to enable more efficient use of existing resources. This translation was accompanied by an implicit delegation to information technology that involved keeping track of resource usage (cf. Bloomfield, 1995). It was in this environment that the hospital studied was selected as one of four pilot sites in a government-instigated initiative in the mid to late 1980s to develop a computerized resource management system capable of linking costs to clinical activities for hospital cost accounting and product line management.

The resulting casemix information system links detailed information on hospital patient treatment and clinical activity with associated costs, as a basis for revealing the relative efficiency of clinical resource usage. Data on resources consumed during patient diagnosis and treat ment are collected from the hospital’s operational areas and transferred into the casemix information system where they are linked with the appropriate patient episode. Patient episodes are then grouped into appropriate categories, and a range of reports generated. The information generated can be used to build a budget for the hospital. It is possible to track individual patient treatments throughout the hospital on a cost and activity basis. Information can be produced at different levels of aggregation, including patient group, medical specialty or even individua doctor.

In 1993, reform of the New Zealand health sector saw the corporatization of public hospitals and the creation of Regional Health Authorities to act as purchasers of health care services. The reforms were intended to force hospitals to take a more commercial approach to health care provision, and to link funding with production in such a way as to make hospitals more accountable. The new environment placed added emphasis on the casemix information system at the hospital studied. Contracting with a Regional Health Authority required the setting of a price for treatment, which in turn required information on costs and volumes. The continued need for information to control costs was now complemented by a demand for information on patient throughputs as part of the contracting arrangements:

It was becoming clear that contracting between purchasers and providers had to be a lot more sophisticated than it was, and was a lot more difficult and complex than it was initially imagined. At the same time people elsewhere were trying to think ‘How do you control costs in the organisation and how do you actually manage revenues?’ (Interview with a corporate manager, May 1996)

Considerable effort was put into re-costing hospital activities, and the casemix system was used to produce detailed information on particular treatment costs and what those costs represented. Financial reports were produced, which tracked patient utilization of clinical resources as the patient progressed through the hospital. Profit figures were reported for var: ious responsibility centres within the hospital and a contribution report was introduced by senior management as a standard way of measuring financial and contractual performance within the organization. The increased visibility offered by the contribution reports enabled management to focus attention on profit or loss-making areas.

One perspective on the implementation and use of the casemix information system in the hospital studied is to view the system as implicated in the attempted normalization of medical practice through the increased surveillance of doctors and clinical activity. Scrutinizing clinica procedures and explicitly linking patient treatment decisions to standard costs make clinica activity visible and susceptible to intervention by management, who can then influence clinica decisions. A casemix information system provides a view on clinical practice that highlights variances between the performance of individual doctors or clinical specialities. From this per spective, a casemix information system is an attempt to influence doctors’ behaviour towards ‘normal’ work practices, through the comparative application of performance information (Chua & Degeling, 1993):

In the name of ensuring efficiency and financial viability, hospital administrators can, and have become, empowered to manage a hospital’s case-mix, to demand greater cost consciousness from clinicians, to experiment with new matrix structures in hospi tals and to implant extensive computerized information systems. (Chua & Degeling, 1993, p. 304)

The active participation of clinical staff within the hospital studied was essential for the generation and collection of accurate and valid data that could be used in the casemix information system. To use Latour’s (1987) terminology, they had to be enrolled in the project. However, hospital staff tended to view the casemix information system with cynicism or even resistance. Computerization in the New Zealand health system has a poor track record, and previous large-scale and expensive centralized health information systems projects had produced little tangible clinical benefit. After years of development and data collection, and after constant requests for input data without witnessing any tangible or usable output, clinical staff became disillusioned with the casemix information system, perceiving it as just another management tool rather than a system with direct clinical benefit.

There was also little incentive for clinical staff to cooperate in the data collection. Time spent on data collection was seen as a distraction from the primary clinical focus of patient care. Compliance with the casemix information system was considered a duplication of established patient management procedures and an imposition on their professional expertise and practice. The failure to obtain clinical buy-in to the casemix information system was succinctly summarized by a senior manager:

So I think, pushed down their throats, seen as a beanie’s [accountant’s] tool, really to do with something they’re not particularly interested in, gives very little benefit at the [clinical] interface and is a duplication of everything they already do. and then used as something to batter them round the ears with, control, an audit. (Interview with a corporate manager, March 1996)

In 1996, hospital management began generating clinical casemix information as well as information on costs, volumes and revenues to encourage clinical interest in the casemix system. Clinical casemix information included measures of length of stay, day surgery vs. inpatient surgery, operating theatre time management and off-hours laboratory usage. Management’s intention in producing and disseminating the clinical casemix information was to interest doctors in monitoring for themselves the resource consequences of their treatment decisions. By costing to a clinical level and making that information available, it was hoped that doctors would modify their clinical behaviour towards efficient practice:

You suddenly realise what’s left is to change clinical practice. In other words, if you’re to reduce your costs and make a profit instead of a loss from how you’re paid for every inpatient admission, you’ve got to change the way the doctors, what tests they’re doing, how long they keep the person in hospital for, because then they flow on to your nursing costs and everything else. (Interview with a clinical director, March 1996)

It was an acknowledgement by management of their inability to manage doctors directly. Instead, the casemix system would be used to produce information that would enable doctors to see their performance contrasted with others (Edwards, 1992):

There are difficulties overall with actually managing doctors. I believe the only way of managing doctors is to get information through information systems that provide them with the sort of reports in which peer pressure will bring some conformance to expenditure. (Interview with a corporate manager, March 1996)

At the hospital, attempts were made to use casemix information to encourage peer review among doctors. Clinical casemix reports were presented to doctors for internal benchmark ing within units that management felt were cohesive in their operation. One senior manage introduced the use of clinical casemix information for developing standard treatment protocols to the senior doctors of a clinical unit. Clinical audit was another common interest that managers could potentially exploit in order to introduce the clinical casemix information to doctors.

The benefits of the clinical casemix information were emphasized in a series of demonstrations to doctors, in an attempt to enrol them as users of the casemix system (cf. Wilson & Howcroft, 2000). Recourse was made to a quality discourse in an attempt to construct the interests of doctors as congruent with those of management. This involved translating the notion of patient care in terms of effectiveness and efficiency:

Effectiveness is basically the target that we’re using for the clinician population because that is still their number one. You know, they want to provide quality patient care. But if you get into true effectiveness, meaning that your resources are being used appropriately . . . then you’re going to come out with efficiency . . . You don’t bring dollars up to them because that’s a conflict of interest, big time . . . That’s where best practice, clinical review, those sort of aspects will be of interest. and you’re not hitting them over the head with costs that they see as a conflict of interest, and it’s going to turn them off and make them very defensive. All that is, is politics. (Interview with an information systems manager, August 1996)

The push for generating and distributing clinical casemix information was accompanied by the 1996 organizational restructuring around clinical units, a new organizational form based on clinical specialities and headed by a senior doctor acting as a ‘clinician manager’. Information from the casemix system offered a way to divide the hospital into visible and manageable parts built around identifiable revenue streams related to clinical specialities. The casemix information system was also needed to coordinate the movement of patients between the new clinical units, and to match the associated resource utilization with the relevant purchaser–provider contracts. Importantly, the recruitment of senior doctors (who it was assumed would respond to the casemix information) into managerial roles theoretically meant that control and review would be performed by the same people (cf. Packwood et al., 1991; Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992). Doctors, or at least a ‘doctor as manager’ hybrid, would influence clinical activity, rathe than direct control by senior management:

Hospitals have been faced with the task of simultaneously creating management information that could be acted on by doctors, and manoeuvring those doctors into defined positions where they accept such responsibility. (Bloomfield et al., 1992, p. 199)

## CIRCUMVENTING THE CALCULATING GAZE

The control strategy adopted at the hospital studied involved making clinical activity more visible and calculable through increased volumes of control information from the casemix system, together with the enrolment of senior doctors as clinician managers capable of changing clinical practice from within the medical profession. The success of this control strategy depended on the acceptance of the casemix information and the new clinician manager roles by the participants within the hospital. Regardless of senior management’s intentions in implementing the casemix information system, the outcome was subject to processes of negotiation by the users of the system and those at whom it was directed. As noted earlier, there is a general tendency among those subject to power and control to resist by challenging the systems and rules imposed on them. At the hospital studied, surveillance through the casemix information system was open to the circumvention of doctors as they attempted to ‘escape the implications of the gaze of normalizing judgement’ (Chua & Degeling, 1993, p. 309).

The surveillance process facilitated through the casemix information system increased the visibility of the resources used for patient care. The specific detail with which clinical practice could be tracked and recorded led to a degree of defensiveness and rejection of the information by doctors:

When you first start producing this information, they become very defensive because this is the first time they’ve ever been tracked. The first time that their practice has ever been put on paper . . . The information can be rejected for the sole fact that they don’t want to be obligated to it, and they don’t want to, you know, be seen with it. (Interview with an information systems manager, August 1996)

[Doctors] don’t like this monitoring business. They don’t mind doing it themselves in their own peer group, but they don’t like managers and analysts saying ‘Hey, why are you doing this, why are you doing that?’ . . . I guess it’s just their culture, their professional culture – tha they’re clinicians and managers shouldn’t be telling them how to treat their patients. (Interview with a corporate manager, December 2001)

From the doctors’ perspective, concern over the casemix information arose from the uncertainty over the processing and use of the casemix information:

There is a lot of information that is produced, that we’re not quite sure, you know, what it means for us . . . Some of my older colleagues felt it was threatening, that you were going to be looking at some of their cases . . . The fear is that it might be used by somebody against them somewhere down the track . . . We’re not quite sure how that information will be processed. (Interview with a clinical director, September 1996)

There was also reluctance on the part of many doctors to use a tool provided by manage ment. Many doctors felt that the information would be used to justify management decisions on financial grounds, ignoring clinical issues. Established clinical values relating to patient care meant that the potential for management decisions based on casemix information would be perceived as an intrusion on the professional autonomy and clinical freedom of doctors. According to one senior doctor, the desire to protect the medical domain was very strong (Interview with a clinical director, December 2001). As an information systems manage summarized:

It’s a perception that [the casemix system] has been a tool provided by management and therefore possibly monitored by management . . . There’s still this sort of dichotomy in management terms between the doctors who are the providers of the service and managers who are the monitors of the service. I think the doctors don’t really want to be managed by somebody who isn’t a doctor. and I don’t think that’s ever going to change. (Interview with an information systems manager, December 2001)

The defensiveness and uncertainty experienced by doctors led to direct and indirect challenges on the casemix information involved. This involved producing alternative explanations for the variations between individual doctors, or querying the accuracy of the casemix information and the validity of the procedures used to construct it.

Doctors commonly challenged comparisons between individual doctors by pointing to other factors producing outlier patterns of treatment (cf. Chua & Degeling, 1993). Casemix information reports comparing the length of stay or average preoperation stay for individual doctors cases within a particular speciality often used aggregated information. In order to account for the differences in aggregate comparative figures, doctors would emphasize the variations in subspecializations between doctors. One senior doctor pointed out the difficulty in using clinical casemix information to compare individual doctors with different subspecializations at a local level, particularly given the size of New Zealand population centres and the number of specialists involved. Other doctors argued that the diagnostic categories used in the casemix system were not sufficiently detailed for clinical management purposes.

Differences between clinical specialties also had implications for the ability of management to impose control through the casemix system. In specialties where types of treatment and patient episodes were well defined, it was likely that control would be more easily imposed. For instance, much of the clinical work in the surgical specialties was standardized, and therefore more readily observable and made visible through the casemix information system. Defining variances in clinical practices became more difficult in some of the medical specialties where physicians often worked in teams and patients moved through several specialties during thei treatment:

They have a different type of patient and their turnaround is very different. It’s much harder to track or to get useful information out of a medical patient as opposed to a surgery . . . You don’t have such a nice, very structured, cut and dried process that a patient goes through. (Interview with an information systems manager, August 1996)

Doctors at the hospital studied questioned the accuracy of the casemix information or the validity of the procedures used to construct it. Many doctors had a poor view of the information generated by the casemix system because of the assumptions, allocations and averaging built into its cost management models. This is consistent with the idea that the scientific and positivistic training of doctors makes it difficult for them to adapt to the more open-ended approaches utilized by managers in making decisions on less than perfect, but available, information:

A clinician sees something that’s been averaged, and says ‘Well, it’s not good information, why should I believe it’. So, if you can give them information they have confidence in, yes, they will move their medical practice. But if there’s any flaw, forget it. You might as well no have presented it in the first place. (Interview with an operations manager, March 1996)

The thing that I see as the major weakness is the weakness of the information. That we are being asked to make decisions often based on poorly collected, largely meaningless information. And when your background is the critical review of information gathered in clinica medicine . . . (Interview with a medical consultant, September 1996)

At times, resistance was more disingenuous. One operations manager commented on the difficulty in interesting doctors in the casemix information. The doctors in her unit actually claimed to be ‘confused’ by casemix information in order to divert her attempts to present the information:

They have no interest in either the financial side of casemix or anything else. ‘They’re here to work.’ ‘They’re here to operate.’ ‘They’re here to look after the patients.’ ‘They find the data suspect.’ I’ve tried to talk to them . . . but ‘don’t confuse them with data of any kind’. They jus get ‘confused’ if you start to feed anything [casemix information] to them. (Interview with an operations manager, September 1996)

In effect, many doctors at the hospital were resisting their constitution as users and subjects of casemix information (Bloomfield & Vurdubakis, 1997; Bloomfield et al., 1997):

I choose to ignore it most of the time . . . All casemix seems to have been so far, to me, is a way for the Regional Health Authority to describe what they’re going to buy, and I guess I’m not prepared to have the case mix dictated in that fashion. If patients need treatment they need treatment . . . I’m not prepared to have my practice organized in that fashion. (Interview with a medical consultant, September 1996)

## CALCULATING CLINICIANS?

Casemix information was gradually becoming an accepted framework for discussion between doctors and managers within the hospital studied. It tended to structure the debate over resource utilization in such a way that clinical efficiency had to be demonstrated in terms of the concepts and definitions associated with casemix management. This was not necessarily onesided. Comparative surveillance systems such as the casemix information system studied are not exclusively constraining. Rather, they open up a new and legitimate discursive space fo action within which doctors may appropriate and manipulate the information and rhetoric of casemix systems, diverting disciplinary practices to their own ends or using the reality constructed and made visible by the casemix information system to demand more resources (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992; Covaleski et al., 1993).

Several senior doctors at the hospital studied were exploring the possibilities presented by the casemix information system in their new roles as clinician managers. The clinician manager of one of the medical specialties related:

I got a subset of [casemix] information that I could put on the PC, and then use in an ordinary, but accessible way with a relational database. That was just tremendously important to me to understand what was going on and to try to see if I could reorganise this medical unit to respond. (Interview with a clinical director, March 1996)

Even sceptical doctors could recognize the usefulness of the casemix information system in arguing for more resources:

I won’t say it’s not useful, because [the clinician manager] is able to show that we’re grossly under-funded. That’s a very useful thing to be able to show . . . We are showing that there are more of certain things being done each year and that we need more money to cope with that. That’s useful. I’ll accept that. (Interview with a medical consultant, September 1996)

From a Foucauldian perspective, one of the more subtle power effects of organizational information systems is the way in which they reinforce a particular understanding of reality through the mobilization of constitutive concepts embedded in them. Organizational participants who reproduce practices associated with these information systems in their daily activities may come to internalize these constitutive norms and values, and draw upon them in their choices of everyday behaviour and actions. In this way, the information system mediates various organizational and societal discourses, helping constitute the truth of what is normal or legitimate in social and organizational relations. As Bloomfield (1995) comments:

Staff . . . become increasingly locked into a whole new ensemble of routinized everyday practices whose regularity is mediated and reinforced through the actions delegated to infor mation systems. (p. 511)

In the deployment of comparative information systems, the operation of disciplinary power would suggest the transformation of individuals into calculating and normalized subjects, whose activities become self-regulated through their participation in the various practices associated with the information system and its use in the organizational context. As noted earlier, this is not the overt extension of control from some centre of power, but the conduct of conduct – influencing the actions of individuals by working on their autonomous subjectivity (Johnson, 1993; Hindess, 1996).

With increasing acceptance and use in the hospital, the casemix information system had the potential to play an important role in mobilizing the concepts and norms associated with the new economic and management perspectives on health care and accountability for clinica resources. It offered an apparently concrete (although partial) representation of organizationa reality, which helped give meaning to the various transactions and organizational practices in which it was utilized. The casemix system became implicated in the daily work of some organizational participants, providing a technical vocabulary to mediate the meanings given to events and relationships such as those between clinical units or with the Regional Health Authority (cf. Orlikowski, 1991). This was particularly so for the clinician managers:

Casemix has become a part of the way we work. Just a day-to-day thing we’re utilising . . . mean, one’s using it all the time. Whenever we’re doing presentations or developing stuff for business plans, volumes for buying capital items . . . We use it for contract stuff. I mean it’s just there; it’s just being used. (Interview with a clinical director, September 1996)

The information provided by the casemix information system mobilized new categories for construing clinical activity in the hospital (cf. Bloomfield, 1991). Doctors were encouraged to view their clinical activity in terms of the discourse of casemix management so that a sense of resource efficiency would be engendered, as the consequences of their patient treatment decisions were made visible. By accepting the role and legitimacy of the casemix system and reproducing the practices based around it, the potential for doctors to regulate their own behaviour so as to accommodate the new understanding of organizational reality and the criteria for decision making provided through these discursive practices existed at the hospital studied.

However, it was difficult to discern any evidence that this self-disciplining phenomenon was occurring among most doctors in the hospital studied. If a discourse is not pervasive, and its associated practices are not routinely performed, then disciplinary power is not exercised. During the 6-year period covered by this study, the casemix information system, and the various inscriptions it produced, did not become widely used by doctors in their every day work and talk.

The significant medical resistance to the casemix system described earlier obviously contributed to this situation. The casemix system was largely dominated by financial and costing perspectives and had produced little information of perceived clinical relevance or benefit. With a few exceptions, doctors at the hospital had a poor opinion of the validity of the casemix information and expressed little interest in using it to inform their practice. There was a perception that the diagnostic coding performed in order to generate the casemix information was increasingly influenced by a financial need to maximize cost recovery rather than by clinical correctness. Crucially, the casemix system and the information it generated was typically perceived as a threat to the autonomy of these medical professionals.

Even the inclusion of clinical information that might be expected to interest doctors in using the casemix system was insufficient to encourage the widespread adoption of information produced. Six years after its introduction, clinical casemix information was used only sporadically by individual doctors, such as junior doctors wanting information for educational presentations. Some doctors suggested that the failure to implement a particular module of the casemix system that acted as an access tool to this information was to blame. In fact, this module had been implemented but its existence was apparently not widely known among doctors. This seems to have been the result of under-resourcing in this area and the lack of a champion to promote the system within the hospital:

The information is there. I think it’s something of a mindset that they [doctors] don’t want to use it just at the moment. I think it will. I like to think that people will start using information and change the way they practise, but they haven’t thought about it at the moment. (Interview with an information systems manager, December 2001)

A related factor explaining the lack of use of casemix information in clinical practice was the relatively undeveloped nature of systematic clinical audit within the hospital studied. Some managers considered that the casemix system was well suited to clinical audit through its provision of comparative information on patient treatments. However, it became clear that doctors at the hospital would not accept a clinical audit solution provided by management. By 2001, a significant budget had been allocated for the development of a clinical audit system – one under the direct control of senior doctors:

It’s a logical step and it’s done in a number of hospitals and institutions. What this audit will be set up to do is to improve the standard of care by monitoring what people do and comparing that to the standards that they’ve preset . . . They [doctors] see it as being something that allows them to set up systems for capturing data. To me. it would potentially duplicate what we already have . . . but people prefer to have their own system that they can control. (Interview with an information systems manager, December 2001)

However, medical resistance provides only a partial explanation for the failure of the casemix information system to play a more powerful role in the hospital. Ciborra & Lanzara (1994) suggest that implementing a new information system often requires ‘changing and restructuring the institutional bonds and background conditions upon which . . . people establish and enact their practical dealings and relations’ (p. 79). They refer to this dimension of information systems design as the ‘formative context’. The formative context provides the taken for granted institutional arrangements and cognitive frames that influence the conduct of routines associated with an information system. In the hospital studied, the formative context within which the casemix information system and its associated practices were intended to operate remained unchanged. As one doctor put it, 'the anticipated management style did not occur – the culture of using information to manage was not there’ (Interview with a medical consultant, December 2001):

I think the organisational culture was such that they didn’t feel obliged to use it in the first place. That’s the one thing. I think the second thing is they didn’t really know how to use it. You see, it didn’t replace their existing reporting. So this was sort of like forced on them . . . and the existing things that they were held accountable to, weren’t changed. Until you actually make people accountable for this data they’re going to ignore it anyway. (Interview with an information systems manager, September 1996)

Two managers reinforced this view by reference to the style of management at another large hospital that they were familiar with:

I think it [casemix information] is probably used in [the other hospital]. They have a dynamic set of managers who are keen on making sure things are managed. I don’t think we have the same dynamic approach here. (Interview with an information systems manager, December 2001)

Yeah, apparently they [the other hospital] are a really good site for casemix and how they’ve developed it . . . It might be just the management culture out there, from what I hear. They’ve put a real focus onto that information getting out and resourced it a bit better than we have here . . . A push from management. (Interview with a corporate manager, December 2001)

This lack of significant change in the formative context was reinforced by the eventua removal of much of the organizational rationale for the casemix information system. In 1999, the hospital reorganized yet again. Clinical units lost much of their business autonomy and were reconstituted into larger structures. The use of contribution reports generated from the casemix information system was never followed though, as management fell back on more familiar budgetary and financial management approaches based on the General Ledger (Interview with a corporate manager, December 2001). The role of clinician managers largely disappeared as the reorganized hospital separated clinical leadership from management, running clinical specialties through clinical directors supported by a range of business and operations managers. The role of the casemix information system became focused primarily on contrac management, a tool for measuring performance against contracts with the funding authority rather than a tool for the comparative evaluation and control of clinical performance (cf. Bloomfield et al., 1997). This shift in meaning, together with the discontinuation of the organizationa forms with which the casemix system had become interrelated, relegated the casemix system to a relatively minor role. The widespread use of casemix information throughout the hospita never eventuated.

## CONCLUSION

Introducing a Foucauldian conception of power to the analysis of the implementation and use of information systems is a theoretically powerful device. It highlights the role that certain information systems play in mobilizing particular organizational realities through the discursive concepts, norms and values embedded within them. The realities they represent help to provide meaning to organizational participants’ actions and hence guide their decisions. The case study presented here is a valuable opportunity to observe the power effects of such comparative surveillance systems in action over an extended period of time.

Calculative practices such as those facilitated by the casemix information system studied render social phenomena visible in a particular way. Some activities are given attention and existence, while others are unrecognized. The detailed information provided by the casemix system made visible the financial implications of clinical decisions. Using this information, managers could make stronger truth claims in their attempts to contain clinical resource usage. The casemix information system was deeply implicated in the operation of clinical specialities as semi-autonomous business units and in the introduction of a standard contribution report for each unit. These arrangements encouraged an understanding of organizational reality grounded in economic notions of value and commodity. The visibility given to concepts incor porated in the casemix information, such as profit or loss, average length of stay or performance against contract volume, introduced new practices of accounting for clinical performance and more defined accountabilities for doctors

In this sense, the casemix information system can be viewed as part of an attempt to constitute doctors as subiects of a management discourse. For example. doctors were defined as capable of reviewing and managing their own clinical practice using casemix information. Cost and activity information mobilized by the casemix system was made available to doctors, in the hope that peer pressure would operate in standardizing clinical activity towards cost-efficient practices. If this occurred, the casemix information would have helped to engender a degree of self-discipline in doctors’ clinical behaviour. Management’s view was that objective information on resource usage would lead to rational decision making by doctors and to a more efficient and responsible medical practice as less expensive treatment protocols were pursued.

However, as this case study confirms, the notion of doctors as the passive subjects of a com puterized comparative surveillance system is too simplistic (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992). Organizational participants are actors who could do otherwise, and the possibility of resistance is integral to the Foucauldian conception of power used in this paper. Part of the ambiguity sur rounding the implementation of the casemix information system at the hospital studied lay in the ability of doctors to resist its application in various ways. This included challenging the validity of the construction of the casemix information, and pointing to other factors that potentially explained clinical outliers or variances between individual doctors’ practices. The ‘doubleedged’ nature of the power exercised through the casemix system meant that some doctors were able to divert the casemix information towards their own ends, principally in arguing for more resources. The implication is that information systems associated with attempts to increase management control of organizational participants are also capable of empowering those over whom control is attempted, by making available a legitimate arena for action and discussion with the organization.

Continued resistance to casemix management (including limited uptake of clinical casemix information) by most doctors, and a reluctance of managers to challenge the long tradition of medical privilege and autonomy (cf. Chua & Degeling, 1993), meant that senior management did not enforce the use of casemix information across the organization. As noted earlier, con tribution reporting from the casemix system was not fully implemented and casemix information was relegated to a contract management role. External pressures on the hospital’s financial performance, and a change in leadership triggered yet another reorganization of the hospital. The casemix system lost much of its significance, as clinical units and the idea of doc tors as managers disappeared.

This meant that the casemix information lost its potential to become the dominant view of organizational reality within the hospital. Without its pervasive use to structure. organize anc manage daily activities in the hospital, the power effects potentially exercised through it did not eventuate. Bloomfield et al. (1997) describe a similar shift in the focus of casemix information systems away from resource management towards the monitoring of contracts in the UK National Health Service. There also, a new arrangement became stabilised in which doctors were involved in management only to the extent that they negotiated for contracts, but were no longer clinician managers concerned with standardising clinical practice.

The development of information systems and the provision of computerized information are not in themselves a sufficient solution to whatever management problem has been articulated. Some form of change in the formative context (Ciborra & Lanzara, 1994) of the organization is required to complement systems development in order that some use or sense is made of the information provided.

This study is part of a growing body of interpretive and critical research on information systems implementation and use within organizations. It demonstrates the usefulness of the theoretical framework on information systems and power discussed in the paper for making sense of the organizational changes that accompany the implementation of large information systems. The situation of the study in specific historical and spatial conditions of possibility suggests the need for longitudinal research, as health care organizations and participants continue to be subjected to structural, processual and value changes. Similarly, studies of similar information systems in other organizational contexts would generate further theoretica insights.
