# The DySIIM model for managing IS integration in mergers and acquisitions

Stefan Henningsson\* & Sven Carlsson<sup>†</sup>

\*Department of IT management, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, 2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark, email: sh.caict@cbs.dk, and <sup>†</sup>Informatics and Institute of Economic Research, School of Economics and Management, Lund University, Ole Römers väg 6, 223 63 Lund, Sweden, email: sven.carlsson@ics.lu.se

Abstract. Corporate mergers and acquisitions (M&As) are prominent means for corporate strategy. In many M&As information systems (IS), integration is critical for achieving the intended goals. By integrating previous research on M&A and IS integration, we develop a six-dimensional theoretical framework for IS integration in corporate M&As. The framework is used to describe and explain IS integration management in four M&As. Based on the four cases, the theoretical framework and the existing literature, an initial model (Dynamic System of IS Integration in M&A, DySIIM) describing and explaining how IS integration relates to the M&A process is developed. Through the combination of the DySIIM’s dimensions, the dynamics of different parts of the M&A process are captured. The model can be used in further research to build cumulative knowledge about how to manage IS integration in M&A. The model can also be used by companies in their M&A since through the combination of dimensions, it shows the IS integration issues, deci sions and actions that a company has to address in the process.

Keywords: DySIIM, IS integration, IS management, IT management, mergers and acquisitions, IT governance

## 1. CORPORATE MERGERS AND ACQUISITIONS (M&AS) AND THE ISSUE OF IS INTEGRATION

When in 1996 the Swedish industry group Trelleborg AB acquired the French hose manufacturer Kléber, it took more than 10 years until the two units’ information systems (IS) were integrated. Until the IS were integrated, the two organisations could not function as one common unit and consequently, could not fully leverage the synergistic effects that had motivated the acquisition in 1996. In 2006, the group acquired another French hose manufacturer, Dynaflex, and the necessary IS integration was completed 3 months after the acquisition deal was struck. In a world where commercial organisations are expected to deliver positive results in every quarterly report, the difference between 3 months and 10 years is monumental. In addition, the time difference represents a significant difference in resources used to make the organisations come together.

History suggests that of the about 40 000 corporate M&As that were carried out in 2010, roughly speaking, two-thirds will be financial failures (Bekier et al., 2001; KPMG, 2001). IS integration is cited as the third most important reason for M&A failure, and generally, approximately 45% of the expected benefits from M&As are directly dependent on the IS being integrated (Rodgers, 2005). In a survey by Accenture, only about 40% of 400 interviewed enterprises reported that their last M&A related IS integration had been successful (Accenture, 2006). Whatever ‘successful’ means in this context, it is a strong statement indicating that something is not working the way it should be. Sarrazin & West (2011) said that many mergers do not live up to expectations because they stumble on IS integration. They also said that often, more than half the synergies available in a merger are strongly related to IS; for example, in the financial services industry approximately 60% of potential synergies are related to IS. Since most enterprises are highly dependent on their IS for carrying out business activities, they can not function in an integrated manner before the IS are appropriately integrated (McKiernan & Merali, 1995; Giacomazzi et al., 1997; Robbins & Stylianou, 1999; Evgeniou, 2002; Wijnhoven et al., 2006; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007).

Despite the documented importance and relevance, IS integration in M&A is still sparsely addressed in the existing literature (Wijnhoven et al., 2006). The existing literature recognises the importance of information technology (IT) integration during mergers (Buck-Lew et al., 1992; McKiernan & Merali, 1995); it presents some factors that seem to affect IT integration success (Stylianou et al., 1996; Giacomazzi et al., 1997; Robbins & Stylianou, 1999), suggests methods for planning of IS integration (Alaranta & Henningsson, 2008) and addresses the strategic alignment between IS and M&A strategy (Wijnhoven et al., 2006; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007). This paper builds on and complements the existing research with a comprehensive view of the managerial considerations in the whole M&A process. Such a view is needed to capture the dynamics between different parts of the IS integration process and to explain how differen aspects of the integration mutually affect each other (Alaranta & Henningsson, 2008).

The research presented in this paper was carried out in a learning partnership with the Swedish industry group Trelleborg AB. A learning partnership is a form of engaged scholarship, in which one or several non-academic organisations are engaged in different ways during the various phases of the research process. Engaged scholarship is ‘a participative form of research for obtaining the different perspectives of key stakeholders (researchers, users, clients, sponsors and practitioners) in studying complex problems’ (Van de Ven, 2007, p. 9). Our learning partnership was characterised by consensus and joint ownership. Consequently, which ‘complex problem’ to study was done by diligently trying to find a project that satisfies the needs of both organisations. In consensus between the senior researchers of the research organisation and senior management of the problem-owning organisation, it was concluded that a broad approach to management of IS integration in M&As addressed both a practica problem of high relevance to Trelleborg and a theoretical gap in the existing literature.

The purpose of this paper is two-fold. First, based on the literature review, it develops a framework that can be used to describe and explain IS integration issues and decisions that management has to consider in its M&As; second, it uses the framework to describe and explain the IS integration management in four corporate M&As carried out by Trelleborg AB. This can be considered as a usefulness test of the framework. By using the framework, comparative studies can be made and cumulative knowledge can be developed. As the framework has a managerial perspective, it can also be used by companies since it shows through the combination of the framework’s dimensions the managerial IS integration issues that companies have to address in their M&As.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Having given the background for focusing the management of IS integration in M&As, we next present a preliminary theoretical framework fo studying management of IS integration in M&A. This is followed by a presentation of a research approach that entails a description of learning partnership and engaged scholarship as well as a presentation of the structured case study method used in the research. The four cases of IS integration management in M&A are then presented. Based on the empirical data, the theoretical framework and the existing literature, an initial model describing and explaining how IS integration relates to the M&A process is developed. The last section discusses the potentia applications of the model and provides suggestions for future research in order to establish the model as a comprehensive and reliable tool for addressing managerial issues and questions of IS integration in M&As.

## 2. A FRAMEWORK FOR IS INTEGRATION AND M&A

With the argument that the distinction between ‘mergers’ and ‘acquisitions’ is only juridical, prior literature generally uses the combined term ‘merger and acquisition’ (M&A) to refer to organisational combinations (Mehta & Hirschheim, 2004; Wijnhoven et al., 2006). What looks similar to an acquisition may, in a legal sense, be a merger and vice versa; what looks similar to a neutral merger might, in a legal sense, be an acquisition (Giacomazzi et al., 1997). In order to extend this cumulative tradition, we use M&A to denote the IS integration context.

This paper has no ambition to give a complete account for all prior research on the topic of M&A but will rather introduce the existing literature that presents conclusions important to approach management of IS integration in M&A. Together with prior research on IS integration management, these theoretical contributions are combined into a preliminary theoretica framework.

## 2.1 Managing IS integration in an M&A context

Despite the demonstrated importance and risk of neglecting IS integration issues in the M&A process, investigations show that IS questions normally receive noticeably little attention in practice (Alaranta & Henningsson, 2008). IS integration is currently only considered after a deal is closed, often when the managers are left with the extremely difficult task of integrating two fundamentally different IS environments (Robbins & Stylianou, 1999; Shearer et al., 2004; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007).

IS integration management can be seen as a subtask of IS management, the ultimate objective of which is to contribute to the organisation achieving its goals (Mata et al., 1995; Gottschalk, 2000). IS management is frequently defined as the tasks and duties of IS managers and is used in the context of ‘key IS management issues’ (e.g. Gottschalk, 2000). IS management is also closely related to IS and IT governance, the latter defined as ‘the framework for decision rights and accountabilities to encourage desirable behavior in the use of IT’ (Weill, 2004, p. 3). If some distinction shall be made between IS management and IS governance, the first is about which specific decisions are being made and the actions taken (implementation of decisions); the latter is ‘about systematically determining who makes each type of decision (a decision right), who has input to a decision (an input right) and how these people (or groups) are held accountable for their role’ (Weill, 2004, p. 3). In this paper, managing IS integration in M&A refers to the understanding of how IS integration relates to the M&A context as well as being able to decide and act upon the understanding. Drawing on general IS management research (e.g. Clemons & Row, 1991; Mata et al., 1995; Weill & Broadbent, 1998; Gottschalk, 2000; Meyer, 2004) and research on IS and IT governance (e.g. Meyer, 2004; Brown & Grant, 2005; Van Grembergen, 2005; Willcocks et al., 2006), IS integration management is refined into three main tasks: identifying the different basic structural options (alternatives) that exist for IS integration in M&A, making decisions based on an understanding of how the alternatives relate to organisational objectives and realising the made decisions.

The basic structural options that exist for IS integration in M&A naturally relate to properties of the IS integration and properties of the M&A that affect each other in different ways. Scholars argue about the three broad strands into which research on M&A can be divided (Larsson, 1990; Risberg, 1999). The first strand focuses on M&A as a tool for corporate strategy, the second addresses the issue of organisational compatibility and the third strand studies the events and activities during the M&A process. Regarding options of IS integration, it should be acknowledged that ‘IS’ is composed of a wide scope of fundamentally different systems (Weil & Broadbent, 1998) that may affect and can be affected differently by the M&A context. Additionally, the existing literature describes two features of the IS integration that may have impact on the M&A process: the architectural approach used (Markus, 2000) and the role of IS integration in the M&A process (McKiernan & Merali, 1995).

The work on IS integration in M&A already includes frameworks for evaluating IS success in M&A (Alaranta, 2005a), assessing IT integration decisions (Mehta & Hirschheim, 2004; 2007), integration alignment (Wijnhoven et al., 2006) and enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementations in relation to mergers (Alaranta, 2005b). However, these frameworks are either limited to one theoretical perspective or limited to one of many managerial aspects of IS integration in M&A. Not only the aforementioned frameworks but also additional literature on IS in M&A (e.g. Buck-Lew et al., 1992; McKiernan & Merali, 1995; Stylianou et al., 1996; Giacomazzi et al., 1997; Robbins & Stylianou, 1999; Hwang, 2004) and the literature review later suggest that there are six emerging research streams that address different aspects of IS integration in M&A that express managerial issues that have to be considered during the M&A process. These six streams are synergistic potential, organisational integration, intentions and reaction, IS ecology, integration architecture and IS integration role. We will hereafter refer to them as the six (A–F) dimensions of IS integration in M&A. Together the dimensions form our theoretical framework.

## 2.2 Synergistic potential

The concept of synergy is essential to understanding the logical reasons why companies engage in M&A (Lubatkin, 1988). Synergy in this context is defined as what is occurring when two units can be run more efficiently and/or more effectively together rather than apart (Cording et al., 2002). The literature describes three basic types of synergies as possible outcomes of mergers: technical economies, pecuniary economies and diversification economies (Lubatkin, 1983). Technical economies are scale economies that occur when the physical processes inside the firm are altered so that the same amount of inputs produce a higher quantity of output or the same quantity of output is produced using fewer resources. Pecuniary economies correspond to the firm’s capability to dictate market prices by making use of market powe achieved primarily by size. Finally, diversification economies are achieved by improving the firm’s performance relative to its risk attributes, meaning to spread the risk among unrelated markets and products through a strategic product portfolio (Lubatkin, 1983).

Giacomazzi et al. (1997) indirectly addressed the question of the IS’ role in the leverage of different kinds of synergy. Diversification economies lead to conglomerates, which do not require any integration. Neither do pecuniary economies in themselves, but M&As related to pecuniary economies logically also involve potential technical economies. As pecuniary economies are related to size, and negotiation power towards customers and suppliers, they involve organisational growth that is related to economies of scale (a technical economy). As far as we know, no research has explicitly addressed the relation of synergy and IS.

## 2.3 Organisational integration

To actually leverage the synergistic potential of a merger or acquisition, the two organisations must be integrated in some way (unless it is an unrelated M&A) (Yoo et al., 2007). Haspeslagh & Jemison (1991) found in their studies that the transformation (degree of integration) in an M&A could be classified into four categories: holding, preservation, symbiosis and absorption. The two dimensions in Figure 1, strategic interdependence and organisational autonomy, were found to be the two most important drivers for deciding on the integration approach (Haspeslagh & Jemison, 1991). Holding (see Figure 1) represents an approach where the acquired unit has been left undisturbed. Preservation includes partial integration of the new entity. Symbiosis refers to a situation where acquirer and acquired (or the entities in a merger) are equally transformed to fit each other. Finally, absorption is the complete incorporation of an acquired unit into the acquiring organisation. The model in Figure 1 encapsulates the essence of organisational fit, recognising that all M&A do not need the same degree of integration and address potential problems of attaining the desired integration level. Determining the desired level of integration is dependent on, in addition to the aspired synergistic benefits, the organisational units in quest for integration.

A second distinction is suggested between integration of the organisation’s primary processes (operational) and secondary processes (supportive, functional) (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). These different kinds of integration can be described with various attributes, including the type of dependency between integrated units, which barriers normally faced, the potentia benefits, efforts needed to leverage these benefits and underlying mechanisms (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). Thompson (1967) argued that interdependencies between units are the starting point for integration. The interdependency types are the following:

1 Pooled, meaning that each part of the organisation makes a contribution to the whole that forms an organisation. The different parts of the organisation do not, however, need to depend directly on each other.

2 Sequential, the output of one is the input for another. A typical example is an industrial value chain.

3 Reciprocal, the output of one part is the input for another, which, in turn, directly or via proxy, is the input for the first unit.

According to Barki & Pinsonneault (2005), the different dependencies are normally not evenly distributed over the organisation. Sequential and reciprocal dependencies are more frequent among primary (operational) than secondary (supportive, functional) processes. The three dependencies are said to be hierarchical, pooled being the basic form, sequential containing a pooled aspect as well as further dependency and reciprocal, in turn, being sequential plus something more. Ceteris paribus, complexity of integration increases with dependency leve (Thompson, 1967). Generally, this means that IS integration of primary processes requires more effort than integration of secondary processes.

## 2.4 Intentions and reactions

The process perspective emphasises that the M&A process is an important factor, in addition to, or even as an alternative to strategic and organisational fit that affects the outcome (Risberg, 1999). The M&A process has been described in models with three to seven phases (Henningsson, 2007). However, conceptually, all models conform to the same idea of progression. In the pre-M&A phase, organisations are preparing and planning for the act, while after the deal is closed, the real work of making the two units function together begins in the post-M&A phase.

One M&A typology that recognises that M&A integration does not always follow the same pattern is the hostility–friendliness continuum by Buono & Bowditch (1989), originally developed by Pritchett (1985). One extreme of the continuum is labelled ‘friendly’ and the other extreme is labelled ‘hostile’. The friendliest form of M&A is the organisational rescue. Generally, this type of M&A is well perceived by the target. The next degree of M&A friendliness is collaboration. This is more in the nature of a neutral merger than an acquisition. The objective is to reach a fair deal for both companies, but some of the problems that arise are related to the way in which the combination is communicated to personnel and the inability to follow up on hasty promises. In contested combinations, only one of the companies wants the deal, i.e. the companies prefer completely different arrangements. Finally, during raids, one company takes over another by bypassing management and directly asks shareholders to sell their shares. An M&A can trigger reactions from employees and these can affect turnover rate and level of distrust (Buono & Bowditch, 1989; Napier, 1989).

## 2.5 IS ecology

The notion ‘IS’ refers to a heterogeneous group of systems that present fundamentally different characteristics relevant in integration. For example, integrating a bank’s transactional system is not equivalent to integrating an email system. A suitable typology for differentiating between different IS is Weill & Broadbent’s (1998) classification based on organisational function to support infrastructural, transactional, informational and strategic. A contemporary IT base is composed of a number of different systems and technologies that are tightly intertwined into an information infrastructure (Hanseth & Braa, 2001) that is difficult to radically transform (Hanseth, 2000; Yoo et al., 2007).

Infrastructural IS are composed of the information road networks: servers, cables and software that permit data flow. Transaction IS are, e.g. sales systems and bookkeeping software. Transaction refers to business transactions. A decision support system serving managers with information on sales figures or customer satisfaction is an informational IS. Finally, strategic IS are IS that can be considered critical to the performance of the organisation and give the organisation a competitive advantage (Weill & Broadbent, 1998).

## 2.6 Integration architecture

There is not one way to carry out IS integration. The literature identifies two ways of describing the bonds that are made in integration activities. First, the integration of IS can be made on different levels (integration level): IT systems level, IS level, and organizational level. The organisation level is relevant for understanding the purpose of IS integration work and how it could contribute to organisational performance. The IT system level, addressing in more practical terms how the enabling IT integration could be implemented, also has significan implications on the IS level. None of these levels can possibly be ignored if one contends to approach the subject of IS integration comprehensively.

Further, linkage between IS can be structured according to different architectural principles (integration structures). Figure 2 depicts five alternatives identified in the literature. The first solution is a point-to-point alternative, where an interface connects two applications directly to each other. To decrease complexity, an approach that uses an intermediate layer between applications and databases called middleware can be used. The third alternative is to adopt an enterprise-wide system, often referred to as enterprise system or ERP system (Markus, 2000). Data warehouses and master data management are central in the fourth conceptual approach to integration architecture. Data are extracted from source systems to a meta-level layer. Finally, the service-oriented architecture (SOA) is still relatively unproven but promise scalable and flexible architectures in that every single module stands alone and might be added or withdrawn as the need for it fluctuates (Zhu, 2005).

The classification above has the advantage of being conceptual rather than being empirically driven. One alternative would be to focus on the numerous techniques and technologies such as service bus and enterprise application integration (EAI) that flourish on the IS integration market; however, differentiations among the concepts are often difficult as limits tend to differ from author to author and also evolve over time. The classification earlier is chosen as it is likely to be stable, thus enabling conclusions that persist during a longer period of time. Technological trends will come and go, but the fundamental architectural principles persist.

## 2.7 IS integration role

Two alternatives to the role of IS integration in M&A have been proposed by McKiernan & Merali (1995): reactive and proactive. The authors argued that IS integration could be used reactively, found to be the normal approach of contemporary business, meaning that once the deal was closed and potential synergies and barriers determined, the task of integrating IS commenced. IS were not seen as a source of synergy nor as a significant barrier for their leverage. The alternative (being proactive), as proposed by McKiernan and Merali, was to introduce IS and IS integration in the due diligence phase (McKiernan & Merali, 1995). Synergies as well as barriers could, they argued, also be found in IS. Therefore, the compatibility of IS should be determined prior to sealing any deal. As the role of IS varied among organisations and individual organisational units (Weill & Broadbent, 1998), the role of IS integration also varied based on the importance of IS.

## 3. RESEARCH APPROACH

The research presented in this paper was carried out in a form of engaged scholarship (Van de Ven, 2007) combined with the structured case study approach (Carroll & Swatman, 2000). Engaged scholarship was briefly described in the introduction. The essence of the structured case study approach (Carroll & Swatman, 2000) is that it forms an iterative, four-phased research cycle (plan, investigate existing theory, collect data and reflect on data/analysis) upon a formal theoretical framework. The initial framework (in our case, the framework presented in Tables 1 and 2) is continuously revised during the cyclic iterations. The combination with engaged scholarship implies collaboration with a professional partner in the cycle.

A comprehensive account for the research approach used can be read in Carlsson et al. (2011). The study presented here was part of a larger research project with the ultimate objective of developing design theory useful for managing IS integration in M&As.

Although the study in this paper is presented as a straightforward process with framework, empirical study, analysis and theorising, the reality was a far more iterative process with alternations between theory, empirical data and analysis (see Figure 3) – as suggested by the structured case method (Carroll & Swatman, 2000).

## 3.1 Problem definition and research objectives

The authors of this paper and Trelleborg AB engaged in a learning partnership with the objective to research issues of relevance for both researchers and practitioners. Learning partnership is best characterised as a form of engaged scholarship (cf. Van de Ven, 2007). Van de Ven (2007) defined four alternative forms of engaged scholarship (Figure 4): (1) informed basic research with stakeholder advice that is undertaken to describe, explain or predict a social phenomenon; (2) co-produced knowledge with collaborators entailing a greater sharing of power and participation between researchers and stakeholders; (3) policy, design and evaluation research undertaken to develop knowledge related to design and evaluation of policies, programmes and models for addressing practical and professional problems; and (4) action and intervention research for solving a client’s problem while at the same time, contrib uting to the academic body of knowledge.

Managers at Trelleborg AB were involved in defining the problem to address: how to manage IS integration in M&As. It was jointly decided that the research should not address pure technical issues but instead, should have a managerial perspective. Jointly, we also discussed what form(s) of engaged scholarship would be suitable. Action and intervention research, as well as design and evaluation research, were ruled out on several bases. First, the current body of knowledge regarding IS integration in M&As was deemed to be insufficient for doing this type of engaged scholarship. Second, as noted above, IS integration in M&As can be long and tedious processes. Given the resources available and the quest for results, it was found that action and intervention would not be suitable. Third, in most cases, an M&A process is a very sensitive and secret process, especially in the early phases. It was judged that at the beginning of the project, it would not be possible to get access to necessary sources in ongoing M&As. Based on discussions with managers in Trelleborg AB, it was decided that the project should start as informed basic research with stakeholder advice.

Generally, engaged scholarship and learning partnership should develop practical knowledge to be used to solve classes of IS problems, i.e. the development of knowledge that can be used in designing, implementing and managing IS initiatives. ## 3.2 Case selection and empirical sources

Case selection in this kind of research always has to contain some degree of eclecticism and pragmatism (Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007). The M&A activity is at the very core of a company’s strategic processes, and the access required to profoundly grasp the essential of the integration is a problem that has to be balanced with the need to develop the theoretical understanding.

Cases were selected in agreement with representatives from Trelleborg who knew the details of the more than 50 M&As Trelleborg had recently been involved in. Four of Trelleborg’s

M&As were included in the study. Table 3 shows the case selection criteria for this study and empirical data for each case. In addition to the four cases, general interactions also took place, including feedback sessions as well as annual and periodic reports to Trelleborg.

The framework presented in Tables 1 and 2 integrated previous research and served as foundation for the study’s empirical part. The framework also contained aspects of improvements and learning from one acquisition to the next, but these aspects are outside the scope of this paper. Empirical data were collected to describe and explain IS integration management and the M&A process according to the six dimensions of the framework. The main method for data collection was interviews. The framework was operationalised into interview questions. Interviewees were selected based on their ability to be able to provide information for the concepts in the dimensions, which mostly meant middle and top management and to some extent, also IT architects and the IS users. Interviews were broad and conversational in order to set the foundation for new and unexpected information that could direct future data gath ering. The interviews were recorded and relevant parts of the interviews were transcribed. Additional empirical sources, such as documentation and observation, were used to complement the interviews and to triangulate research findings. Sensitive documents (due diligence reports, internal evaluations and other strategic documents) were studied at Trelleborg’s premises.

## 3.3 Analysis and evaluation of contribution

Data analysis was an ongoing process that commenced right after the first interview, with sorting information into the framework dimensions through coding. Coding was made by numbering the dimension (A–F), their subcategories and their values. Information not possible to classify into the framework dimensions and concepts were given special codes and marked for potential future extension of the framework. During the transcription of the interviews, we marked occurrences of the dimensions, subcategories and values (as well as uncategorised but still potentially relevant information). We then rewrote the interviews into elaborated case stories according to the six dimensions, maintaining the coding of the data. For each dimension, it was then possible, by use of the codes, to identify information on how it related to other dimensions. For example, after a reconsideration of the desired organisational integration (from absorption to symbiosis), it was found that the new organisational objective demanded integration of other IS (transactional IS instead of informational IS). This indicated a new relationship, BD3, between dimension B (organisational integration) and dimension D (IS ecology).

The ambition of the study presented in this paper was to produce theory for describing and explaining (cf. Gregor, 2006). With the framework, it was deemed possible to describe the decisions on IS integration taken and to explain which consequences they had. Identifying relationships between dimensions are functional explanation according to the typology by Hovorka et al. (2008). ## 4.1 Kléber

When Trelleborg and the Michelin Group finally signed the deal transferring the ownership of the Kléber unit, it was the start of a 10-year integration process. The consolidation was primarily a market extension:

In terms of production, the two units [. . .] were very compatible. [. . .] Overlapping was also limited in geographical terms. Trelleborg was more Nordic, more niche. Kléber was more Latin, had a larger product range, and had a wider distribution network. Basically, the companies only competed in the same niche in Germany. [Trelleborg Industrial Hose (TIH) Chief Financial Officer (CFO)]

It was expected that some of the two companies’ similar products would be produced jointly, thereby enabling larger volumes and lower production prices per unit. For not directly substitute products, the deal would open up new markets and increase sales. To leverage synergies, it was decided to create a new unit within the Trelleborg group composed of the acquired Kléber unit and the corresponding unit from the Trelleborg group – TIH.

Trelleborg was the purchaser and initially pursued an absorption strategy. A Swedish management team was installed and Swedish business practice was introduced into the new unit. It was decided that the IT systems at the old Kléber unit should be replaced by the systems used in Trelleborg. Although the Trelleborg group was substantially larger than the Kléber unit, the relation was the opposite within TIH. Seven hundred fifty persons worked at the old Kléber site at Clermont-Ferrand, but only 200 worked for TIH in Trelleborg. Some production was also moved to Clermont-Ferrand to profit from scale advantages.

During the first 2 years, TIH had Swedish management, and integration projects were carried out by Swedish people in large project groups who tried to reach consensus in important decisions. Swedish suppliers were most often chosen. At this point, integration of informational IS was in focus as the central management wanted transparency. In 1998, the absorption strategy was abandoned for a more symbiotic approach. A new, originally French, management team was installed. At this time, both the way of managing and the desired IS integration changed. The spotlight turned to the transaction IS as focus shifted from control to efficiency improvements.

What we decided when I started in 1999 was to change the organisational structure completely. Within six months we had changed the picture completely. We decided at that time to have a new chart with one logistics manager reporting to me in Sweden, one logistics manager reporting to me in France. One purchase manager for both, one IT manager for both. That was perhaps the first time we crossed the former organisational borders to have common functions independent of location. It was completely different from having one spli responsibility at the two sites. [TIH operations manager]

The Swedish management team installed after the acquisition faced a complex IT infrastructure. At the Clermont-Ferrand site, an ERP system developed in-house called Bergounix was running, and in all the countries where Kléber was represented by sales organisations, individua

ERP installations were found. The former Trelleborg part used Movex, an ERP system from Intentia (now Lawson) in all countries. Also, here, every country had, in reality, its individual installation of the system. A decision was made to replace all Bergounix installations with Movex installations. Although these new systems would all be Movex systems, it was not just one single system being implemented but rather seven different instances. Different architectures fo integration, including both point-to-point and middleware solutions, were considered.

At the end of 1998 they arrived to the conclusion that it would take about 1000 working days to implement the outlined Movex system, to make the specific development on top of the standard system. When then the new manager arrived in the end of 1998 he said: ‘1000 days just for the specific development . . .’. It was too much. It couldn’t be motivated with future savings. In addition it was the problem that the existing system would not make the year 2000 problem. At the end it was decided to take people from the outside to fix the old systems, just to pass the shift to year 2000. We then decided to replace everything with one single installation. This was the most efficient way of getting the integration we needed. [TIH operations manager)

In order to achieve the new organisational structure, national centres needed to be fully integrated with the stock locations, production facilities and logistics departments. It was thus decided to eliminate the national ERP installations and make all use the same system as was used in Clermont-Ferrand. At this time, there was still the modified Bergounix system. Later on, the Bergounix system was replaced by a Movex installation. In 2005, the old Trelleborg factory also switched from one individual Movex installation to using the enterprise-wide system, and 10 years after the acquisition, the new unit had become integrated in terms of the IT system. By combining the findings in each dimension of the framework, the Kléber case presents fou By combining the findings in each dimension of the framework., the Kléber case presents four relations between the dimensions of the framework:

1 BD3: B. Organisational integration – D. IS ecology: as the whole approach to the integration changed from absorption to symbiosis, this led to a new focus of IS integration. Rather than focusing on informational IS to achieve control, integration in transactional IS was sought to leverage synergies and increase profits. The symbiosis approach favoured other IS function ality than the absorption strategy.

Between 1996 and 1999 there were basically no changes in terms of integration. A lot of planning but only marginally implemented in reality. The only thing integrated was the reporting to the group. [. . .] What we decided when I started in 1999 was to change the organisational structure completely. All IT investments after that were to realise this new chart. [TIH operations manager]

2 BE1: B. Organisational integration – E. Integration architecture: Trelleborg evaluated the option of doing required integration with middleware architecture. They found that the available technology at the time of the late 1990s would not permit the tight integration they required. The conclusion was that it was not efficient to undertake higher degrees of integration using middleware.

If you look here [showing a requirement specification for the later rejected system] it is a detailed description of what everyone was doing already. [. . .] In this setup it was very difficult to know the activities in the organization . . . with eleven companies . . . seven ERP systems . . . products had different prices in the stock locations and so on . . . if you look at the picture of IS at that time we had Movex, Movex, Movex, Movex, in almost all the places. Not the same Movex. Not the same implementation, not the same parameters, but nevertheless the same Movex. The plan before 1999 was to link these systems with middleware. This was simply too costly. [. . .] The Unity-project was not at all about linking different ERP, but to have only one ERP for the whole business unit. [TIH operations manager]

3 BF1: B. Organisational integration – F. IS integration role: when the new unit TIH acquired the niche company, Dynaflex, TIH used its new platform as a proactive use of IS integration. The objective was absorption, and this was possible as the existing systems permitted inclusion of new organisational units – an expressed objective of the new setup. Proactive use of integration enabled smooth absorption.

Our approach is not to build a new application at every country unit rather it is to expand the central Movex system and integrate other country business units in to this rather than working on local applications or systems. Back in 1998–1999 we had eleven companies and distribution centers in eleven countries and seven ERP systems to manage these different unit’s activities. [. . .] Now we have one [. . .] IT-department and one IS to manage and provide visibility for all these. This means that we have been able to make a lot of rationalisation due to the IS integration. This also means that we have full visibility of our business units through the central Movex system, and that we easily can add new units to the system. [TIH operations manager]

4 CD1: C. Intention & reactions – IS ecology: as the perception of the acquisition changed from contested combination to collaboration, this resulted in a different functionality focus. The focus shifted from informational IS to transactional IS.

The group wanted of course insight into the business of the acquired unit. Neither the Trelleborg unit nor the Kléber unit were doing well financially and group management wanted full insight into the business. Since the results improved we’re more trusted and can focus on more on the internal business processes and how to improve them. [TIH CFO]

## 4.2 Dynaflex

In 2004, Trelleborg extended its hose business as the niche company Dynaflex was bough from Manuli to complement the existing repertoire.

The Trelleborg group does not simply acquire a company that is already successful. Rather, it prefers to see future potential and either develop the company or introduce its products to new countries. For example, in this case, we launched Dynaflex products in the U.K., where Dynaflex previously had not had any customers. [TIH operations manager]

The most striking difference compared with the Kléber case was that the integration took 3 months, not 10 years. The short integration time was essential as before the deal, Dynaflex was an integrated part of the Manuli organisation.

Basically the Dynaflex acquisition was managed by the sales director and one week before the contract was to be signed they came to us at the IT department and told us that the current owner of Dynaflex was going to continuing to support and keep alive the current IS for only six more months. Or actually, he came and said that we were thinking about buying this company and I asked ‘what about the IS?’ ‘Good question’, he responded and came back saying that they would keep it alive for six months. [TIH Operations manager]

The approach chosen was to integrate Dynaflex into the IS of TIH. Dynaflex was mapped as a third production unit with location in the enterprise-wide Movex installation of TIH. It was only 3 months after the closure of the deal that Dynaflex was integrated with the Trelleborg Group in terms of IS.

As TIH become responsible for IS integration of Dynaflex, the integration project became the concern of the very same individuals that just were about to conclude the integration of the whole TIH organisation. This meant that they could use their personal experiences from rolling out the Movex system in all previous locations

We had been the same team for 3–4 years. We knew what had worked before. That was why they asked me. [. . .] We didn’t really need to program that much. We used existing processes that we had created for the previous project. I think we only created one o two processes. For the rest we could either reuse what we had or use Movex’ standard processes. We needed to investigate the databases and move the items to Movex. [TIH system integrator]

In terms of the framework for IS integration, the Dynaflex case represents a proactive approach to IS integration; however, IS was not part of the due diligence process, and the proactive use was limited to IS integration capability improvement, which meant creating the potential for smooth integration of any M&A.

1 BD4: B. Organisational integration – D. IS ecology: when TIH annexed Dynaflex, TIH wished not to destroy the specific value of Dynaflex and saw little need to integrate business activities. However, transparency and the ability to control the progression of business were wanted. The solution was to integrate only Informational IS. When striving for preservation, there is no need for integrating transaction IS.

The TIH sales department also did not want to implement a complex standard package. Dynaflex was a small, efficient and flexible business. If we had implemented the full Movex package, we would have destroyed that flexibility. [TIH operations manager]

2 BE2: B. Organisational integration – E. Integration architecture: although preservation was the chosen approach to organisational integration, TIH still went for enterprise-wide integra tion. They did so not for integration purposes but for standardisation reasons. Implementing the same system was expected to be the most cost-effective approach. The lesson learnt is that integration may come as a by-product, triggered by actions that are driven by other mechanisms.

We knew that we needed to do something. But what was the best IT solution? Should we install a system only for them, should we not install any system at all . . . or should we integrate them into our existing platform. Because of the short time available, there were not many options. We used what we had already. We only created a new financial unit and a new company so that they would have their own databases and invoices. [TIH system integrator]

3 DE3: D. IS type – E. Integration architecture: the view of IS at Dynaflex was that it was not strategic; therefore, it could be replaced by reason of cost savings. If it would have been regarded as being strategic, it would have been risky to replace it.

For each process we investigated ‘how are they doing it today’ and ‘how should it be made in the future’. And for most processes we could decide that the latter was the standard Movex way. Later Isabelle and I went through all processes in detail investigating if it really was possible to work in that way. [TIH system integrator]

4 EF2: E. Integration architecture – F. IS role: TIH had, during the last decade, developed a functional enterprise-wide system that was fairly simple to transform and extend. Without this system in place, it would not have been possible to choose the enterprise-wide integration architecture as the short time frame would not have permitted bulky transformations.

Not with the system we had in 1996. I think it went this easy because we were in a process where we had integrated several other country business units into one system that worked well and that we had lots of experience of. With seven different ERP systems . . . then we would have had to set up a new one also for Dynaflex. [TIH operations manager]

## 4.3 CRP Group

In January 2006, Trelleborg, through the Trelleborg Engineered Systems (TES) division, completed the purchase of CRP Group from Barclays Private Equity. CRP Group, which was founded as late as 1974, was an engineering company with annual sales of slightly more than <sup>€</sup>100 million and 500 employees, primarily in the UK and in the USA. CRP’s operations related primarily to systems for the subsea sector of the oil and gas business. The company was active in systems for seismic surveys, drilling operations and subsea production, with solutions for deepwater flow assurance and buoyancy systems.

The CRP acquisition was a step into an attractive segment. We already had some business within this segment, but with CRP we at least tripled our presence in that segment. [TES CFO]

The deal was justified with the already profitable business of the annexed company as well as potential savings in marketing, sales and product development. The deal also represented diversification economies. It was said to have its business in a potentially growing market. There was an apparent overlap with the business within Trelleborg. However, whereas Trelleborg had a strong presence in Northern Europe, CRP had similar business mainly in the UK and in the USA. The M&A can thus be seen as a market extension, similar products but at different markets. Although the complementarity opened up potential economies of scale in production, scheduling and logistics, it was not the ambition to seek these synergies.

The unit was profitable in itself and there was no immediate need for consolidating the business with other Trelleborg business. [TES CFO]

However, it was not a pure holding approach that took place. The ambition was to integrate sales, marketing and product development. Trelleborg also wanted the new division to culturally take part in ‘some Trelleborg spirit, whatever that might be’ (TES CIO). The group management also wanted some control over what was going on in the business unit, although the unit was to be highly decentralised.

With respect to IS, integration was mostly taking place on the infrastructural level. CRP was included into the corporate network of Trelleborg. The infrastructural IS had to be altered to comply not only with the user directory system of Trelleborg and security standards for the group but also be a part of the intranet and mail architecture. The choice of integration level was highly related to the choice of not going for synergies in scheduling, logistics or production. As such, the unit could be kept apart and rather undisturbed in its daily operations.

Expected synergies were fewer than in the Kléber case, which naturally had an impact on the level of integration as a whole. Compared with the Dynaflex case, where a limited set of synergies were sought, the CRP unit was substantially bigger. The difference of synergistic potential was found to be the underlying reason for a new set of relations between different dimensions in the framework. By contrasting them with how the same issues were solved in the Dynaflex case, we find some explanations as to why the decisions went in the directions they did. The following relations were found in the CRP case:

1 AD1: A. Synergistic potential – D. IS ecology: synergies were sought in sales, marketing and production development. But as only infrastructural IS was implemented, these technological synergies could never be leveraged. This also confirms that IS integration has a significant role in the leverage of some synergistic potential and the precise finding that for synergies in sales, integration of transactional IS is needed.

At first we decided that we only wanted CRP as an independent unit within the group. For this we needed only to integrate the basic IT; email, intranet, and so on. Much later we focused on the sales synergies and then we had to merge CRP’s ERP system with the ERP of another unit within the group. Otherwise we couldn’t have enabled these synergies. [TES Chief Information Officer (CIO)]

2 BF2: B. Organisational integration – F. IS integration role: the striving for organisationa integration was preservation. Preservation implies leaving the acquired unit mostly undisturbed in terms of IS integration. As such, IS integration issues play a minor role and can thus be treated reactively.

Did we think about IS and IT before the deal? Yes. At least I did. We discussed the ERP a little bit and knew what they had, and that we had to replace it by time. In that way it was simple, we knew that we had to replace most of things in a near future but most things were not related to synergies and could be dealt with as normal system updates. [TES CFO]

3 CD2: C. Intentions & Reactions – D. IS ecology: In the CRP case, only infrastructural IS was integrated. The task was mainly of technical nature, and the integration of operational processes was not required. Therefore, the M&A context had only limited impact on the integration work. The dimension of intention and reaction primarily refers to human reaction, which is important when integration is needed in operational processes.

In this case only the email, intranet and these things were critical. The rest we could do by time. We didn’t really affect the way they were working in CRP. [TES CIO]

4 DF1: D. IS Ecology – F. IS integration role: for the same reasons as mentioned earlier, integration in only infrastructural IS was not severely affected by the general M&A characteristics and did not, to a high degree, affect the outcome of the general process. Therefore, if integrating only infrastructural IS, the IS integration can assume a reactive position without endangering the M&A initiative.

I learnt about the deal only weeks before it took place. This is how things go normally. You cannot prepare for everything; you discuss and plan for numerous deals simultaneously. Most of them will never take place in reality. Then one deal suddenly becomes reality and you have to deal with that one. I don’t think anyone can foresee which deals that will become in the end. As long as the company can go on using the systems they have for some time, then IS integration won’t be a deal breaker. Integrating the basic infrastructure is something we know how to do. [TES CIO]

## 4.4 Chase-Walton Elastomers

The three presented cases were all M&As made by the TES division. As Trelleborg was, and still is, a highly decentralised organisation, the division involved in the M&A had impact on how the process unfolded. Chase-Walton Elastomers, until 2005 a North American-based production-oriented actor in the sealing and damping business with production facilities located in Hudson (MA), was annexed by Trelleborg Sealing Solutions (TSS) for mainly two reasons.

[First] Relatively we were not very strong in the Americas (22%). [. . .] We were close to being the leader in Europe, but we’re just another player in the US. So we wanted more rapid expansion in the Americas. [. . . Second] We identified some years ago that Aerospace was an area where we were not as strong as we ought to be. It fitted with where we and our customers see ourselves – that we’re providing high quality and high tech solutions. So we set out that an ideal acquisition in that area would be a company manufacturing and delivering Aerospace products in the US. And Chase-Walton fitted that. [TSS CFO]

To describe the IS integration, it is needed to understand the business model of TSS. The business was roughly divided into three main activities: marketing, logistics and production. Of these, production was the least emphasised. As the incorporation of ‘solutions’ into the division name suggests, TSS offered complete solutions to customers. The products in these solutions could just as well be purchased from outside Trelleborg. Therefore, TSS searched absorption of Chase-Walton’s logistics and marketing activities, but preservation of the production activity. This was reflected in the related IS integration. TSS did not make any elaborated due diligence regarding IS.

Because, no matter what they used, we’d be replacing their systems with our systems, ou own ERP. [TSS CFO]

TSS’ business model, with focus on marketing and logistics, was dependent on an enterprise-wide architectural solution. For manufacturing, integration was not considered criti cal. The integration was carried out via middleware interfaces exchanging data via electronic data interchange (EDI).

The Chase-Walton case proposes the following relations:

1 BE3: B. Organisational integration – E. Integration architecture: At lower levels of integration, drivers other than the architecture’s enabling properties seemed to be decisive, such as cost savings by standardisation and knowledge of specific systems.

We might set up EDI for the time being or . . . usually there is no need. They are rather simple transactions, we can eventually treat them like an external supplier but they are part of our intern group. [. . .] It is just a normal trading relationship whether they are internal or external, that is always an option even if it is not no. 1. [. . .] By time we will integrate them into our ForthShift manufacturing system as we roll it out also in the US. It’s just less expensive to use one single system. [TSS IT specialist M&A unit]

2 DE4: D. IS Ecology – E. Integration architecture: if the company strives for a higher degree of integration and at the same time, regards the supporting IS to be of importance, enterprisewide integration is the choice. The less important IS becomes for the activity, which is intended to be integrated, the lesser the reason for enterprise-wide integration. This relation seems to be decided by the integration architecture’s ‘enabling’ characteristics.

When we acquire a company, all the selling and marketing activities will be taken off it, and they’ll be bolted into the business model that we have in the US. All the manufacturing operations will then join this BU [Business Unit] and then become part of it. And if we’ve already thought that there is another product line in other locations that complements this product line, so we’ll be moving it in there. So within 6 months they’ll be cut in half and completely following our business model. [. . .] Marketing and logistics activities will be incorporated in the existing systems we run for these activities [JD Edwards World in Europe and JD Edwards One World in the Americas]. The production unit will be just another supplier. [TSS CFO]

3 DF2: D. IS Ecology – F. IS integration role: not all IS in a company are of equal strategic importance, and the degree of proactiveness in the Chase-Walton case was directly related to the importance of the IS. For activities where IS had an important role, leaning towards a dependent character, a clear plan was available. In areas where IS had a lesser important role, the integration could assume a more reactive process.

Coming back to the IT aspect, not once did we consider how the systems worked, how the would interface with ours. Because no matter what they used, we’d be replacing whatever they got with our systems, our own ERP. If a company had systems that couldn’t give us what we needed, we’d replace it in within the first week. It’s not a feature of the process and it doesn’t affect the decision. We’ll see what we’ve got just prior the acquisition and it won’t affect the decision. [TSS CFO]

The Dynaflex case showed that when striving for preservation, there is no need for integrating transactional IS. Integration may come as a by-product, triggered by actions that are driven by other mechanisms. If the IS in the company to be absorbed is non-strategic, enterprise-wide integration may be used for cost-saving reasons rather than integration reason, and unless there is a flexible and extendable enterprise-wide system already in place by the time of the deal, the enterprise-wide option normally is too time-consuming with regard to the time pressure to leverage synergies.

In the CRP case, it became clear that when only infrastructural IS was integrated, the synergies sought in sales, marketing and production development could not be leveraged, IS integration could be dealt with reactively when the objective was preservation, infrastructura IS integration was mostly a technical project with limited dependence on human reaction and finally, if integrating only infrastructural IS, the IS integration could assume a reactive position without endangering the M&A initiative.

Finally, in the Chase-Walton case, it was found that for lower levels of integration, drivers other than architectures’ enabling properties seem to be decisive, such as cost savings by standardisation and knowledge of specific systems. The less important IS became for the activity, the lesser the reason for going for enterprise-wide integration. For activities where IS had an important function, leaning towards a dependent character, a clear plan was available. In areas where IS had a lesser important role, the integration could assume a more reactive process.

## 5.2 Evaluating the framework

As explained in the methods section, based on Gregor (2006), three criteria were suggested for the continuous evaluation of the framework: usefulness (explanatory potential), distinctiveness and simplicity. The evaluation, summarised in Table 7, found that three of the dimensions met these criteria. Of the remaining three dimensions, one (A, synergistic potential) could be simplified because of an unnecessary classification concept (Diversification economies) that did not link to IS integration. Dimension D, IS ecology, turned out to not live up to the condition of explanatory potential as it missed relating its constituting characteristics to the M&A phenomenon. The original classification was too simplistic. Increased complexity by dividing the concept into its constituents, supported activity and strategic importance of IS, increased both distinctiveness and explanatory potential. Finally, dimension F, IS integration role, also had to be reconceptualised since the empirical data clearly pointed to a third approach, i.e. to IS integration, in addition to the two described in the literature. In these cases, proactive measures were taken not by including IS integration into the due diligence phase bu by preparing the existing information infrastructures in a way that made them extendable when required.

## 5.3 An initial systemic model of the dynamic relationship of IS integration in M&A

Well aware of the thin line between frameworks and models, we present the dimensions and their mutual relations as an initial version of a systemic model of IS integration in M&A. The reason why this contribution is called a model is that based on the identified relations, it is possible to position the constituting components to each other. A framework is here regarded as a set of mutually independent entities that highlight specific aspects of a phenomenon prospectively useful in understanding the phenomenon, i.e. creating a model that explains how the aspects relate to each other. A framework does not contain information that explains, only the aspects that could be useful in explaining.

## 5.3.1 The Dynamic System of IS Integration in M&A (DySIIM) model

In total, 23 unique ways in which characteristics of IS integration relate to characteristics of the M&A process have been identified. Eight of the relations are derived from the preliminary theoretical walk-through and empirical validation. The remaining 15 relations have been found as patterns in the empirical material and provide additional insight to the dynamics in the process. The relations in the model refer to what we found in existing literature and in our four cases. The model does not make any claims to be all-embracing nor does it give a complete picture of all dynamics between IS integration and the M&A context.

The dimensions and relations are depicted as a dynamic system in Figure 5 in the nex section – the DySIIM model. ‘Dynamic’ in this case refers to the meaning of an active and changing system. With time, decisions and actions likely alter the properties of each dimension – thus, the shape of an arrow.

Presenting the model as a system embodies the idea of a set of elements connected together to form one entity, thereby showing properties that are properties of the whole, rather than properties of its component parts (cf. Hanseth, 2000). A system might be defined as a coherent set of interdependent components that exists for some purpose, has some stability and can be usefully viewed as a whole (Checkland & Scholes, 1990). The interdependen components in this case are the individual dimensions. The purpose is to efficiently support the general M&A objectives expressed by dimension A. The effects from M&As are long-lasting; swells sometimes have impacts even after 10 or 20 years, and as long as these effects prevail, so does the relation between IS integration and M&A.

The DySIIM has properties as a whole, such as resource use, lifetime and impact on business. It has also, or at least could have, impact on other dynamic systems of IS integration in M&A. That is, which architectural principles are used to realise integration will have impact on the future integration initiatives in accordance with the path dependency of emerging information infrastructures (Hanseth, 2000).

## 5.3.2 Model application and impact

With the use of the model, it is possible to see how changes in one dimension might trigger and enable changes in other areas that, in turn, trigger or enable new changes that trigger or enable still other changes. All cases include elements that indicate that the matter works as a system where all dimensions are dependent on others. The Kléber case illustrated how a shift in intentions towards more collaboration altered the agenda. In the Dynaflex case, the development of the IS integration capability enabled an enterprise-wide solution that actually led to more integration than was needed. For CRP, the idea of preservation and only pursuing some of the potential synergies meant that synergies could be leveraged by only integrating infrastructural IS, which, in turn, limited the organisational integration possible but decreased the risks of resistance among employees. Finally, in the Chase-Walton case, we saw how the clear differentiation of the degree of importance between information flows as a result of a proactive approach to IS led to activities and systems being integrated differently.

The DySIIM model should be regarded as an initial model that, in many aspects, requires further research to increase validity. One way to enhance the model and further validate it would be to, through methodology for design science research, restate its explanatory elements to design propositions and design exemplars that could be introduced to practitioners and then be evaluated based on the usefulness in managing IS integration in M&A. Conclusions from this exposure could then be used to further reinforce the existing explanatory elements.

In the learning partnership with Trelleborg, we used the model to reconsider and reshape the way Trelleborg managed IS integration in their M&As. With the model, an IS manager who faces a merger or acquisition has an outline of managerial decisions that have to be considered during the process, e.g. which integration architecture to use. The associated Table 6 gives information on some of the consequences of the decisions. In the case of the integration architecture, the model suggests, among other things, that an enterprise-wide architecture might damage a preservation strategy and that if it is necessary to integrate transactional IS, middleware and point-to-point architectures turn out very complex. In line with the ideas of engaged scholarship, this knowledge is abstract to its nature and has to be contextually interpreted by educated and experienced IS professionals. IS integration in M&A is too complex to ever be boiled down to a cook book-like recipe. Yet, the use of the model enabled our partner Trelleborg AB to understand, restructure and improve how it could work with IS integration in its M&A.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS, TRENDS AND GENERAL RECOMMENDATIONS

Using a theoretical framework and four case studies, this paper has described and explained IS integration issues, decisions and actions that management has to consider in their M&As. Our findings are integrated in the DySIIM model that depicts a system of IS integration and M&A process attributes. In our cases, the system was dynamic and changed over time as changes in one attribute triggered changes in another attribute, which, in turn, triggered new changes, and so on.

## 6.1 Theoretical and practical contribution

Apart from the theoretical framework that integrates existing research on IS integration in M&A and the four cases in an area where still very little is known, this paper’s main contribution is the DySIIM model with the associated explanatory tables. The model can be seen as an elaborated starting point to build cumulative knowledge on the influence of IS integration and the M&A process. Current research on IS integration and M&A is scattered and inconsistent in terminology and conceptualisations. Consequently, it is difficult to compare, contrast and integrate research findings in the area. The comprehensive approach that the DySIIM mode represents enables a cumulative knowledge process. Much of the model’s theoretical value lies in its comprehensiveness that enables positioning of existing and future research findings in the scattered field. Additional value is in the findings that IS integration and the M&A process forms a dynamic system where the final shape is not the result of an initial design but of an evolutionary process. These dynamic and systemic characteristics are not described in the literature.

With the four cases studies and the subsequent evaluation, we demonstrate that the basic constructs of the model are useful for explaining how decisions on IS integration influence and are influenced by the M&A context. Herein lays an important contribution to practice as demonstrated earlier with the use in our learning partnership. Trelleborg also used the model to develop a learning and improvement strategy, considering the possibility for improvement in each of the models dimensions to proactively improve the IS integration capability and thus facilitate future M&As.

## 6.2 Related trends and future research needs

Researching IS integration in M&A is similar to hunting for a running target as both business and technologies for IS integration constantly evolve. The recent financial crisis changed overnight conditions for business. For some companies, quick divestments of unprofitable units or mergers to enable cost cuts and synergies became a matter of existence. For better positioned companies, opportunities for acquisitions arose. Our cases show that a company’s ability to deal with IS integration issues is an important constituent of the overall ability to deal with M&A. The TIH unit enabled by their renovated IS infrastructure and improved competence smoothed the integration of Dynaflex. In Trelleborg and many other companies, M&As have become an integrated part of the companies’ growth strategies. As such, the learning and improvement processes related to IS integration become highly important since the companies need to improve their capacity to integrate acquisition targets. In some companies, this capacity seems to be a core competence and a means to gain a competitive advantage (Carlsson & El Sawy, 2008). An example is the global Mexican cement company, Cementos Mexicanos, which is known for being able to incorporate acquired companies into its existing operations fast and effectively. This paper has not addressed the learning dimension of IS integration in M&A, but the framework used to collect empirical data contained aspects related to improvements and learning that will be accounted for in a subsequent paper.

Our research identified 23 relations between different parts of the system. Although we searched for differences in our case selection, there are still many attributes, and combinations of attributes, in the framework that were not covered. In addition, our research approach with interview-based case studies was better suited for finding some relations than for others. The approach only generated a superficial view of the intentions and employee reactions to the M&A. To fully grasp this side of an M&A, an ethnography-inspired study would probably be necessary. Consequently, a limited number of relations connecting dimension C to the rest of the DySIIM model were identified. Further extensions of the model should in particular focus on intentions and reactions when developing new relations. The four cases are from Trelleborg AB, but they represent different value configuration models (Stabell & Fjeldstad, 1998). TES is a value chain with the value creation logic of transforming input to output. TSS is a value shop with the value creation logic of solving customer problems. Stabell & Fieldstad's (1998) third model, value network, was not represented by the cases. It would be useful to validate the model in this context, e.g. by studying a merger of two banks.

Not only the business aspects of M&A, but also the technical aspects of IS integration are continuously evolving. Some of our 23 identified relations will, with time, be irrelevant as the practices that they describe fade. On the other hand, new practices will require revisions o attributes in the model and perhaps also in the model’s dimensions. The development of industrial process standards (e.g. the Supply-Chain Operations Reference model) migh enable more standardised ways of dealing with IS integration in M&A. Similarly, the increased use of Web services, SOA, enterprise architecture, EAI and enterprise service bus is a trend that will affect the potential for IS integration. These and other developments will, from a technical view, make integration and de-integration easier to accomplish. Still, the managerial issues in the presented framework and model will be the same.
