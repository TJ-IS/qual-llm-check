# A framework for evaluating organizational choice and process redesign issues

CAROLE BROOKE

Faculty of Business and Management, University of Lincolnshire and Humberside, Brayford Pool, Lincoln LM6 7TS, UK

This paper presents a new framework for the evaluation of organizational choice primarily within the context of process redesign issues and the information technology support of those ideas. Through a process of scenario generation, the framework is designed to identify and evaluate choices in work organization and to make more explicit the implications that such choices might have for people and work roles. A central aim is to encourage the participation of different organizational stakeholders in the evaluation process. Process redesign has hitherto tended to be explored predominantly through software engineering and functionally driven approaches. The framework presented here offers a counterbalance to those works by offering an organizational perspective. It is based on a broad, scenario-generating technique which is  rmly rooted in the organizational vision of a broad range of interest groups. Some work has already been undertaken in this  eld. Previous research has demonstrated the value of taking a broader organizational approach to workplace technology redesign and shown that the involvement of a wide range of stakeholders during evaluation can result in some very creative, practical and productive solutions. Nevertheless, this paper identi es some potential weaknesses in that previous body of research and proposes a new approach. The author draws upon earlier work in order to develop a framework which will provide more guidance to stakeholders during the scenario-generating process and which will make even more explicit the values and assumptions underlying alternative choices. The framework enables an evaluation of those technological choices at a fairly deep level, whilst remaining relatively simple to apply. The new approach presented in this paper is currently being adopted by an EPSRC-funded research team exploring issues of the relationship between business processes and legacy systems (for details of the project, please see web site: www.dur.ac.uk/CSM/SABA). Future work will concentrate on producing further, industry-based case studies of its application and assessing its value when applied alongside traditional and technically focused software engineering methods of technological evaluation and development.

## Introduction

The inspiration for this research comes from years of teaching executive Master of Business Administration (MBA) students about the human and structural implications of information technology in the workplace. In reviewing existing theories and concepts, it became apparent that some of the material could be combined to form a more satisfactory approach to understanding the issues. Thus it was that the conceptual ideas of authors such as Zuboff (1988), Wittig (1978) and Harrington (1991) came to be combined with the more empirically focused works of Clegg (1993) and Scott Morton (1991) to form a practical evaluation technique that, nonetheless, gives serious treatment to the social and political values which are inherent in every organizational decision-making process.

The technique presented here enables organizations to identify for themselves choices in the application of technology, particularly (but not exclusively) information technology in the workplace. The choices are identi ed through a process of scenario generation involving different stakeholder groups across and at different levels of the organization. The use of scenario building in the context of technology is not new. It is an established qualitative methodology for generating future scenarios and can be used as part of a wider forecasting activity (e.g. see City University Business School, 1991). As Carroll (1996) noted: ‘. . . everyone speaks the language of scenarios, which can serve as a bridge to cognitive science or computer science jargon. A scenario-based design discourse helps to maintain a focus on usability and usefulness throughout system development, from requirements and envisionment to documentation and training’ (pp. 266–275).

Workplace technology and systems redesign have tended to be explored predominantly through software engineering and functionally driven approaches which have repeatedly neglected a range of organizational and human issues. For example, Clegg et al.’s (1996) study of systems developers and of the 15 different principal methods and tools in their use highlighted a number of neglected areas (Clegg et al, 1996). These were: the design of work organizations and the planning of organizational impacts, job design, the allocation of tasks between humans and computers and the usability of new systems. It is important, therefore, to incorporate human factors into the development of technology. This paper seeks to augment that incorporation and is based on the belief that technology results from the shaping effects of cultural, political and economic processes. In contrast, organizational practice still seems to be dominated by technology-driven change, a form of technological determinism, rather than change driven by social and psychological needs. This lack of attention to human issues has long been shown to have negative effects upon performance in the workplace (Pettigrew, 1975; Symon and Clegg, 1991; Clegg, 1993; Knights and Murray, 1994).

The sociotechnical approach to systems design, which attempts to incorporate social as well as technical issues and to give them equal status, is well established in the literature (e.g. the ETHICS method of Mumford (1986)). Despite these developments (which research has shown to be underused), in practice the technical issues still tend to override the social issues to the extent that they become marginalized. This situation is not coincidental but rather a product of the way in which our own social and educational systems are organized (Clegg, 1993), in particular the educational divisions between arts and sciences and professional training for functional specialisms.

## Clegg’s organizational scenarios tool

Primarily because human factors may be both intractable and highly contingent, it has been argued that it is dif cult to incorporate prior knowledge of them into the development stages of technology (Grudin, 1991). Clegg (1993), however, proposed that it was possible to apply practical tools early on in the information technology decision-making process in order to elicit in ‘real time’ an organization’s speci c human and technological needs (see also Eason, 1988; Sauer, 1993).

It is perfectly possible to identify a range of stakeholder groups which might be involved in an information technology development (Earl, 1989). Yet even where stakeholder groups are involved by management, it is often only during the development phase and not earlier in the evaluation process. Analysis and feasibility studies are considered the realm of the specialist or senior manager and not for stakeholder debate. This increases the tendency for decisions to be based purely on the interpretations of managers, who may have particular political agendas, and whose perspective is  ltered through the organization’s dominant logic (Hall, 1995). Other perhaps less powerful stakeholder groups may not be adequately represented (Ezzamel and Hart, 1987; Knights and Murray, 1994, p. 348). In contrast, the generation of organizational scenarios puts these different groups at the centre of the evaluation exercise. By way of example, a brief illustration is now given of a previously published organizational scenarios technique (Clegg et al., 1996).

The application of the technique is led by a facilitator (preferably someone who was not previously a stakeholder) and begins with a group analysis of the existing organizational scenario (which may or may not already involve technology) against eight criteria: boundary, vision, logic, structure, roles, costs, bene ts and risks (see Table 1). Clegg (1993) described the eight criteria as follows.

Boundary refers to the scope of the area under evaluation. It could be a particular department, for example or it could be the whole organization. In the example in Table 1, the sales function has been taken as the boundary. Vision is a global summary of how the organizational unit being evaluated exists. In Table 1, the description is based on an actual example of a manufacturing company in the north east of England. The sales department is a specialized area of activity and the organization is arranged along functional boundaries. Logic refers to the rationale which underpins and drives this arrangement. Selling is specialized because production has been centralized. The structure  ows from this. The role of sales is to support production because production sits at the heart of the business. In this example, the boundaries are clearly de ned. Work roles can be described as single skilled and functionally based. The major costs and bene ts are then considered and,  nally, the risks associated with this type of arrangement. The results of this preliminary description of the status quo are entered into the matrix as ‘scenario 1’.

The participants are then asked to form groups and use the same eight criteria to generate their own alternative scenarios. For instance, they might propose alternatives featuring increasing levels of restructuring and decentralization of control. However, the act of generating such scenarios is highly political and the tool does not explicitly address this aspect of the evaluation. It necessarily requires some self-analysis and some critical insights into the way the status quo operates so that sensitive handling of the stakeholder groups will be vital (Knights and Murray, 1994). It may not be appropriate, for example, to mix different stakeholder groups or levels of seniority when generating the scenarios. If more than one group is involved simultaneously in the exercise, then there will be more than one scenario produced during the  rst stage. Where there is disagreement between them, the plenary discussion can be used to conduct a post-mortem and, depending on the number of stakeholder groups involved, could be used to work towards reaching a better understanding of the different perspectives.

There is no need to reach a consensus before moving on to the next stage in the way that is required, for instance, by the Delphi technique (for a brief summary of Delphi see Georgoff and Murdick (1986)). The purpose of the exercise is to elicit different perspectives and encourage mutual understanding of them, not to make judgements concerning their validity or to remove variability. One effect of going through this process is the opportunity for managers and their staff to reassess the values which underpin their decisions. This is particularly important since, for a long time, the literature has argued that there is a lack of selfawareness and a need for the assumptions which underpin management goals to be made more explicit (Buchanan and Boddy, 1983; Bailey, 1993).

Clegg’s tool has been applied within different types of  rm (both service and manufacturing) and used to select an approach to work redesign. One of its major advantages seems to be that it encourages participants to think beyond simple mechanization or computerization of tasks to a more holistic picture where the purpose and nature of work is re-evaluated and technological solutions are not assumed (Clegg et al., 1996). The tool was designed to be fairly generic and to be applied across a range of organizational contexts rather than simply for the evaluation of technology alone. This may be one of the reasons why little consideration is given to the de nition of technology itself and why there is little discussion of the potentially different (and con icting) perspectives which people may have on the nature of technology, its use and its human and organizational implications.

Whilst it may be true that the resulting scenarios will, by implication, suggest certain attitudes to technology, the tool does not provide for an explicit analysis of these outcomes either by the participants themselves or by the facilitator. In fact, there is little explicit guidance on the generation of alternative scenarios beyond the formulation of scenario 1. This may be a particular weakness in the light of more recent developments in organizational uses of technology (such as the Internet) which could have a direct bearing on the scenario-generating activity. This is discussed below.

## The new framework

At the beginning of the paper it was suggested that the generation of organizational scenarios should support the elicitation of different (and perhaps con icting) views on the nature of technology and its use within organizations. Crucially, the opportunity should be taken to evaluate the values which underpin these different perspectives. Therefore, changes have been made to Clegg’s (1993) tool in order to achieve these aims better. This section considers these changes (see Table 2). Their application is discussed in a later section.

The  rst major change is the addition of a ninth criterion: ‘view of information’. Although at  rst sight this might appear to be a minor change, in fact it has a signi cant impact on the nature of the evaluation exercise and its outcomes for technological choice. View of information is a rather more conceptual criterion than the others and requires some detailed explanation. Reference to the work of Harrington (1991) is helpful here.

## Organizational choice and process redesign

According to Harrington (1991), an organization which appreciates only its more physical and tangible attributes (e.g. buildings, equipment and formalized hierarchies) can be characterized as adopting a ‘resource’ view of information. In essence, it re ects a rational view of organization. In contrast to this, a perceptual view of information recognizes the importance of the role of the individual. A ‘perceptual’ view gives more explicit emphasis to the less tangible aspects of organization (e.g. culture, beliefs and personal values). It is argued that this re ects a more phenomenological approach to organization. An organization’s preference for a resource view of information rather than a perceptual view of information (or some mixture of the two) will result in very different technological choices (see Table 3).

Information viewed as a resource is seen as independent of the receiver and little role is given to the individual in its reinterpretation. Consequently, information can be passed round the organization with the expectation that there is no legitimate change during its transmission. It has a consistent and predictable value. In contrast, a perceptual view would say that this is not information at all, but merely data. Put simply, the individual has a key role in the interpretation of data in order to produce information. Information is the result of a social process. Only data can be transmitted since information has no existence beyond individual perception. This sounds rather abstract until it is grounded in a consideration of the implications for technology deployment and use. An organization which appreciates mainly its own physical attributes will tend to adopt a resource approach to information. The distribution of information will be regarded more or less as the distribution of objective fact with little consideration of the scope for individual reinterpretation by the receiver. The technology itself will be viewed primarily from the physical consideration, too. Systems will be implemented to mirror the physical activities of the business and training will focus on the physical skills needed to operate the equipment rather than the need for analytical and intellectual skills.

In contrast, Harrington (1991) argued that a perceptual view recognizes the dangers of sti ing innovation with rigidly designed technology which fails to recognize the role of the individual. From a perceptual standpoint, technological design has to be driven by user need because it recognizes the central role of the individual. Additionally, by emphasizing the importance of analytical and interpretative skills in technology use, this approach is suggesting that there is no corporate objective truth as such. Everything has a subjective element which requires human negotiation and interpretation. Interestingly, it was these sorts of skills which Zuboff (1988) stressed would determine the extent to which technology would provide added value to organizations. Technology must, therefore, be people focused.

Harrington (1991) believed that all organizations have some resource and some perceptual tendencies. The key point is that the deployment of technology in its role as a communications and structural conduit makes the tendency towards one or the other more intense and more critical. This argument is reminiscent of Zuboff’s (1988) discussion of technology rendering organizational practice more transparent, a phenomenon she termed the ‘information panopticon’; we will return to this below.

By polarizing views of organization and information in this way, Harrington (1991) was not arguing that the organizational world can be forced into one of these extremes. Rather, he proposed them as starting points for evaluating an organization’s general attitude towards technology. This is why his work can contribute to a more effective evaluation process. These are important issues for all stakeholders. By incorporating these issues into the scenario-generating exercise, the participants can explore possibilities within a broader context than they might otherwise have done. In triggering consideration of the organization’s attention to physical and virtual aspects, as well as its tendency towards a resource versus a perception approach, it is argued that Harrington’s (1991) concepts can stimulate an analysis of the values that underpin both the status quo (expressed as scenario 1) and the alternative scenarios subsequently generated.

There are diverse and interesting discussions in the literature concerning the nature of information production and use. These range from viewing it in terms of mechanistic production processes to symbolism and individual perceptions (Wittig, 1978; Nurminen, 1988; Zuboff, 1988). Nevertheless, Harrington (1991) argued that the dominant organizational world-view is based on a resource approach to information. The ways in which we evaluate technology tend to be located in a much more rational and tangible domain. The wider effects, particularly the social and psychological, are ignored because they impact on our lives in ways which are dif cult to capture. It is for this reason, he said, that the tremendous potential of information technology to transform organization is poorly understood and even less well translated into practice. Before we can appreciate the signi cance of this statement, we must  rst consider the nature of organizational transformation and its potential relationship with technology. This becomes clearer as we move on to consider other features of the new evaluation framework presented in this paper.

## Automate, informate and transform

Clegg’s (1993) organizational scenarios tool was designed such that the  rst scenario to be generated re ects the participants’ view of the status quo. They are then left to generate other alternatives. Although evaluation of an organization’s ‘view of information gives added form and richness to these, more could be done to guide participants. The pro les of ‘automate’, ‘informate’ and ‘transform’ have been devised to assist in the generation of further alternative scenarios and to clarify the different value sets that underpin them. The terms automate, informate and transform have become relatively well established in the literature, but some explanation is needed here of what they mean and the values that they might imply particularly with respect to human factors. A useful guide was given by Cash et al (1994): ‘When information technology substitutes for human effort, it automates a task or process. When information technology augments human effort, it informates a task or process. When information technology restructures, it transforms a set of tasks or processes’ (p. v).

The drivers of ‘automation’ and its substituting effects have been debated in the academic literature ever since the industrial revolution. So, too, has the question of whether or not it is a good thing. Technological determinists have argued that technology drives change and society (e.g. Freeman,

1987), whereas social constructivists have interpreted technological developments through the lens of social relationships. Some have seen automation as offering freedom from non-productive activities, while others have seen it as degrading, even threatening to the human condition (Rosenbrock, 1981). Although discussion of the wide range of perspectives on automation presented in the literature is beyond the scope of this paper, it is important to recognize that automation, in itself, does not imply a certain set of values. These are contingent upon the way in which the automation is introduced and its effect upon the perceptions and work organization of those involved. The most important consideration is that the impact on human factors is fully evaluated when considering the potential for automation. The point is well made in an article by Rosenbrock (1981), that whilst it may be possible to automate a particular task or function fully, it may not be sensible to do so and he warned of the values that underpin such a narrow focus on the role of technology. There are connections here with Harrington’s (1991) resource view of information.

The term ‘informate’ is generally credited to Zuboff (1988) and her book In the Age of the Smart Machine. Informating potentially has both positive and negative outcomes for stakeholders, depending on the context. For example, she discussed the nature of work with information technology as ‘intellective’ and as requiring higher order skills. Information technology, she said, produces information as a by-product and this information can have value-adding qualities for the individual and their role within the wider organizational context. Realization of this value is made at the individual level. In this, she connected with Harrington’s (1991) perceptual view of information.

She described the use of information technology as leading to an information ‘panopticon’, a sort of gold- sh bowl where organizational worlds are rendered more transparent to more people, more of the time (Zuboff, 1988). Whilst this could have bene cial effects (such as management making their motives more explicit) it could equally be viewed as a ‘big brother’ scenario, where everyone is watching everyone else and there is no sense of privacy or mutual trust. Even where the effects may appear to be bene cial, they may have less positive unforeseen consequences. A good example of this is given in a case study by Mueller (1991) where the Canadian Government’s Department of Informatics introduced an e-mail system. In this case study, the organization introduced a system which it thought was ‘harmless enough’ and yet had a signi- cant impact on the organization’s structure and job roles, leading to unforeseen con ict, mistrust and eventual loss of jobs. This is a classic case of an organization failing to appreciate the perceptual aspects of

Transform became an established term following the work of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) 1990s research team in the USA (Scott Morton, 1991). The basic argument is that it is very tempting to apply information technology in order to achieve ef ciency through automation (and sometimes it is also the simpler route) but that long-term bene ts only come from applying it in a way which literally transforms the business. The de nition of ef ciency here is based on Bedeian and Zammuto’s (1991) ‘doing things right’, in contrast to effectiveness which they de ned as ‘doing the right thing’.

The MIT researchers based their ideas on Leavitt’s (1965) diamond and viewed transformation as the consequence of change in  ve areas (technology, individuals and roles, structure, management processes and strategy). However, these changes are dependent upon the appropriate prior preparation. The conditions for its achievement include, for example, the alignment of corporate and technology dimensions, heavy investment in human resources and the willingness of management to change its own attitudes. Indeed, the preparatory stages could be viewed as the greater challenge. Thus, transformation in this context means a deeper structural change than the informating scenario. Whereas informating might involve the restructuring of jobs and roles, a transformation involves a re-evaluation of the very nature and purpose of the business itself. It, thus, follows that the boundary of the organizational unit under evaluation may change (probably expand) when considering organizational transformation (see the discussion of the criterion in Table 1).

## Applying the new framework

The three pro les of automate, informate and transform should be explained to the participants by the facilitators of the scenario-generating exercise. It should not be assumed that an organization’s approach to technology will exactly mirror one of the three pro les. Nevertheless, these (which are in essence) stereotypes have a useful function. They speed up the scenario-generating process and, when plotted against the dimensions given on the vertical axis, provide a substantial foundation for generating and evaluating other alternatives. No upper limit is assumed but the participants are usually encouraged to generate no less than three additional scenarios of their own, labelled as scenarios 2, 3 and 4, etc. An example of such alternatives is given in the section on empirical work below.

Before empirical material is presented, it is important to note that the MIT team would consider a move from automation through informating to transformation as progressive. The implication is that the further along this route the organization travels, the more likely it is to be successful. However, this is a dangerous assumption. Despite some rhetoric concerning the role of people, the emphasis of transformation is on process. There is minimal analysis of the potential social and psychological effects of technology. This does not mean that such an analysis cannot be undertaken, simply that it was not explored in any depth by the MIT research team. Given that the objective of the modi ed framework presented here is to place the human implications of technology at the centre of the evaluation, stakeholders should be encouraged to identify both the positive and negative effects of all three pro les and articulate their views on these. A succinct characterization of each of the three pro les in Table 2 is now presented.

An automate approach to technology is based on the principles of the substitution of human labour and cost ef ciency. The organization’s vision will, therefore, tend to be limited (hence, the narrow boundary of the evaluation) to a view of the business which maximizes technological control and streamlines work processes. There will be a tendency towards specialization. Physical resources will be stressed: labour, machinery,  nances and so on. Bene ts will tend to be measured on a short-term basis which is focused on reductions in overheads, labour and increased management control. The participants in the scenariogenerating exercise should be encouraged to explore these issues and to clarify their views on automation as they apply to their own speci c contexts.

An informate approach to technology is based on the principles of augmenting human effort and helping individuals to add value through the application of intellectual skills. The rise of cross-functional activities and the ability to see relationships between different parts of the business and to have more of an overview of the work context will enable individual workers to identify and anticipate new business opportunities and novel ways of working. Job expansion, quicker response times and improved market sensitivity are some of the characteristics that de ne the informate pro le. The vision of the business has moved beyond specialization and tightened control to an individualized approach which pushes more of the decision and control mechanisms out from the centre and closer to the customer interface (note that the boundary of the evaluation has been expanded to include customer-facing departments).

A transform approach to technology takes the informate pro le to its extreme. The structure of the organization has become much more diffuse and dif - cult to identify physically. Staf ng and groupings are constantly changing to re ect the requirements of the business. Indeed, the very nature and purpose of the business itself requires re-evaluation (the boundary of the evaluation is now the whole organization). An increase in specialist contract staff may occur alongside the multiskilling of core staff so as to maximize the organization’s ability to anticipate and respond to change. The organization becomes fully networked in its use of technology so that relationships within and between organizations can be maintained. Management’s role has become one of coordination rather than direction and control. The business needs to be very pro-active in its approach to the market-place and, therefore, innovations in structure and roles as well as in products and services come to the fore.

All three pro les have apparent advantages and disadvantages and every organization will possess some of these characteristics to a greater or lesser extent. The issue is not that one of these pro les should be seen as a solution in itself but that the options it begins to open up trigger a fuller debate and bring critical issues out into the open. In particular, it introduces stakeholders to different approaches to information production and highlights their different implications for technological design and use.

## Empirical work

Both Clegg’s (1993) original tool and the newly developed framework were applied with four separate cohorts of MBA and MSc students at two different universities between 1996 and 1998. A total of 62 students were involved, the largest cohort being 25. All were organizational employees at various levels of seniority and from different cultural backgrounds. The exercise with each cohort took half a day (approximately 5 h). Personal work experiences of the students as well as examples from the author’s and from Clegg’s (1993) own research were used as case studies to try out the two different approaches. Given the class sizes, only one facilitator was needed working with groups of a maximum size of six people each. Approximately one-third of the time was spent explaining the two approaches and two-thirds of the time applying them. In each case the original tool was presented and applied  rst, followed by the new framework for comparison.

When applying Clegg’s (1993) tool, all groups experienced some dif culty distinguishing between the eight criteria whilst attempting to capture the status quo. However, with some guidance from the facilitator, they were able to generate their own scenarios. When the newly modi ed framework was explained and the three stereotypical pro les presented, the participants made quicker progress. As a result of this experience, several of them commented that it might be useful to present the three stereotypical pro les before evaluating the status quo. They felt this would help to speed up the process further. However, they noted that it might also in uence their approach, for instance encouraging them to make premature decisions about their organization  tting into one or other of the three pro les. For this reason, the decision was taken not to amend the framework at this stage of the research.

The main advantage of the new framework over and above the original tool was reported by the participants to be extending the scope of their thinking (‘creativity’ was mentioned several times) and to lend more substance to the evaluation than did the original tool. In addition, they reported a keener awareness of the implications of their decisions within the groups, which had a material impact on stakeholder discussions. More attention was given to the human implications of the scenarios. Where groups consisted of individuals with very different approaches to the view of information (i.e. resource versus perception), the debate about the implications of technological choice became sharper and more focused. When the groups reconvened to share their scenarios in a plenary, these debates led not to a consensus of opinion but to a consensus on the nature of the process. That is, with the new framework, the dominant values driving the resulting choices were much more exposed and more likely to be acknowledged by all participants, whether or not they actually agreed with each other about the outcomes.

The participants expressed a recognition that, even if their preferred scenario was not selected by the group, at least the thinking behind it had been recorded so that if, at a later stage, hindsight demonstrated that an inappropriate choice had been made, their arguments could be more easily resurfaced and revisited. Many commented that the new framework had been straightforward to apply and that view of information was a useful concept because it helped to bring them each to a deeper awareness of the dominant values within their own organizations. Overall, the exercises indicated that the framework could be explained and applied within a manageable time frame and met the primary objectives of the research, i.e. to provide more guidance to participants whilst remaining relatively simple to apply, to make more explicit the assumptions underpinning technological choice and enable evaluation at a fairly deep level whilst promoting a people-focused approach.

## Organizational choice and process redesign

Table 4 presents an example output from one of the workshops using the new framework which included experts from the  nancial services industry. The case example used here was corporate banking in a particular bank, leading to a re-evaluation of the clearing sector in the UK. The  gure shows the mapping of the status quo as scenario 1 and (having considered the three pro les of automate, informate and transform) the generation of three alternative scenarios. A brief discussion of the group’s thinking during the exercise then follows. At their request some details have been adapted so as to preserve the anonymity of the companies and participants involved.

In analysing the status quo (scenario 1), the group noted that technological changes are eroding the competitive advantage of many banks, yet it seems to have taken the industry by surprise. Their vision and structure remain largely unaltered – focusing on centralized control and resource-based views of information. In addition, banks are not suf ciently aware of the restructuring of relationships which accompanies technological innovation. This is particularly apparent in that many are still unwilling to place key decision makers physically closer to clients. The group argued that banks are no longer only in the money market but also in the information market, a market which they believe the German banks have been exploiting for years.

Scenarios 2–4 are the alternative scenarios.

In scenario 2, the information  ows brought about through on-line accounting, intranets and other technological innovations have ‘informated’ the bank– corporate relationship itself but many technological initiatives still risk failure. As was noted by a survey in The Economist (1997) In the past technology merely automated the process of  nance, everyone assumed the existing model was the right one and that information technology would make life more ef cient.

The group argued that this lack of innovation has resulted in a technological race within the banking community at the expense of personal interaction between client and bank. They gave the ‘dehumanization of banking’ as the main reason for failure, coupled with inappropriate measures of technological investment, such as ROI (return on investment). Research has suggested that such short-term critera are inappropriate because the returns on relationships are long term and dif cult to quantify, though ultimately of more importance (Gupta and Colins, 1997). Therefore, whilst the banking community appears to be investing in technical infrastructures, the investment in tacit knowledge to provide the interface with the client of the relationship is neglected. Overall, there is a mismatch of goals with practice.

Many of the procedures involved in corporate banking can be automated. However, the assumption that almost all services can be mapped into a technical, virtual framework reveals the already prevalent focus of banks on replication, a feature often associated with bureaucracy. Trust, management quality and sincerity, though essential to partnership alliances, cannot be codi ed for an anonymous user. The high tacit knowledge content of managers in the early years before centralization provided the foundation of client relationships. This tacit knowledge content itself cannot be replaced by technology. If this were possible, then the competitive advantage of the banks would, indeed, be destroyed by the very nature of replication itself. Perhaps more crucially, whilst the technology is able to provide a tailor-made, one-toone communication channel between bank and client, it cannot replace the communication of tacit knowledge which stems from having an informed and recognized human being on the end of the telephone or e-mail terminal.

As research at MIT has demonstrated, information technology investments are usually dependent upon heavy investment in work reorganization, particularly personnel and training (Scott Morton, 1991). Whilst the banks have been quick to respond to the technological race and employ individuals adept at collating market information, they still suffer from a skills and training gap. The marginalization of human and social factors has been held accountable for the failure of many re-engineering initiatives in the  nancial services industry, a view which Clegg’s (1993) earlier research also highlighted.

By scenario 3, the realization had dawned on clearing banks that competitive success cannot be forged with microchips. Rather the roles of managers are augmented and decentralized into closer working relationships between bank and client. The greater need for human-to-human contact appears to be moving banks further towards a participative and longterm lending strategy, with information technology facilitating this process. By way of comparison, the group considered the German banking system, which has remained decentralized and devolved, opting instead to complement the high tacit knowledge content of local empowered managers and consultants, with a signi cant amount of codi ed data relating to customers and their markets. The group noted that before a series of banking crises and recessions had hit the UK economy (during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries) this method of local, close-tocustomer operation was commonplace within the UK.

Scenario 4 was described by the group as ‘unexpected prior to the exercise’ and ‘unnerving for clearing banks’. The recon guration of the bank–client relationship through information technology empowers (previously) more subordinate and smaller individual organizations. This empowerment may, in the absence of close relationships between bank and business, allow clients to switch electronic cash instantaneously. Furthermore, the empowering capability of information technology may enable  rms to source  nance using the Internet, conceivably from literally anyone who is willing to lend it. Retail cyberbanks are already a reality. The group felt that the best chance clearing banks have of avoiding this scenario is the power of their brand, but even this is a short-term solution. With the Internet providing a secure medium (people readily give out credit card numbers by telephone, which is arguably less secure) for credit ratings to be outsourced, the lending reputations of cyberbanks may be quickly established.

Scenarios 3 and 4 demonstrate that the future of the clearing banks is certainly not secure within the corporate market. Indeed, the market appears to be driving a move towards partnership banking. Hand in hand with partnership banking comes the empowerment of those individuals who were originally bereft of power over (and, hence, of control over) their actions. This applies as much to the ‘old school’, locally knowledgeable employees of the pre-centralized banking era as it does to their clients. The advent of electronic commerce may well strip the banking community of all its traditional power base and fundamentally alter the perception of the ‘cap in hand’ approach, if such a thing still exists (see the discussion in Maruf (1997)).

The ability of information technology to transform banking to various extents is highlighted in scenarios 3 and 4, but the group felt that a resource-based view of information and a mechanistic approach to information technology would constrain the banks’ ability to change. The tendency towards a process-oriented view of work is characterized by the overarching belief that almost all value-adding activities can be transformed into a virtual framework and will, thus, ultimately become more ef cient. This mind set only serves to reinforce the old fears of loss of control rather than to reinforce the relationships that characterize partnership banking as practised in Germany. The group’s conclusion was that UK banks have yet to recognize the more humanistic aspects and the particular needs of a ‘virtually managed relationship’ which scenarios 3 and 4 would offer.

Whilst it was clear that information technology could bring about the organizational transparency typical of close working relationships, the technology itself could not be the driver. It appeared that nothing less than a complete change of mind set amongst the clearing banks was required.

## Conclusions

Perhaps the greatest management challenge presented by this method of evaluation is the simultaneous letting go (being explicit about values) and taking part (alongside a variety of political stakeholders). Above all, a critical approach is needed. Conducting what amounts to a self-evaluation is not an easy task and it may be tempting to avoid issues which make stakeholders feel uncomfortable. If, as has been argued, established methods of evaluation are biased towards tangible and technical issues and away from perceptual and humanfocused issues, then it seems likely that the mapping of the status quo will reveal a tendency towards a resource view of information (in Harrington’s (1991) terms). The implications of this potential bias for technological implementation have been outlined (see Table 3). The new framework presented in this paper contributes to redressing this imbalance by exposing the values underpinning different choices and by centring on human factors in the development of technology.

One advantage of the new framework is that it can be developed in use and facilitators of the exercise should encourage the participants to tailor the framework to their needs where appropriate. For instance, the costs, bene ts and risks criteria could be modi ed to re ect the organization’s own project pro ling methods, and criteria could be weighted.

Different approaches to the production and use of information remain largely unexplored at the level of management practice, particularly with respect to workplace technology (Zuboff, 1988; Harrington, 1991). This paper makes a contribution by developing a framework which gives those different perceptions a central role in the decision-making process. By encouraging organizations to consider different organizational scenarios and their underlying values and assumptions, decisions around technological choice can be made in a more explicit and informed way. Finally, the outputs from the exercise form a valuable archive of an organization’s decision-making processes and provide opportunities for more in-depth and critical postimplementation reviews.

The new framework is currently being adopted by an Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC)-funded research team exploring the issues of the relationship between business process change and legacy systems. Future work will concentrate on producing more industrial case studies of its application and comparing its contribution to systems evaluation and development alongside more traditional software engineering methods.
