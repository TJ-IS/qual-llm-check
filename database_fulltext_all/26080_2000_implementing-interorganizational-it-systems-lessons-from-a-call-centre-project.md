# Implementing interorganizational IT systems: lessons from a call centre project

DAVID BODDY

Department of Management Studies, University of Glasgow, 59 Southpark Avenue, Glasgow, G12 8LF, UK

The growing power of computer–telephony integration (CTI) systems is encouraging many companies to create call centres. These deal with a growing range of business processes and, in doing so, can be used to challenge established organizational arrangements. The range of human and management issues that need to be dealt with has yet to become clear. Some insights into these are offered from a study of one call centre over 2 years, from shortly before its physical introduction to the present day. These empirical observations are set within the wider literature on organizational change and information technology (IT), particularly the processual and integrationist perspectives. The paper uses the evidence of the case to elaborate the integrationist model so that it re ects the areas of human action more fully. It concludes by outlining the implications of this perspective for managers seeking to build effective call centres or other forms of interorganizational IT system.

## Introduction

Organizations are clearly at a point of revolutionary change in their ability to gather and use information. The convergence of voice, data and broadcasting devices (telephones, computers and televisions) and the development of ever-more powerful communication networks open up boundless prospects for data transmission. It has never been easier or cheaper to transmit data and information around and between businesses. Technological developments mean that new possibilities will continue to emerge. Institutional developments (such as trade liberalization) will encourage suppliers to incorporate these possibilities into attractive commercial applications.

Much of the commentary on the business implications of these technological developments has a distinctly deterministic  avour. Even well-informed and carefully researched works such as those by Hammond (1996) and Cairncross (1997) give this impression. They portray a world in which technology independently and inevitably has consequences for individuals, organizations and society.

Yet experience warns us that few technological changes turn out as expected. Empirical work such as that by McLoughlin and Clark (1994) and Boddy and Gunson (1996) show how the results of technological innovations have an emergent rather than a planned character. Management has some goal in mind when they initiate the investment process, but the outcome several years later is usually quite different. Part of the reason is that projects often incorporate recent and often incomplete hardware and software. The project itself represents an intervention in an established organization. Given the systemic nature of organizations, that disturbance is likely to set off unpredictable ripples. Finally, the change is usually being introduced into a business that is itself part of a volatile environment, so that what needs to be done changes as the project itself takes shape. It should be no surprise that the results of information technology (IT) projects differ from expectations.

These considerations are likely to apply with at least as much force when organizations introduce IT systems which cross their boundaries. These are likely to involve changes in the behaviour of customers or suppliers. This will magnify all the change management issues which arise in a single enterprise and add some more. The issues to be handled will be more complex in themselves and the processes of change within and between separate organizations will need to be managed coherently. This paper studies the speci c case of call centres, which are now widely used to link organizations with off-site staff and customers.

The paper draws mainly on a 2 year study of an organization that has been introducing a call centre. The next section outlines the distinctive features of call centres and their growing use. The third section considers the models of change that may help us understand the problem and which have informed the research. The fourth section presents the case study and the following section interprets this in the light of the integrationist model. The  nal section puts the study in a broader theoretical perspective and suggests some practical implications for those managing boundary-spanning IT changes.

## Call centres – what they are and what they do

Modern call centres combine customer information systems with telephony systems handling a conversation. This increases the information about that customer which the person handling the call has available to them during the call. Centres typically manage large volumes of calls though they vary in the facilities they offer. Examples include the following.

(1) Telesales (Direct Line, Royal Bank of Scotland and Abbey National).

(2) Help desks (BBC Radio Helpline and British Telecom).

(3) Contact  eld service staff (RAC, British Gas and Cable Tel).

(4) Dealer support (Suzuki and General Motors).

(5) Account enquiry (Midland Electricity and TSB).

(6) Reservations (Forte Crest Hotels and Virgin Airlines).

(7) Direct response advertising (use of 0800 numbers).

Call centres are made possible by automated call distribution (ACD) technology which automatically feeds incoming telephone calls to available agents according to a set of predetermined rules. This allows calls to be routed deliberately and provides on-line information about operators’ performance and current status. As a by-product it can provide comprehensive management information about centre activity and performance.

Many call centres now use computer–telephony integration (CTI) systems, in which the telephone system is integrated with the supporting IT systems. This can greatly increase the value of the system as a selling tool, as information about the customer can be presented on a screen as staff receive their call. It can also move customer details automatically from one operator’s PC to another when a call is transferred.

Many centres operate continuously and take all calls for an organization or one of its services, irrespective of the location of the customer. Staff receive or initiate calls through a head set, enter information directly on to their PCs or terminals and respond to the customer. How staff conduct the conversation with the caller clearly affects the quality of the transaction, which in turn affects wider business performance.

Call centres are complex organizations, the successful implementation of which requires management action in several areas. At the very least, they are likely to require a signi cant change to the internal processes required to deliver the service promised by a telephone contact. However, the issue may be wider than that and perhaps different in nature. To understand just how much wider and different, this paper considers two issues.

The  rst is how much attention to pay to external parties who use the system to contact the organization. Wide-ranging internal change may not be enough. For the systems to work effectively customers may also need to behave differently. Sometimes the change is trivial – ringing a central 0800 number rather than a local of ce. The change becomes less trivial if for any reason the response from staff answering the 0800 number and the subsequent service is unsatisfactory. Since this is the  rst and perhaps only link with the customer, severe damage may be done if the transaction fails to meet customer expectations. Moreover, ensuring that external callers receive a good response, both to their call and throughout the transaction, depends on planning and implementing other changes within the organization.

This leads to the second issue which is about the extent to which the  nal form of the call centre can realistically be planned in advance. An apparently simple change such as centralizing the point of customer contact appears to send unforeseen ripples around the organization. While some may be unproblematic, experience suggests that others will cause dif - culty and perhaps set off other ripples. How all these consequential issues are (provisionally) resolved will affect the nature of the call centre and the business bene ts that it produces. Yet at present we know little about the processes by which such interorganizational projects emerge and develop (Doz, 1996). The case study may add to that knowledge when set within a wider model of organizational change.

## Models of IT and organizations

The empirical research for this paper was informed by processual (Dawson, 1994) and integrationist models (Kimble and McLoughlin, 1995) of change. These view major change as a non-linear, interactive process in which outcomes are shaped by the way people interpret and act on their contemporary and historical contexts. Kimble and McLoughlin (1995) contrasted the integrationist approach with technological imperative and strategic choice models.

Writers in the technological imperative tradition, from the early work on computing by Leavitt and Whisler (1958) to recent predictions about the Internet (Cairncross, 1997), treat technology as a relatively independent in uence on people or organizations. Organizations more or less inevitably adopt available new technologies to survive in a competitive economy (Hammond, 1996).

The strategic choice model places more emphasis on the in uence that human beings have on the technology. People shape what science develops and how this is applied and used. Managers have minds of their own, as do the people who work with and for them. They decide which technologies to develop, promote and install to further their interests. Others decide the details of how they will use or react to it – again in the light of their interests. In this view, technology is a dependent variable, re ecting the interplay of forces within and around the organization. The effects are uncertain, as they are heavily in uenced by human choices (Macdonald, 1985).

The integrationist model – referred to as the structurational model by Orlikowski (1992) – builds on these approaches. It proposes that the results of IT investment depend on the interaction of both technology and people over an extended period. Information systems are both a product of human action and an in uence on human action. People initiate, design and use an IT system. Designers physically construct the technology in the light of management priorities and expectations. Users then react in some way – welcoming, rejecting, learning to live with or deciding which features to use, ignore or adapt to suit their way of working. In doing so they socially construct the technology in the sense that, with continued use, these human choices become established features of the operation. They become taken for granted as the accepted way to work. They then shape later human action – as when it becomes customary for professional staff to use keyboards in a way they would have vigorously resisted a few years before.

This reaction between the people and the system continues inde nitely. The attitudes and actions of people change and so does the form of the technology. This continuing interaction means that the results eventually obtained are likely to be quite different from those which the people promoting the change originally expected. ‘Technology does not impact on its social environment, or vice versa but, over time, each shapes the other’ (Kimble and McLoughlin, 1995, p. 58).

The integrationist model implies that people can modify technologies throughout their existence – from design through to continuing use – as people and technology interact.

Orlikowski (1992) also introduced the idea of the ‘interpretive  exibility’ of IT. This is the (variable) degree to which users construct the form of a technology as they work. In this view, an IT system is not a  xed, given piece of technology. It alters, sometimes beyond recognition, over its lifetime. She suggested that to understand how a business can realize these opportunities, we must attend to the main human agents involved (designers, decision makers and users), the nature of the physical technology that people use in their work and to the institutional properties of the organization (such as its culture or power relationships).

Figure 1 illustrates this relationship between IT and organizations. It proposes the following.

(1) Technology is the product of human action during design and use (a).

(2) Technology can facilitate or constrain human action (b);

(3) Human action is shaped by the institutional properties (or context), leading to different interpretations and uses of the technology (c).

(4) Human action with technology also shapes the institutional properties of the organization such as its culture or the distribution of power (d).

Since many IT systems now cross organizational boundaries, the utility of the model in understanding and managing current innovations may be enhanced by more explicitly considering the in uence of the external context. Indeed, most analyses of organizational change now routinely emphasize the in uence of the historic and contemporary context (Pettigrew 1985, 1987; Clark, 1995). Implementation occurs within the historic context of the organization, which in uences management action. Past decisions shape the business as it is today and affect how easy it is to change. The past affects the receptivity of an organization to change and its ability to change (Pettigrew et al. 1992). It affects how people react to proposals.

The contemporary context has both internal and external dimensions. The former includes current structures and cultures, as well as the prevailing distribution of power (Pfeffer, 1992). It also includes simultaneous changes taking place elsewhere in the business – a new IT system is usually only one of a cluster of parallel changes. The latter includes technological and competitive developments amongst customers and alternative suppliers. We therefore suggest re ning the model by adding a factor which explicitly recognizes this external context: the context shapes human action (e) and in turn the institutional properties of the organization (a and g). The context is itself partly shaped by human action (f) (see Figure 1).

Thus, we approach the case study with six questions suggested by these theoretical perspectives. How did human action shape the evolution of the call centre technology during design and use (a)? How did the call centre facilitate or constrain human action (b)? What institutional properties shaped the way people interpreted the call centre (c)? What changes did people make to the institutional properties of the organization as the change evolved (d)? Did events in the external context shape human action, technology and its institutional context (e, a and g)? Did human action help shape the context (f)?

The use of a single case study as the basis for the analysis re ects the nature of the issues under discussion. We are posing ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions (Yin, 1989), exploring how the organization introduced a particular form of IT, and why it was done in a particular way. The issues within these questions are still unclear, as there have been few academic accounts of the introduction of call centres. Moreover, our model of organizations implies that how the players deal with the issues will re ect their interests and the context in which they work. Therefore, we needed to collect information in its natural setting to try to understand the nature and complexity of the processes taking place (Benbasat et al., 1987). This approach, particularly when carried over an extended time, allows us to trace the evolution of the innovation and the interaction between the application and its context.

Initial access was negotiated through the chief accountant of the company in 1996. Over the following 2 years the author visited the call centre on  ve occasions, during which time interviews, lasting approximately 1 h, were held with the company’s general manager, industrial relations manager, call centre manager, IT director, the manager of one of the branches who subsequently moved to one of the new ‘superbranches’ and the chief accountant. Shorter discussions were held with four members of staff working in the call centre. The account emerging from these discussions was presented to the interviewees and revised slightly on points of detail in the light of their comments.

## Case study

## Introduction

Contracting Services (a pseudonym) maintains and repairs refrigeration equipment for retailers throughout the UK. In 1997 there were 22 service branches and 250 service engineers. Each branch employed a manager, a working supervisor and three or four administrative staff. The branches operated quite independently on most matters and were treated as distinct pro t and loss centres. The only advantage of being part of a larger company was that they had greater buying power. Trading conditions were dif cult. Entry was easy and many small local suppliers had led to overcapacity.

The typical process was that the manager of a supermarket would call the nearest branch to report an equipment fault. The telephone operator in the branch would take the call and pass the message to one of the  eld engineers through a pager. The engineer would  nd a telephone and call the branch. The branch controller would give the details of the call and the engineer would visit the retailer, typically a grocery store or supermarket. If the parts were in the van, he would do the repair. If not he would return to the branch or visit a wholesaler to collect the part. When the job was complete the engineer would complete the paperwork and pass it to the branch (usually on his next visit) for processing. Invoices and performance monitoring data would then be prepared in the branch from this paperwork.

As well as high branch costs (approximately £1.4 million a year excluding the cost of the engineers), customers found the service inconsistent between branches and by time of day. Branch managers varied in the performance data they provided to customers (which was expected by national chains). The pager system caused communication problems – so much so that some service engineers had bought mobile phones at their own expense. After of ce hours calls were passed to answering machines and then to an outside agency who contacted standby engineers. Inventory was hard to locate. An independent survey showed that customers regarded the service as poor.

In late 1996 the company won a facilities management contract with a major supermarket group for 44 of their stores (approximately one- fth of the total). The facilities covered were all the electrical, plumbing and mechanical facilities within a store – lifts, doors, heating, lighting, etc. Faults in these service had previously been dealt with by the store manager telephoning a local contractor. The supermarket group decided that they would prefer to have all these matters dealt with by a single contractor. Although Contracting Services experience was in refrigeration, they decided that this would be a valuable diversi cation and bid successfully for this contract. It meant that their staff would now deal not only with refrigeration, but with all other mechanical and electrical facilities in the stores. Small competitors were unable to offer such a comprehensive service and the company was keen to make a success of this initiative.

## Phase 1 – design and piloting

The IT director, amongst other managers, had been considering the possibility of using call centre technology for some time. He had won some support from the board for his idea, though they were reluctant to make the investment. The annual running costs would be approximately £0.5 million for a centre with 32 agents. The facilities management (FM) contract (covering not only refrigeration but other mechanical and electrical services) had been running for approximately 6 months, handled through the branch network. The customer now wanted to be able to phone one number for all services and was keen on the call centre. This turned the argument in favour and the board decided to set up a pilot call centre to deal only with this contract. As the IT director commented ‘The concept was already being discussed within the company but the FM contract switched the argument in favour’. If the pilot was satisfactory, the board would consider extending the system to the whole business.

## The pilot

The branch network was seen as costly and the board was attracted by the substantial cost savings they would gain if they eliminated it. The IT staff developed the system, with little input from the service side of the business. The call centre manager reported to the IT director. He recruited staff from within the business and arranged their training. The call centre went live in February 1997. Four staff took calls from the stores and gathered as much information as they could using a script – a list of prompt questions. They recorded the replies on screen and when the call was complete, transferred the information electronically to the despatchers – four experienced service staff familiar with the work involved. A further group dealt with administration and invoicing.

A short survey was conducted by the chief accountant approximately 2 months after the centre went live, to assess progress. The store managers were generally impressed with the attitude of the operators, though some had experienced dif culty with engaged lines. Some contacted the engineer directly rather than going through the call centre. Only two of the store managers had been noti ed by their company of the change to the call centre and many were not advised of the telephone number until they called the usual branch. Only one had had their opinion of the change sought by the super-market company’s senior management (though they had been fully involved at all stages).

The survey also asked 15 engineers for their views. Two-thirds felt communication had improved – others had problems with engaged lines and the accents of the agents. Only half felt that they had received a knowledgeable response from the operators, who did not appear to understand basic technical issues. Although an informal survey, this contained warnings of some issues which would later arise much more forcefully.

## Phase 2 – 1 year later: from pilot to full implementation

The board was satis ed with the pilot and decided to extend the call centre concept rapidly to cover all aspects of the business, not just the FM contract. Their clear aim was to close branches, centralize administration and operate an electronic monitoring and reporting system. This extension took place in the autumn of 1997. All the engineers were issued with Apple Newton computers which were linked to the call centre by a mobile phone. It had a quarter-size screen and could take voice mail or data. The job instruction could be sent to the engineer, who could print the job sheet, read the instruction and do the work. They could record what they had done on the Apple Newton and send it to the call centre electronically.

The call centre developed rapidly and the company soon closed 17 of the 22 branches. In December 1997 they also won a contract from the supermarket chain for another 45 FM stores and for 132 stores from another chain in a smaller facilities management deal.

However in early 1998 they changed policy and stopped closing branches. There were several problems. The most alarming was that they quickly began to lose a signi cant proportion of the traditional, non-FM business. As a former branch manager pointed out: ‘Closing branches hurt us because although 75% of the business is now with the supermarkets the other 25% is quite pro table’. One reason was that, by then, staff in the call centre were used to taking FM calls, following a detailed script. This was not the right way to deal with a call from a small retailer, who was calling in and being asked questions that did not mean anything to them. The system was too complex for those customers.

In addition, the success and growth of the FM work meant that the number of calls from this source was greater than expected. Although the call centre staff were now familiar with the system they were unable to cope with the volume of calls. Customers had to wait an unacceptable time before their call was answered. The call centre manager observed that ‘we did not expect to be doing 5000 jobs a month for FM. The business has simply grown very rapidly: taking on a second region, although good business, knocked us back again.’

Thus, from the traditional (small) customers’ point of view, the effects of the new technology were unwelcome. They typically experienced a delay in getting through and when they were connected, staff asked unnecessary questions. Some were concerned about the cost of the call when they heard an unfamiliar accent – even though it was a freephone number. Many turned to local service companies.

Communications with the small customer was a big problem. We were under pressure to save costs but we have realized that we made a mistake in not going to the customer base and selling the idea to them properly. We did not sell or explain the idea to customers. So when the customer rang the call centre and got a poor response he did not bother to call again but went to one of the independent service engineers. We could have sold the bene ts better. We did not take our branch managers or our customers along with us (call centre manager).

The company also found that they had lost some local control of the engineers. In the past the customer could ring back and ask where the engineer was. The branch manager could quickly  nd out and keep the customer informed. Now if the customer rang back all the call centre staff could say was that the call had been dispatched. They did not know where the engineer was or what they were doing.

The phone bill rose rapidly. The engineers now used the mobile phones to talk to each other between jobs. Part of this was to exchange task-related information. A substantial amount was also believed to be social chat which previously took place when they met one another at the branch: ‘Now that they are isolated they use the mobile to keep in touch with each other and that proved very expensive’ (former branch manager). The former branch manager also acknowledged that:

We went too fast in trying to close the branches without  rst getting the management and supporting processes in place to control the mobile workforce without a physical branch. We did not de ne the job and we lost control of our engineers in terms of where they were and what they were spending their time on.

The change in structure had had major effects on the management of the branches. The supervisor in effect became a working engineer again. The branch manager (now working from home) was expected to concentrate on generating new business and had to restructure how they worked. They felt insecure and no longer had a physical place to report to. They had line responsibility for the performance of their engineers but did know what the engineers were doing or where they were – staff in the call centre had dispatched them. Most had never seen a laptop computer: instead of encouraging the engineers to overcome dif culties they were dismissive of all the changes: ‘The branch managers who were being displaced by the new system were able to have a very demotivating effect on both the staff and customers’ (chief accountant).

## Phase 3 – Recovery?

Faced with the loss of an important part of the business, management quickly realized that they had taken a wrong turning. In March 1998 the service director reached retirement age. His replacement made two signi cant structural changes in May 1998.

First, he put a new middle management structure in place (the call centre manager observed that ‘cost pressures from senior management had led us to miss out that step’), by retaining a smaller network of  ve regional branches, known in the company as superbranches. The call centre would still take all calls, but the super-branches would be responsible for managing the engineers. The call centre received the call using the prepared script and then passed the details immediately to the relevant super-branch. Staff there allocated the call to an engineer and managed the transaction from then on, including invoicing. The call centre retained the task of managing the performance statistics which were a critical part of the relationship with the FM customer.

The second change was that the service director ensured that the call centre manager would in future be responsible to him. The call centre thus became a service function, supporting the relationship with customers. The IT department became an internal supplier to the call centre.

In conjunction with these developments the call centre manager changed the structure within the centre. At  rst staff at an inbound desk took the calls and those at the outbound desk dispatched engineers. As call details were now sent directly to the superbranches the manager decided that the inbound call staff should be trained to dispatch some simple calls themselves. These were mainly where there was a single route to a subcontractor (for example, any call relating to forecourt petrol pumps is sent to company X). Conversely, as the load on the dispatch desk diminished, they would begin to take inbound calls. The manager preferred inbound calls to last longer – the structured script on the screen was intended to ensure that staff gathered all the details they needed about the fault before passing it on to the branch and the engineer.

More broadly, the company acknowledged that it was now managing two different types of business: the traditional small retailer and the large supermarket chains with whom they were building FM work. The growing FM work prompted structural changes within the company. At  rst calls from the FM customer had been handled by whichever engineer was available in the local branch. The FM customer then asked the company to provide a dedicated service, so 11 engineers were allocated to this, working for them at all times. Their employment contracts were renegotiated in conjunction with the customer, who wanted to ensure that service staff were on contracts that would provide adequate cover for the stores.

FM is a very visible in uence on the company, a very dynamic management style. The link to them means that we are more customer focused. Certainly the FM deals are changing the nature of the company. We are now getting into the mind set of (the FM customer). Before we were just a fridge company and that was the mind set. The people now in facilities management are different – they know what the customer wants. As far as facilities management goes the call centre is working (call centre manager).

## Interpretation in terms of the model

This paper has explored the application of emerging call centre technologies from the perspective of the integrationist model of technology and organization. Two aspects of interpretive  exibility have been highlighted – the interaction between technology and the external customers of the organization and the emerging, iterative nature of the project as a whole.

The case con rms the inadequacy of technological imperative models. From that perspective, the application of the call centre technology would have gone according to plan, customers would have used it as expected and branches would have been eliminated and the planned cost savings achieved. The reality was about as far from that as can be imagined. The technology itself has continued to evolve (tighter scripts, the retention of some physical branches and the transmission of information to branches), as has the way it has been used (some customers rejecting the system and engineers using their mobile phones to compensate for lost personal contact). Faced with the practical dif culties in operation, management has deliberately adapted both the technological and organizational dimensions of the system.

The case also shows the limitations of the strategic choice explanation. Clearly the organizational actors did make choices on design and use. Senior managers chose to invest in the call centre. Engineers used their mobiles for social as well as task purposes. Yet in other important respects the scope for choice was constrained. A new and important FM customer made it clear that they expected the company to build a call centre. They later sought a dedicated group of engineers, separate from the rest of the staff. Pro table traditional customers started to take their business elsewhere, which prompted the company to re-establish a (smaller) branch structure which mediated the link between the call centre and the engineers. In these areas management had little choice about the decisions to take.

The account is much more consistent with the notion of interpretive  exibility which stresses the interaction between users, technology and institutional contexts. The system clearly emerged (Mintzberg, 1994) from a series of interactions between external and internal actors, focused on a speci ed set of dimensions. The events can be summarized in terms of the questions posed at the start of the case.

How did human action shape the evolution of the call centre technology during design and use (a)?

(1) The IT director promoted the idea to the board.

(2) The IT director and staff developed the original system.

(3) The new supermarket (FM) customers encouraged the decision to pilot.

(4) Traditional customers took their business away, prompting change.

(5) Engineers used the mobile phones to compensate for lost personal contact.

(6) Management abolished and then partially reinstated the branch structure.

(7) Management decided to send call information from the call centre to the super-branches.

(8) Alienated branch managers did not encourage use by engineers or customers.

How did the call centre facilitate or constrain human action (b)?

(1) It was facilitated by enabling engineers to provide an improved service to FM customers, and generating more rapid and accurate performance data.

(2) It was constrained by closing branches, reducing face-to-face contact between engineers, less management control of engineers, diminishing the role of branch managers and offering a less responsive service to small customers.

What institutional properties shaped the way people interpreted the call centre (c)?

(1) The IT director’s promotion, design and initial responsibility for the system detracted from a service focus.

(2) The diminished branch managers’ role meant they did not encourage staff or customers to make the system work.

(3) The new service director’s reinterpretation of the centre as a source of service bene t led to the introduction of super-branches.

What changes did people make to the institutional properties of the organization as the change evolved (d)?

(1) The service director partially reinstated the branch structure.

(2) The board changed the reporting relationship of the call centre manager from IT director to service director.

(3) The call centre manager changed working structures within the centre.

(4) The service director dedicated some service engineers to FM work, with different contracts.

(5) Management recognized that the company now had two separate and distinct businesses.

Did events in the external context shape human action, technology and the institutional context (e, a and g)?

(1) The board’s concern with costs shaped the initial design (branch closures) and contrasted with customers’ concern for service.

(2) Supermarkets with the FM contract exerted pressure to open the call centre.

(3) The supermarkets awarded extra FM business, partly due to the service from the call centre, which unexpectedly slowed call response times.

(4) Small customers exerted pressure to adapt the original system to their needs.

(5) Supermarkets with FM contracts required the company to allocate staff exclusively to their work, with different conditions.

Did human action help shape the context (f)?

(1) Management persuaded the board to change their policy on branch closure.

(2) The decision to use the call centre technology supported the growth of FM business.

(3) Management were willing to develop a closer relationship with FM customers.

(4) Management considered the structural implications of two increasingly diverse businesses.

This analysis provides clear evidence of the signi - cance to the project of forces external to the project and to the unit in which it was implemented. The conclusions are entirely consistent with the integrative model, but add to it by emphasizing the external pressure behind the interpretive  exibility.

The case also shows the scope for interpretive  exibility which exists in a project of their nature. With a still novel technology, internal ripples from the change and an uncertain customer base, the outcome was dramatically different from that which the board had in mind when it initiated the pilot programme. At the close of the study period, the links with the FM customers were still developing rapidly. As already noted they were expecting different relationships with the service engineers. This was leading to changes in the focus of their work and potentially to changes in their contracts of employment. Even more widely, managers were considering whether to extend its FM competency by offering such services directly, rather than using contractors. This was raising new questions about the strategic positioning of the business.

## Practical implications

Two implications for practitioners arise from the evidence of this study, relating to the signi cance of external links and the scope for interpretive  exibility. As technological developments are increasingly applied to support links external to the  rm, it becomes crucial for management to focus on these external ‘users’. In the rush to implement a new system, perhaps for cost or other pressures from higher or more distant levels of the organization, it is easy for the interests of this critical group – customers (in this case of different types), suppliers or other agencies – to be ignored. The danger then is that their needs or requirements are taken for granted or misunderstood. This company clearly acknowledged their omission and took steps to rectify it.

The study also shows the scope for interaction between technology, institutional context and people. Few if any of these interactions were anticipated or planned for in advance. It is hard to see how they could have been, given the novelty of the systems, their application and the volatility of the wider environment. This does not mean that management are helpless bystanders. Some interactions happened spontaneously as staff adapted the system. Others happened as management chose to intervene in a particular way in the stream of events – sometimes for the better, at others for the worse. They needed to be continually vigilant as to the progress of the project, the nature of these external interactions and the emergence of new issues which may have required their attention.

Moreover, the change was continuing to evolve – it was not a discrete project, completed within a speci ed period of time. Management needs to be prepared for the long time-scale of such projects and the many diversions and interruptions on the way. Cross-organizational IT projects are most accurately seen as a continual process, in which the outcomes continue to evolve from the pattern of interactions this paper has described. Those responsible for such innovations need to be clear in their own minds and, ensure that others share their perception, that a project of this sort will not be followed by a new stability, but by persisting instability.
