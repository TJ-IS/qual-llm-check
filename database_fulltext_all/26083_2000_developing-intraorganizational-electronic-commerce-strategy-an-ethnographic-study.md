# Developing intraorganizational electronic commerce strategy: an ethnographic study

BRIAN J. CORBITT

Department of Information Systems, University of Melbourne, Parkville, 3010 Australia.

This paper presents an argument for qualitative research and the use of ethnography in information system (IS) research using a detailed investigation of the types of strategies used in implementing electronic commerce in a corporate  nance institution. The paper describes how these strategies were developed and why and models those responses. This paper con rms other studies which argue that certain key elements must be in place to ensure the successful implementation of information technology (IT) or electronic commerce. However, this research has highlighted the fact that there are important in uential issues operating at another level within the organization studied. Politics and the power of vested interest groups within an organization and managerial misunderstanding about what electronic commerce is created unrealistic expectations of the innovation by management.

## Introduction

This paper reports on an investigation of the process of implementation of electronic commerce in a major corporate ‘ nance institution’ (the  nancial institution involved has not been identi ed because of security issues and ongoing research commitments). This investigation searched for a detailed, rich understanding of the barriers to implementing electronic commerce and the processes used to overcome those barriers and implement various electronic commerce technologies successfully. Sustained research reported in the management information systems (MIS) and information systems (IS) literature suggests that there is significant understanding about the levels of success and failure of IS implementation within business organizations (Ginzberg, 1981; Barki and Huff, 1985; Davis and Olson, 1985; Raymond, 1985; Cheney et al., 1986; Zmud, 1986; DeLone, 1988; Montazemi, 1988; Davis, 1989; Kydd, 1989; Cragg and King, 1993; Lacobou et al., 1995; Poon and Swatman, 1998; Parr et al., 1999).

The failure of IS implementation has been linked to the absence of an IS champion or change agent (Parr et al., 1999), lack of management support (Ginzberg, 1981; Kydd, 1989), strain on already restricted managerial time (Cragg and King, 1993), poor attitudes towards IS (Ginzberg, 1981; Corbitt et al., 1997), the absence of education and training (Cragg and King, 1993), organizational problems (Stroeken and Coumans, 1998), technical problems (Cragg and King,

1993), and perceived gaps between the expectation of IS supporters and those expected to use the system (Kydd, 1989).

On the other hand, research has also shown that success in implementing IS or MIS in business organizations is more common than failure (Kydd, 1989). As an organizational analogy, success in implementing intraorganizational IS is attributable to a number of success factors. These include organizational commit ment (Chan and Swatman, 1999), the existence of an executive sponsor within the organization (Raymond, 1985; DeLone, 1988), the existence of an operating sponsor within the organization to provide quick feed back across the organization (Montazemi, 1988), the existence of dedicated facilities within the organization (Corbitt et al., 1997), software and hardware support by suppliers and frequent access to software and hardware developers by users, fast iteration of software changes throughout the organization, the existence of training, outputs from the new system meeting managerial expectations and a positive evaluation of the costs and bene ts of the information process being used.

Cragg and King (1993) suggested that the imple mentation of information technology (IT) in small businesses occurs most successfully where there is a demonstrated relative advantage in terms of time saved, bene ts accrued or discomfort decreased and where competitive pressure could be addressed as IT was seen as an ‘enabling technology that could make the  rm  exible and pro table’ (p. 53). This research also highlighted the positive role that managerial enthusiasm together with an external consultant could play in the successful implementation of IT in small  rms.

This literature has applicability to existing research on the success and failure of adoption of electronic commerce (Husein and Moreton, 1996; Auger and Gallaugher, 1997; Mason, 1997; Kang, 1998; Bell, 1999; Poon and Swatman, 1999). Corbitt (1997) suggested that there are two common issues which indicate the success or motivating factors and those factors which appear to inhibit the implementation of, in this case, electronic commerce in business organizations. These two themes are uncertainty and equivocality. Uncertainty refers to the absence of information and equivocality refers to the existence of ambiguity and the existence of multiple, con icting interpretations about a given organizational situation (Kydd, 1989). The resolution of uncertainty depends on knowledge and information as this equips the manager or potential user of an electronic commerce form with the power to deal with and/or understand that process. Equivocality suggests that some state of confusion exists and that, as a result, solutions require discussion and debate suggesting ‘social interaction so that intuition, judgement, and beliefs can contribute to the enactment of a shared interpretation’ (Kydd, 1989). Daft and Lengel (1986) suggested that the interaction of equivocality and uncertainty in organizations can provide a useful model for understanding the important role of information in the adoption and implementation of IT.

Cragg and King (1993) discovered that the strongest inhibiting factors for small  rms were lack of IS knowledge, lack of managerial time, poor support and limited  nancial resources. ‘Lack of IS knowledge’ can be assumed to be a derivative of uncertainty and even equivocality. If management is not made aware of new and enabling technology, then it is not surprising that they are unwilling to adopt electronic commerce. If management has been given information from two parties which is con icting in nature, management would rather postpone implementation or even shelve it altogether; equivocality. ‘Limited  nancial resources’ could also be a derivative of ambiguity and equivocality, as the  gures within  nancial reports do not necessarily re ect reality within the  rm. A ‘lack of managerial time’ re ects a  rm where leadership is weak or virtually non-existent and, thus, suggests an atmosphere of ambiguity within staff members in the lower ranks (Kong, 1999). The latter are unsure about management’s intentions and future plans with electronic commerce. In a study of why small businesses are on-line, Poon and Swatman (1999) found that, whilst management support was evident, the small companies were faced with the inhibiting facto of having to pay exorbitant charges for access to ‘banner’ advertising. Although, confronted by such an ambiguous and equivocal issue, it was encouraging that those participants were still willing to stay connected to the Internet.

Corbitt et al. (1997) suggested that motivators are essentially addressed by a demonstrated relative advan tage in terms of time saved, bene ts accrued or discomfort decrease and where competitive pressure could be addressed by IT, which is seen as an enabling technology which makes the  rm  exible and prof itable. Cragg and King (1993) further suggested that the owner should be enthusiastic towards the tech nology. Being so, they can demonstrate ‘relative advantage’ to the other stakeholders. Poon and Swatman’ (1999) study of governments’ push on small businesses to adopt Internet commerce  ts in with Corbitt et al.’s (1997) study. It is possible that governments perceive global competitive pressure and are using Internet commerce ‘as an enabling technology’ to make their economies ‘ exible and pro table’. On the other hand, in addressing the inhibitors, knowledge and information must be available to managers and potential users. This knowledge and information equips them with the power to deal with and understand organizationa processes, discussion and debate. Thus, intuition, judgement and beliefs can contribute to the enactment of a shared interpretation, eliminating equivocality.

Chan and Swatman (1999) noted that management involvement is imperative for the success of electronic commerce implementation. Emmelhainz (1992) and Roberts (1995) further suggested that top management is the most in uential group affecting implementation success. Chan and Swatman (1999) documented the impediments (inhibitors) and adopter/driving factors in the implementation of business-to-business electronic commerce in one of Australia’s largest raw materials manufacturing companies. However, Chan and Swatman (1999) suggested that the lower rungs of management, particularly those with direct supervisory accountability and responsible for the implementation, is the most crucial element in ensuring success. Organizing training and awareness programmes to make staff conversant with what was happening had a signi cant effect in improving understanding of systems and technologies, which in turn led to greater acceptance of the implementation. Assisting customers by providing technical assistance, even performing a cost–bene t analysis for them, may form part of a proactive strategy in marketing the concept of electronic trading. However,  nancial barriers when dealing with small and medium partners were dif cult to overcome due to the relatively high costs associated with the start-up and ongoing activities.

Chan and Swatman (1999) found that the inhibiting factors for electronic commerce include inadequate management support, lack of technical knowledge and support, lack of understanding of technology and technical and software obstacles at the early stages of implementation. In addition, they noted a number of management and business problems including changing work practices and culture, which, due to the implementation, later emerged as important and relevant issues. The immaturity of the industry standards and coaxing customers into getting involved were complicating factors. Initial concerns about security, speed and timing of delivery and message capacity made the organization cautious about both the new media and about changing business culture and resistance to change due to the complexity of the applications adopted. The importance of managerial support in implementation was argued by Parr et al. (1999) for ERP systems and by Duchessi et al. (1989) for IT projects.

Adopter and driving factors can be identi ed under a number of categories. Internal in uences are those which occur within the organization and exist in the form of misguided conceptions, lack of knowledge and even political modus operandi. The latter in uence originates from outside the organization and sometimes it is uncontrollable. In order to move a step forward, as much uncertainty and equivocality should be resolved within the organization. One way, as suggested earlier, is to demonstrate the advantages and bene ts gained, addressing the motivating factors. Most importantly, management play signi cant and valuable roles in in uencing implementation success. They have to be there to provide support, both technical and emotional, to staff in the implementation. Last, but not least, those in charge of overseeing the entire process have to be convinced about the project’s necessity and usefulness and be enthusiastic about the whole idea.

The success or failure of electronic commerce within an organization can be summarized as a response to both internal factors driven by critical issues (Camp and Sirbu, 1997), such as managerial response and organizational culture and by external factors (Thong and Yap, 1994; Thong et al., 1996, 1997) such as the need for competitiveness and adding business value (Figure 1). Equivocality and ambiguity on the part of management within organizations directs the push and pull factors towards each other as the levels of uncertainty about the impact of adoption occurs. To enable a better understanding of how this process of electronic commerce strategy development occurs the researcher adopted an ethnographic approach.

## Ethnography, IS and research methodology

This paper reports on an interpretative ethnographic study based within accepted research principles within IS (Walsham, 1993; Klein and Myers, 1999; Corbitt and Thanasankit, 2000), of a planned strategy for elec tronic commerce implementation.

Interpretive research methods have not been used widely in IS research (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). Such an approach assists the researcher in gaining more understanding about human thought and action in social and organization contexts. Interpretative studies also enable the researcher to be able to gain deeper insights into IS development (Klein and Myers, 1999). Walsham (1993) argued that interpretative studies emphasize those theories which suggest that the world is socially constructed. Interpretive studies try to gain some understanding of that reality. This study also aims to develop a deep understanding about Thai systems analysts and their clients’ behaviour during requirements engineering and attempt to gain more understanding about why they behave and act the way they do. This can best be achieved by employing an interpretive research approach (Gibbons, 1987; Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). This study seeks to understand how the electronic commerce adoption process was in uenced by the context of the organization (Walsham, 1993). The study also recognizes that those engaged in the process are constrained by various forms of social, cultural and resources limitation (Klein and Myers, 1999).

Research of this kind is used to answer questions about the nature of phenomena with the purpose of describing and understanding the phenomena from the participant’s point of view (Leedy, 1997, p. 104). Qualitative research studies may be done using various methods, including ethnography, using a case study approach. According to Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) and Klein and Myers (1999), the case study research method is the most widely used qualitative research method in IS research. Benbasat et al. (1987) and Klein and Myers (1999) argued that the method is particularly appropriate for the study of IS develop ment, implementation and use within organizations. Qualitative research is used to answer questions about the nature of phenomena with the purpose of describing and understanding the phenomena from the participant’s point of view (Leedy, 1997, p. 104).

This is a study in which the researcher participated in an organization over a period of time watching what happened, listening and recording what was said, asking questions and collecting data to try and explain social interaction throughout policy implementation (Hammersley and Atkinson, 1983). Ethnographic research takes the researcher close to the ‘reality’ of people’s lives (Becker and Geer, 1960). It allows the researcher to develop theory from observation and practice (Hammersley and Atkinson, 1983) and to test theory (Ball, 1981). Ethnography, as both the stimulant and evaluant of theory, requires contextualization. Essentially, this means that, through participant observation data, the researcher is attempting to generate data from the perspective of the individuals being studied (Wagner, 1990).

Four features which are common to ethnographic studies (Burgess, 1985) formed the framework of the study. Firstly, as a researcher, the author participated in the natural setting of the process or problem being studied. In this way, insight into the participants accounts of the process was gained. Secondly, ethnographic studies are  exible and change as the circumstances warrant. Ideas can and in this case did change and this meant that the conceptual nature of the research itself needed to be reformulated throughout. Thirdly, this piece of qualitative research was as much about the observation of social process as it was about the search for meaning given to the process by the participants in it. Finally, Burgess (1985) proposed that, in qualitative research, data collection and analysis occur simultaneously and, as a result, it is dialectical in that theory emerges from the data rather than from the imposition of theory on data. Such a dialectic approach has developed throughout the study.

Ethnography has variously been de ned as an attempt to describe and then interpret social situations or events (Hammersley and Atkinson, 1983), as the science of cultural description (Wolcott, 1975, p. 112) and as ‘thick’ description which locates the multilayered signi cance of events within their social contexts (Geertz, 1973, p. 63). Dorr-Bremme (1985, p. 68) proposed that ‘constitutive ethnography’ is a method by which group members generate and sustain organized social relations. In its various forms, ethnographic research seeks to describe one, some or all of social processes, historical change, social structures or social change. At various times ethnography also seeks to describe human interaction, group interaction, anthropological change, culture, religion, politics, economy or the environment. This study is premised on the view that the subjective elements of human social action must be a prime element of a study of electronic commerce implementation. This study will attempt to research differing constructions and perceptions of the policy to increase the use of electronic commerce throughout a corporate  nance institution in an attempt to describe what happened throughout the development of the strategy for implementation and develop some understanding of what happened.

## The research process

This research began with an invitation from the consultant engaged to facilitate the implementation of electronic commerce in an organization. During the consultancy, the researcher observed the process in operation and interviewed the key players in the process. These interviewees included the  ve member of the electronic commerce group within the organization and the management team (four senior staff) they reported to within the organization. All of the interviews were recorded and transcribed and reviewed by the interviewees for accuracy. During meetings the researcher took notes and recorded statements from the participants about the processes and issues being raised and addressed in the meetings. This interview process took approximately 4 weeks in total with numerous meetings spread over a period of some 5 months.

Following this process, the story observed by the researcher was written and checked by both the consultancy company involved and the organization. The result is an agreed accurate account of the implementation process and the issues raised during that process.

The ethnography reported in this paper is necessarily brief but those critical and signi cant parts of the story which developed are reported with quotes which re ect the intent of the story’s meaning as it has been interpreted by this researcher. It is important to note that this same data and the story constructed would be subject to different interpretations and recon struction by other researchers.

## Implementing electronic commerce in a corporate  nance institution – telling the story

## Part 1: developing the strategy

The process of the development of an electronic commerce strategy in a corporate  nance institution began in 1994 with strategic decisions made over 2 years before this study began (1996) in order to develop and implement software to allow and encourage companies within Australia to move to electronic document exchange and reporting. Subsequently, the development and promotion of electronic commerce throughout Australia encouraged the establishment of an electronic commerce group within the corporate  nance institution and the desire on the part of the electronic commerce manager and his staff to enhance the electronic commerce capabilities of the corporate  nance institution further. For reasons mostly to do with internal corporate  nance institution politics, to be discussed later in this paper, the electronic commerce group of the data division of the corporate  nance institution decided to engage a consultant to assist with the development of an electronic commerce strategy. The consultant’s draft report was reviewed by staff of the data division of the corporate  nance institution and was adopted for implementation. This process is illustrated in Figure 2.

Until the introduction of the consultant this development process had, in the eyes of those involved, been logical, and planned from the institution’s viewpoint. In their view it was apparent that the development process had the characteristics reported in many other systems’ development processes. It was structured and logical (Wu and Wu, 1994; Fertuck, 1995) and, in this case, it was shown to have been an iterative process (Corbitt, 1997), ensuring that there was regular feedback throughout the development process and from the delivery of the draft strategy until its adoption.

To meet certain Australian corporate legal demands, the corporate  nance institution established a data division to provide a corporate database, deliver and disseminate information from the corporate database and collect revenue on behalf of a government. The information division’s  rst initiatives in electronic commerce were the development of a number of electronic commerce interfaces to databases using the World Wide Web (WWW) including LIMIT to receive and store company annual returns, SANDOWN to enable company searches and THEATRE to facilitate the exchange of information with corporate watchdogs in the  nance industry (these are contrived names because of the con dentiality issue). In addition, the data division, through another WWW interface program called REPPAGE, established a storage/retrieval facility for all formal paperwork sent by companies to the  nance institution.

To facilitate further development of the use of electronic commerce within the corporate  nance insti tution, the data division established four criteria to be met within the electronic commerce strategy (source: internal ‘ nance institution’ document ‘Electronic Commerce Strategy, Information Division, “Finance Institution”’).

(1) To assist the corporate  nance institution in developing a more comprehensive and accurate database.

(2) To assist the corporate  nance institution in improving and extending the comprehensiveness of its services and improve the quality and timeliness of the service provision to the public.

(3) To realize ef ciencies in the collection of revenue.

(4) To position the corporate  nance institution in order to take advantage of future developments in electronic commerce and gain access to government services.

When asked about why this strategy was developed, the Electronic Commerce Manager replied that ‘The issue of the future is critical . . . we are now in the transition phase . . . in the next 12 months [The Director] wants to move us all to a more electronic form . . . he wants to add value’.

The rest of the Corporate Finance Institution see us as a cash cow . . . if there are opportunities they will always rob us . . . its very political indeed (staff member 1).

As part of this strategy it emerged that the electronic commerce section of the data division felt that the way to achieve the best result was to use an external consultant to not only improve the impact of LIMIT but, importantly, to develop a more diverse use of electronic commerce throughout the corporate  nance institution. It is at this point that the research project began.

## Part 2: introducing the consultant

The electronic commerce section of the data division of the corporate  nance institution engaged a research centre from a university and a consultant (ECOM Solutions) to undertake the development of an electronic business strategy. This decision was identi ed in a brie ng document to the consultant. The electronic commerce group identi ed that ‘there is a desire within the Division to adopt appropriate electronic commerce infrastructure that is governed by the necessary standards to provide maximum  exibility’ (electronic commerce manager).

In addition, in the  rst meeting with the consultant they noted, that the following change drivers were signi cant in developing an electronic business strategy for the institution as a whole.

(1) Reduced budget allocations.

(2) Providing a single logical entry point to the division was desirable.

(3) Increasing client expectations – more, quicker and better.

(4) Options for different (document) lodgement channels being available.

(5) Widening the purview of the corporate register.

(6) Provision of free access to the database.

(7) Expansion of the broker network.

(8) Placing the name index into the public domain.

(9) The development of a data integrity project.

(10) The possibility of introducing electronic prospectuses.

(11) Sharing data with other government organizations in order to improve operational de ciencies.

When asked by the consultant why these features were important change agents, the manager of the electronic commerce group replied that there were interna reasons why they had to do this (reported above) and that the general manager of the data division ‘wants us to move very strongly to having a greater role for brokers and for retailing of our information . . .’. Noting the importance of the client suite of the corpo rate  nance institution, another participant from the electronic commerce section added that ‘the manager needs to come up with strategies to manage us through the process . . . we need them to take on new technologies . . . we are dealing with conservative groups out there’ (staff member 4).

The problem had been that the adoption of use of the LIMIT electronic lodgment system had been ‘slower than we thought’ (electronic commerce group manager). Another staff member added that ‘we have to  t in with the information direction – have to provide the director with a database as an economic product in the market-place . . . make the data base more commercial’ (staff member 2).

Following the  rst meeting, the consultant summarized the purpose of the electronic business strategy as a policy.

To assist the corporate  nance institution to develop an overall electronic commerce strategy which will determine three to four major areas of focus for further electronic commerce implementation. The strategy will take a short-term (next 12 months) and longer term perspective (next 3 to 5 years), and will contribute to the achievement of the objectives of the corporate  nance institution business plan over the same period.

Following a series of three meetings over 4 week discussing the needs of the proposed electronic commerce strategy, the consultant then wrote a draft document for institutional discussion and modi cation. This process took another month and a  nal version of the document was accepted some 8 weeks after the introduction of the consultant.

## Part 3: identifying the barriers to implementation

As part of the ongoing process of developing the electronic commerce strategy for the corporate  nance institution, the researcher asked the corporate  nance institution participants to identify those features and issues which would act as impediments to the development of an electronic commerce strategy, its adoption by the corporate  nance institution and its implementation. This process was conducted in parallel with the meetings with the consultants, in parallel with the development of the draft policy and in parallel with the process of adoption of the policy. This happened over the same period of 8 weeks.

The electronic commerce group staff of the corporate  nance institution identi ed a large number of factors which they believed had impacted on the development of the policy and which would impact on its implementation. These factors can be categorized into two groups.

(1) Internal in uences within the corporate  nance institution.

(2) External in uences out of the control of the corporate  nance institution.

## Internal in uences

There was an obvious concern that electronic commerce was not fully understood within the institution and that people had various expectations of what electronic commerce may or may not deliver. For example, it was stated that ‘there is a view that electronic commerce will solve and deliver glory and god . . . but understanding of electronic commerce is like “I do not know Kevin” . . . in the absence of a strategy that “I do not know” version will be the prevailing view’ (staff member 3).

In addition, it became clear that there were other interests within the institution which were also having an in uence on senior management about the information direction of the corporate  nance institution. For example, it was stated that ‘there are vested interests that want us to pursue certain other interests, e.g. CD-ROM, which is appropriate in some areas not all (senior management 2) and ‘there are certain levels of competitiveness between “systems” managers which in uences where we should go’ (electronic commerce group manager).

In addition, it was clearly stated that electronic commerce was seen as an economic ‘saviour’ to economic pressures within the institution. This institution had been directed to become more cost-effective and begin to become able to expand within its own cost and revenue structures.

The executive director does have the view that the corporate  nance institution will be moving to a more electroni ed (sic) view of the future and that electronic commerce implementation is seen as a strategy for downsizing and meeting ef ciencies and also maintaining service levels (electronic commerce group manager).

Recognizing the corporate policy demanded from above, the electronic commerce section staff members collectively suggested that the electronic commerce strategy itself had to be simple, suggest simple processes, be quality oriented, indicate a reduction in waste and indicate improved functionality for senior management to accept it.

It was also recognized that there were limitations within the institution about the technological levels of the structures and processes within the institution. For example, one staff member mentioned that the systems were old and another that the abilities of the staff were already stretched: ‘The system is an old batch-based system not on-line. It is connected to a VAN, which should be scrapped and replaced by the web. That will cost’ (staff member 2) and ‘There are distinct limitations to our own abilities to do this. We would need some outside help. Senior management might balk at the additional costs involved’ (staff member 1).

The electronic commerce group in the institution knew there was ambivalence among senior manage ment, that there could be some resistance when addi tional costs were involved, that senior management were unsure about the WWW (in 1996) and that there was some degree of competition between the data division and the electronic commerce group about what should be done and, thus, who should control what was done.

## External in uences

The  rst and most signi cant external issue recognized by the electronic commerce section staff was simply that they still had a long way to go to convince corporate  nance institution clients about the advantages of using electronic commerce and electronic lodgement of documents: ‘There appears to them to be nothing in it for clients (sic)’. This issue was related, in the view of the electronic commerce section staff to inertia in the market-place. The electronic commerce manager noted that: ‘The lodging parties are accountants who are not very with it . . . one even asked me the other day: do I need a PC? . . . clients are inherently conservative and certainly do not rush into things . . . the bulk of the clients just hold back’.

What was also of concern to the electronic commerce section staff was their lack of understanding of what should be done to counteract this inertia: ‘that is one of the reasons you guys are here, to help us (electronic commerce manager to the consultant). He continued that ‘we see nothing obvious to overcome inertia’. When asked what about simpler forms of elec tronic communication more prevalent in the business community, faxes and the Internet, the responses from the electronic commerce group staff included: ‘e-faxing was rejected on the grounds of legality and there were queries by our clients about the ef ciency of it’ (Staff member 2) and ‘e-mail is another issue. We have not got into it. There is no indication that our trading base are ready for it’ (staff member 4).

The  nal external issue identi ed by the electronic commerce section staff concerned the Corporations Law within Australia. The law was said to be ‘archaic’. They saw the law as an impediment because of its demands for ‘signature, the use of speci c prescribed forms and the demands for payments to be linked to lodgements of documents’ (electronic commerce manager). One staff member added, that ‘all the Corporations Act does is limit what can be done and add more cost for the clients in the lodgement process (staff member 3).

Accepting these limitations and building on the material gained from the initial set of three meetings and two subsequent meetings to review drafts of a proposed electronic commerce strategy for the institution over a period of 2 months, the consultant delivered a comprehensive electronic commerce strategy document which was formally accepted for implementation by the institution. This policy had been initiated 2 years earlier but because of the limitations imposed by internal politics, managerial inertia and ambivalence and intrainstitutional politics and control and because of uncertainty in the client population, nothing was able to be generated until an external consultant was hired to assess the situation and establish strategy and process to make what was seemingly needed a reality. Some 6 weeks after acceptance of the electronic commerce strategy, the policy was implemented and the electronic commerce group was given responsibility and a 12 month period to implement the proposal. When reviewed in early 1999 by the researcher, the electronic commerce manager noted that the policy was fully operational and that the clients had fully accepted the new ways of on-line interaction.

## Analysing the ethnography

The ethnography described above suggests that the development and adoption of an electronic commerce strategy within a corporate  nance institution was affected by a number of internal and external issues. The strength of the ethnography is that it enabled the researcher to become immersed within the iterative behaviour of the organization itself. Being part of 6–8 week period of consultant intervention, draft policy development and eventual adoption, facilitated a deeper understanding of what was driving the initial state of inertia on the part of senior management and what were seen as drivers and impediments to electronic commerce adoption from within those parts of the organization responsible for change to more electronic business operations. What was most interesting in the research process was the ability of the researcher to note how the goals of senior management became inculcated within the actions and statements of those pushing for the change itself.

## Matching goals and reality

On the one hand, there were forces within the insti tution trying for the adoption of alternative technologies as their primary goal. However, across the institution, there was uncertainty about what electroni commerce was and how it could help, even though the manager of the electronic commerce section was quite explicit that ‘all the management structure (in the corporate  nance institution) is supportive and staff in this section are very supportive . . . look we are here’. There was also a driver within the institution which suggested that electronic commerce was a technolog and business technique which could/would deliver ef - ciencies, reduce costs and improve service delivery al at the same time. The expectations of senior management were perceived as being economically rationalist rather than effectiveness and quality driven. This was recognized by one electronic commerce section staff member who noted that ‘anything in a quality framework is what we need . . . we need to link everything to quality’ (staff member 2). The staff within the electronic commerce group recognized that their goals and objectives for electronic commerce adoption had to re ect in words and deed these goals of cost reduction and ef ciency within a quality framework. Over the 8-week period, it became more and more obvious to the consultant and the researcher that their advice to the electronic commerce group to adopt this as a strategy was accepted. In essence there were both push and pull factors operating within the institution itself. The push was from the electronic commerce group and the consultant and the pull from senior management. The ambivalence and uncertainty inte gral to the latter group were addressed by the electronic commerce group working with the consultant by wording the electronic commerce strategy in ways which re ected the pull of the senior management business objectives for the organization.

The story presented above also highlights the perceived drivers, both internal and external, which were positively pushing the expansion of electronic commerce within the institution. These drivers framed the needs of the electronic commerce group within the institution as they presented their case to the consultant about the need for expanded implementation of electronic commerce within the organization. Again, there was a perceived change as a result of the consultant seeing the need to match the needs of electronic commerce adoption with the needs of the business as a whole. The story told in this case re ects on the importance of internal processes as a driver towards change. As shown in the quotations throughout this paper, there was recognition by both senior management and the electronic commerce group within the organization for adoption of the new technology. However, there was a clear mismatch between what they were striving for. What was missing was recognition that the business objectives of the organization could be met with the new technology. The electronic commerce strategy adopted eventually re ected this. It was the consultant meeting with both groups together who highlighted the nature of what was needed. Once this was recognized the electronic commerce group could then concentrate on dealing with the external issues of getting clients to change and use new systems.

In summary, the adoption of an electronic commerce strategy within a corporate  nance institution is a complex set of interrelated issues which are summarized in Figure 4.

This analysis suggests, albeit based on only this one case study, that the general notion of a model which proposes that the central role of management in elec tronic commerce adoption is crucial and that this role is affected by both push and pull factors both internal and external to the organization itself (Figure 1). However, such generalization ignores the complexities which underpin those apparent processes. Deeper rooted and more in uential issues including power and the perception of needs drive both the push and pul factors and are central to any complete understanding of electronic commerce adoption by organizations.

Forester (1989) saw power as political communication and concluded that ‘power works through the management of competence, or obfuscation; of trust, or false assurance; of consent, or manipulated agreement; and of knowledge or misrepresentation. Each of the three modes of power works in this way, either to thwart articulate democratic participation and encourage positively, or to encourage articulate political action and the rationalization of a democratic planning (policy) process’ (p. 45). Following the argument of Foucault (1971, 1979, 1981), Forester (1989) argued that these three modes of power derive their effectiveness from differential levels of knowledge existing in society. Foucault (1977, p. 66) argued that power is inseparable from knowledge and that, since knowledge requires records and a system of communication, it in itself is a form of power. In the case of the corporate  nance institution, the communication medium argued for is electronic commerce and the managers of that process will then maintain the power base within the organization. It is problematic whether any formal recognition of this process as deliberative will ever emerge. However, the discussion and analysis presented certainly does raise evaluation of the issues which drive the implementation of electronic commerce into realms unreported in the literature and suggest that the narrative of ethnography could be a very useful tool in understanding another level of understanding of the issues involved.

## Conclusions

This paper began by listing those issues and factors which have been identi ed as being important in the implementation of electronic commerce, IS or IT in organizations. The story of the development of an electronic commerce strategy for a corporate  nance institution has con rmed that those issues marked as most important in that literature have maintained an important status in this process as well. Failure is most commonly associated with the absence of an IS champion or change, lack of management support, strain on already restricted managerial time, poor attitude towards IS, the absence of education and training, organizational problems, technical problems and perceived gaps between the expectations of IS supporters and those expected to use the system. In the corporate  nance institution case study there was a de nite electronic commerce champion, managerial support and appropriate education and training driving the implementation of electronic commerce. However, there were important political and interest differences within the organization and a de nite difference in perception between the supplier of the electronic commerce technologies, the corporate  nance institution and the users, clients and corporations in Australia about its worth.

Success in implementing IS or electronic commerce in business organizations is attributable to a number of factors, including organizational commitment, the existence of an executive sponsor within the organiza tion, the existence of an operating sponsor within the organization to provide quick feedback across the organization, the existence of dedicated facilities within the organization, software and hardware support by suppliers and frequent access to software and hardware developers by users, fast iteration of software changes throughout the organization, the existence of training, outputs from the new system meeting managerial expectations and a positive evaluation of the costs and bene ts of the information process being used. Each of these drivers for success was evident within the corporate  nance institution and supports research primarily devoted to IT and IS.

However, this research has highlighted the fact that there are important in uential issues operating at another level within this organization. Politics, power and in uence are also important to consider in the success or failure of electronic commerce within organizations. The existence of vested interest groups within an organization, managerial misunderstanding of what electronic commerce is, disparate units within the organization and unrealistic expectations of the performance of technology by management all operate as important drivers of those issues listed above, which the literature has highlighted as being the factors related to success or failure of the implementation of IT, IS and/or electronic commerce in organizations. Telling the story, using researcher participation in the development and implementation process and deconstructing the discourses in operation in the organization, can add a new dimension to realistically understanding the way an implementation process may eventuate within an organization. Such ethnographies reveal more than is apparent. They engage the researcher in gaining a deeper understanding of what is fundamentally of consequence within the organizational context. In this study it was an issue of power and an issue of learning to match managerial rhetoric with proposed action and policy suggestions from below. Once some degree of parity was reached and the underlying interests of power and control were exposed, policy acceptance was initiated with substantial gains to the organization itself and to its business practices.
