# The duality of science: Knowledge in information systems research

Alan R Hevner

Journal of Information Technology 1–5 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2020 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839622094571DOI: 10.1177/0268396220945714 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Rigorous research in Information Systems requires an understanding of how scientific foundations drive both the process and outcome. This science duality addresses the effective application of the scientific method (process) as well as the advance of knowledge (outcome) from the research project. This commentary responds to Siponen and Klaavuniemi’s paper with a focus on the uses of knowledge in Information Systems research.

## Keywords

Philosophy of science, science duality, scientific method, knowledge, design science research

Science: The investigation of natural phenomena through observation, theoretical explanation, and experimentation, or the knowledge produced by such investigation.

—The American Heritage Science Dictionary (2011)

## Introduction

The Information Systems (IS) discipline faces many challenges as we address important research questions surrounding the analysis, design, implementation, operations, and evolution of socio-technical systems (Sarker et al., 2019). Rigorous research in our field requires an understanding of how scientific foundations drive both the processes and outcomes of our research projects. The definition of science seen above clearly states this important duality. Science is both a verb—investigation—and a noun— knowledge. Thus, rigorous IS research must support an effective application of the scientific method (process) as well as produce an advance of knowledge (outcome).<sup>1</sup>

The interesting and insightful paper by Siponen and Klaavuniemi (2020) calls out instances of misunderstandings and misapplications of natural science methods in IS research. While these examples provide largely valid critiques, I observe that the focus of the essay is on the use of scientific methods (verb). I suggest in this commentary that the dual lens of scientific knowledge (noun) offers a more precise and, perhaps, a more useful way of evaluating scientific advances in IS research. I begin by briefly responding to several points in the Siponen and Klaavuniemi (2020) paper that reference quotations from the Hevner et al.’s (2004) paper on design science research (DSR). The remainder of the commentary applies the lens of advancing scientific knowledge to IS research.

## Truth as a goal of science

Michela Massimi (2019), in her excellent essay Getting It Right on the relationship of truth and science, leads with the following quotation:

Truth is neither absolute nor timeless. But the pursuit of truth remains at the heart of the scientific endeavor.

It is this understanding of science as the pursuit of truth that supports our statement in Hevner et al. (2004), “The goal of behavioral science research is truth” (p. 80). However, Siponen and Klaavuniemi interpret this statement as more dogmatic than intended throughout their discussions. We do not say that IS research must achieve some form of absolute and timeless truth, only that goal of scientific research is truth.

Later in the paper, the authors present another quote from Hevner et al. (2004), “laws are crucial components for design-science research” (p. 88). Regrettably, this quote is taken out of context. The full passage is

Design is essentially a search process to discover an effective solution to a problem. Problem solving can be viewed as utilizing available means to reach desired ends while satisfying laws existing in the environment (Simon, 1996). Abstraction and representation of appropriate means, ends, and laws are crucial components of design-science research.

In the spirit of Simon (1996), our intention in this passage is to model (abstract and represent) the problem space in the presence of available resources (means), research goals (desired ends), and natural constraints (laws). Simply quoting the final words of this passage in a way that changes the meaning of the sentence is misleading.

In spite of these minor concerns, the paper by Siponen and Klaavuniemi does a great service by identifying important issues with how IS research projects appropriate and use scientific methods. The history and philosophy of science are fraught with debates and conflicts on the selection and use of scientific methods (see Godfrey-Smith (2003) for a survey). So it is not surprising that IS research also struggles to Get It Right, using Massimi’s phrase. Thus, I propose in this commentary to study the dual science lens of evaluating IS research by assessing its impact on advances in scientific knowledge.

## Scientific knowledge

The quality of IS research should be judged by both its practical and scientific impacts (Baskerville et al., 2018). Scientific impact can be measured by the amount of new knowledge that is contributed to the world’s diverse knowledge repositories. In this vein, Massimi (2019) concludes her essay with the following:

Let there be no doubt: scientific knowledge is the product of our getting it right across our perspectival multicultural scientific history. Scientific knowledge is not a prerogative of our Western cultural perspective (and its discipline-specific scientific perspectives) but the outcome of plurality of historically and culturally situated scientific perspectives that, over millennia, have reliably produced knowledge with the tools, resources and concepts, respectively available to each and every one of them.

Drawing from the economics of knowledge field (Mokyr, 2002), Shirley Gregor and I (Gregor and Hevner, 2013) propose that useful scientific knowledge in IS research can be divided into two distinct types.<sup>2</sup> Descriptive knowledge (denoted Ω or omega) is the “what” knowledge about natural phenomena and the laws and regularities among phenomena. Prescriptive knowledge (denoted Λ or lambda) is the “how” knowledge of human-built artifacts. Both Ω-knowledge and Λ-knowledge comprise a comprehensive knowledge base for a particular application domain for IS research. IS research projects demonstrate their rigor and relevance by how they consume existing knowledge and how they produce new knowledge in the application knowledge bases (Gregor and Hevner, 2013).

## Descriptive knowledge

The descriptions of natural, artificial, and human-related phenomena are composed of observations, classifications, measurements, and the cataloging of these descriptions into accessible forms. In addition, scientists discover knowl edge of the sense-making relationships among the phenomena. This sense-making is represented by natural laws, principles, regularities, patterns, and theories. Together, phenomena and their sense-making relationships provide the natural, artificial, and human (social) science bases for the world in which we live. An addition of knowledge to Ω is a discovery of new facts or laws that had always existed but had not been understood and described until now. Over time, the Ω-knowledge base accumulates the “body of knowledge” (BOK) surrounding a natural, artificial, social, or human phenomenon.

## Prescriptive knowledge

Prescriptive knowledge concerns artifacts designed by humans to improve the natural world. Simon (1996) labels such knowledge as belonging to the sciences of the artificial. Hevner et al. (2004) define four types of prescriptive knowledge: constructs, models, methods, and instantiations. Nascent design theories that attempt to capture initial understandings of “why” artifacts are useful and also provide prescriptive knowledge, so the Λ-knowledge base includes the following:

Constructs, which provide the vocabulary and symbols used to define and understand problems and solutions; for example, the constructs of “entities” and “relationships” in the field of information modeling. The correct constructs have a significant impact on the way in which tasks and problems are conceived, and they enable the construction of models for the problem and solution domains.

Models, which are designed representations of the problem and possible solutions. For example, math ematical models, diagrammatical models, and logic models are widely used in the IS field and new and more useful models are continually being developed. Models correspond to “principles of form” in the Gregor and Jones (2007) taxonomy: the abstract blueprint of an artifact’s architecture, which show an artifact’s components and how they interact.

Methods, which are algorithms, programs, practices, and recipes for performing a task. Methods provide the instructions for performing goal-driven activities. They are also known as techniques (Mokyr, 2002) and correspond to “principles of function” in the Gregor and Jones (2007) taxonomy and Bunge’s (1998) technological rules.

Instantiations, which are the physical realizations that act on the natural world, such as an information system that stores, retrieves, and analyzes customer relationship data. Instantiations can embody design knowledge, possibly in the absence of more explicit description. The structural form and functions embodied in an artifact can be inferred to some degree by observing the artifact.

Design theories, which are abstract, coherent bodies of prescriptive knowledge that describe the principles of form and function, methods, and justificatory theory that are used to develop an artifact or to accomplish some end (Gregor and Hevner, 2013).

## DSR and scientific knowledge

In order to demonstrate the interplay and synergy between descriptive and prescriptive knowledge, this section provides a conceptual model of a DSR project and its use of existing knowledge and production of new knowledge. The relationship of specific design knowledge created in DSR projects and the general descriptive and prescriptive knowledge bases are illustrated in Figure 1. This figure is adapted and simplified from Drechsler and Hevner (2018) and clearly shows paired modes of consuming and producing knowledge between the DSR project and the descriptive and prescriptive knowledge bases. The Λ-knowledge is divided into two sub-categories. Design Entities collect the prescriptive knowledge as represented in the tangible artifacts and processes designed and applied in the solution space. The growing of design theories around these solutions is captured in the Design Theories knowledge base. Knowledge can be projected from the application into nascent theories around solution actions, entity realizations, and design processes based on the new and interesting design knowledge produced in a DSR project. Thus, we can describe the interactions of a DSR project with the extant knowledge bases in the following consuming and producing modes:

Modes 1 and 2—Building on and contributing to Ω- Knowledge. Ω-knowledge informs the understanding of a problem, its context, or the development of a design entity (Mode 1—Grounding Theory to Design Entity/Theory). The design and real-world application of solution design knowledge changes the world and, thus, induces testing and building of Ω-knowledge, which has the potential to enhance our descriptive understanding of how the world works given the new design knowledge (Mode 2— Design Entity/Theory to Descriptive Theory Extension).

Modes 3 and 4—Building on and contributing to design theory as Λ-Knowledge. Solution design knowledge, in the form of growing design theory, informs the development of a design entity, that is, a design process or a design system (Mode 3— Grounding Design Theory to Design Entity). Within a DSR project, effective principles, features, actions, or effects of a design entity are generalized and codified in solution design knowledge (e.g. design theories or technological rules) (Mode 4—Design Entity to Design Theory Extension).

Modes 5 and 6—Building on and contributing to design entities as Λ-Knowledge. Previously effective design entities and design processes are re-used to inform novel designs of new design entities (Mode 5—Grounding Design Entity to Design Entity). Within a DSR project, effective design entities are contributed to design knowledge (Mode 6—Design Entity to Design Entity Extension).

By assessing the relevance and rigor of IS research through a lens of knowledge consumption and production, we are able to better measure the quality of the research contributions. The IS discipline relies on evidence-based science. Our discipline supports a wide range of different research methods, experimental strategies, fundamental theories, analysis techniques, and, even, values. We are a diverse bunch. However, the one inviolate thing that unites us is the requirement that all research must be grounded on solid, irrefutable evidence. No worthy research project fabricates evidence, manipulates evidence, or ignores evidence. Evidence is measurable and the rendering of research evidence into descriptive and prescriptive knowledge is an essential skill set for IS researchers.

The six modes of producing and consuming scientific design knowledge illustrate the multi-faceted opportunities for knowledge accumulation and evolution that arise when looking beyond single DSR projects and organizing design knowledge contributions over time and across projects. The topic of design knowledge accumulation and evolution is thoroughly explored by Vom Brocke et al. (2020).

## Conclusion

The dual nature of science in terms of its processes (verb) and its outcomes (noun) provides complementary but separable lens for viewing the quality of scientific research in IS. While Siponen and Klaavuniemi (2020) critique IS research projects on their appropriation and use of scientific methods (process view), this brief commentary argues for assessing the quality of IS research projects on their innovative research contributions (outcome view) to descriptive and prescriptive knowledge bases. This knowledge-based outcome view emphasizes a philosophy of pragmatism, which is expressed in DSR as the requirement to solve real-world problems with practical solutions (Hevner et al., 2004).

In conclusion, the goal of this commentary is to balance the dual process and outcome perspectives of science in IS research. While both perspectives are essential for judging the rigor and relevance of a research project, the ability to identify and measure evidence-based knowledge contributions provides findings that are more defensible.
