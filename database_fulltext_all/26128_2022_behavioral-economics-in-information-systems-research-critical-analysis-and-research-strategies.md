# Behavioral Economics in Information Systems Research: Critical Analysis and Research Strategies

## Abstract

Theories of decision-making have long been important foundations for information systems (IS) research and much of IS is concerned with information processing for decision making. The discipline of behavioral economics (BE) provides the dominant contemporary approach for understanding human decision-making. Therefore, it is logical that IS research that involves decision making should consider BE as foundation or reference theory. Surprisingly, and despite calls for greater use of BE in IS research, it seems that IS has been slow to adopt contemporary BE as reference theory. This paper reports a critical analysis of BE in all fields of IS based on an intensive investigation of quality IS research using bibliometric content analysis. The analysis shows that IS researchers have a general understanding of BE, but their use of the theories has an ad hoc feel where only a narro of BE concepts and theories tend to form the foundation of IS research. The factors constraining the adoption of BE theories in IS are discussed and strategies for the use of this influential foundation theory are proposed. Guidance is provided on how BE could be used in various aspects of IS. The paper concludes with the view that BE reference theory has the potential to transform significant areas of IS research.

Keywords: behavioral economics, information systems, bounded rationality, dual process theory, heuristics and biases, prospect theory, nudges.

## 1. Introduction

Theories of decision-making have long been important to information systems (IS) research, especially in the fields of decision support systems, intelligent systems, IT governance, systems development, business intelligence, and business analytics. As Goes (2013, p.iii) suggested “Much of what the IS field is about relates to information processing for decision making.” Decision theory can be broadly divided into economic decision-making and behavioral decision theory. Economic decision-making is largely prescriptive in nature and focuses on maximizing the expected utility of a decision budget and resource constraints) normally to reach an equilibrium in s me form of a market. Economists have long believed that maximizing expected utilities is the basis o an effective descriptive theory of decision making in addition to the perfe eal. Behavioral decision theory on the other hand is primarily descriptive in nature on understanding how and why people make decisions. Browne and Parsons (2012) argued that behavioral decision theory has had a profound effect on IS scholars Currently, the dominant form of behavioral decision theory is behavioral economics (Fox, 2 5). Behavioral economics (BE) is a collection of theories of decision making in econ tuations that explains how humans actually make decisions. BE relaxes some of the core assumptions of neoclassical economics, including the assumptions that decision makers always make rational decisions and have perfect knowledge of a decision and its context, have unlimited information processing abilities, lack any emotional involveme decision, and have no time constraints in a decision process. That is, BE relaxes a core belief of neo-classical economics, the belief that decision making is perfectly rational.

BE is founded on the principle of bounded rationality (Simon, 1955) that holds that humans have cognitive limitations that prevent them from making perfectly rational economic decisions. When humans make decisions, they use two cognitive systems, System 1 and System 2 (Sloman, 1996), which are respectively dominated by fast, intuitive thinking and rule-based deliberate reasoning. System 1, in particular, is driven by general heuristics that can be subject to many cognitive biases that diminish decision effectiveness (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Behavioral economists have combined various aspects of BE to create important theories like prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979a) and intervention methods like decision nudges (Thaler & Sunstein, 2009). BE is not a single reference theory, it is a complex web of interacting theories, phenomena, methods, and processes.

In the area of the IS discipline most concerned with decision making, decision support Jelassi, 1994; Arnott, 2006; Bhandari, Hassanein, & Deaves, 2008; Huang, Hsu, & Ku, 2012; Arnott & Pervan 2014). Goes (2013) in an MIS Quarterly editorial echoed these calls. There have been two reviews of BE in IS research that indicated that BE as a reference discipline has a low use in IS research (Fleischmann et al., 2014; Odnor & Oinas-Kukkonen, 2017). Most recently, Arnott and Gao (2019) provided an overview of BE and suggested 10 areas where BE could be used more widely in DSS projects.

This paper adds to this previous work and aims to increase our understanding of how BE is used, and could be used, in general IS research. It approaches this task through a bibliometric content analysis of high-quality recent IS research. The literature analysis forms the foundation for considering how IS projects should and could use BE reference theory. Ultimately, the paper addresses the questions:

1. How has behavioral economics been used in IS research?

2. Why don’t we use behavioral economics in IS research projects?

3. How could we use behavioral economics in IS research?

The paper is organized as follows: the next section provides an overview of mainstream BE. Previous literature reviews of the use of BE reference theory in IS research are then discussed. The design of this paper’s literature analysis is then presented followed by the results of the review. The paper then addresses the three research questions mentioned above.

## 2. Behavioral Economics

For much of the 20<sup>th</sup> century the dominant approach to decision making, both descriptive and prescriptive, was neoclassical economics. The microeconomic approach to decision making was articulated by Marshall (1890) and has been taught to every generation of economists ever since (for example, Samuelson & Nordhaus, decision making and optimization leading to equilibrium in markets. Economists assume that a rational decision involves consistent preferences, logical reasoning, perfect information about all aspects of a decision, and unlimited processing capacity. In making a decision with these conditions decision makers effectively ration scare resources by maximising their utility or satisfaction in either consumption or profits from production of goods and services. The idea of utility maximization in markets can be traced to Adam Smith (Smith, 1776) and was expressed in its current form of expected utility (EU) theory by Von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944). Under expected utility theory, decision makers maximize the expected values of their choices under strict normative conditions of mathematical logic. By participants in a market acting in this almost superhuman rational utility maximizing manner, markets attain an optimum equilibrium between consumption and production. Prior to the development of BE there were a number of critiques of using the maximization of expected utility as an effective descriptive theory of decision making including from mathematical economists (for example, Arrow, 1950), statistical decision theorists (for example, Alias, 1953; Savage, 1954; Ellsberg, 1961), post-war microeconomists (for example, Akerlof, 1970; Shiller, 1978), and from institutional economists (for example, Galbraith, 1952). These challengers attacked one or more of the rigorous assumptions of neoclassical economics. Despite these critiques, expected utility maximization largely survived as the accepted descriptive economic theory of decision making until the rise and acceptance of behavioral economics. It remains the preeminent prescriptive, or normative, approach to decision making.

Nobel Prize for Economics winner<sup>1</sup> Herbert Simon laid the foundation of BE<sup>2</sup> in the seminal papers “A behavioral model of rational choice” in the Quarterly Journal of Economics (Simon, 1955) and “Theories of decision-making in economics and behavioral science” in The American Economic Review (Simon, 1959). For Simon, the ideal view of economic decision rationality was at odds with his studies of administrative managers in the 1940s. He argued in Simon (1997/1945) and Simon (1955) that decision makers could not have perfect knowledge of a decision situation and, further, they are significantly limited in their cognitive and information processing abilities. In addition, they are normally subject to time pressure that means that perfect computations of utility and other functions are not possible. Simon’s key insight was that decision makers’ rationality was bounded rather than perfect. Under bounded rationality decision makers use heuristics or rules of thumb rather than the optimization processes that underpin neoclassical economics.

Following bounded rationality, the major aspect of Simon’s decision-making theory is the phase model of decision-making, a descriptive and explanatory model of the process of decision-making under bounded rationality where the phases of intelligence, design, and choice define a decision process (Simon, 1960). The phases can be executed in different sequences and are iterative and recursive. A wellaccepted extension of Simon’s phase model is the five-stage model of Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret (1976). The phase model led to the concept of decision structuredness. Simon offered the following definition: “Problems are well structured when the goal tests are clear and easily applied, and when there is a well-defined set of generators for synthesizing potential solutions. Problems are ill structured to the decision; a totally unstructured decision is one where no aspect of the decision process can be articulated. For Simon, the decisions people make are largely unstructured, where the decisions of economic man are, by definition, completely structured. The phase model has, unfortunately, largely failed in empirical validation (Lipschitz & Bar-Ilan, 1996) but bounded rationality remains the accepted foundation of all BE theory.

Chapman and Pike (2013) in a significant review of the BE literature argued that Simon and colleagues typify the “old” BE, while Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler and their colleagues typify the “new”. This paper uses the terms “early” and “contemporary” BE as they are less value-loaded and more accurate. Theories and concepts from the early BE of Simon and colleagues are shown in Table 1. In Tables 1 through 5 classic, seminal, and well-cited BE reference articles are included in the Indicative References column. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky took Simon’s challenge to the rationality of economic man into the laboratory and developed the heuristics and biases research area (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). The first economist to publish in this “new” area was Richard Thaler (Thaler, 1980). Kahneman won the Nobel Prize for Economics in 2002<sup>3</sup> and Thaler in 2017<sup>4</sup>. This paper takes the view that mainstream has become the scientific orthodoxy in the understanding of human decision-making. In a commentary in the Harvard Business Review about the impact of the various streams of BE on business and management, Justin Fox concluded “The Kahneman-Tversky heuristics-and-biases approach has the upper hand right now, both in academia and in the public mind.” (Fox, 2015, p. 84). Fox reported that 90% of current BE is in the Kahneman and Tversky camp. Thinking Fast and Slow (Kahneman, 2011) provides a definitive summary of contemporary mainstream BE while Thaler’s

Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioral Economics provides a chronicle of the history and overview of the discipline (Thaler, 2015), as does Camerer and Loewenstein (2003) and Corr and Plagnol (2019).

There are other significant branches of descriptive decision-making study arising from Simon’s early BE, most notably the research associated with Gary Klein and Gerd Gigerenzer. These two quite different research groupings share two propositions: decision-making is best studied in the field with real decision makers, and expert intuition, through the action of heuristics, is a source of decision effectiveness rather than a flaw in decision making. Klein is best known for his landmark study of fire commanders in the field (Klein, Calderwood, & Clinton-Cirocco, 2010). His resulting used in military tactical and strategic decision-making (Klein, 1999, 2004). Klein also collaborated with Kahneman to investigate under what circumstances expert intuition is preferable to analytic decision-making (Kahneman & Klein, 2009). Gigerenzer’s program of research is deeply critical of the heuristics and biases approach, even proposition is that heuristics are a source of effectiveness in decision-making and his approach is summarized in Gigerenzer and Selten (2001), Gigerenzer and Brighton (2009), and Gigerenzer (2014).

Contemporary mainstream BE has two main foundations: the first is the dual process theory of decision-making cognition; the second is a set of heuristics and cognitive biases that, in some circumstances, systematically prejudice decision quality. Kahneman (2011, Part 1) placed dual process theory firmly at the foundation of contemporary BE. This theory holds that decision-making occurs within and between two cognitive processes or systems. Kahneman and Frederick (2002) typified them as two families of cognitive operations. In an influential paper, Stanovich and West (2000)

termed them System 1 and System 2 in order to avoid descriptive labeling. System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless, and intuitive. When facing a decision, System 1 is the first in action. It operates through innate, instinctive behavior. System 2 is slow, deliberate and requires significant cognitive effort. System 2’s abilities are not innate and must be formed through education, both formally in training courses, schools, and universities, and less formally in families and social interaction. Over time, System 2 tasks can be converted to System 1 through exposure and experience. While described as discrete systems, System 1 and 2 can operate at the same time and can interact. Evans (2003) described the situation as like two minds in the same body. Kahneman and Frederick (2002, p. 51) relate: “System 1 quickly proposes intuitive answers to judgment problems as they arise, and System 2 monitors the quality of these proposals, which it may end rse, correct, or override.” Aspects of dual process theory are shown in Table 2.

The second major foundation of mainstream contemporary BE, heuristics and biases, is essentially devoted to understanding System 1 decision processes, how they are effective and how they fail. The seminal publication is Tversky and Kahneman (1974). Heuristics and biases are best thought of as a large collection of phenomena and effects where each phenomenon describes a particular aspect of human decisionmaking. Heuristics and biases can be explained by theories from psychology, sociology, and BE itself. Further, some theories explain aspects of multiple biases. Unfortunately, this collection of disparate effects makes it difficult to have a coherent overall view of the field. As Fleischmann et al. (2014, p. 5) relates “… there is no standardized, generally accepted and scientifically grounded framework for cognitive biases”.

While general heuristics are a source of effectiveness in human decision-making, they are subject to cognitive processes that can lead to poor decisions and, in rare cases, catastrophic failure. These processes are the second part of the heuristics and bias component of BE. Cognitive biases are cognitions or mental behaviors that prejudice decision quality in a significant number of decisions for a significant number of people; they are inherent in human reasoning. In the literature, these effects are often termed decision or judgment biases, illusions, or effects. Tversky and Kahneman (1974, p. 1130) viewed biases as failures of general heuristics: “…they (heuristics) occasionally lead to errors in prediction or estimation.” An important word in the quotation is “occasionally” and the key issue is knowing when a particular bias is likely to adversely affect a particular decision. All else being equal, biases are likely to affect complex decisions with time pressure and decisions that are new to the decision maker. Dozens of biases have been discovered in psychology and BE experiments and this body of work is summarized in Sage (1981), Kahneman, Slovic, and Tversky (1982), Kahneman and Tversky (2000), Gilovich, Griffen, and Kahneman (2002), and

Kahneman (2011). The discovery of biases has become somewhat of an academic industry and a confusing aspect of the area is that researchers use various and confusing names for the effects (Sage, 1981). One strategy to aid the overall understanding of the range of cognitive biases has been to develop classifications and typologies of biases. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) classified biases by their three general judgment heuristics. Bazerman and Moore (2013) followed a similar approach and argued for the primacy of overconfidence biases. Bazerman and Moore’s typology is aimed at managers and senior professionals. Hogarth’s (1987) typology is ordered according to his general model of decision making. The Lovallo & Sibony (2010) typology is from a McKinsey & Co report and is aimed at management consulting. Finally, the typology of Arnott (2006) was developed specifically for systems analysis in DSS development projects. Prospect theory is a descriptive theory of decision making in risky and uncertain situations. As such it complements rather than replaces the prescriptive theories of decision making of neoclassical economics. Kahneman and Tversky developed prospect theory because they didn’t believe that expected utility theory adequately explained how humans actually make decisions especially where there is a possibility of significant losses. Figure 2 shows prospect theory’s value function. Each individual will have different shape to their value function, but Figure 1 portrays the most common shape based on empirical studies (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979a). The horizontal axis in Figure 1 measures the monetary gains or losses that follow a decision relative to a reference or starting point. The vertical axis measures the psychological value that a gain or a loss yields to an individual. In Figure 1, segments 2 and 4 represent risk seeking behavior and segments 1 and 3, risk avoidance. On the dashed line in Figure 1 decision makers treat the value of losses and gains equally; an assumption underlying the economic theory of expected utility.

Three cognitive features, all operating characteristics of System 1, are the foundation of prospect theory (Kahneman, 2011, p. 281-2). They are: 1. Evaluation is relative to a neutral reference point. This point defines what is a gain and what is a loss; 2. There is diminishing sensitivity to both increasing values and increasing gains or losses; and 3. Losses loom larger than gains in decision maker’s minds. Risk seeking is the dominant behavior when assessing losses (segment 4) while risk avoidance is the dominant pattern when assessing gains (segment 1). Prospect theory shows that humans do not behave as neoclassical economists assume, especially when risk aversion is in play. As Camerer and Loewenstein (2003, p. 20) related “Prospect theory … explains experimental choices more accurately than EU because it gets the psychophysics of judgment and choice right.” Thaler (2015, p.353) observed “Prospect theory is, of course, the seminal evidence-based theory in behavioral economics.”

Nudging is not a theory of decision making but rather a strategy and process for using BE in an organizational setting. Nudges are essentially field experiments for organizational rather than academic ends. They are based on the action of dual process theory especially where the action of System 1 overwhelms System 2 thinking in negative ways. Nudges are argued to be needed to lead people to superior decision outcomes ecause of the inherent flaws of human decision making, that is, the negative consequence of cognitive biases. The concept of nudges was developed by Thaler and Sunstein (2009) who defined a nudge as “any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people’s behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives” (p. 6). Thaler, Sunstein, and Balz (2014) provide a discussion of the six principles of an effective choice architecture. Perhaps the most famous example of a nudge is changing the level of peoples’ pension savings in a positive way by altering the default employm contracts from opt-in to opt-out. An opt-in default requires effort to co ce or change pension saving plans. This effort added to decision inertia means that people often do not take the effort to opt-in and thereby ensure a comfortable retirement. Nudging, by changing the default to opt-out, changes the effort frame such that many more people save for their retirement and reap the rewards of long-term compound interest. The explicit use of the nudge strategy has been most prominent in government. In 2008 Sunstein (of Thal and Sunstein) was appointed to head the White House Office of Information and Regulat Affairs (OIRS) by President Obama. This office was charged with using a BE informed government action. In 2010, on the other side of politics, a conservative UK gover established the Behavioural Insights Team (BIT) in 10 Downing Street with a similar to OIRS. BIT is credited with saving many millions of pounds of government revenue and significantly changing citizen behavior towards better personal outcomes (Halpern, 2015).

## 3. Previous Reviews of Behavioral Economics in IS Research

Tables 1 through 5 include a number of citations to IS research that have used one or more aspects of BE as a major part of their work. While helpful, these examples do not show the depth and breadth of the use of BE in IS research in a rigorous and systematic way. To further this understanding there have been two literature reviews of BE in general IS research. Fleischmann et al. (2014) conducted the most detailed review to date of cognitive biases in IS research. They used a sample of the Basket of Eight (Bof8) set of journals, as defined by the AIS Senior Scholars,<sup>5</sup> augmented by two journals and two conferences. Through keyword searches using 120 terms they identified 84 articles from 1992 to 2013 that used biases in their research. They then coded and analyzed their sample using content analysis and found that the use of biases in IS research has been steadily increasing over time. Their analysis showed that framing and anchoring were the most popular biases studied in their sample and that prospect theory (13/84 articles) was used to develop research models in IS research. Most of the IS articles that Fleischmann et al. (2014) reviewed used laboratory experiments or field experiments as their research strategy. The second literature review, Odnor and Oinas-Kukkonen (2017), analyzed a wider range of aspects of BE than Fleischmann et al. in the Bof8 from 2006 to 2014. However, their article selection criteria of experiments and experiment-like surveys was quite restrictive and yielded a BE-using sample of only 15 articles. They found significant diversity in the small sample with the main focus being e-commerce, and on systems design and use. In a sub-field of IS, Arnott and Pervan (2014) in their general analysis of the DSS discipline, found that the change of foundation theory from the early to contemporary BE was progressing slowly and citations to the early BE still dominated. They argued for a swap to contemporary BE reference theory.

## 4. Research Method and Design

## 4.1 General Approach

This paper’s research involved the bibliometric content analysis (Weber, 1990) of representative IS research that used BE as foundation or reference theory. Bibliometric content analysis involves the coding and analysis of a sample of research articles. In this approach, data capture is driven by a protocol that has predominantly quantitative questions but can also include some qualitative responses. This form of data capture is labor intensive in that each article in the sample has to be read in full, sometimes multiple times. The approach has the advantage that it can illuminate the deep structure of the field in a way that is impossible to achieve with other literature analysis approaches.

As will be discussed in the next sub-section, the article sample covers five years of recent IS research. As a result, no analysis of trends over time is possible with this sample. In this way the research in this paper is best thought of as cross-sectional in BE been used in IS research?” Where possible, reference is made to previous literature analyses to identify some temporal trends.

## 4.2 The Article Sample

To understand how BE is used in IS research, a representative sample of research articles that used behavioral economics was required. In determining the time-period of the sample, 2014 was chosen as the starting year. This date marks the end of the samples of previous relevant literature reviews (Fleischmann et al., 2014; Odnor & Oinas-Kukkonen, 2017). Further, 2014 represents a likely time for the influence of Thinking Fast and Slow (Kahneman, 2011) to affect journal publication. Thinking Fast and Slow led to widespread exposure of BE to researchers, business professionals, and the public. The end year of the sample is 2018 as this was the latest complete year of journal publication available to the researchers at the time of coding. The sample period is therefore 2014 to 2018.

The Basket of Eight set of journals was chosen as the journal sample as the Bof8 arguably represents the pinnacle of scientific IS publishing. The Bof8 is the European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ). Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), Journal of Information Technology (JIT), Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), and MIS Quarterly (MISQ). The Bof8 provides a good mix of IS research traditions, epistemologies, and methods and is equally split between USA and European journals. The literature review sample strategy of using the Bof8 journals is common in IS theory and review papers (for example, Haddara & Hetlevik, 2016; Prat, Comyn-Wattiau, & Akoka, 2015; Tarafdar & Davison, 2018; and Ozdemir, Smith, & Benamati, 2017).

In identifying potential articles for coding, only original research was included. This means that editorials, short commentaries on other papers, and responses to critiques, are not part of the sample; however research notes and essays were included. The selection of potential articles for the sample was not able to be made by automated search. This is because the classification of articles in the various journals is not consistent. Further, some editorials, responses and commentaries are not clearly identified as such and can appear in a search as a research article. The process adopted by this research was for the second author to carefully read each issue of each journal to identify the articles to consider for the sample. This process maximized the sampling reliability and yielded a set of 1,348 articles. The overall sampling process is shown in Figure 2.

Searching keywords was not a priate as not all articles that used an aspect of BE nominated an explicit keyword to identify this use. Further, searching on BE terms yielded extraneous results. For example, searching for “bias” yielded a number of non-BE concepts such as sampling bias and common method bias. ## 4.3 Coding Protocol and Procedures

In bibliometric content analysis data is collected using a structured protocol that mainly requires quantitative responses. This approach has been used in IS literature reviews (for example, Arnott & Pervan, 2014; Sivarajah et al., 2017; Trieu, 2017). The protocol was designed to minimize interpretive and subjective assessments and it is believed that other researchers could apply the protocol and code similar data. One way of assuring inter-coder reliability is for all coders to code all articles and then evaluate their reliability using a statistic like Cohen’s kappa. However, with this particular sample such an approach is impractical. The sample contains 216 articles and coding each article requires careful reading and coding decisions. Difficult or complex articles took up to four hours to code and “normal” papers at least an hour. As a result, the two authors shared the coding of the sample and a different approach to ensure inter-coder reliability was followed. A codebook was developed that specified how each item should be coded and included definitions of the constructs and concepts that were being assessed. The authors then coded a pilot set of two articles that prima facie had a significant use of BE. They then compared and discussed their coding and the protocol and codebook were amended. This cycle was repeated three times after which the coders were well calibrated, and the protocol required no further change. be coded by both authors; the coding of around 50 articles was debated by the researchers. A condensed version of the article coding protocol appears in Appendix A. The full protocol and codebook are available from the authors.

The protocol has three main sections: general research questions, general BE questions, and detailed BE questions. The general research questions addressed the article type by research method, epistemology, unit of analysis, the IS field, research domain, the task addressed, and the organizational nature of the task. The general BE questions were used to assess the overall nature of BE citations in the paper and how important the BE citations were to the article’s research. The citation count for all BE reference articles were recorded including where in the article it was cited. This section of the protocol contained an important item that required subjective judgement by the coder. This item, the degree of use of BE, was judged as major, moderate, or minor. Where there was uncertainty with an assessment, the higher score or code was assigned. This is called coding with a generosity bias. The section of the protocol with detailed BE questions addressed the particular aspects of BE that were used and the specific heuristics and biases that may have been involved. Cognitive biases were coded using the typology and definitions from Table 4. Anchoring and adjustment was a special coding case and was coded either as a bias or a heuristic depending on the meaning of the effect that was used in an article. In addition to “pure” BE reference theory, economics research that has founded some BE research was identified and coded. An example of this foundational economics work is Akerlof (1970). The relationship of this foundational economics to BE is similar to that of the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1973) to the popular technology acceptance model in IS (Davis, 1989).

## 4.4 Data Entry and Analysis Procedures

The 216 coded protocols were entered into SPSS for analysis. A research assistant entered all data and reviewed all coding during the act of data entry using the codebook as a reference. If there were any issues identified during data entry, the second author SPSS data to ensure data entry accuracy and completeness. A number of cross tabulations and frequency analyses were conducted to ensure data integrity before the data analysis was conducted. Because of the nature of the protocol and the size of the sample, the analysis focuses on descriptive statistics and cross tabulation. This analysis tactic has been used by other IS literature reviews (for example, Fleischmann et al., 2014, Chiu et al., 2019, Tummers, Kassahun, & Tekinerdogan, 2019).

## 6. Discussion

This section is structured around the questions articulated in the Introduction:

1. How has behavioral economics been used in IS research?

2. Why don’t we use behavioral economics in IS research projects?

3. How could we use behavioral economics in IS research?

The discussion begins with issues relating to the literature analysis findings and then moves to more speculative discussion. In this sense addressing Question 1 involves descriptive analysis with critical aspects, Question 2 critically examines issues with IS research, and Question 3 involves theorizing about the future use of BE in IS research. Answering Question 3 provides guidance on which as ects of BE are relevant to particular areas of IS.

## 6.1 How has behavioral economics been used in IS research?

The results of the literature analysis in Section 5 show a complex relationship between the BE and IS disciplines. They show that BE seems to be important to IS research but when only the major use of BE is considered, the use of BE in IS is relatively small for such a major behavioral theory, only 3.5% of IS research. The analysis shows that when using BE, IS researchers do have a broad awareness of contemporary BE and, unlike the findings of earlier studies, they predominately use or cite contemporary rather than early BE in their work. Further, the articles that use BE in a major way, compared to those that cite BE in some way, shows that although researchers are aware of BE, only a relatively small number of IS studies use BE as a main or important reference theory.

In the major BE use sample, e-commerce was the most researched area (23.4% of the sample). Adding our findings to Fleischmann et al. (2014)’s and Odnor and Oinas-Kukkonen (2017)’s shows that e-commerce has been the most prominent BE-using IS area since 1992. Finance/investment was the next most popular, but DSS was the least area researched. This is a very surprising result, as by definition, DSS is the area that is most concerned with decision making - what BE is concerned with. DSS occupies a much larger fraction of all IS research than the 4.3% of the major use research are not clear but research methods, as discussed below, could be one factor. A detailed reading of the articles in the major use sample showed although ecommerce had the highest frequency of use, the security and privacy articles were the most intensive users of BE research; that is, they cited the most BE articles and often used more than one aspect of BE in a major way in their projects.

One reason why e-commerce is particularly attractive to BE reference theory use could be e-commerce’s general focus on IS use by an individual. The literature analysis shows an overwhelming focus (72.5%) on an individual unit of analysis in BE-major use articles. As e-commerce research in the sample only involved operational tasks performed by a potential customer, the individual nature of general BE research, especially cognitive bias, is a logical fit (72.7% of e-commerce articles). The next most popular unit of analysis, a process (12.5% of the major use sample), involves projects that focus on a decision-making process, either the phase model or dual process theory. This is the highest level of abstraction in decision-making research.

In terms of the tasks involved in major BE-using IS articles, operational tasks dominated the sample (74.5%). This is understandable and could represent a reasonable distribution of tasks in organizations, although the around 10% strategic task share is probably higher than the organizational average. Around half of the tasks researched using a major BE foundation involved a strong interaction between System System 2 decision making. This implies an overall cognitive process that is towards an equal use of the two systems. The low level of System 2 dominant tasks in both samples was a surprise as it was expected that IS’s main focus would be on automating System 2 style tasks, the operational tasks identified in Table 17. This represents a different story to IS research in general, and even the DSS sub-field. This result could also be a further consequence of the dominance of e-commerce in the BE using samples.

One theme that emerges from the analysis in Section 5 is the strongly positivist nature of BE-using IS research. The discipline of BE is itself almost entirely positivist in nature and it could be that BE reference theory is especially appealing to positivist IS 14 shows that experiments and field studies are used in the majority of IS studies that use BE in a major way. It is clear that IS researchers who are skilled in experimental research are attracted to reference theories that are dominated by experimental methods. One aspect of this positivist orientation is that important BE articles can be difficult to follow without considerable expertise in experimentation; they are often difficult reading. This may contribute to the low use of case studies and design science by BE-using IS projects. The literature analysis shows the distribution of methods in BE-using IS research more closely mirrors the methods of the reference discipline rather than the methods used in IS in general. It appears that research method rather than research area is a determinant of BE use in IS and this could explain why use of BE in DSS research is lower than expected; DSS is dominated by design science methods and experiments are declining in their popularity for the area where BE should have the greatest impact (Arnott & Pervan, 2014).

The use of BE as major reference theory in the literature analysis has an ad hoc feel. In general, researchers seem to adopt a specific aspect of BE that they believe is relevant to their research and only cite articles related to their focusing aspect. In the article sample, there was little or no consideration of why a particular aspect of BE is the most relevant for the research and there was little explicit justification of their choices. There was also little critique of the aspects of BE that were used. This suggests that some IS researchers may not be aware of the controversies and limitations of the BE they are using. On the other hand, some articles in the major use McConnel, & Smith, 2015; Wang, & Rao, 2016; Adjerid, Peer, & Acquisti, 2018).

Turning to aspects of BE that have been used in IS research, as mentioned above, the articles with a major use of BE overwhelmingly use contemporary BE. Early theories systems analysis practice, were not used at all. The most used BE aspects in the major use sample were cognitive bias (61.7% of articles), heuristics (42.6%), and dual process theory (42.6%). Bounded rationality, the original foundation of all BE theory, was mentioned in 38.3% of major use articles but the use tended to be providing context for the research rather than acting as foundation theory in its own right. Dua process theory is the foundation of contemporary BE (Kahneman, 2011, Part 1) and should be considered as key to any IS research that involves decision making.

The Results section showed that although heuristics are superficially important for IS research, a closer examination of the articles showed that only seven IS articles of the 1,348 published in the journal sample actually used heuristics as a major reference theory. Further, the analysis showed that only the affect heuristic has been used since 2014. This is in line with a greater acknowledgement of the role of emotions in human behavior, a trend that is evident in IS scholarship. In a sense this trend is a correction away from an overemphasis of cognitive factors in social research.

The derived BE theories, concepts, and methods of prospect theory (used in 31.9% of the major use sample), nudges (12.8%), and debiasing (12.8%) show an increasing popularity over time in IS research. Further, when they are mentioned in an article, they tend to form a major part of the foundation and execution of the research. This major use should continue to increase over time.

Cognitive biases form the most granular level of abstraction in BE and as mentioned This level of abstraction fits the positivist paradigm where a research task is reduced in scope, complexity, and level of abstraction in order to be amenable to experimentation. In terms of bias categories, the literature analysis found that the confirmation, framing, and overconfidence categories were the most popular in IS research. The analysis showed that no bounded awareness biases were mentioned in any way in the article sample. This surprising result will be addressed in more detail in Section 6.3. The Results section also showed that at the individual cognitive bias level, half of the biases from the BE literature were not used in the articles in the samples. This builds on the previous observation that BE use in IS has an ad hoc feel. When using biases, IS researchers seem to focus on a small set of biases and typically focus each study on only one bias. One reason for this focus could be that some biases are much more difficult to research than others, especially in IS field studies. While biases can be effectively studied in a psychology lab, studying IS phenomena in the field can require significantly more effort and time in data collection and analysis. For example, the conjunctive and disjunctive events bias (Teigen et al., 1996) where outcome likelihoods are often overestimated in compound conjunctive problems and underestimated in compound disjunctive problems could affect IS management of large-scale enterprise systems projects. Studying the influence of conjunctive and disjunctive effects on large scale IS project management could significantly improve practice.

Following on from the bias category analysis, the most researched individual biases were framing (19.1% of major use articles), anchoring (10.6%), confirmation (10.6%), and the endowment affect (8.5%). These four biases are among the easiest to understand and have been written about in popular non-academic magazines and into the use of BE. While the 38 cognitive biases are in no way equally important in their negative effects and relevance to IS research, many of the unused biases are of equal or greater relevance and importance to those that were used in the samples. ## 6.2 Why don’t we use behavioral economics in IS research projects?

As discussed in Section 2, BE is a complex set of theories, phenomena, methods, and processes with overlapping concepts and effects and suffers from significant confusion that is caused by aspects of BE being presented in the literature under different names or titles. The complexity, even messiness, of BE could be a major impediment to BE use. It may also be a reason why most IS articles that use BE concentrate on only one aspect of BE; it can take a long time to learn about each particular aspect of BE, so to master a number of BE aspects may involve time that some researchers are not willing, or able, to commit to.

A more likely reason for the slow take up of BE reference theory in IS could be the conservative nature of academic activity. This conservatism can lead to researchers studying areas and using theories long after their relevance and utility has waned. Researchers devote significant time and effort to learn theories, design and execute a research project, and publish in a scientific journal. They can become resistant to changing the foundations that they have mastered, even when there is little or no evidence for the efficacy of the theory. Kahneman termed this situation theory-induced blindness; once you have accepted a theory and use it as a tool in your thinking, it is extraordinarily difficult to notice its flaws. If you come upon an observation that does not seem to fit the model, you assume that there must be a perfectly good explanation that you are somehow missing” (Kahneman, 2011, p. 277). IS researchers have increasingly limiting their attention to prior work on their topics within their own paradigm, ignoring contributions within other paradigms.” Street and Ward (2019) in a critique of IS journal reviews identified an ideological bias where “well-known theories and orthodoxies are favored over less well-known ones” (p. 55). It can be difficult for new theories to displace reference theory that has been used for many years.

The use of rational economic models as descriptive theories in IS research is an example of theory-induced blindness. Prescriptive models should only be used as descriptive theory with significant empirical support. Rational decision models from neoclassical economics does not have such support. The same validity criteria also applies to descriptive theories of decision making. The phase model of decision making of early BE is a product of its time and is a theory that has not stood up to empirical validation. Even though it lacks descriptive validity it continues to be popular in many IS studies. The use of this model should be augmented with the models, theories, and processes of contemporary BE.

## 6.3 How could we use behavioral economics in IS research projects?

Despite justifiable concerns about the use of theory in IS research (Avison & Malaurent, 2014; Hirschheim, 2019), for much of IS BE provides a well-researched and validated theory foundation especially in the areas concerned with the management and governance of IT, IT development and use, and directly supporting decision making in organizations. IS researchers should use the most appropriate reference theory available and BE currently provides the best descriptive foundation theories of decision making as well as some prescriptive methods for interventions in organizations. The literature analysis in this paper shows that BE can be a valuable and effective reference theory for many areas of IS research.

One strategy in overcoming the constraints to using “new” theory in IS is to increase the awareness of the BE theory base for IS researchers. Section 2 of this paper, and in particular Tables 1 through 5, and 23 and 24 below, can be used to locate a concept or effect that could be relevant to a particular IS research project. The classic, wellcited, BE publications in the tables should provide a start to understanding how each concept could be used in IS research as can the identification of IS areas that can use each BE aspect. In this way the tables can be used to guide further reading and to assess if a particular aspect of BE is relevant to a particular research project.

There are two major ways of considering how BE could be used in future IS research: first by identifying areas of BE that have not been used in IS research, or not used to their potential, and secondly, considering those areas of IS that could benefit from a greater use of BE. Surprisingly, dual process theory is a BE area that could feature more in IS research. The literature analysis found dual process theory was used mostly in the samples to provide context for the research. The lack of use of dual process theory as a major foundation theory could be a consequence of the positivist orientation of the samples where research designs favor the lowest levels of theoretical abstraction. Yet, an understanding of the roles, strengths, and interactions of the two cognitive systems could potentially change the nature and content of IS development methods and IT management processes.

The most obvious aspect of BE that can be expanded in IS research is to include the set of 19 biases that were not mentioned in the samples. For example, the literature analysis found that none of the bounded awareness biases featured in the article samples. Bounded awareness biases involve systematic and persistent failures to blindness (Mitroff et al., 2002; Tenbrunsel & Messick, 2004). Under this bias decision makers may not notice obvious change, especially if it occurs over time in small frequent episodes. This bias is especially important for business intelligence reporting and data analytics. Developers of these systems need to be aware of the potential of reducing decision-making effectiveness through the frequent presentation of information to users. Another bias that was absent in the Basket of Eight sample is the completeness bias which is sometimes termed overprecision. The action of the completeness bias is to create a perception of an apparently complete or logical data presentation which can stop the search for omissions or errors (McKenzie et al., 2008). This bias can also adversely affect reporting and analytics systems.

Another bias that was not used in the article samples was the hindsight bias (Hertwig et al., 2003), which affects our ability to interpret and learn from past decisions. This bias is one of the strongest in its effect and is particularly relevant to project management. IT projects have a persistent reputation for exceeding timelines and budgets. The hindsight bias can lead IT managers to overestimate their ability to judge the time, personnel, and other resources required for a project. As a result, failures in judgement about a completed project do not always lead to improved decision making in the next because of the action of the hindsight bias. Escalation biases have been successfully used by IT management researchers and other areas of IS could benefit from studying these effects.

Turning to other areas of IS, in the general DSS discipline and in particular executive information systems and business intelligence, research has had great difficulty in studying top management decision making (Adam & Murphy, 1995; Arnott, 2010). In practice, IT departments, consultants, and vendors have had a similar lack of success known for over 40 years that senior executives face radically different decision tasks and environments than other managers and professionals (Mintzberg, 1973; Kotter, 1982; Carpenter, Gelatkanycz & Saunders, 2004; Mintzberg, 2009). Yet, organizational IT departments, consultants, and vendors mostly use philosophies, methods, and technologies related to large-scale operational enterprise systems to support these most senior users. BE theories could be used to provide a better understanding of how these senior executives make decisions and how to support decision making is common for top management. There are many possible consequent biases that impact this decision making and debiasing them could be a rich area for IS research.

One of the largest areas of IS research over the last 25 years has been about how people adopt and use various types of IS (Taylor et al., 2010). IS as a discipline has relied on theories like the technology acceptance model (TAM), the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT), and the task technology fit model (TTF) to investigate how users can effectively adopt and use IT systems. Venkatesh, Davis, and Morris (2010, p. 279) in considering the history and status of these theories in IS adoption research argued that “an excessive focus on replication and minor ‘tweaking of existing models can hinder progress both in the area of technology adoption and in information systems in general.” This focus can add to the conservatism of IS research described in the previous sub-section. BE research has much to offer IS in this area. Dual process theory can provide a context for decisions on when or whether to use a certain system, as can prospect theory. Many of the cognitive biases, especially overconfidence, confirmation, and bounded awareness biases, may help explain use and adoption behaviors in more nuanced and fundamental ways than the current IS expanded the understanding of UTAUT studies by using the anchoring and adjustment effect from BE; Chen and Konfaris (2015) used the overconfidence bias to investigate why normally beneficial DSS features can lead to negative outcomes; and Schmueli, Pliskin, and Fink (2016a) used BE to examine an area of software development that has resisted explanation by previous studies.

The literature analysis’s finding of the dominance of positivist experimental and quasiexperimental methods in the article samples hints at further possibilities for BEfounded IS research. Other methods, especially case studies, action research, and design science, could use BE to investigate virtually all aspects of IS. Case studies can illuminate the nature of IS/IT phenomena in ways that are not possible with experiments and surveys. The use of descriptive BE theory in IS case studies, particularly exploratory case studies that aim for deep understanding of a development or management process or method could lead to a significant increase in our knowledge of IS phenomena. Design science is an increasingly important method for IS research. A difficulty with some design science research is the lack of an organizing construct or method to guide an intervention. Nudges are one aspect of BE that could provide this overall direction, debiasing is another. Both these aspects of BE could also help with the convincing evaluation of design science artifacts and action research interventions.

It is important to note that IS is not always an importer of reference theory. IS research that uses BE as foundation theory can in turn contribute to BE knowledge. In this way the use of BE in IS research need not just be a one-way transfer of knowledge. For example, in the major BE-use sample, Zheng et al. (2018) added hedonic principles to prospect theory to explain behavior in crowdsourcing contests. This is a significant contribution to IS theory but also a contribution to BE. Another IS contribution to BE researchers. These researchers have focused on identifying and understanding the causes and nature of the biases rather than developing processes to mitigate the negative effects of the biases. IS has a lot to offer to debiasing efforts as its systems analysis and design methods can be used or modified to debias decision making. (2006), Bhandari et al. (2008), and Cheng and Wu (2010).

## 7. Concluding Comments

Much of IS research concerns human behavior, especially human decision making.

The current orthodoxy for understanding this behavior is behavioral economics. IS researchers should use the highest quality and the most relevant reference theory available for their work, so it follows that IS research that addresses human behavior in some way should consider using BE as a part of its theory foundation. This does not mean that BE should feature in all these projects, only that it should be seriously considered.

This paper adds to previous work by Sage (1981), Browne and Parsons (2012), Goes (2013), Fleischmann et al. (2014), and Arnott and Gao (2019) that urged IS scholars to make significant use of BE foundation theory. The paper has provided an overview of the BE discipline, provided a critical analysis of how BE is used in IS research, of these aspects of BE. This guide to the detailed use of BE in IS research is a major contribution of the paper.

Before summarizing and concluding the paper, it is appropriate to consider the limitations of the empirical work and to make an observation related to the review method. The first limitation relates to sampling; no sample is a perfect representation of its population. This research used a small number of high-quality journals to represent general IS publication. However, important uses of BE in IS research could have been published outside the Basket of 8. Further, some IS articles use BE ideas but do not cite any BE articles; these would have escaped our sampling procedure. As was discussed in Section 4.2, arriving at a sample of BE-using articles in a reliable way was very time consuming. Attempting to rigorously sample BE use in all IS-related journals may be impossible. For the reasons given in Section 4.2 we believe our sample is a reasonable representation of all IS publication. The second limitation relates to the subjective coding of some items in the protocol used for the literature analysis. The most important of these items is the degree of use of BE in an article. This item is important as it was used to determine the second BE-using sample – those articles with a major use of BE as reference theory. The quality of this sample depends on the judgment of this item. Some other items in the protocol involved free text coding that was later analyzed and classified. The greatest care was taken with the coding of these items and we believe that other researchers could generate similar data using the project’s protocol and codebook.

In addition to the limitations of this research, it is appropriate to comment of the efficacy of the bibliometric content analysis approach that was the empirical method used in theory. As a result, a citation analysis would have reported that prospect theory was the most used aspect of BE in IS research. However, when the actual content of the articles was examined (Table 19), prospect theory although highly cited was only meaningfully used by 20.4% of BE-using IS articles (31.9% of major using articles). From the content analysis it emerged that cognitive biases are in fact the most significant aspect of BE for IS research (61.7% of major use articles). Citation of a theory does not always mean the cited theory is integral to the research and considering the actual use of a citation provides a richer and more accurate understanding. However, the analysis of citations as an adjunct and guide t the content analysis has proven useful in this research.

In conclusion, the empirical part of this paper found that many IS researchers do have a broad understanding of contemporary behavioral economics theory but the fraction of IS articles using the theory is relatively small for such a major behavioral theory. The use of BE as major reference theory in the literature analysis has an ad hoc feel. Researchers tend to adopt a specific aspect of BE that they believe is relevant to their research, often with little explicit justification and little critique. E-commerce was the IS area most researched with BE reference theory, while surprisingly, DSS was the least researched. Operational tasks dominated BE-using IS research which is strongly positivist in nature. Experiments and field studies are used in the majority of studies. Cognitive bias is the BE aspect most used in IS research. The confirmation, framing, and overconfidence bias categories were the most popular in IS research, and there were no bounded awareness biases used in the analyzed research. The derived BE theories, concepts, and methods of prospect theory, nudges, and debiasing show an increasing popularity over time.

In the more speculative part of the paper it was argued that dual process theory is a fundamentally in IS research and that an understanding of the roles, strengths, and interactions of the two cognitive systems could change the nature and content of IS development methods, IT management processes, and support for senior management decision making. The most obvious aspect of BE that can be expanded in IS research is the set of 19 biases that were not mentioned in the samples. Many of the cognitive biases, especially overconfidence and bounded awareness biases, may help explain use and adoption behaviors in more nuanced and fundamental ways than the current IS theory set. Research methods other than experiments and field studies, especially case studies and design science, could effectively use BE to investigate virtually any aspect of IS. This could lead to a more detailed understanding of key IS phenomena. A major IS contribution to BE knowledge could come in the debiasing area. Finally, we believe that BE reference theory has the potential to positively transform significant areas of IS research.
