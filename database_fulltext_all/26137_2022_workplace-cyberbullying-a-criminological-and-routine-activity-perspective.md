# Workplace cyberbullying: A criminological and routine activity perspective

Sixuan Zhang<sup>1</sup> , Dorothy Leidner<sup>2</sup>, Xin Cao<sup>1</sup> and Ning Liu<sup>1</sup>

Journal of Information Technology 1–29 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2021 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/02683962211027888DOI: 10.1177/02683962211027888 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Extant research on the antecedents of workplace cyberbullying pays little attention to the role of perpetrator traits in influencing workplace cyberbullying, as well as the unique occurrence context that distinguishes workplace cyberbullying with juvenile cyberbullying, workplace bullying, and adult cyberbullying in general. To fill these gaps, we consider the antecedents of workplace cyberbullying under the theoretical lens of the general theory of crime and routine activities theory. We build a model incorporating low self-control, a widely discussed perpetrator trait in criminology theories, with three types of routine activities representing the unique occurrence context for workplace cyberbullying—mWork, boundary-spanning in enterprise social media, and proactive email checking. We tested our model with 2025 employees in the United States. Our findings demonstrate that low self-control and the three routine activities are strong motivators for workplace cyberbullying. Our findings further show that the effect of low self-control on workplace cyberbullying is amplified by the three routine activities. The study contributes to our understanding of why workplace cyberbullying occurs and offers potential implications for managers interested in reducing incidences of workplace cyberbullying in their organization.

## Keywords

Workplace cyberbullying, antecedents, general theory of crime, routine activity theory, low self-control, mWork, boundary-spanning in ESM, proactive email checking

## Introduction

Workplace cyberbullying is increasingly being recognized as an important issue facing organizations with recent studies across various countries finding that between 2.6% and 17% of employees report being victims of cyberbullying (Cassidy et al., 2014; Coyne et al., 2017; Forssell, 2016; Gardner et al., 2016). Defined as an incident in which an employee enacts work-related negative acts toward her or his supervisors, coworkers, or subordinates by means of information and communication technologies (ICTs; Zhang and Leidner, 2018), workplace cyberbullying entails a power imbalance, that is, a perpetrator has more formal (i.e. higher hierarchical status) or informal (i.e. networks of people) power than a target, making it difficult for the target to defend him/herself against the negative acts (Zhang and Leidner, 2014).

While previous research into youth cyberbullying and workplace bullying set a foundation for understanding workplace cyberbullying, there are important differences that underscore the need for research specifically focusing on workplace cyberbullying. First, workplace cyberbullying cannot be simply conceptualized as youth cyberbullying moving to the workplace (Vranjes et al., 2018b) because most juvenile cyberbullying entails the intent to harm (Kowalski et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2008). However, intent to harm is not a defining criterion in workplace cyberbullying; indeed, perpetrators of workplace cyberbullying might have as an intent to increase productivity and might not even recognize their own behavior as bullying, such as when a supervisor publicly criticizes a subordinate’s work output with the goal of urging the subordinate to elevate his or her work (Coyne et al., 2017). Second, workplace cyberbullying is different from workplace bullying in that ICTs blur employee work–life boundaries thereby extending the reach of cyberbullying to the home domain, a situation seldomly seen in non-cyber forms of workplace bullying (D’Cruz and Noronha, 2013). Moreover, ICTs enable a variety of new means to cyberbully and, thanks to the perceived invisibility, asynchrony, or anonymity embedded in many workplace ICTs, remove barriers for perpetrators (Slonje and Smith, 2008; Zhang and Leidner, 2018). In addition, the concreteness and permanence features of cyberbullying instances make the repercussions of cyberbullying difficult both to escape and endure (D’Cruz and Noronha, 2013). Finally, because of the enduring repercussions of individual cyberbullying incidences, workplace cyberbullying includes one-off behaviors, whereas workplace bullying is typically framed as frequent and persistent negative acts perpetrated by the same individual against the same victim (Farley et al., 2016; Mackey et al., 2018; Vranjes et al., 2018b). Indeed, many one-off cyberbullying acts (i.e. unfairly blame a subordinate’s work under public network) often result in frequent and persistent injuries of victims because of the constant sharing (Vranjes et al., 2018b).

For victims of workplace cyberbullying, the potential negative consequences are many, including mental strain (D’Cruz and Noronha, 2018; Farley et al., 2015), anxiety and depression (D’Cruz and Noronha, 2013; Kowalski et al., 2018), low job satisfaction (Coyne et al., 2017; Snyman and Loh, 2015), counterproductive work behaviors (Keskin et al., 2016), and intention to quit (Muhonen et al., 2017). Given the severity of these consequences to victims, research seeking to understand why individuals commit cyberbullying acts in the workplace is emerging. Early research in this area has focused on perpetrator motivations (Choi, 2018), cyber features (Zhang and Leidner, 2018), and organizational factors (Gardner et al., 2016) to predict cyberbullying incidences. In terms of perpetrator motivation, research has found that employees’ perceptions of the expectations of superiors and employees’ belief that their colleagues do not engage in cyberbullying negatively affects their own intention to cyberbully in the workplace (Choi, 2018). In terms of cyber features, research has found that neutralization techniques adopted by perpetrators are more likely to lead to workplace cyberbullying when perpetrators perceive invisibility, asynchrony, and anonymity (Zhang and Leidner, 2018). And in terms of organizational factors, there is evidence that organizational antibullying strategies to dissuade workplace cyberbullying can be effective although style of leadership is not influential (Gardner et al., 2016).

While these studies shed light on the motivations for workplace cyberbullying, we highlight two issues that require further study. First, none of these studies to date have explored the role of perpetrator traits in motivating workplace cyberbullying. Studies in the context of workplace bullying as well as juvenile and adult cyberbullying have discovered that Machiavellianism<sup>1</sup> (Pilch and Turska,

2015), low self-control (Bayraktar et al., 2015), and low self-esteem (Lianos and McGrath, 2018) are perpetrator traits related to bullying behavior. These results might not be directly applicable to workplace cyberbullying. Cyber features such as anonymity, invisibility, and asynchrony might interact with personality traits to create an online disinhibition effect, making negative acts such as workplace cyberbullying more frequent and intense (Suler, 2004). Second, none of these studies have accounted for the organizational cyber context in which workplace cyberbullying takes place. Certain features of the organizational cyber context, for example, a highly mobile workforce and routine use of enterprise social media (ESM), might likewise magnify the effect of perpetrator traits on workplace cyberbullying (Pilch and Turska, 2015). Exploring the effects of perpetrator traits, especially their interaction with the workplace cyber context and cyber features, will advance our understanding of why some individuals are prone to workplace cyberbullying. Such knowledge is useful both theoretically in explaining the workplace cyberbullying phenomenon, and practically, in providing insights for organizational policies designed to deter such behavior. With this in view, our research question is: how do perpetrator traits and the organizational cyber context influence workplace cyberbullying.

To address our question, we consider the antecedents of workplace cyberbullying under the theoretical lens of the general theory of crime (GTC; Gottfredson and Hirschi, 1990) and routine activities theory (RAT; Cohen and Felson, 1979). At the individual level, the GTC enables us to examine how perpetrator traits motivate workplace cyberbullying behaviors. At the organizational level, RAT provides us opportunities to explore how organizational cyber context influences workplace cyberbullying. Next, we describe both theories, as they relate to our model, and present our hypotheses. This is followed by our method, analysis, discussion, and conclusions.

## The GTC

In this section, we draw upon GTC to explore the role of perpetrator traits in motivating workplace cyberbullying. Developed by Michael R. Gottfredson and Travis Hirschi in 1990, GTC is one of the most important criminology theories in Western countries. The theory represents a trend in criminology that attributes crime and analogous behaviors to the life course of the family (Grasmick et al., 1993). Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990) define crime and analogous behaviors as acts “of force and fraud undertaken in pursuit of self-interest” (p. 15). Self-interest is equivalent to pleasure and benefits (Cochran et al., 1998). Crime and analogous behaviors are not always equated with illegal behaviors.<sup>2</sup> That is, they might be illegal in some cultures but not in others, and might be illegal at some points in the history of a society but not at others (Grasmick et al., 1993). Such a description applies to the perceptions and attitudes of workplace cyberbullying in different societies and historical periods. For example, 25 states in the United States have laws against certain cyberbullying acts (Wright, 2016), while in most eastern countries cyberbullying at work is still not illegal.

According to GTC, criminal and analogous behaviors are explained by the personality trait of low self-control in the perpetrator and the presence of criminal opportunity (Bolin, 2004). Low self-control is an enduring and stable trait that develops in children when deviant behaviors are not effectively monitored, recognized, and punished by parents (Gottfredson and Hirschi, 1990). Once established in early childhood, such a personality trait remains relatively stable. People with low self-control tend to be impulsive and insensitive to others, tend to favor physical over mental activities, tend to be risk-taking and short-term oriented, and tend to be temperamental (Grasmick et al., 1993; Longshore and Turner, 1998).

Low self-control has been shown to explain deviant behaviors across cultures and societies (see Table 5 in Appendix 1). In the context of workplace bullying, personality traits such as narcissism, psychoticism, aggression, and sensation seeking, which are related to the low selfcontrol attributes of insensitivity toward others, impulsivity, and risk-taking (LeBreton et al., 2006; Linton and Power, 2013; Pilch and Turska, 2015), are associated with workplace bullying. And research on antecedents of juve nile cyberbullying and general adult cyberbullying ha shown a positive relationship between low self-control and cyberbullying incidents (Li et al., 2016; Lianos and McGrath, 2018; Vazsonyi et al., 2012; Wong et al., 2018). In the specific context of workplace cyberbullying, there are several reasons to expect that the personality trait of low self-control will be related to the perpetration of workplace cyberbullying. First, the cyber context provides ample opportunity for deviant behavior, with easy access to various electronic tools that enable those who are bad-tempered to release their negative emotions by publicizing humiliat ing information. Second, the cyber context enables those with impulsive tendencies and short-term thinking to act out their impulsions very quickly without reflecting on the long-term consequences. The notion of impulsivity include a tendency to conduct behaviors that bring immediate gratification (Gottfredson and Hirschi, 1990). Workplace cyber bullies may perpetrate for the instant release of workplace stressor–evoked negative emotions (Vranjes et al., 2018a). They may justify their negative acts as a response to the frustration and conflicts that happen at work (Li et al., 2016). Moreover, since individuals with low self-control tend to be risk-takers, they will be comfortable ignoring potential organizational sanctions against cyberbullying behaviors (Li et al., 2018). Finally, the self-centered nature of individuals with low self-control makes them less attune to the suffering of others (Grasmick et al., 1993), thereby making it easier for them to ignore the hurt their behavior causes others and deny the injury of the victims (Zhang and Leidner, 2018). Hence, we hypothesize that:

H1. Low self-control in an individual is positively associated with the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

While GTC helps explain the role of low self-control in predicting crime and analogous behaviors, the theory’s discussion of the criminal opportunity concept is relatively insufficient (Bobbio et al., 2019; Grasmick et al., 1993; Sellers, 1999; Seipel and Eilfer, 2010). To better understand criminal opportunity and its effects on crime and analogous behaviors, we turn to RAT (Cohen and Felson, 1979), as recommended by Gottfredson and Hirschi (1990).

## The RAT

To understand the paradoxical trends between 1947 and 1974 when the crime rate in the United States increased with the social and economic conditions, Cohen and Felson (1979) developed RAT. Cohen and Felson (1979) argued that in the aftermath of the second world war, the United States experienced a major structural change of routine activities from household and family activities to nonhousehold–nonfamily activities. Routine activities occurring within or near the home and with family members generally entail a lower risk of criminal victimization because of capable guardianship. Nonhousehold–nonfamily activities increase the risk of lone victimization in places such as streets and parks and the chance of burglary and illegal removal of vehicles parked away from home. Based on these observations, the theory posits that structural changes in routine activity patterns can influence the crime rate by increasing the probability of the convergence of the elements necessary for a crime to occur (Groff, 2008) with the elements consisting of a motivated offender, a suitable target, and the absence of a capable guardian in a convergence of time and space (Jansen and Leukfeldt, 2016).

RAT does not explain why individuals are inclined to commit crimes but rather takes criminal inclination as given and focuses on examining how the spatial and temporal structure of routine activities provides opportunities for individuals to transfer their inclinations into criminal behaviors (Yar, 2005). Hence, RAT complements GTC by providing a theoretical approach to analyze the criminal opportunities for criminal inclination, that is, low self-control. While GTC claims that the interaction between low self-control and criminal opportunities predict crime and analogous behaviors, RAT proposes that criminal opportunities also predict deviant behaviors. Various empirical studies have integrated the theoretical lens of both theories to predict deviant behaviors (for details, see Table 6 in Appendix 1) and many studies have adopted RAT to predict cyber deviances (for details, see Table 7 in Appendix 1). According to Cohen and Felson (1979), routine activities are defined as “any recurrent and prevalent activities which provide for basic population and individual needs” (p.593). In this study, we have identified three types of routine activities, mWork, boundary-spanning (BS) in ESM, and proactive email checking,<sup>3</sup> which are brought by the material property of the three IT artifacts ubiquitous in the workplace, that are, mobile devices, ESM, and email (Table 2).<sup>4</sup> These three routine activities may influence the frequency of workplace cyberbullying behaviors by affecting how the motivated perpetrators virtually reach suitable targets in the absence of capable guardians in the organizational cyber environment. ## mWork

The development of ICT such as mobile Internet has fundamentally changed when, where, and how employees engage in work tasks (Boswell and Olson-Buchanan, 2007). The pervasiveness of mobile devices has increased employee opportunity to engage in work during family time. For example, the iPass mobile workforce report found that more than 60% of mobile workers sleep with their smartphones (iPass, 2011). The Small Business Trend also reported that 51% of Americans read, answer work calls and/or emails in the bathroom on their smartphones (Starr, 2019).

mWork is defined as the activities of using mobile devices with Internet access to conduct work tasks during family time (Ferguson et al., 2016). In the context of workplace cyberbullying, mWork increases the chances that a motivated offender virtually reaches a suitable target in the absence of capable guardians. The pervasiveness nature of mobile devices enable potential workplace cyberbullies to easily access suitable targets (accessibility of target; D’Cruz and Noronha, 2013). The pervasiveness of mobile devices also renders it more difficult for targets hard to escape from the work-related bullying acts (target with low inertia; Holt et al., 2016). Moreover, in the family domains, potential cyberbullies are physically and psychologically distant from social guardians such as supportive bystanders to the victims and organizational policies against cyberbullying at work. Those cyberbullies can also virtually escape from such potential physical guardians such as corporate filtering and antibullying software (absence of capable guardians).

mWork, therefore, increases the opportunities for workplace cyberbullying. According to RAT, criminal opportunities predict the corresponding deviant behaviors. Hence, employees who frequently use mobile devices to conduct work in the family domain are more likely to engage in workplace cyberbullying behaviors because there are more opportunities to virtually reach suitable targets in the absence of corporate guardianship. Potential workplace cyberbullies are thus more likely to engage in cyberbullying if they are active mWorkers as opposed to if they are not. We, therefore, make the following two hypotheses:

H2a. The frequency that an individual engages in mWork is positively associated with the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

H2b. The frequency that an individual engages in mWork positively moderates the relationship between low self-control in the individual and the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

## BS in ESM

The success of today’s businesses relies heavily on the efficient flow of BS knowledge (Marrone et al., 2007). This has led to the growing adoption of ESM technologies, which aim to overcome detrimental knowledge silos within an organization and to enhance BS activities (Van Osch et al., 2015). Boundary-spanning is referred to as team (or business unit or workgroup) members’ behaviors that are intended to establish relationships and interactions with external members in meeting a team’s performance goals (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992; Marrone et al., 2007). Studies of ESM use indicate that ESM tools facilitate BS activities within organizations by enabling the interactions with relevant external members and the discourse of important information (Van Osch et al., 2016).

Three distinct BS activities that have been proposed and empirically validated in previous literature are representing, coordinating, and scouting (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992; Grabher, 2004; Van Osch et al., 2015). Representing a vertical form of BS involves team members negotiating with corporate hierarchy to protect the team from external pressure and persuade managers or senior managers to support the team (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). Coordinating is a horizontal form of BS that involves interactions across teams aimed at facilitating effective decision-making and coordinating technical or design issues through crossboundary strategizing, planning, and evaluating (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992; Van Osch et al., 2016). Scouting, also a horizontal form of BS, involves general scanning for ideas, information, and expertise from the external team environment (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992).

ESM facilitate these different types of BS activities often as the goal to improving a team’s performance through creating interaction opportunities with external members (value of target) (Van Osch and Steinfield, 2018). ESM, thereby, increase the opportunities that team members have to virtually reach other organizational members outside the team, horizontally or vertically (Van Osch et al., 2015, 2016). Boundary-spanning activities facilitated by ESM enable potential bullies to know where and how to access those targets in the cyberplace (visibility and accessibility of target). External members are more suitable targets than internal members because they are less socially connected with potential perpetrators inside a team. Research shows that individuals who are more socially connected inside a community are less likely to be bullied by the community members (Francioli et al., 2015). This indicates that those who are outside a team (normally less socially connected with the team members) are more likely to be the bullying targets of potential perpetrators inside a team (target with low inertia).

Moreover, cross-boundary bullying behaviors in ESM generally happen outside both the perpetrator’s and the target’s team domains. Bullying targets outside a team are less likely to physically approach and virtually connect with the perpetrators to express their injury and feelings of the bullying acts (target with low inertia). Hence, it can be difficult for social guardians such as team leaders and team members who oppose bullying behaviors to detect and prohibit workplace cyberbullying (absence of capable guardians). We thus hypothesize:

H3a. The frequency that an individual engages in BS in ESM is positively associated with the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

H3b. The frequency that an individual engages in BS in ESM positively moderates the relationship between low self-control in the individual and the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

## Proactive email checking

While several communication technologies are adopted in the contemporary workplace, email remains the most widely used electronic tool for online communication at work (Stich et al., 2019). As an asynchronous communication medium (James, 2016; Sproull and Kiesler, 1986) that allows messages to be sent anytime without interrupting the recipient and to be accumulated in the recipient’s inbox until being handled (Barley et al., 2011), email consumes a significant amount of time and attention of employees (Dabbish et al., 2005). For example, in 2012, the McKinsey Global Institute found that, on average, an employee spends 13 h per week in handling emails, making it the most time-consuming work activity that costs 28% of our work time. To deal with the increasing volume of email, organizational members adopt different emailchecking tactics. Some employees show a pattern of clustering or batching their email checks to certain times of a day (intermittent checkers), while others tend to check emails as soon as they notice a new incoming message (proactive checkers; Dabbish and Kraut, 2006; Mark et al., 2016).

Proactive email checking is the near-continuous checking of email throughout the day. Proactive email checkers are likely to stop and read emails as their system alerts them to an incoming email as opposed to waiting to read the email at self-determined set intervals. Proactive email checking increases the frequency that employees engage in asynchronous email communications compared to those who employ tactics such as batching and predetermined intervals. For proactive email checkers, reading and responding to email is a digression from their current work, whereas for intermittent email checkers, reading and responding to emails is their current work. Because proactive email checkers are interrupting their current work to read and respond to email as they arrive, the proactive email checker may respond in haste to return to their work rather than carefully thinking through their response. Such haste in response is particularly probable for impulsive individuals and increases the potential for poorly crafted messages that are experienced by the recipient as cyberbullying (target with low inertia). Such haste also reduces the likelihood that the proactive email checker considers the potential harmful effects of his or her response and increases the likelihood of mistakes or thoughtless behaviors (e.g. cc-ing too many individuals sensitive information, responding to an entire group instead of an individual with sensitive information) that can be humiliating for the recipient (accessibility of target). Not only might proactive email checking result in unintentional cyberbullying, it is also possible that proactive email checking creates feelings of stress and overwork, which then prompts displaced online aggression as a means of unleashing the stress (Vranjes et al., 2020; value of target). Finally, proactive email checking might be used as a tool of power to force subordinates and/or coworkers to also engage in frequent email checking (Thomas, 2015), in which case proactive email checking itself becomes a form of cyberbullying with negative consequences for team performance and individual well-being (target with low inertia). We thus hypothesize:

H4a. The extent to which an individual engages in proactive email checking is positively associated with the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

H4b. The extent to which an individual engages in proactive email checking moderates the relationship between low self-control in the individual and the individual’s workplace cyberbullying behaviors

## Methods and results

To test our hypotheses, we chose to use an anonymous selfreported cross-sectional survey to collect data and used SmartPLS 3.0 (Ringle et al., 2005), a partial least square (PLS) structural equation modeling (SEM) tool, to analyze the collected data. Next, we describe the methods and results in detail.

## Data collection and advanced sample filtering to improve data quality

A challenging aspect of our research design is to mitigate social desirability biases (Podsakoff et al., 2003) that are likely to occur in the examination of controversial topics such as workplace cyberbullying (Zhang and Leidner, 2018). We thus needed to collect honest responses from our respondents in an anonymous manner (Awad and Ragowsky, 2008; Posey et al., 2015). An anonymous selfreported cross-sectional survey was launched on the specific panel of Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Self-reported cross-sectional surveys have been widely and efficiently used in studies of online deviant behaviors (Hinduja, 2007; Lowry et al., 2016) and workplace deviances (Bennett and Robinson, 2000; Lowry and Moody, 2015), as well as studies that examined GTC (Bobbio et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018) and RAT (Choi, 2008; Vakhitova et al., 2019). In addition, MTurk allowed us to recruit respondents from a variety of demographic backgrounds. It ensures the anonymity between the respondents and the researchers by allowing each respondent to participate in the survey with an anonymous MTurk ID.

To improve data quality, we used MTurk with the Qualtrics online survey that offered strong screening capabilities (Boas et al., 2018; Landers and Behrend, 2015; Lowry et al., 2016). To reach respondents appropriate for our research context, we employed multiple screening questions to ensure that respondents who took the survey met the following criteria: full-time employees who lived in the United States (for consistency in employment status as well as culture and laws related to workplace cyberbullying), worked in companies where emails and ESMs were provided for work, and mobile devices were allowed to be used for work tasks. In addition, we used MTurk screening capabilities to make the survey available only to respondents who had completed at least 50 human intelligence tasks (HITs)<sup>5</sup> in MTurk and whose approval rate for their works by HIT requesters was more than 95% (Peer et al., 2014). We also paid a reasonable amount of compensation for respondents based on US minimum wage per hour.

Multiple techniques adopted from previous literature were implemented to reduce monomethod bias (Cheung et al., 2017; Landers and Behrend, 2015; Lowry et al., 2016). First, we randomized the survey questions and reversed the scaling of half of the questions. Second, we used four randomly presented attention-trap questions to ensure the respondents were being honest and carefully reading and understanding the questions. Third, we emphasized the scientific importance of the study and asked each respondent to confirm that she or he was not a robot at the early stage of the survey. Fourth, we tracked respondents internet protocol (IP) addresses and time spent in completing the survey to identify responses from the same IP address and respondents who rushed through the survey. Finally, we introduced a marker variable<sup>6</sup> (organizational commitment (OC)) to ensure that the common-method bias was absent (Lindell and Whitney, 2001).

## Instrumentation

In terms of instrumentation, all the construct items were measured on a five-point rating scale. The measures for workplace cyberbullying were based on workplace cyberbullying measure (WCM; Farley et al., 2016) and inventory of cyberbullying acts at work (ICA-W; Vranjes et al., 2018b). WCM is a single-factor measure composed of 17 items, including work-related and person-related cyberbullying. ICA-W fits with a three-factor structure including 10 items categorized as person-related, work-related, and intrusive. In measuring our dependent variable, we excluded the person-related items from WCM and the person-related and intrusive items from ICA-W since these items cannot reflect the work-related features of workplace cyberbullying. For example, both the person-related item from WCM “Had negative rumors or gossip spread about you” and the intrusive item from ICA-W “Somebody shares photos or videos of you on the internet to make fun of you” are similar with existing items for measuring juvenile cyberbullying (Menesini et al., 2012) and thus cannot be simply categorized as work-related negative acts.

For the remaining items, we combined the similar items and dropped those lacking work-related features (e.g. “Received messages that have a disrespectful tone”). Since both WCM and ICA-W were developed from the perspective of victims, we adopted an active formulation technique frequently used in previous workplace bullying studies to reframe the remaining items (Lee and Brotheridge, 2006; Li et al., 2016; Linton and Power, 2013; Pilch and Turska, 2015), and finally generated a seven-item measurement for workplace cyberbullying. In addition, the measures for low self-control, mWork, BS in ESM, and proactive email checking were developed based on existing measures and their validity and reliability have been well-established in studies that applied them (Ferguson et al., 2016; Grasmick et al., 1993; Mark et al., 2016; Van Osch and Steinfield, 2016). The measures were further adapted to the workplace cyberbullying context. Full details on measurement with sources, prompts, scaling, and survey logic are presented in Table 8 in Appendix 1.

## Data

A total of 4652 respondents with different ID/IP addresses clicked our survey link on MTurk. Of the 4652 respondents, 61 resided in countries other than the United States,<sup>7</sup> 281 were not full-time workers, and 225 were under the age of 18. We dropped out these respondents, leaving 4085 respondents in the survey. In all, 1180 of the 4085 respond ents reported that their companies did not provide ESM or did not allow them to use mobile devices or email for work, and thus were eliminated. ## Discussion

## Interpretation of results and contributions

Our study makes several contributions to the understanding of workplace cyberbullying. First, at the individual level, we examined the extent to which GTC can account for workplace cyberbullying. While studies of cyberbullying and workplace bullying show certain perpetrator traits as strong predictors of bullying behaviors (Li et al., 2016; Lianos and McGrath, 2018; Linton and Power, 2013; Pilch and Turska, 2015), research on workplace cyberbullying has paid little attention to the role of perpetrator traits in predicting workplace cyberbullying. Our study addresses this unexplored issue by demonstrating that employees with low self-control have a greater tendency to be workplace cyberbullies.

Second, at the organizational level, we are among the first to explore the unique occurrence context for workplace cyberbullying. Based on RAT, we identified three types of routine activities, mWork, BS in ESM, and proactive email checking, that stem from the material property of the three most ubiquitous communication technologies in the contemporary workplace. In terms of mWork, while the impact of the pervasive nature of mobile devices on workplace cyberbullying has been highlighted (D’Cruz and Noronha, 2013; West et al., 2014), little attention has been paid to the extended use of mobile devices for work in the family domains (mWork) and its influence on workplace cyberbullying.<sup>13</sup> Whereas in the past, organizations have been concerned about employees using technology at work to conduct personal affairs, in today’s environment it is work that is being taken into the family domain, creating ever more stress on employees and the potential for both impulsive reactions, careless mistakes, and displaced frus trations. Our findings suggest that mWork indeed amplifie the inclination of individuals with low self-control to engage in workplace cyberbullying. In terms of boundaryspanning in ESM, the extant research has mainly focused on the “bright side” and the many advantages of BS (i.e. team effectiveness, collaboration, knowledge sharing, innovation, etc.; Van Osch and Steinfield, 2016, 2018). Our study takes a different focus by addressing the “dark side,” the potential cost, of boundary-spanning in ESM, and demonstrating that BS in ESM contributes to incidences of cyberbullying against external members. And in terms of email, extant research on email has uncovered many potential negative effects of email such as lowered work engagement, increased work stress, and disrupted work–life balance (Barley et al., 2011; Reinke and Chamorro Premuzic, 2014; Waller and Ragsdell, 2012). Our study extends this research by showing that the potential negative consequences of email extend to cyberbullying, in particular, by those who engage in proactive email checking. Ou study highlights that proactive email checking increases cyberbullying incidences and also intensifies the adopting tactics that increase opportunities for employees to engage in asynchronous email communications, and may further increase opportunities for potential perpetrators to conduct cyberbullying acts.

Finally, previous studies provide weak and inconsistent empirical support for the usefulness of RAT in explaining deviant behaviors that occur in cyberspace. This might be attributed to the lack of principles for applying the main theoretical concepts of RAT to cyberspace (i.e. exposure to risk, proximity to offenders, target attractiveness/suitable target, and capable guardian) (Vakhitova et al., 2015; Yar, 2005). Specifically, most of these studies operationalize the key theoretical concepts of RAT separately (see Table 7 in Appendix 1). Indeed, Cohen and Felson (1979) highlight that it is the convergence in space and time of the three elements, rather than a single element that contributes to the criminal opportunities. In addition, in the cyber world, the line between these concepts is blurred (Vakhitova et al., 2015). Hence, scholars call for studies that develop constructs that consider the main theoretical concepts of RAT simultaneously (Reyns et al., 2011; Tillyer and Eck, 2009). We answer this call in the context of workplace cyberbullying by identifying three routine activity constructs that accounts for the occurrence contexts in which motivated perpetrators virtually reach the suitable targets in the absence of capable guardians.

## Practical implications

Several practical implications can be drawn from our findings. While self-control is a stable trait that is difficult to change (Gottfredson and Hirschi, 1990), our post hoc analysis shows that workplace cyberbullying is more likely to be affected by the three routine activities in the low selfcontrol group. Hence, managers should pay more attention to the three routine activities to intervene and prevent workplace cyberbullying.

Regarding email checking, we recommend employees to adopt an intermittent email-checking strategy at work by decreasing number of unique checks to the email client, clustering or batching their email checking, and checking email by self-interruptions. Managers should develop a work environment in which employees should not feel compelled to check and respond to emails instantly and should avoid sending emails late in the evening and on the weekends (Thomas, 2015). Some employees choose to check email continuously and respond to email immediately when getting involved in emergent work issues. Organizations might also consider the incorporation of intelligence technology with email systems (Zhang and Leidner, 2018). These technological tools could be programmed to detect inappropriate content in images or text and caution senders to recraft the message prior to sending.

## Limitations and conclusions

Our study has several limitations. First, our model was tested with cross-sectional data. Hence, the causation is inferred primarily from the theoretical lens of GTC and RAT. To demonstrate such causation in empirical settings, future research should employ other methodologies such as diary design<sup>14</sup> (Bolger et al., 2003). A diary design not only allows researchers to trace the experience of respondents in a natural setting but also mitigates the retrospection issue by shortening the time span between when the incident happens and when it is recalled (Vranjes et al., 2020).

Second, the generalizability of our findings might be affected by our sampling strategies. For consistency in culture and laws related to workplace cyberbullying, we only allowed employees who lived in the United States to participate in our survey. Moreover, we employed a self-selection technique, which makes our sample composed of respondents who are willing to participate in our survey. Hence, our sample cannot represent respondents who do not want to disclose their workplace cyberbullying behaviors, even in an anonymous manner. Future studies are thus suggested to test our model in other countries and in a more representative sample.

Fourth, while low self-control is a widely discussed trait of perpetrators in criminology theories, other perpetrator traits should also be explored in the context of workplace cyberbullying. In addition, while we aim to examine the unique occurrence context for workplace cyberbullying, we have identified only three types of routine activity constructs. Future research is thus recommended to explore other routine activities and further study how these routine activities affect workplace cyberbullying.

In conclusion, this study integrates GTC and RAT to study cyber deviance. It is one of the few studies that explores the antecedents of workplace cyberbullying and is among the first to investigate the perpetrator traits and unique occurrence environment in the context of workplace cyberbullying. Future research is recommended to build upon the preliminary findings of this study to gain further insights into why individuals engage in workplace cyberbullying and what measures are effective at discouraging the behavior.
