# ITs and structural change: the case of local area network implementation

A.B. (RAMI) SHANI

Stockholm School of Economics, 1383 Stockholm, Sweden and College of Business, Cal Poly State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

JAMES A. SENA

College of Business, Cal Poly State University, San Luis Obispo, CA 93407, USA

New ITs are having a widespread impact on information flow within and between firms, on management practices and, on the basic processes and the structural configurations of the organization. This paper investigates a specific information system – Local Area Network – and its impact on the structural configuration of the firm. An initial discussion about a proposed framework for analysis is followed by an examination of the implementation of a local area network in a software development organization.

## Introduction

The revolutions of new technologies and IT are changing the dynamics of the work place. Telecommunication network systems using microcomputers, terminals, processors, mainframes and software are being implemented at an unprecedented rate. New organizational forms and processes are beginning to emerge in order to cope with environmental and technological forces and trends. Business performance and productivity are directly effected by the changes (Sena and Shani, 1993).

The advent of global real-time information systems that involve the non-tangible transmission of machine-readable data for processing, storage and/or retrieval should alter the ways firms organize their activities (Hagstrom, 1991). Although information systems, new technologies for information processing, organizational forms and structures have prompted much attention and research separately, the intersecting set of the three has attracted surprisingly little scholarly interest.

Sociotechnical System thinking coupled with Strategic thinking provides a basic language and analytical framework that can advance the investigation of the relationship between information systems and structural configurations (Checkland, 1990; Grant et al., 1991). The objective of this paper is to establish a framework that can aid managers in both deciding the most appropriate information-system-technology for their company and in the examination of the complex causal relationships between information systems and the organizational configurations. The framework that we propose is based on our observation and analysis of the evolution of the implementation of Local Area Networks (LANs) in a firms.

## Towards an alternative approach

Given complex and difficult-to-quantify costs and benefits of alternative ITs, and given the organization-wide implications of introducing them, standard approaches may not be appropriate for appraising the potential investment in the new ITs. This paper proposes a promising approach that recognizes and integrates the strategic and the sociotechnical character of the investment and applies the principles of sociotechnical system and strategic analyses to these decisions. Investments in IT are strategic in the sense that they are important – they are critical to the overall performance of the firm, they involve significant resource commitments, they are long term, they may require major realignment of the firm, and they are not easily reversible. A contingency-based strategic approach to IT recognizes that the optimal IT for a business is dependent on the nature of the business environment, the strategic goals which the firm is pursuing, the resources which the firm has available to it and, the business design configurations and processes. Figure 1 illustrates the basic framework for analysis.

At the most basic level the sociotechnical systems perspective considers every organization to be made up of a social subsystem (the people) using tools, techniques and knowledge (the technical subsystem) to produce a product or a service valued by the environmental subsystem (i.e., Trist, 1982). The degree to which the design of technical subsystem, social subsystems, and the environmental subsystem are integrated determines how successful and competitive the organization will be (Pava, 1986). Thus, while every organization is perceived as a sociotechnical system, not every organization is designed using sociotechnical systems design principles, methods, processes and philosophy (Kolodny and Dresner, 1986; Shani and Elliott, 1989; Pasmore, 1988, Susman and Chase, 1986). The economic results of organizations designed according to sociotechnical system design principles are significantly better than comparable organizations of conventional design (Hanna, 1988; Pasmore, 1988; Stebbins and Shani, 1989).

The framework identifies five key factors that have an impact on organizational performance. The framework is especially useful when one is attempting to understand why an organization's results are what they are and to plan changes that will lead to improved results. Figure 1 illustrates organizational performance as an outcome that is influenced by how well the five critical factors are balanced (or fit) with one another. It also depicts the cause-and-effect chain that produces business performance.

The business situation factor is comprised of elements and forces in the market place in which the organization competes. The factor of strategic goals of the firm refers to the elements that influence the organization's ability to address issues associated with environmental uncertainty. Different ITs offer different benefits with regard to competitiveness, flexibility, productivity, quality improvement, and efficiency. The primary requirement is that the IT chosen is consistent with and supports the strategic goals of the firm. In selecting the most appropriate IT a firm must explicitly define its IT operations. The firm's resource base refers to the firm's internal resources such as physical resources, financial resources, technology, and employee skills and knowledge.

The business design elements are concerned with the firm's tools and organization to achieve its strategy such as tasks, structure, reward systems, control systems, decision making processes, and organizational culture.

The organizational culture refers to elements such as the work habits and practices, assumptions, values, rites, rituals and emergent role systems. Top management investment in new IT requires the adjustment of organizational structure to the needs of the IT being adopted. Bureaucratic structures with high level functional specialization and numerous levels of hierarchy are suited to the efficient operation of highly mechanized operations under static conditions. Conversely, flexible information systems require organizations with high levels of inter-functional collaboration. The business performance factor refers to the multiple methods and criteria used by the firm to measure its success.

A fundamental axiom of Sociotechnical system thinking is that whatever decisions are made about or within any one of the organizational subsystems they should meet the demands of the remaining others (e.g. Shani et al. 1992). Any decision about work design should meet the demands of the technical, social and environmental subsystems. In the same vein, a strategic decision to increase the market share of a specific product requires not only understanding of the industry, competition, customers demand and marketing expertise but also the technological ability to increase production, the trained people to do the work and the organization design to support it.

A strategic decision to move towards a LAN environment as a means of conducting business needs to follow a careful assessment and a matching process of the overall business strategy and goals, business situation, business resource base and the business design configuration. Cultural and quality issues may impact the selection and use of the most appropriate network system software. A redesign of a company's organizational structure and processes could be warranted in order to achieve full utilization of the new systems' potential.

## The software development company

The case that we focus on is a software development company (SDC) that has recently undergone the transition from stand-alone personal computer systems to a series of LANs. We will examine this organization by using the framework outlined above, while focusing on the changes that have taken place as a result of the introduction of the new IT. The introduction of the LAN can be seen as an integral part of the company's natural evolution. The decision of the company to install a LAN was based on competitive positioning.

## The business situation and strategic goals

The primary products produced by SDC are PC and network utility software for disk and file server management as well as utility tools for menu development. These products are developed internally by the software engineering staff and through the use of contractors. At the time of the LAN implementation SDC maintained a relatively small sales and marketing staff that operated through distributors and resellers. In their product line they have a wide market penetration and visibility. Their disk and server management products are the industry leaders.

To achieve a leadership position in its niche within the software industry – the firm's strategic goal – SDC was faced with making several major decisions regarding venture capital, growth, market penetration, product concentration and diversity, customer relations, and competitive positioning. One major decision was to market their products internationally. This necessitated that SDC translate their software products and documentation into multiple foreign languages. The LAN introduction was viewed as an enabling technology that complemented the knowledge worker's environment. They also had to develop an international marketing and sales staff. The anomaly of an international group that spanned their traditional Sales and Marketing departments is depicted in Figure 2. This confounded their computing problem by indicating the need for a shared development system. Under such a system all software versions needed to be maintained in a single library such that all software developers and translators have access to a common, standard set of software. There was a need to better manage the software development and support process.

## Business design configurations and processes

SDC, prior to the introduction of the networks, was organized into six departments – two software engineering groups, accounting, sales, marketing, and administration. The structure of the organization is depicted in Figure 2.

There were several work groups that overlapped these departments – Technical Support and International. Compounding this overlap is the issue of owner-management. There were three owners and founders of SDC: the President, a marketing executive; the Executive Vice President and Engineering Vice President – both software engineers. The Executive Vice President handled the support of the day-to-day operations of Sales, Marketing, and Accounting. He was also responsible for overseeing the development of one of the major product lines.

The Engineering Vice President was not involved in any of the support activities other than Technical Support for existing and planned products. He managed and directed the remaining planned and existing product lines. Technical Support consisted of a single computer with the various products on-line to handle troubleshooting and technical inquiries. Any questions that he could not answer he would direct to the appropriate software engineer. Thus a significant amount of developer time was spent aiding the technical support effort.

The backlog of unanswered calls for assistance, the lack of timely response to user requests and/or inability to respond created an external image of customer dissatisfaction with technical support. The customer database could not be verified when a potential unlicensed caller would ask for assistance. In essence, free advice and even upgrades were given to users without verifying actual ownership.

Marketing and Sales were directed by the President. He employed two managers who operated at a low level of decision making. An international Manager, operating out of a European office, coordinated Sales and Marketing efforts with these managers and the President. Liaison with Sales and Marketing was accomplished by a link-pin person who coordinated the beta testing on Customer sites with Marketing, Sales and technical aspects with Sales and Accounting, and quality control and scheduling with Production.

The other two departments, Production and Accounting, operated relatively autonomously. Accounting had to approve any major sale involving credit. This frequently caused a delay in order processing. Each department was self sustaining. The Controller made an effort to tightly control and manage finances throughout the organization without a defined policy. Production relied on the hiring of college students and temporary help to assemble the software products for distribution to the distributors using managers skilled in directing the work of transitory personnel.

## The business resource base

The technology supporting the company's strategic goals consisted of ‘islands’ of processing. Each department functioned autonomously with respect to computing. Communication was written, oral, or physically passed by diskettes being interchanged. Service bureaus were frequently used to process information without adequate supervision, control and verification. We will be describing the operations of the Sales and Technical Support areas to illustrate the nature of the technology used to create, produce, market and support the organization.

## Sales operation and its technology

The Sales Department conducted their information processing using manual processes and performed some analysis and reporting activities with individual microcomputers. These systems were not connected under any network. Many heterogeneous tasks and activities composed the department work. Some of the major tasks included the following: Sales Order Preparation; Sales Activity Reporting; Sales Tracking Activity; Distribution Reporting and Commission Computations; Information Request Form [IRF] Processing; Lead Request Form [LRF] Processing; Return Material Acceptance [RMA] Processing; Trade-show Lead Processing; Tear-off Card Processing; Authorized Reseller Processing, and; Network Bundle Processing.

Only the Sales Order, Activity, Tracking and Distribution reporting processes were computerized. The remaining activities were processed manually. Extensive memo writing, oral communication, phone conversations and phone-tag, and liaison efforts were required to communicate with the other departments and organizational entities. Sales Order preparation depended on them being manually processed and hand carried to the Production department.

Sales Activity reporting and tracking was accomplished through coordination with Marketing and the maintenance of spreadsheets by the Sales Manager. Within the department three different spreadsheet software products (Lotus, Twin and Excel) were used to process the spreadsheet and generate the reports. The Information Request Forms were completed manually and hand-carried to Production for physical handling. The Lead Request Forms were produced in coordination with the Technical Support function. Sales representatives completed manual LRF forms and hand carried them to the Technical Support person who enters them onto his computer using the Paradox database manager. Parts of the Customer data base were stored on dBase III+ files. Reports and correspondence by secretarial and support staff were generated using RapidFile. Periodically the Tech Support person would generate a list of leads and deliver them to the Sales Manager. The LRF list is then manually cut and pasted in order to send leads to the distributors and resellers.

## Technical support operations and its technology

The technical support operations, included the following major tasks: Technical Support Processing; Registration Card Processing; Information Request Form Processing and; Customer RMA Processing. The Technical Support person recorded all activities on his computer using the Paradox database manager software. Most of the recording was done in the way of memos or notes. No actual verification of Customers having a valid/registered copy could be accomplished when calling with a problem. The actual registration database was too large and disorganized to be queried in a timely manner for verification. Another problem was the actual provision of technical support. Since only one person was designated as the Hot Line respondent there was a dependence on Engineering and Sales to assist in trouble shooting and customer service.

## Business performance pre-LAN

In assessing business performance we will examine the business results in terms of internal and external efficiencies; and behavioural performance.

Efficiencies in the area of technical support can serve as a good indicator of the value of the LAN. Prior to the introduction of the LAN there were more calls unanswered than answered – directly resulting in customer product dissatisfaction as indicated by low customer support ratings in reviews in magazines (e.g., Pc Week).

Other factors related to the technical support performance related to the inability of the tech support person to handle increasingly complex calls for technical support. This led to a phenomenon similar to that experienced in many mainframe computer operations where an increasing amount of development time was diverted to maintenance – in this case technical assistance. On any given occasion approximately two to three software engineers would spend three to four hours per day on technical assistance calls.

Behavioural performance was most readily indicated by employee morale and turnover. There was a lack of communication, a sense of not knowing what was going on in both sales and marketing. Over a period of six months there were three different marketing managers. Marketing personnel had a turnover rate of about three months. Similar figures occurred in Sales and somewhat in Accounting.

## Toward an improved new IT for SDC

The introduction of new IT raises an interesting issue for our analysis of SDC. Thus far, our analysis has been within a static context: the IT for a firm is contingent upon the current state of its internal and external environments and their degree of uncertainty. Since SDC external environment is subject to a variety of forces that foster uncertainty such as competition, their ability to sustain a competitive advantage requires continuous investments in new IT. It is not concerned simply with static improvement, but also with dynamic enhancements. The selection of a time path of IT improvements and change permits a competitive advantage to be sustained within a changing external environment. Choosing new IT is not simply a question of the technology which offers the best combination of performance attributes given strategic goals, resources availability and the firm's design configurations but, more importantly, the IT which offers a faster rate of performance improvements. Top management analysis of SDC identified a LAN as an appropriate new IT.

## Local area network

A LAN is a system of computers (typically personal computers) and associated peripherals that are physically connected to allow the interchange of information. Network systems are designed to distribute data and computing services to users in the organization. The real essence to the optimization of local area networking is that an organization must use software applications of a distributed nature that take advantage of the network's capabilities for resource sharing. The LAN provides an ideal resource-sharing environment for programmes that use data of importance to a number of users. For example, a single database or series of related databases containing customer information can provide statistical data for management, sales prospects for marketing, billing information for accounting and so on. But to be useful, one department's alterations must immediately be available to other departments. The instant the accounting department decides not to extend a past customer further credit, the sales staff must have access to this information. If a customer information is shared by means of a LAN, all users can be assured of having the most up-to-date data (Sena and Shani, 1991).

## SDC with a Local Area Network

The introduction of the LAN can be examined in terms of its effect on the strategic goals, the firm's resource base, the business design configuration, and the business performance.

## Strategic goals

In order to assure growth and market penetration (an integral part of the firm's strategic goals) owner management introduced a layer of professional managers – Vice Presidents of Marketing and Sales. The growth of the computer industry, especially in the area of local area network software/hardware, was occurring simultaneously with the firm's introduction of their LAN. In essence, the firm was shifting its product to support LAN software – acquiring knowledge and expertise of LAN management and use became a strategic goal.

## Business design configuration

After the LANs were introduced information processing (over time) became centralized, standardized, formalized and primarily automated. In a sense the information was centrally located but logically distributed. Each department's processing was integrated both within the department and among/between other departments. This was accomplished by package acquisition, software development and standards in software use. In conjunction with the introduction of the LAN additional changes were introduced.

Technical Support was placed under the Sales Vice President – an adequate staff of trained hot-line personnel were hired or transferred; engineering support to technical support was formalized; and a communication system consisting of voice mail and computer driven telephone response system was introduced to appropriately route and monitor incoming calls. The revised organization structure is shown in Figure 3.

## Firm's resource base: engineering

Marketing and Sales personnel embraced the LAN not only as support but as a means of comprehending the changing use of their product – thus providing the company with a reinforced strategic advantage. At the time that other similar software firms were declining in revenues, experiencing lay-offs, and a general down turn in business, this firm had a significant growth in sales and personnel.

## Business performance

The introduction of LANs can be examined in terms of its effects on the organization level, department-unit level, and the individual level. Figure 4 provides a summary of the effects of the new technology.

## Organizational level effects

As the use of the LAN evolves, the organization's ability to process and utilize information matures. The impact of voice mail, the move to expand and consolidate technical support, and the introduction of a layer of professional management all supported the growth of the organization.

Over the two-year period that the LANs were introduced the organization's size increased from about thirty to seventy persons and sales increased from about US\$5 million to US\$21 million.

Communication was streamlined through the use of e-mail. The cost of a lost or misplaced message cannot be readily assessed. However, with the use of e-mail all users are provided with appropriate and relevant information through routing and grouping on a timely basis. Immediate response to messages/mail is realizable. Less and more meaningful face-to-face, telephone tag, and memo pasting is required – a central repository for messages mail is now in place. Mail can be routed, saved and retrieved.

Through the sharing of printing and disk storage resources the demand for local personal computer disk storage was reduced. In effect, the acquisition of new computers for the increased personnel and the replacement of out-dated systems on the desks of other workers addresses the need for better processors (e.g., 386 and 486 machines) without concern for storage and/or printers. The overall cost savings is significant – each printer, in this case about forty units would cost in the range of US\$200 to US\$350 each; the increased disk space (e.g. 100 Mbytes drives) each would cost about US\$350.

Similar cost savings and efficiencies were obtained through: standardization in software use and templates for reporting and record keeping; automation of manual tasks speeding up processing and making information more readily available; more timely response to customer inquiries; and improved levels of inventory.

With continuing advances in software the assignment of standards, choice of software packages, and development of specific software-dependent systems is uncertain. Initially, SDC attempted to use IBM's system application architecture (SAA) model. With the acceptance of Windows they modified their LAN to a windows-based interface.

## Departmental level effects

Departmental and inter-departmental changes occurred as a direct result of the introduction of the LANs. Among these changes were impacts on management processes and roles; relationships between subunits; and differences in socialization.

Prior to the introduction of the LANs the Sales manager devoted most of his time to semi-automated record keeping and reporting. Individual salesman maintained manual or personal computer-based logs of sales contacts. Secretaries and support staff used whatever word processor software they chose. There was no effective intra-communication among sales staff. With the introduction of the LAN records of sales, sales projections, budgets, marketing, customer information, etc. were now available and addressable on demand. A sales contact package, TeleMagic, was installed that allowed all of the sales staff to share the Customer database as well as segment groups of customers into territories and/or by salesman. In effect, the sales effort became centralized and more efficient. Timely reporting on sales activity was available to management.

Significant sales increases were recorded. With the LAN, direct sales could then be introduced (previously pre-LAN operations could not handle the volume of calls and associated credit card/order processing.) Two years after the introduction of the LAN it is estimated that direct sales were about 25 % of the business; sales through distributors, prior to the LAN, increased by an average of 20 % per year – following the LAN introduction they increased by about 40 %. Upgrade sales increased by an average of 25 % prior to the LAN – increased to about 50 % per year after the LAN. These major increases in sales for the company was a definite factor due to the LAN because engineering now had an improved ability to develop new and improved products; marketing had the ability to publicize the products, and sales had the ability to handle the increased volume.

The entry of sales orders, credit approvals, volume discounts and other work performed or requiring approval by other departments was centralized by the introduction of an integrated enterprise accounting LAN system, the Platinum Accounting software package. Orders could be entered by sales staff, placed into a pending file, and approved by accounting, processed by production, and made available to interested management without any use of manual, paper, or phone transmissions.

The use of electronic mail as well as peer-to-peer communication altered the way that all staff communicated. Rather than check the front counter or their desk for paper messages, the first thing that each person would do on coming to work was to bring up their system and view their e-mail. Currently, efforts are now underway to introduce work group software in marketing, sales, and especially in engineering. The goals of this software are to introduce project and work management, facilitate appointment and meeting scheduling, and share work results. The e-mail has not only changed the pattern of communication but has also introduced a new level of social interaction. Staff members somehow feel less threatened to communicate problems when not directly interacting with peers, superiors, and subordinates. To some extent the degree and tenor of face-to-face communications was reduced or mollified.

## Individual level effects

For the individual, the LAN has increased their productivity; changed the way they work; altered their commitment to their work; and increased motivation and self-perception. The introduction of the marketing and sales Vice Presidents lead to a higher degree of professionalism. The organization progressed from a start-up or entrepreneurial stance to that of a professional software house. The way that personnel were treated indicated that they were part of a team. Each person knew their job, their responsibilities, and to whom they were accountable. Each staff member had their own workstation connected to the LAN. Specialized menus were established such that each person or group's work area was tailored to support their work. To illustrate, consider the technical support situation where a customer calls for support. Technical support has staff members who monitor all in-coming customer inquiries, determine the problem area and route the call-back to the appropriate technical support person. When such a technical support person began his/her day, he/she would check the technical support log for the customer and their problem, call them back and address the problem, record the information on the technical log and make an entry into the customer database. Not all of the Tech's day is spent responding to customer inquiries. They work with engineering to maintain and improve their software knowledge and skills and test software to stay aware of nuances and problem areas. Performance ratings by third party (e.g. Pc Week) indicated significant improvements in technical and hot-line support for the company products.

## Conclusions

The main conclusion which arises from the integrated sociotechnical and strategic approach to the selection of new IT is the recognition that each business is different in terms of its situation, strategic goals, resource base and design configurations. The interface between ITs, organizational features, and environmental uncertainties creates complex business dynamics that must be examined on an ongoing basis. The appropriate IT for a business depends upon the circumstances of that business with regard to its strategic goals, the business environment, the resources which it owns, and the characteristics of its design configuration. As a result, the network system has to be tailored to the specific needs of the organization. The design of the LAN, professional management, changes in the structural configurations, and the staffing of Technical Support produced significant results.

Merely introducing a LAN does not guarantee that the staff will change their work habits. The movement to the LAN was evolutionary. An examination of the business design configuration became a necessity. As a consequence a consultant was brought in to facilitate the firm's transformation process. It is beyond the scope of this paper to describe all the activities, phases, and processes that took place in order to facilitate the acceptance, understanding, and adequate utilization of the system. Facilities provided on the network such as e-Mail and Messaging, Work Group, Integrated Accounting, standardized software and access to shared databases made the network use attractive. Management's commitment to the introduction of the new technology enabled the myriad of changes that occurred. The development of proprietary databases and the automation of manual tasks heightened professionalism, enhanced business performance, and improved team-work.

Four main lessons can be drawn from our study. First, for the mature small firms, an evolutionary approach to new IT upgrading is likely to offer the best combination of static and dynamic performance results. Second, the decision to invest in new IT requires a careful assessment of the business situation, the firm's strategic goals, the firm's resources base and the business design configurations. Third, the implementation process and the introduction of new IT can be enhanced by an incremental approach. Fourth, the introduction of LAN requires major realignment of the organization.
