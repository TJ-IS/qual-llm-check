# Division of labour and the design of systems for computer support for cooperative work

KEN EASON

Department of Human Sciences, Loughborough University of Technology, UK

The design of systems which provide computer support for cooperative work (CSCW) has been dominated by models of collaborative teams in academic or design environments. Such settings are characterized by a relatively egalitarian power distribution and a division of labour established locally by the collaborating parties. This paper describes other types of collaborative work in which the r“les of participants are characterized by pre-established divisions of labour and unequal power distribution. A case study is presented of a collaborative group allocating and scheduling service calls in an electricity company. Using the Organizational Requirements De® nition for Information Technology (ORDIT) methods for responsibility analysis a number of alternative organizational structures are described which may serve this task. The alternatives are based upon different job design structures which distribute responsibilities to work r“les by different rationales. An analysis is offered of the different CSCW systems that would be necessary to support these alternatives. The paper concludes by examining the implications of pre-established but variable organizational structures for the design of generic CSCW systems.

## Classifying types of cooperative work

The development of the computer support for cooperative work movement is a welcome recognition that major organizational tasks are undertaken by teams of people. There is, however, a worrying tendency for systems developers and researchers to concentrate upon cooperating groups close at hand ± research teams, design groups, and so on ± and many systems are being designed to support communication and cooperation in such groups. The danger is that we will repeat the error of believing all computer users are like the programmer who developed the system or, perhaps worse, the university student who is the most convenient subject for an experiment.

An analysis of the papers presented at the Conference on Computer Support for Cooperative Work (CSCW-92) (Turner and Kraut, 1992) reveals that of the 36 applications reported, 23 were to be used by academic or design groups. There are a few examples in the CSCW literature of studies in other settings, notably Hutchins (1990) of ship navigation, Hughes et al. (1992) of air-traf® c control and Heath and Luff (1991) of underground train control rooms. Nevertheless we are in grave danger of developing systems to serve ourselves as academics or designers and of making the assumption that forms of cooperation found in academic and design environments are characteristic of the many other industrial, commercial and professional settings in which people cooperate to undertake work.

## Division of labour and the distribution of power

The purpose of this paper is to explore the implications of the different ways in which people collaborate at work. The classi® cations found in the CSCW literature tend to focus on the location of collaborators in space and time (McCarthy, 1995). Less attention is paid to the different kinds of contribution collaborators make to the work or the reasons why contributions differ. It is my contention that there are a variety of forms of collaboration and that we need to pay systematic attention to these alternatives if systems to support cooperative work are to be effective in the mass market beyond academic and design settings. In the long history of organizational and administrative theory two concepts have been especially important to understanding how people cooperate in performing large tasks: division of labour and power distribution. The seminal work of Weber (1947), for example, showed that a large organizational task such as building a motor car could be undertaken by a team of people if the overall task was divided into subtasks and these allocated to members of the team. Task specialization is thus a pervasive characteristic of large organizations. Once the large organizational task has been divided and allocated there is then a question of coordination and control and the traditional approach has been to create a hierarchy in which managers have responsibility for these control tasks. As a result there is an uneven distribution of power in organizations. These properties of organizations are basic to any understanding of how collaborative work is undertaken in industry and commerce. The modern movement towards more teamwork and ¯ atter organizations is reducing some of the emphasis upon task specialization but, as I hope to show, there are good reasons to believe that division of labour and power distribution will remain very signi® cant factors in organizational life.

## A classi® cation of cooperative work

There are a number of ways in which task specialization occurs and power is distributed. Figure 1 offers a simple matrix which depicts four kinds of cooperative work based upon different approaches to division of labour and power distribution which have different implications for the design of CSCW systems.

The majority of cooperating groups have some degree of division of labour and the important question is when and how this division takes place. In many instances the division occurs at the time the work is undertaken. Members of the team may \`sign up’ or agree to make particular contributions. In other circumstances many of the allocation decisions are predetermined (by, for example, legal issues of ownership, contractual agreements, democratic election, quali® cations and training, and so on). Where there is a major division of power and in¯ uence, the decisions about division of labour may be taken by those with power. Obviously there may be different degrees of speci® city in the division of labour. A lot of the job-design literature concerns broad preset allocations within which there is autonomy for people to agree speci® c duties at the time work is undertaken.

In Figure 1 research teams and design groups are identi® ed as collaborative groups of type A where power is relatively equally distributed amongst cooperating members and r“les and responsibilities are to a large extent determined by group members during the course of undertaking the work. In this respect they are like the semi-autonomous work group favoured by many sociotechnical systems theorists (Weisbord, 1990) as a means of establishing local discretion and autonomy.

In the case of type B cooperating groups there is a clear separation of power but little predetermined division of labour. An army unit may be an example, in which the of® cer assigns the duties to his troops as he sees ® t. A builder who assembles a task force of labourers is in a similar position. In type C there may be a relatively equal distribution of power but a high degree of pre-established distribution of labour. A group of professionals in a case conference on, for example, child care, might be an example with medical, educational, legal and other specialists contributing their own perspectives and knowledge.

The majority of industrial and commercial cooperative groups are likely to be type D. There will be owners or managers accountable for group actions and an array of collaborators who are there because they have speci® c contributions to make. A crime investigation team is an example in which the collaborators come to the task with an array of predetermined responsibilities and accountabilities. Under the general direction of the of® cers in charge there may be detectives, pathologists, ® ngerprint experts, lawyers, and so on. This kind of cooperative work involves collaboration between people occupying highly differentiated work r“les and quite different responsibilities and contributions from each work r“le.

Another example is a medical team undertaking an operation. In an observational study I undertook of a surgical team at work one of the striking features was the way new staff, for example nurses and technicians, ® tted so quickly into the team. Their duties were prescribed and everybody had a ready-made set of expectations of the r“le they would play. Contrast this with the average research team or design group where there may be a great deal of latitude for negotiation and renegotiation of the r“les to be played. \`Signing up’ and committing yourself to speci® c actions is not a feature of the work of a team undertaking an operation on a patient.

## Technology and the division of labour

The impact of technology on power structures and division of labour in cooperative work settings has been widely researched by organizational theorists (Long, 1987). Of the many kinds of effects it is useful to consider two because they are both relevant to CSCW: the way technology can create new tasks and the way it in¯ uences informational exchange between cooperating individuals. The introduction of new technology often establishes speci® c subtasks which have to be undertaken. For example, in the medical team someone has to operate the machines which monitor the condition of the patient, and once you have a photocopier in an of® ce, someone has to operate it. In theory any member of the cooperating group could use the technology which is what often happens to the photocopier now that its operation is relatively straightforward. If specialist training is required to operate the technology it is more likely that one member of the group will be designated to use it. This will probably be the case with the patient monitoring equipment.

The second kind of effect concerns the way information and communication technology in¯ uences cooperation when it acts as a mediating medium between cooperating individuals. The computer impact literature contains many studies of the way in which computer use has affected power relations and the relations between work r“les (Bjù rn-Andersen et al., 1986). Although these are, for the most part, studies of mainframe based information systems, they nevertheless show very clearly the way in which changes in access to information can dramatically affect the relationships between work r“les in an organization. If a new system means, for example, that a manager can gain access to the working documents of a subordinate at any time, it is possible for the manager to take more direct control of the subordinate. Such centralization of power has been found in a number of studies, for example Child et al. (1984). If a new system means a wide array of people have access to information not previously available, they may be able to use the information to in¯ uence events: this kind of decentralization or democratization has also been found, for example, Barras and Swann (1983).

The application of CSCW usually involves issues about access rights to the information base of the teamwork; issues of \`read rights’ (who can access what parts of the database); and \`write rights’ (who can change and update the database, authorize actions through the system, and so on). In a design team it may be appropriate for each member to have equal access to information about the work of the team and an equal opportunity to contribute to the work. Often as the work proceeds, contributions become more specialist and implicit or explicit agreements have to be made about access rights. In a medical team, however, the surgeon and the hospital porter have different responsibilities: the surgeon needs different access to information and more rights to change it than other members of the team. There is a growing body of literature that shows that emerging forms of CSCW systems are running into dif® culty when they are not sensitive to the differences in power and duties of participants in collaborative groups.

Grudin (1989), for example, has shown that it is unacceptable to many people who have power and discretion to allow a collective diary keeping system to book their time automatically. The evaluation of twenty of® ce automation systems sponsored by the UK Department of Trade and Industry (Pye et al., 1986) showed that case applications, where professionals worked in collaborative teams, were dif® cult to support because of the subtle nature of responsibilities and accountabilities. It seems likely that CSCW systems will not be widely accepted in situations where there are important differences in contributions of participants until they are able to closely map the nature of these differences and support both the r“le of the individual and the performance of the overall group task.

In order to explore in more detail the implications of work r“le differentiation and power structures for the design of CSCW systems it is instructive to examine a case study of a type D cooperating team. The next section describes such a case study in which new forms of computer support were being examined which could have quite profound implications for the nature of cooperation.

## Service calls in an electricity company

This case study is of a task familiar to many people. If, as an electricity consumer, you contact the electricity company you may initiate a process which leads to an electrician visiting you. The purpose may be a routine installation or repair of an electrical appliance or to deal with an emergency, such as loss of supply to your premises. The underlying ¯ ow of work we found in studying several electricity companies is depicted in Figure 2.

The process begins with a request for service from a customer and these requests are separated into emergency calls (off-supply calls, dangerous situations, etc) and non-emergency calls (repairing cookers, central heating systems, etc). Thereafter, in each case the task is allocated to a service engineer, the emergency calls being given priority.

## Division of labour in handling service calls

This is a deceptively straightforward scheduling task but in the electricity companies it causes considerable problems. The electricity industry in the UK has recently been privatized and this has led to a demand for faster responses to service calls which have on average been several hours for emergencies and several days for non-emergencies. One of the reasons for the delay in responding is the dif® culties of communication between the respective work r“les involved in service calls. The division of labour which currently supports the service call task is given in Figure 3. It overlays the component tasks in the work ¯ ow with the current work r“les responsible for each task.

The normal route for a service call is for the customer to telephone the service reception centre of the company. Although each company covers a very large geographical area, for reasons of ef® ciency only one reception centre is maintained. Out of normal of® ce hours calls are directed to the electricity network control centre because they are usually reports of no supply. During the day a clerk at the reception centre has to log the call and decide if it is an emergency or non-emergency. If it is an emergency the enquiry is diverted to a radio operator who can make contact with the radio in the van of an electrician who can attend to the job. Each electricity company has a series of depots to cover each part of the geographical area covered by the company and a team of service electricians operate from each depot. The radio operator normally contacts the shift electrician of the relevant depot who is responsible for general duties including emergencies.

He then assigns the work to one of the electricians for the following day. The foreman also ensures relevant spares are retrieved from stores and loaded onto the vans for the following day. After making their visits, electricians report whether the job has been completed and, if not, further visits are scheduled.

## A data transmission system

The electricity companies needed to improve customer service because the present system had several faults. The process for dealing with non-emergency work took a minimum of one day and if the job had been incorrectly identi® ed it could take longer. The emergency route could be much faster but the radio contact depended on the electrician being in the van and able to receive the call. If the electrician was on a consumer’s property undertaking a repair, the radio link was lost and the call had to be stacked for later transmission.

One electricity company had undertaken a trial of a system to overcome these problems. This system provided a data link between the radio operator and all vans. It enabled the radio operator to send a message to a van giving the address of a consumer and the job that had to be undertaken. Once the van acknowledged receipt of the message, the job was considered to have been assigned to the electrician. The great bene® t of this system was that the message could be transmitted while the electrician was not in his van. A scheme showing this communication system is provided in Figure 4. All emergency work could be routed this way and any spare capacity in the electrician’s daily schedule could be taken up by routing non-emergency work directly to him.

Trial use of this system demonstrated its technical feasibility but revealed a number of serious organizational implications. In a rather simple way the data transmission system replaced (or more speci® cally enhanced) the old computer system for collaborative work. In practice it speeded up collaborative work but also rendered some forms of collaboration much more dif® cult.

## The ORDIT method for analysing organizational implications

The HUSAT Research Institute has worked with the electricity supply industry for a number of years on user-centred design methods (Gower and Eason, 1985; Eason et al., 1987) and we were thus able to study the ® eld trials of the data link. In the analysis presented below the methods developed in another project, ESPRIT project ORDIT (Olphert and Harker, 1994), have been used to provide a systematic review of the organizational implications of the data link.

ORDIT (organizational requirements for information technology systems) is a set of methods for the articulation of organizational requirements by modelling future sociotechnical systems and exploring the implications of the different possibilities. At the heart of ORDIT is a modelling language which uses responsibility analysis as a way of exploring the way in which social and technical systems combine to achieve cooperative tasks. The underlying concept is that large tasks are achieved by assigning responsibility for different subtasks to members of the social system and, in order that they can execute their responsibilities, they need access to the tools, resources, information and so on, appropriate to their r“le. The responsibilities assigned to work r“les are then the pivot upon which an effective sociotechnical system must rest; the responsibilities de® ne r“le relations between members of the social system and the necessary distribution of technical resources. The ORDIT method models the sociotechnical character of a cooperative group by de® ning the work r“les in the work system as a series of responsibilities they undertake. Each responsibility carries with it rights and obligations, including rights and obligations with respect to technical resources. Figure 5 presents a simpli® ed ORDIT model of the major work r“les and relations in the customer service case study.

It will be noted that each list of responsibilities contains not only the major operational requirements that a task analysis would identify, for example allocate routine enquiries, but also the less operationally de® ned responsibilities upon which organizational success may depend, for example taking care of customer relations when customers are anxious about emergencies or angry when service electricians do not call on schedule. Figure 5 also speci® es the major relationships between r“les. We distinguish between functional relations where responsibilities for work are passed from one r“le to another to enable the next function to be undertaken, and structural relations such as supervision which imply a power relationship enabling one r“le holder to exercise coordination and control over another. For simplicity, the customer service example is treated as a network of four work r“les serving a customer. In practice there are also r“le relations with others in the electricity supply company: for example the control room and the power engineers who maintain the electricity network, senior and middle management at headquarters and at depot and the depot storemen who maintain the stock of parts. There are also responsibilities associated with each of the work r“les identi® ed in Figure 5 which involve relations with other work r“les, for example, the depot foreman has a supervisory relation with the depot storeman. This is an example of a problem for all CSCW applications: the cooperating group is unlikely to be independent of all other groups and each work r“le is likely to be part of other cooperating teams.

A full ORDIT model would also list the technical resources necessary for each work r“le to ful® l its list of responsibilities. The clerk, for example, needs a means of communication with the customer and a means of logging and transmitting enquiries to other work r“les. The radio operator has to be able to communicate with all vans and must have an up-to-date knowledge of the location and current schedule of each van. The depot foreman has complex requirements because of the many responsibilities he holds. He must know the forward load of enquiries, the existing load on each electrician, the capabilities of each electrician, the spares held on each van and those needed to complete each job, and so on. Finally, the electrician needs a van containing the parts relevant for the jobs allocated to him and a means of communicating whilst away from the depot.

## Organizational implications of the data link

Into this organizational structure was introduced the mobile communications data transmission system. As we have found in other case studies (Eason, 1988) there was no planned change in the r“le structure to accompany the technical system. In practice, however, the new system had profound implications for the ability of work r“le holders to ful® l their responsibilities.

The data transmission system enabled the radio operator to allocate work directly to the electricians’ vans. This has implications for the allocation of many of the responsibilities listed in Figure 5. The radio operator is now allocating some non-emergency work as well as emergency work. The clerks therefore have to know electrician availability in order to transmit non-emergency work to the radio operator that could be done on the day of reception. The foreman is no longer the primary allocator of work to the electricians. In fact because the data link is a one-to-one connection between the radio operator and a van, the foreman does not know when new work has been allocated to an electrician. Previously the radio broadcast could be heard by everyone with a receiver and the foreman knew when emergency work was allocated. The same-day allocation of non-emergency work also meant the foreman had to increase the parts provision to vans in the hope the electrician would have the parts necessary to do the new jobs. Finally, the electrician found that his daily schedule might be different every time he returned to his van. The system contained no provision for the electrician to accept or reject new work, only for the van to acknowledge reception of the transmission. The electricians therefore lost any discretion over the acceptance of jobs and most of the control over their schedule.

These implications could be predicted from the ORDIT model and they closely mapped the outcome of the ® eld trials in which the depot staff (foreman and electricians) experienced a loss of control over service enquiries as power shifted to the reception clerks and the radio operator. In practice the new process was not effective because the staff making the allocations were not suf® ciently sensitive to operational dif® culties in the ® eld: for example, dif® culties obtaining entry to the customer’s premises, delays on the road, ® nding that the job speci® cation was wrong, and so on. Under the previous conditions the foreman and the electricians would use the radio system to reallocate jobs to overcome local dif® culties but this process was now confounded by the \`silent’ allocations from the centre to each van.

## Examining sociotechnical alternatives

The ® eld trial led to a strong feeling amongst depot staff (foreman and electricians) that control had moved too far towards headquarters staff. These complaints were not simply a resistance to personal loss of power. There were many examples where loss of local control meant that a lower quality of customer service was provided because there was no opportunity to undertake local rescheduling. In this case as in many others (Eason and Sell, 1980; Eason, 1988) a new technical system with the potential to enhance company performance has failed to do so, because it did not support the work r“les in the social system through which enhanced performance has to be achieved.

As a result of this experience the electricity company began looking at other ways of using information technology to enhance customer service. On this occasion they were conscious of the need to enhance the opportunities of members of staff in the key r“les. With the help of the ORDIT methodology we were able to present a number of sociotechnical opportunities and to examine the organizational implications of each option. The process of developing alternative sociotechnical structures is in four parts. First, the responsibilities necessary to achieve the overall task are listed without reference to the work r“les. This produces a statement of what has to be achieved but without the social and technical structures by which the overall task is normally undertaken. The second step is to regroup the responsibilities by reference to functional relationships and by the need to achieve major goals, for example, faster customer service and local adaptability. The third step is to reassign to work r“les those responsibilities that group together and detail the relations that are produced between the r“les. Typically this process leaves some responsibilities unassigned and, as long as they remain necessary to the ful® lment of the overall task, they have to be allocated before the social structure is complete. This process of job design is accompanied by an examination of technical systems necessary to support each new work r“le structure. This is an iterative process because, not only do new work r“le structures create demands for new technical systems, but new technical opportunities may make it possible to think of new work r“les that were previously unrealistic. The fourth step is to test the new structures by asking how well they would meet the organization’s many requirements. Typically every structure has pros and cons and it is for the members of the organization to determine where priorities lie.

In the customer service case this process identi® ed four major opportunities which have become the subject of ongoing debate within the electricity supply companies. Each option is characterized by a different solution to the problem of allocating and scheduling work but has many other consequences. The alternatives are identi-® ed in Figure 6. A full speci® cation of each alternative would be too lengthy for this paper. A short account of each is provided together with the major implications for the organization.

## Central allocation

One option is to sustain the concept that the centre undertakes work allocation. To avoid the confusion and rigidity of the trial system, all emergency and nonemergency work could be allocated from the centre but with facilities by which electricians could talk back to the reception clerks. The great advantages of this option are that the reception clerks can give customers an immediate indication of the timing of the service call, can ensure service electrician resources were fully loaded and can ensure the earliest possible visit. The disadvantages are the loss of local control which could be mitigated by a system facility that enabled electricians to talk back to clerks and report dif® culties with allocations and for the clerks to be responsible for rescheduling. This still leaves the problem that the quality of the work of the electricians is the responsibility of the foreman who now has no part to play in the allocation of new work. He would still deal with second visits and with certi® cation of the work undertaken. This option may require a major rethink of the r“le of the supervisor: perhaps the r“le becomes speci® cally that of quality assurance. Another issue is whether a separate radio operator is necessary: the clerks could transmit data directly to the vans of the electricians. The technical system to support this structure would be an extension of the data transmission system with a talkback facility and a temporary allocation store which holds allocations until the electrician has con® rmed or rejected the allocation.

## Allocation by foreman

If the signi® cant issue is considered to be the reestablishment of local control and the removal of ambiguity in the control of electricians, a structure can be created in which the foreman in each depot makes all the allocation and scheduling decisions for the electricians of that depot. Under this arrangement the clerk would allocate all emergency and nonemergency jobs for a given geographical area to the depot foreman who would be responsible for subsequent distribution to the electricians. The radio operator r“le would disappear. The same talkback and temporary store arrangements could be established between the foreman and the electrician to provide electricians with the opportunity to negotiate the allocations. The advantage of this arrangement is that it gives full control at a local level, which should provide much more informed ¯ exibility, and the electricians have only one boss to answer to. There are two major disadvantages. First the foreman traditionally spends much of his time out of the depot supervising the work of the electricians. Having a real time duty to allocate all jobs as they arrive could render him deskbound. An alternative is to create a mobile system which provides facilities for the foreman to receive jobs from the clerk and to allocate them from his own van. The second disadvantage is that the clerks do not know the allocations and schedules that have been created in each depot and cannot tell customers when to expect visits. This problem could be overcome by providing clerks with read only access to the allocations and schedules as they are set up in each depot. This structure is more distributed than the previous one and as a result the technical system that is required is more sophisticated.

## Electricians as an autonomous work group

One of the informal ways in which service enquiries were managed in the radio-based approach was that electricians, hearing the conversations between the radio operator, another electrician and the foreman, could offer to help with any dif® culties. This element of team support was lost when the data transmission system was introduced because allocation messages were no longer broadcast. The local work force claimed that they could be much more ¯ exible and effective if they knew what needed to be done and could organize themselves to do it. A possible organizational structure, therefore, is to create a distributed semiautonomous work group in which the electricians receive the information about jobs to be done in the depot area and they make their own allocation decisions. In this scenario the clerks would send the jobs to the depot as in option B and the foreman has a quality assurance and overall team performance supervision r“le. Each of the electricians would need equipment in their vans to enable them to receive details of all jobs and they would have to establish group rules which would preallocate some types of jobs, e.g. emergencies, and set up times when they would consider as a group how to distribute the work. Such an approach depends heavily upon the creation of a system which would enable a group to communicate effectively when distributed across the geographical area of the depot.

## Electricians as subcontractors

A major debate within many companies is the extent to which they can contract out the work they need to be undertaken. Within an electricity distribution company there is a possibility that service calls could be contracted out to electricians working as self-employed subcontractors. The management of the electricity companies were considering the radical proposal that their electricians should become subcontractors in the belief that this would cut direct labour costs and may increase productivity. They believed that people working for themselves and being paid for each service call they completed would be motivated to maximize the number of calls made each day. If implemented this could produce an organizational form in which the clerks received the calls from consumers and passed them to the foreman in the relevant depot. The foreman could then offer them to an approved list of electricians who could bid to do the job. The foreman would decide on the basis of price, quality and the speed of the call who should undertake the job. It would be inef® cient to negotiate every service call and no doubt some longer term agreements would be made. Nevertheless, to sustain ¯ exibility, it would be sensible to be able to check who could meet the customer’s needs most swiftly. This approach has considerable bene® ts for the organization although it would need to establish good quality control procedures. It is not so clear whether it would bring bene® ts to the electricians. It would change the foreman’s r“le considerably, from a supervisor to a contract manager. The information system would need to contain considerably more information than in the other options because of the contracting and pricing arrangements. The system would now need access points outside the organization which would demand a greater degree of security. Information would not now be shared amongst electricians because they would be bidding against one another to secure work.

## Selecting the way forward

This kind of analysis reveals the diversity of ways in which people can be organized to undertake a complex task. There are certainly other variants to the organizational options presented here. It is rarely a case of selecting the best option. Each of these options is good on some criteria and not so good on others. It is for the stakeholders (the managers and staff of the company) to decide which criteria are most important and choose the option that meets these criteria. In this case the different electricity companies are taking a variety of ways forward. In one case they have established a centralized system with two-way communications between clerks and electricians but, for the longer term, are looking at the possibilities of contracting out part of the work. It is a normal part of the ongoing management task to seek alternative ways of organizing human resources which may achieve organizational tasks more effectively.

## The design of CSCW systems

The network of human work r“les that undertakes service enquiries is an example of a very common form of cooperating group ± with a hierarchy of power and clear, semipermanent distributions of labour. In most of the options considered the functional responsibilities of each r“le holder are relatively permanent: only the distribution of work is established locally. All of the information systems which have been outlined are examples of CSCW. The data transmission system is a very basic kind of CSCW; the other systems support much more sophisticated forms of cooperation. What then are the implications of this analysis for the design of CSCW systems?

Suppose an electricity company wishes to purchase a system to support its service calls operation. In the past it would probably have commissioned the development of a bespoke system to meet its own particular needs. The design team charged with this commission would no doubt have adopted an engineering philosophy (Hales, 1994) in which all the components of the technical systems and the communication ¯ ows between cooperating agents would be fully speci® ed before the system was introduced. The system could have a similar functional structure for all the options outlined above because the same information is being handled in each case. However, to specify the complete system the design team would need to decide which of the organizational structures outlined above would be implemented because other technical requirements are quite different in each case. In the ® rst (central allocation) option for example, the clerks have full read and write access to the database of outstanding service calls; in the second option it is the foreman who has this access; in the third it has to be available to all the electricians; and in the case of electricians as subcontractors it is under the foreman’s control but the database has to include information about contracts and pricing. In each option, the partners in the cooperative task have partial read and write access of different kinds. One of the problems in the past when organizations commissioned bespoke systems was that it was rarely possible to update the existing system when the company wished to change from one organizational form to another; a new system had to be developed.

We are moving into an era where organizations will be buying CSCW systems off-the-shelf. The service call paradigm as a cooperative task is a very common one. It is common to all utilities, emergency services and to other organizations who offer services to consumers in their own homes. It is easy to imagine a software supplier concluding there is a market for a generic CSCW system for service calls. The supplier would have little dif® culty establishing a functional speci® cation to meet the requirements for information collection and storage. But how should the supplier meet the need for communication facilities to match the diversity of organizational forms there might be in the market? Following the engineering philosophy they could produce a fully speci® ed single solution and they would build into the product assumptions about the division of labour in the user organization. The data transmission system did this by incorporating a one way communication process of transmission and receive between the radio operator and the electrician’s van. Many other products assume (often implicit) divisions of labour and supervisory structures, for example, meeting management systems which assume there is a chairperson and monitoring systems that report operator performance to a supervisor. The problem with this approach is that the system may be rejected by organizations which have a different organizational pattern or, worse, it may force a company to follow a particular pattern because it has unwittingly purchased a system without appreciating the organizational implications. If this approach to CSCW development becomes common it will be a new form of technical determinism ± the technology supplier will be determining the organizational structure in the user organization and preventing the stakeholders in the user organization from debating the kind of issues illustrated in the electricity service calls example. If this approach to CSCW development is followed perhaps the implicit organizational structures in each product should be prominently advertised. In the service calls case we could, for example, have the centralized, the distributed, the democratic and the subcontracted versions.

An alternative approach is to create a product which leaves the decisions about who does what where they rightfully belong: in the organization that implements the product. This implies that it is possible to create a product which can be customized to match any organizational structure. We might, for example, design a system as though one person was performing every function but offer tools that make it possible to separate the functions and produce communication links between them at the point of application. Whatever the technical problems of taking such a route, there is a serious organizational problem. Such a product would need a major customization effort on the customer’s premises and the evidence to date suggests that if this is left to the customer, it will not be done very effectively because they have little relevant experience of this way of working with technology. A sophisticated after-sales service might become an essential part of the CSCW systems supplier’s marketing strategy.

Such an approach to technology development would be a fundamental change in design philosophy ± away from engineering a solution towards \`facilitation, nurturing and husbandry’ (Hales, 1994). The designer of the generic system is now establishing infrastructures and tools which others can use to fashion solutions for themselves. As Hales (1995) illustrates, by reference to work with a major supplier of generic systems, it also raises many issues about the way in which suppliers support service delivery. This is no longer merely an installation and technical support process but needs to be a fully developed participative design process in which local stakeholders play a part in establishing future work practices and con® guring systems to support them. The story that Hales reports demonstrates that suppliers may have dif® culty adjusting to the scale of change this necessitates in the way they work.

It is possible that a strategy could be created that lies part way between the supplier making the organizational decisions or leaving them to the customer. We shall call this approach \`design at the locus of decision’. An analysis of the kind presented in this paper reveals the generic character of the cooperative task and, from this, it is possible to discern at what point in the development and application of a CSCW system decisions about the division of labour can be taken. In most instances there will be a four stage process.

## Analysis of the cooperative task

The initial stage is a study of the cooperative task as it appears in several organizations. The aim is to determine both the informational requirements to support such a task and the variety of divisions of labour that are possible. Some of the possibilities may not currently exist but may be made possible by new forms of technology. An analysis tool such as the ORDIT method will be necessary to explore these possibilities in a systematic way.

## Determination of the generic system

The generic form of the system will support the general information handling requirements of the task and embody the invariant relations that may exist in the cooperative task. These may result from legal or institutional requirements. In the service calls case the obvious example of an invariant relationship is the customer± service provider relationship. In other cases there may be legal requirements for quali® ed staff, for example medical staff, to take speci® c decisions. The analysis should also reveal the major variations in the division of labour and the generic system could be structured to be customized along these dimensions.

## Matching the generic system to the application context

At the point where a speci® c application is planned the product will be customized to match the division of labour required by the organization. Where a semipermanent work r“le exists this will involve matching the functionality, access rights, read rights, and so on, to the speci® c responsibilities of the work r“le holders. Such a process may involve some modelling of alternatives to help the potential user community appreciate the possibilities provided by the technology. The customization process should leave scope for detailed customization at the time of undertaking the task.

## Customization at the time of task performance

In most cooperative tasks there is some scope to agree the assignment of duties at the time of task performance. In many industrial settings the scope may be limited: in research, design and professional settings it can often be very large. The product should be capable of local matching and rematching to the responsibilities as they are agreed. Such a customization process has to be very easy to accomplish because it should not interrupt task performance and it will probably have to be accomplished by the users themselves.

## Conclusions

Cooperative work is a fundamental and pervasive feature of organizational life. Much effort is spent in organizations in the search for effective ways of deploying human resources so that the collective endeavour is effective. Division of labour has been the most common organizational tool for prede® ning the form the cooperation will take. It speci® es who will take what responsibilities in the collective task and thereby de® nes the relationships that will exist between the people who cooperate. There is a greater interest today in producing forms of organization that emphasize team work. In this approach the team is given the responsibility for the overall task and makes its own decisions on divisions of labour. However, in most organizational settings, there remain good reasons why a great deal of the division of labour is prespeci® ed. Contractual, legal and accountability reasons provide a fundamental underpinning to which, within organizations, we can add such dimensions as the use of scarce trained resources and technology, quality assurance and the exercise of power by managers to achieve goals such as ef® ciency, optimal utilization of resources, and so on. Even when a teamwork solution is evoked there often remain some areas of predetermined division of labour. In the third option of the alternative forms of organization for service calls, for example, the autonomous work group is limited to the electricians: there remain prede-® ned divisions of labour for the customer, the clerk and the foreman.

In the short history of efforts to create generic CSCW the issue of division of labour has not been dominant. Two kinds of assumption can be traced. The ® rst is that the cooperating group is initially unstructured and will make its own decisions about division of labour (and thereby who needs what access and control over the informational resources). This approach is not surprising because it accords broadly with the experience of cooperative work of the researchers and designers who are producing these systems. The second assumption is that the designer of the CSCW can determine the division of labour within the cooperating group. This often seems to result from studying a particular example of a cooperative task and reproducing the division of labour that is found. There is a failure to recognize that whilst the collective task may be the same across many organizations, there can be considerable variety in the organizational structures created to undertake the task.

Through the presentation and analysis of a case study of a cooperative task typical of many to be found in industry and commerce, this paper leads to two conclusions. First, we need methods of analysing cooperative work which will enable us to detect where there are basic, unalterable, structural relationships between people (the customer± service provider relationship is a case in point) where there are likely to be semipermanent divisions of labour which will vary from one application to another, and where the division of labour will take place in situ at the time of task performance. The ORDIT methods of responsibility analysis have been used here to demonstrate what is required.

Second, we need methods of CSCW design which can be generic but which enable decisions about division of labour to be made by the people who need to make them at the time they need to be made. I have suggested that unvarying structural relations can be built into the generic system, that the semipermanent but variable divisions of labour be established at the point of application and that support be given to enable group members to make local divisions of labour as they feel appropriate. Only by the development of such analysis and design methods can we do justice to the variety of organizational forms that underpin cooperative work. By such approaches we also avoid the charge of technical determinism because we empower the users of the technology to make their own decisions about their desired forms of cooperation.
