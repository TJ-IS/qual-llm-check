# Organizational politics, strategic change and the evaluation of CAD

MARTIN HARRIS

Department of Management Studies, Brunel University, London, UK

This paper uses a \`case in sector’ analysis to investigate the extent to which the success criteria imposed by cost accounting constrains the ability of engineers to acquire and make effective use of computer aided design (CAD). The paper examines two periods of CAD investment in a sample of UK manufacturers. In the ® rst \`early adoption period CAD proposals were made by junior engineers on a locally initiated \`bottom up’ basis. Proposals were made according to the dictates of conventional cost bene® t analysis, engineering and accounting priorities wer separate, and senior management were not involved. In the second \`general adoption’ period there was a general uptake of CAD throughout the sector. The paper demonstrates that the evaluation of CAD was in¯ uenced not just by the rival claims of accountants and engineers, but also by long run changes in the operating environment, and by the control devices employed by senior management. The ® ndings bear out the view of technological change as a socially negotiated process. However, it would appear that the oranizational practices associated with CAD evaluation are continually re-negotiated in the light of dynamic organizational interests and changes in the broader sectoral context.

## Introduction

Recent years have seen a growing interest in the procedures and techniques adopted by those who select and evaluate technologies prior to their implementation. Much of this research is focused on the ways in which new technology is justi® ed with respect to accounting regimes and resource allocation procedures.\* Several commentators have argued that the process of selection and justi® cation has a strong agenda-setting in¯ uence on the subsequent introduction of the technology (Boddy and Buchanan, 1983; Voss, 1986; Child and Smith, 1987; Child et al., 1987). Choices made at the evaluation or preimplementation stage determine the size of technology investments, and the success criteria applied to technology projects.

Work on institutionalized \`accounting lag’ (Hopwood, 1985; Kaplan, 1985; Child et al., 1987) is concerned with the ways in which the selection, justi® cation and evaluation of technology is shaped by deeply entrenched institutional continuities, and by the political interests and occupational priorities of those in control of technology spending.\* A number of studies have focused on the evaluation and cost justi® cation of computer aided design (CAD). CAD forms a key element in ¯ exible manufacturing, allowing a variety of advanced production technologies to be integrated. A distinctive feature of these technologies is their potential for strategic company-wide bene® ts and ability to improve non-price factors like lead time reduction and tender response. However, these bene® ts are dif® cult to predict\* and have proved elusive in many cases.²

Senker (1984) and Currie (1989) bring the accounting lag thesis to bear on the question of how CAD is assimilated into manufacturing organizations. These studies highlight the inherently political nature of the capital investment process, and demonstrate that CAD proposals are biased towards the short-term planning horizons imposed by accounting conventions. Accounting lag inhibits the strategic use of this technology in two ways. First, CAD justi® cations are aimed at cost-cutting rather than at the more intangible company-wide bene® ts.³ The need to provide accountants with a clear-cut quantitative case for CAD encourages engineers to justify CAD in terms of savings in drawing labour and estimates of productivity. Second, those responsible for CAD proposals occupy relatively junior positions. Those managers who initiate technical changes on a bottom up basis lack the authority required for a thorough analysis of more ambitious CAD/CAM applications and their organizational implications. Most companies in Senker’s (1984) study treated CAD as a design of® ce investment; few recognized its signi® cance as a strategic step towards CAD/CAM, and senior management were conspicuously absent from technical and ® nancial evaluation/ justi® cation exercises.

The evidence presented in this paper is drawn from a \`case in sector’ study (Whipp and Clark, 1986; Child and Smith, 1987) of CAD use within the UK traction engineering industry. The paper identi® es \`early adoption’ and \`general adoption’ periods of CAD investment within this sector, and uses case study analysis to investigate the ways in which accounting and engineering priorities were juxtaposed in the ® nancial and technical evaluation of this technology, highlighting the political role of senior management. The ® nal part of the paper summarizes the ® ndings and draws out a number of implications for the accounting lag thesis.

Early adoption and general adoption periods of CAD investment in the UK traction engineering industry

The ® ndings presented in this paper are drawn from research on the four ® rms which together comprise the UK traction engineering industry. Two of the four ® rms are engaged in the design and manufacture of railway vehicles (described below in the Locobuild 1 and Locobuild 2 case studies). The remaining two ® rms design and manufacture propulsion and control equipment for railway vehicles (described in the Traction A and Traction B case studies).

Investment in CAD within the UK traction engineering industry has occurred in two phases. First, there was what could be termed an early adoption phase in the years between 1977 and late 1979 during which CAD was con® ned to the Traction A company. A second phase of general adoption occurred in the years between 1983 and 1986. Figure 1 shows the sectoral pattern of CAD adoption in the 1977± 1986 period.

Three out of the four cases invested for the ® rst time in turnkey CAD equipment which had become cheaper and more reliable, and in software which was more easily matched to speci® c traction engineering applications. This generalized investment took place in a context of increased price and non-price competition, key shifts in the design requirements for propulsion and control equipments, and changes in manufacturing policies. Case study accounts of CAD adoption and evaluation

## Locobuild 1

Investment in CAD at Locobuild 1 took place against a background of impending privatization of the design and manufacturing capacity owned by the British Railways Board (BRB). Engineers at Locobuild 1 made extensive use of CAD from 1983 onwards. The company spent approximately £8m on CAD workstations, and networked engineering databases in the 1984± 1986 period. Approval for spending on CAD was given on a \`rubber stamping’ basis. Engineering and accounting aspects of the investment were entirely separate.

In the run-up to privatization in 1989 the investment climate changed dramatically, as senior management and accountants emphasized the need to cut engineering costs. The two CAD installations suffered from processor downtime, poor work organization and training, and failure to impose shift working. The engineering department proposed spending a further £3m on engineering databases in 1987. This proposal emphasized the company-wide bene® ts of integrating manufacturing and engineering databases, whilst ignoring the issue of costs. This proposal was rejected by senior management and accounting personnel. Further investment in CAD and related systems was halted completely following privatization.

## Locobuild 2

Locobuild 2 is a private sector company engaged in the design and manufacture of urban mass transit systems. The company made a £0.6m investment in 1983 in a technically ambitious attempt to integrate a CAD system with MRP (a previous proposal made by junior engineers was rejected in 1979). There was no formal technical or ® nancial justi® cation for CAD/MRP: the investment was closely associated with a company survival crisis, with the decision to dispense with manufacturing and with a ® nancial rescue package which allowed the company to continue operations in a situation of severe market constraint.

Senior management supported investment in CAD, MRP and mainframe computers, but responsibility for detailed involvement in selection, implementation or monitoring was left to a junior engineer. Productivity was monitored on an informal basis and the equipment was utilized on an ad hoc basis providing uncertain productivity bene® ts. A number of mechanical and electrical engineering applications were successfully introduced. However, the company experienced problems with processor downtime and failure to link CAD and MRP. These problems derived from the poorly executed technical evaluation and from a lack of systems development expertise.

## Traction A

Engineers at Traction A had accrued substantial experience with CAD in the early adoption period. In 1984 the company was earmarked by the corporate centre for wholesale restructuring, investment in more advanced CAD and CAD/CAM, and plans for computer integrated manufacturing (CIM). A broad range of personnel were involved in the CAD/CAM initiative, including senior engineers, production and ® nance management, systems development staff and personnel management. The CAD/CAM project engineer was a company director, responsible for a broad range of commercial, engineering and manufacturing aspects of the initiative.

The company spent £1.3m on the ® rst of several CAD modules in 1986. The proposal was made on the basis of lead time reduction and other company-wide bene® ts rather than on costs. These bene® ts were identi® ed and built into a three phase implementation plan which replaced the earlier ad hoc approach. CAD/CAM was proposed by senior management in the broader context of restructuring. This allowed previously sacrosanct risk spreading ® nancial controls based on two year payback to be relaxed.

The primary concern was with systemic companywide bene® ts, and the key targets set were technical (e.g. the routinization of the CAD/CAM link) rather than ® nancial. The technical and ® nancial evaluation of the ® rst CAD module was informed by the argument that shortened lead times and the reduction of work in progress over the long term should be the criteria of success. The CAD module, and then the CAD/CAM system, had to produce operational bene® ts before the next part of the plan for CIM was allowed to go forward. A number of bene® ts accrued to the CAD system and to the newly established CAD/CAM applications. Lead times, work in progress and costs were substantially reduced and a number of quality control problems were eliminated.

## Traction B

Traction B is a private sector supplier of propulsion and control equipment. The company invested £500 000 in four workstations in 1984. The investment climate in this case was dominated by tight product markets, corporate retrenchment measures and the need to cut costs. Engineers in another company within the group had experienced severe technical and organizational dif® culties with CAD in 1982; this earlier investment had been written off as a ® nancial loss, and senior management throughout the group were not well disposed to investment in CAD.

This situation changed when a senior engineer with substantial experience of CAD was recruited into the company. This individual advocated a more comprehensive approach which combined technical evaluation, ® nancial justi® cation and implementation planning. Senior management, junior engineers, accountants and personnel were involved in planning and in setting targets for the system.

The fact that the system was cost-justi® ed according to conventional two year payback criteria obscures the political complexity and technical learning involved. The selection of vendors was preceded by a very rigorous benchmarking exercise which focused on previously problematic areas including software customization and processor capacity. Engineering and accounting priorities were jointly addressed within the justi® cation, and ® nancial aspects were linked to operational targets. Savings in materials and reduction of subcontracting costs were closely monitored over a two year period. The two year payback was achieved from savings in subcontracting costs. The system was regarded as a ® nancia and technical success.

## The changing context of CAD investment

The case study evidence suggests that the issue of CAD needs to be understood with respect to the surrounding context. Two aspects of the overall context are of particular signi® cance in the later general adoption period (1983± 1986). The ® rst point is that the UK traction engineering industry was being restructured in line with broader developments in the product market and in the European policy arena. Senior management readings of these developments in the 1983± 1986 period produced a climate of investment which was more propitious than had been the case in the early adoption period. This conjunction of external and internal changes had a formative in¯ uence on the basic character of the CAD proposals investigated. ## Accounting lag and computer aided design: key ® ndings

## Senior management involvement in the early adoption period

The central feature of the accounting lag thesis is the view that locally initiated (bottom up) proposals, lacking the political support of senior management, need to be expressed in terms which will convince accountants that an acceptable return on investment (ROI) will accrue to technology projects. In the studies of CAD referred to in the introduction, Senker (1984) and Currie (1989) demonstrated that accounting lag blocks the successful implementation of this key technology in manufacturing organizations. In a very comprehensive comparative study of banking, health care and retail organizations Child et al. (1987) showed that short termist ROI calculations, and the cost saving objectives of accountants, act to avoid organizational redesign and to direct technology proposals away from comprehensive company-wide bene® ts.\*

How far can these points be squared with the ® ndings presented in the present study? The evidence from the early adoption period supports the view that locally initiated CAD proposals may be blocked by accountants. In this period, awareness of CAD was con® ned to drawing of® ce staff and engineers. Senior management were not involved in the details of CAD selection and justi® cation. However, the conditions in the early adoption phase of investment were succeeded by radically new conditions after 1983, when a number of large contracts ended and the political and ® scal uncertainties in the UK domestic market became more serious. These dif® culties notwithstanding, the advent of relatively cheap, technically sophisticated turnkey CAD hardware and software meant that this technology became a highly visible spending issue outside engineering departments after 1983. Both senior management and ® nance personnel became familiar with the question of investing in CAD and other computer aided systems.

## Senior management readings of technology and environment: CAD evaluation in the late adoption period

As noted above, protagonists of the accounting lag thesis take the view that accountants will emphasize the need for cost cutting at the expense of vital non-price manufacturing advantages associated with CAD and related technologies.\*

Each of the four companies investigated faced sustained pressure to enhance non-price performance (for example improvements in tender response and product performance). However, management also experienced a pressing need to make savings in engineering and manufacturing costs.² The need to compete simultaneously on price and non-price criteria has been described in terms of a productivity± innovation dilemma (Clark et al., 1985, pp. 425± 9). In the UK traction engineering industry, the potential offered by CAD for both cost reduction and company-wide advantages bulked large in the minds of the engineers, accountants and senior managers involved in the evaluation of this technology.

The productivity± innovation dilemma was sustained by a diverse set of exogenous conditions which included: the persistence of \`stop go’ policies on UK railway infrastructure investment; the prospect of a single market in Europe, and changes in EC procurement law; and moves to smaller production runs and more customized propulsion/control equipment.

Perhaps the most pressing consequence of the prospect of a single European market was the expectation that costs would continue to be a key factor as protectionist barriers were removed and as the number of European railway suppliers was reduced. Investment in CAD during the general adoption period can thus be seen as part of a much broader sectoral pattern than is considered by proponents of accounting lag.\* The senior management actors responsible for investment in CAD and related technologies responded to a range of contextual referents and readings of structural changes in the operating environment. In three out of four cases company restructuring represented the main contextual in¯ uence on investment in CAD, and the need to reduce manufacturing and engineering costs was a salient in¯ uence on those senior managers responsible for authorizing spending on CAD. However, the emphasis on cost reduction cannot be equated with a ritualized rejection of proposals for spending on CAD and CAD/ CAM equipment. At Traction A and Traction B, serious attempts were made to evaluate both the qualitative and quantitative cases for investment in these technologies. The realignment of engineering and accounting priorities at Traction A and Traction B owed much to the ways in which senior management in both companies were able to deploy political resources in support of CAD and CAD/CAM. The in¯ uence of senior management was re¯ ected in the status and sponsorship accorded to projects and in the symbolic and material resources extended to project management teams.

A brief reconsideration of the Traction A case serves to illustrate a particularly sharp contrast between early justi® cation of CAD and later justi® cation of CAD/ CAM. In the early adoption period, CAD was costjusti® ed on a more or less arbitrary (or ritual) basis, and the operating bene® ts were con® ned to the drawing of® ce throughout the early adoption period. It was only after a lengthy period of technical learning, and a complex of diverse but cumulatively signi® cant changes in a range of key internal and external aspects that the case for company-wide non-price bene® ts could be voiced by divisional management negotiating with the corporate centre.

## Conclusion

The evidence presented in this paper suggests that the technical, political and ® nancial issues associated with investment in CAD formed part of a broader and more complex pattern of change than would be implied by the accounting lag thesis. The question of investment in CAD and related technologies was informed, in all cases, not just by the rival claims of accountants and engineers, but also by long run changes in the operating environment, and by the control devices deployed by senior management. The ® ndings bear out the view of technological change as a socially negotiated process. However, it would appear that the organizational practices associated with CAD evaluation are continually re-negotiated in the light of dynamic organizational interests and changes in the broader sectoral context.
