# Measuring the economic value of information systems

BERT VAN WEGEN AND ROBERT DE HOOG

University of Amsterdam

Department of Social Science Informatics

The determination of value of information or information systems is a basic issue for information management. In order to solve it several questions must be answered like: what is the object of valuation; how is value de® ned and measured; and what constitutes a coherent and usable method for valuation. In this paper an approach is outlined that combines the information commodity approach, activity-based costing, and graph modelling. The ® rst is used to de® ne the object of analysis (an information commodity) and the nature of value (the demand value at the marketplace). The third allows the modelling of business processes in terms of activities and cost relations between activities. The second enables the assignment of costs to activities modelled in the graph. Together they constitute a coherent and usable method for determining the value of IS. This is illustrated by means of a case study.

## Introduction

Effective management of information requires the assessment of the value of information or IS. The added value of IS in the attainment of the organizational goals is crucial. This is a complex issue, requiring the solution of a number of problems, including the following:

l what object should be valued;

l how is value de® ned; and

l how can one measure this value

This article contains an answer to these questions based on the information commodity approach (Mowshowitz, 1992a). Whether the object of assessment should be the information or the IS will be discussed in the next section. The successive section will discuss the measurement of economic value. The production digraph is presented as a modelling method. How to measure the value with the production digraph model and activity-based costing will be shown. The article is concluded with an illustration of the method which is evaluated, and dif® culties in applying it are discussed.

## The object of valuation: information versus information commodity

The choice of an object of valuation is a prerequisite for the de® nition of the value of information. The object of valuation depends on the goal of valuation and the assumptions made in de® ning information or IS. Several authors have been working on the value of information (for an extensive overview see Repo, 1986; Mowshowitz 1992a) and their assumptions about this signi® cant issue vary widely. Some approaches are brie¯ y discussed below.

An approach that set the stage for many scholars was the work of Shannon and Weaver (1949). Elementary in their approach is their entropy function that assigns probabilities to states. Amongst a variety of ® elds this function has been applied to measure the value of information. In that ® eld the entropy function is used to determine a unit of information. However, the main limitation of this approach is that there is no notion of the relevance to the task at hand (Mowshowitz, 1987). Messages can have the same entropy value but when they originate from different situations their value can be different.

Following this, other approaches were proposed to assess the value of information. Marschak and Radner (1972) de® ned the value of information in terms of probabilities of events and utilities of different action state pairs. Other researchers have analysed the value of information on the micro (Mason and Sassone, 1978) or macro level (Gotlieb and Parsons, 1991).

Glazer (1993) focused on information instead of information commodities. The author claimed that the role of the information carrier does not need consideration because the cost of the technology is minimal with a suf® ciently high volume of processing. The contradiction here is that Glazer de® ned the value of information in terms of the value in use, whereas the value of the information carrier is de® ned in terms of its cost. Our claim is that both the information involved and the information carrier are responsible for the added value in use.

These approaches lead to two major problems. First, de® nitions of information proposed thus far have failed to specify the domain. Shannons model did not take the meaning of the information and aims of the user into account. Glazer excluded the added value of the technology that contains the information. The main point here is that the focus on information only misses the furnishing aspect of information. The furnishing of information is the processing of the information required to

l send the information to the right person;

l at the right time and location; and

l in the right form.

Second, the level of analysis also introduces problems in value assessment. Marschak and Radner’s expected utility approach (1972) seemed to focus on a level of analysis that was too ® ne-grained for practical applications: determining the value in decision making requires a large amount of data about the decision that may not be easily obtainable. In micro and macro level approaches the complete set of IT resources in an organization is the object of analysis. The total IT expenditure is evaluated on the level of a ® rm. These approaches failed to differentiate between different information products and services. The types of information and how they are embodied are not considered, although it is clear that not all information has equal value, and there are different ways of furnishing information. In addition, these ® rm level performance measures are distorted by intervening variables (Lucas, 1992). For example, the state of the national/global economy and changes in the marketplace affect a ® rmlevel performance measure such as pro® tability.

A solution to this problem lies in de® ning a different object of valuation: the information commodity (Mowshowitz, 1992a). The value of information cannot be determined independently from the medium that encapsulates and processes it. The assessment of value of only the content of information seems a natural corollary of the use of information as an input for decision making. However, although information of course acquires its value from its role in decision making, the information product as a whole also adds (furnishes) value to other activities in information processing (Taylor, 1982).

A simple example will clarify the importance of the furnishing of information. Suppose there exist two dictionaries that contain the same de® nitions of Dutch words. One of them, however, is not formatted nor indexed. Obviously, the indexed dictionary will be of greater value to the user. Therefore, both the furnishing aspects of an information commodity and the value of the information in decision making should be involved in the assessment of value.

The focus on the information commodity enables differentiation of various information products or services which is not possible at the micro and macro level. In addition, the environmental in¯ uences on the success of the system in terms of increased revenues or strategic impact can be identi® ed and assessed. So the focus on the information commodity solves important problems that emerged using the other approaches.

We have de® ned the object of valuation to be the information system that is a special type of information commodity. Now we turn to the question of how the value of IS as a commodity can be assessed.

## The value of an IS

The information commodity approach assumes that the value of an IS is restricted to the economics of the marketplace. To be traded on the marketplace a commodity must be appropriated and valued. Because the information is captured in a commodity it can be appropriated. Furthermore, the indices of the information commodity can be used to assess the use of information. Once that can be assessed, the potential value of the information can be estimated. Trading the information system at the marketplace means that its value is assumed to be a function of the demand value and the supply value.

The pricing model introduced by Mowshowitz (1992a) applies only to the production of information commodities offered for sale by suppliers and the use of them by clients as an intermediate good. This excludes goods for ® nal consumption. For these goods the demand value is derived from subjective client preferences, and differs from the demand value of intermediate goods. For intermediate goods the cost of production by the supplier ± assuming that the supplier at least wants to break even ± is an estimator of the lower bound of the market value. This is the supply value of the IS. The production costs of an IS can be determined by measuring the costs involved in building the system (see Mowshowitz, 1992b).

The demand value at the marketplace of an IS is the average value that is generated when the IS is deployed in organizations. This is an estimator of the upper bound of the market value. The demand value is a function of the value generated in the organizations that use the information commodity. Therefore, we need a measure of the added value for each separate organization. The demand value for an organization is derived from the contribution of the system to its goals. We assume that the goals will vary within and over organizations. A business organization is assumed to aim for maximalization of pro® t and continuity of the business, or

Then for a business organization the maximum demand value of an information commodity equals the increase in pro® ts that results from use, or the loss of not using it (Mowshowitz, 1992c). However, there may be other relevant goals like the goals aimed for by various stakeholders. The problem is that these goals are not always explicit (Farbey et al., 1993) when an IS is evaluated. We will see that these goals can be important, depending on the type of investment in IT.

An important issue is at which level of performance the demand value of an IS should be measured. In other words, one can represent the goals of an organization as a tree in which strategic goals are decomposed into less general tactical goals and operational goals. The performance of an IS can be measured at each level of this goal tree. Benson and Parker (1989) traced the value of IS in a business organization to goals like customer choice, production quality and ® nancial performance. For these goals they de® ned the following measures respectively: market share, product returns and pro® tability. However, measurement of value at the ® rm level is complicated because of the intervening variables (Lucas, 1992). The change in pro® tability of the ® rm is not caused by the application of the IS alone. The combined action of organizing, strategic planning and application of IT together with the dynamics of the marketplace determine the ® rm’s pro® tability (Earl, 1989). To avoid these intervening variables, they should be made explicit ® rst. In addition, performance measurement should focus on lower level variables that relate performance directly to the application, and the impact of the intervening variables should also be considered.

In the next section several approaches to determine the demand value of an IS are reviewed. In addition, our method will be compared with these approaches.

## Approaches to economic value in business

The investment in an IS should provide added value to justify the expenses. As argued before, this value is derived from the contribution of IS to the attainment of the goals of the organization. During the past decade several investment analysis approaches have been proposed to measure the value added by IS. Frequently used indicators in investment analysis express the value added in monetary terms ± more speci® cally cash ¯ ows ± over the period studied. Examples are net present value, pay back period and bene® t to cost ratios. They re¯ ect single objective deterministic methods (Soni et al., 1992). These methods assume that the value of the impacts can be predicted and measured in dollars. The complexity and uncertainty of IS impacts in more radical and strategic investments in IT have given rise to a variety of other approaches (for a comprehensive overview see Farbey et $a l . . .$ , 1993). In such circumstances objective computation of the monetary value is no longer suitable and evaluation can be characterized as a \`social action’ (Farbey et $a l . ,$ , 1993). This means that we can measure the value only by a qualitative evaluation process in which all relevant stakeholders are involved.

The situation in which a social action perspective prevails can be de® ned with the variables listed below.

The contribution of the IS to the attainment of goals may be uncertain. There is uncertainty about what exactly causes a bene® t or whether the bene® t will be accomplished (Farbey et al., 1993). For example, strategic investments may contribute to the competitive position of a company, but other organizational changes may have been more important to achieve this. Similarly, this strategic investment may not lead to a better competitive position because competitors may apply the same or even better strategies. In cost bene® t analysis this problem is overcome by giving a money value to this type of bene® t, based on i) detailed analysis, ii) surrogate measurement, or iii) just a notional valuation (Farbey et al., 1993).

The bene® ts of an IS may be dif® cult to measure. That ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ some cannot be measured in monetary terms, but their importance in a speci® c case might require to include them in the evaluation. Multi-objective multi-criteria methods enable the decision-maker to rank options on dimensions that are measured on different value bases (Parker et al., 1988; Farbey et $a l . ,$ 1993; Remenyi et $a l . ,$ 1993).

There may exist uncertainty about the goals themselves. Different relevant stakeholders may disagree on which goals should be attained. Evaluation methods should than take account of consensus building (Farbey et al., 1993).

The variety in deployment situations calls for a contingency approach, because there is no one best method for the evaluation of IS Remenyi et al. (1993) and Farbey et al. (1994) proposed schemes which use the above mentioned variables for selection of the proper evaluation method given a speci® c situation. The approaches in the investment evaluation literature all assume certain data to be present. They do not tell how to determine the values of the variables that are input to the decision making process. This is a critical shortcoming of these approaches. The method of valuation proposed by Mowshowitz (1992a) is a tool that attempts to remedy this shortcoming.

With this method of measurement the bene® ts that accrue from the ef® ciencies of IS can be measured. The ef® ciency bene® ts that are relevant for the decision are those that reduce the process costs and which lead to reductions in future cash out¯ ows. Accordingly, the estimates of future reductions in expenses can be used to determine return on investment or can be input to a multi-objective multi-criteria method like information economics (Parker et al., 1988)

## The measurement of value in the information commodity approach

The measurement method proposed in the information commodity approach focuses on the impact on the user’s production process (Mowshowitz, 1992c). Mowshowitz restricted the measurement of the demand value of an information commodity to an estimate of the cost reduction in the production process that results from its use. The production process is represented as a chain of activities that incur costs and that are linked to each other when they have a cost relation. Bellin (1991) and Chandansingh (1995) have successfully applied the method for the analysis of impacts of respectively CASE-tools and CAD systems.

The focus on activities is a major methodological commitment. It is assumed that an IS changes the production process in terms of structure and cost. The idea is not new, \`the most useful approach for evaluating of® ce productivity gains is by tracking changes in the total cost for delivering an identi® able work product . . . To gather such information systematically, one must classify work into its components’ (Strassmann, 1985). This point of view ± analysis of a production process through the analysis of activities and their cost relations ± will require, as we will see later, a speci® c type of cost assignment.

The production environment is modelled with the production digraph. The basic idea is that both the original and the modi® ed production process will be modelled in terms of activities and their costs. Therefore, this method cannot be used for situations in which there is no original process. The cost reduction can be determined by comparing the cumulative cost of both models.

The production digraph is a special type of graph (for a general introduction in graph theory see Harary, 1969). Four basic elements of a production process are represented: activities, activity costs, cost relations between activities, and their weights. Nodes represent activities, and edges represent cost relations between activities. Special annotations at nodes and edges refer to activity costs and cost relation weights. Two special node types, the source and sink, initiate and terminate the production process. A directed edge from node \`activity A’ to node \`activity B’ means activity B receives an input from activity A to perform its function or requires a certain quality of what is produced by activity A. The cost of an activity is de® ned by the sum of costs that is input to the activity and the costs of execution of the activity itself. A weight represents a part of the activity cost assigned to a particular successive activity. In this way the costs accumulate from the source to the sink. The source by de® nition has no input relations. The sink by de® nition has no output relations, no execution cost and receives the total accumulated costs from the input relations. ## Measuring the economic value of IS

To model the production process by means of a production digraph the following steps must be executed.

(1) Determine relevant process(es). The relevant processes are obviously those in which the system is/will be used. In addition, the system may change other processes as well, by changing the procedure or output of activities and processes, and these should also be incorporated. Therefore, the scope of analysis should be broad enough to keep an eye on possible side effects elsewhere. Note that the use of IT reallocates cost from direct labour (local) expenses to overhead and capital (global) expenses (Strassmann, 1985).

(2) De® ne activities. The activities incorporated in the relevant processes should be de® ned. However, the focus should be on those activities in the process that are likely to change in terms of cost because of the IS. They should be speci® ed at a level of granularity at which the impact of the IS on the activity cost structure can be made clear.

(3) De® ne activity relations. The structure of activities should be de® ned with the cost perspective in mind. A cost relation exists when one activity requires some achievement by another. In other words, this can be seen as the demand imposed on the other activity by the activity under consideration.

(4) Estimate relevant activity costs. Resources can be used or consumed by different activities. This means that we have to ® nd how costs of resources used should be assigned to each activity. This is called cost assignment and how we should perform this task is described in the next section. To compare the production digraphs with different volumes of production and different products, the activity cost must be calculated per product.

(5) Estimate activity relation costs. We have to determine how the activities are related in terms of cost. This can be done by analysis of their relations in quantitative output terms and qualitative output requirements.

(6) Determine changes in the production digraph. The important changes are the changes of activities, activity costs, activity relations, and activity relation costs. The cost analysis can be performed before or after the introduction of the IS. Before the introduction of the system the cost analysis requires that one can predict the activity and cost changes. The primary key to prediction of change in costs and structure is the experience of both relevant users and system developers. In addition, the cost structure of the original situation may support this task. Only when the system is operational the real cost changes can be measured.

(7) Derive potential cost reduction. The change in cost of resources as a result of the introduction of the IS is the potential cost reduction. The prerequisite is that this will not affect the quality of production.

(8) Derive feasible cost reduction. Recall that the result of the method should be an estimate of the reduction of future cash out¯ ows. This refers to future cost of resources that are avoidable (Coulthrust and Piper, 1986; Hirsch and Nibbelin, 1992; Lewis, 1995). Part of these resources will not be purchased or they can be reallocated to another activity for which they had to be purchased otherwise. If, for instance, an employee’s capacity is freed, it should either be possible to reallocate that capacity and avoid the hiring of another person or to lay off the employee. The cost reduction that will be feasible depends on the depreciation scheme used for the IS and the future required capacity, and both are strategic choices.

At the core of these steps is the question of cost assignment. Without an explicit technique for this, the method will be less powerful from a valuation perspective. The next section addresses this issue.

## Costing the production digraph

In this section the Activity-Based Costing (ABC) technique is described for cost assignment. Its application for the production digraph is shown. However, before the technique is introduced some relevant general concepts in costing are discussed.

## Costing in general

This section presents a simple typology of costs that is needed for understanding the basic issue in cost accounting: assignment of indirect cost. However, ® rst concept itself must be de® ned. \`We de® ne costs as a resource sacri® ced or foregone to achieve a speci® c objective’ (Horngren, 1991).

The so-called object of calculation is de® ned as any activity or item for which a separate cost measurement is required (Horngren, 1991). Examples are a product or a service, a client, a department, or an activity. The concept of directness of a cost to an object of calculation is fundamental in cost accounting. Costs are direct costs if they can be identi® ed speci® cally with the object of calculation; otherwise the cost is indirect (Horngren, 1991). Note that the object of calculation determines whether the cost is direct or indirect. The directness of relevant costs to the object of calculation has implications for the possibility to assign the costs in a non-arbitrary way.

There are several ways to assign cost to an object of calculation (Keys, 1994). If the cost is direct it can be assigned using the causal relation between the object and the cost: the cost is traced to the object of calculation. Some indirect costs can be traced as well, using an intermediate object of calculation. Meij (1960) in his work on the production-centre approach proposed that indirect costs can be dealt with using two direct relations representing an indirect relation; one between the indirect costs and the achievement of a goal and one between this achievement and the product. However, if for a cost type no (indirect) causal relation with the object of calculation exists, then the cost is allocated to the object. For example, the cost of general management cannot be traced. It will be allocated to the product on some reasonable grounds which cannot be proven. Consequently, traced costs are more likely to lead to accurate cost information than allocated costs.

Cost calculation techniques differ in their approach (and effort) to trace and allocate costs. For our present purpose we need a calculation technique that ® ts the process modelling approach. This is activity-based costing.

## Activity-based costing

In this section the theory of ABC and then its implementation is discussed. The use of ABC is explained for our purpose which is assignment of the costs of relevant resources to the elements of the production digraph.

## Theory of activity-based costing

The most attractive property of ABC from our point of view is the central focus on activities of the enterprise. As Staubus (1971) stated: \`Objects of costs are always activities. We want to know the cost of doing something’. Additionally, the validity of the cost data resulting from ABC is higher compared with traditional approaches that do not share this activity orientation. This claim is valid when the costs of activities are not proportional to the volume of outputs produced, whereas many traditional approaches are biased to cost assignment based on volume (Cooper, 1990; Boons et al. 1991; Cooper et al., 1992). Also, when considerable amounts of total costs consist of indirect costs that are variable in the long term, the ABC approach is more accurate than other approaches (Berliner and Brimson, 1988).

In Figure 2 the basic idea of ABC is depicted. Costs of a resource to an activity should be assigned based on a measure of the demand of that activity for a certain resource. A resource driver is a variable this represents that demand. The values of the variable are in proportion with the cost of the corresponding resources. Resource costs will be assigned to an activity proportionally to the value of the activity on the resource driver. An example of a resource driver of the resource \`mainframe’ can be \`CPU seconds’. Each activity (order processing in this example) will be monitored on its CPU use and the costs of the mainframe will be divided according to the share of an activity in total used CPU seconds.

An activity may be incorporated in different production processes for making different products or services. To trace the activity costs to products an activity driver is required (see Figure 2). The activity driver is a variable that re¯ ects the frequency and intensity of the demands placed on activities by the product or service. Some problems in combining ABC and the production digraph model

Theoretically the application of the method outlined so far seems to be smooth, but two practical problems during implementation were encountered (1) at what level of abstraction should an activity be speci® ed; and (2) how can we identify and select resource and activity drivers. These questions will be answered in this section.

(1) The aggregation level of the activities that are de® ned in ABC is not arbitrary. An activity should be homogeneous in cost behaviour (Boons et $a l . ,$ 1991). An activity is cost homogeneous if it is possible to de® ne an activity driver that represents the demands placed by products on each of its constituent sub-activities. In other words, the demands on one sub-activity should be proportional to the demands placed on other sub-activities, independent of the product. Consequently, activities should be decomposed until they are cost homogeneous. This principle guides the second task of de® ning activities of the method.

(2) Several methods are proposed for identi® cation of resource and activity drivers. With statistical methods like multiple regression and factor analysis a selection of variables can be made based on the measure of correlation. The methods are quite straightforward but require a lot of data. When this is too expensive or when the data is not available the practitioner should rely on observation in production, and interviews with relevant employees.

To reduce the expense of implementation of the ABC technique the resource and activity drivers should be selected according to their relative accuracy and the relative ease of implementation. If the cost of an activity is small compared with the total costs, the accuracy of their calculation decreases very little when it shares an activity driver with another activity.

## GRASSO Products BV Holland

GRASSO Products BV is a subsidiary of the German GEA AG company. It develops and produces components for industrial refrigerating and air conditioning systems. There are different types of products; screw and piston compressors, and these can be sold in units or in packages. The products are sold, engineered and produced on order. The continuing increase in volume of these products resulted in increasing standardization of the products and production. However, the market demanded the opposite. The scope of possible client-speci® c orders was growing as well as the assortment of new components. To cope with the growing complexity of the con® guration task, the employees at the sales department restricted the possible con® gurations to a few types. This resulted in suboptimal solutions and incorrect con® gurations. The IS that supported the sales department were very expensive in maintenance and were the main cause of the long timeto-market for new products.

Top management wanted the organization to become more ¯ exible and anticipating and deciding to introduce of the knowledge based system GPVOS (GRASSO Products sales support system). The goal of this system was to increase the information processing capacity at the sales department, lower the cost of maintenance and reduce the time-to-market for new products. The impact of the system in terms of the production digraphs and cost structure and cost volume is now presented.

## Processing and the introduction of GPVOS

This section describes the relevant processes that were subject to change: quotation processing, ordering processing and construction.

In the original situation (see Figure 3) the quotation process started when a quotation was requested and then had to be registered (see the activity \`register quotation’ ). After this a product con® guration was made according to the functional and technical requirements by the activity \`conceptualize’. Subsequently, the activity \`determine price release quotation’ detailed the con® guration in terms of a speci® cation of the numbers of components and determines the price according to standard price lists. Then the quotation is typed, signed and sent to the client.

In order processing, the activity \`process and check’ collects the data and checks whether the speci® cations are suf® cient to detail the order; that is the activity \`detail speci® cation’ . In \`con® rm order’ the purchases are speci® ed, time for drawings is reserved at the construction department and the logistics are checked.

The \`rework’ activity at the construction process is required to correct mistakes and omissions.

Quotation and order processing were originally supported by two IS. One system supported the activities conceptualize and detail speci® cation, but only for screw compressors. This IS generated a global technical speci® cation but the component numbers and the prices had to be determined by hand by the employee. The other IS supported all these activities for piston compressors. As both systems were built with conventional software, system maintenance was very expensive. The \`rework’ activity at the construction process is eliminated because the quality of the orders processed by GPVOS has improved. This of course does not mean that the complete construction process is eliminated, but only the activity in it that was relevant for our cost oriented analysis.

## The cost assignment and cost reduction

Only the relevant costs for the activities should be incorporated in the analysis, that is only the costs that are expected to change substantially. GPVOS is expected to accelerate the cycle time for all activities at quotation and order processing, except system maintenance. System maintenance does use a different labour resource than the other activities. The labour cost of system maintenance is relevant because knowledge technology is expected to be more suitable for this type of application and will result in less time consuming system maintenance. The 100% correct con® guration output of GPVOS will eliminate the rework at construction. Concluding, there are three resources relevant here: labour cost at the sales department, the IT department and the construction department.

The feasible cost reduction consists of those resources that are expected to have changed and for which future cost can be avoided or reallocated. In this case it is expected that all the human resources can be reallocated. In the next sections how the costs of the activities in the original and new situation were calculated are discussed.

The cost effects of GPVOS can be derived from the changes in activity drivers in the new situation.

(2) The activity driver of system maintenance is the basis for improvement: the time per change is reduced. ## The demand value of GPVOS

In the previous sections the cost per activity per product was calculated. The volumes of quotations and order for each type of product multiplied with that cost result in the total expense. That cost can be calculated for both the original and new situation in order to determine the total cost reduction. Note that the calculation of the cost reduction is made on a ceteris paribus basis. It is assumed that the labour-costs reductions can be reallocated or avoided, therefore they wil lead to a decrease in future cash out¯ ows.

The reduction in labour costs for quotation and order processing is the major part. The total time reduction caused by the deployment of GPVOS can be used to reduce the capacity requirements of the sales department. The total reduction in labour cost would then be f 146,559 for quotation and f 320,584 for order processing.

The decrease in time per software change has decreased the system maintenance cost from f 44,765 (the sum of column labelled SC in Table 4) to f 7,149 (the sum of the same column in Table 8). Thus the cost reduction for system maintenance amounts to f 37,616.

The rework at the construction department is calculated for piston compressors only. The elimination of rework results in a cost reduction of f 22,516.

Finally, under the assumptions stated above we now can determine the total cost reduction. This is f 527,276 per year. The demand value of the GRASSO organization for GPVOS amounts at least to this value.

## Measuring the economic value of IS

In addition to the cost bene® ts that accrue from improved ef® ciency there are other bene® ts.

The time-to-market has improved signi® cantly resulting from the technology used. This increases the ¯ exibility of the organization, which is crucial for the continuity of this company.

The lead-time of quotations is reduced substantially. This may result in selling more products.

The quality of the con® gurations has increased. This may result in higher client satisfaction.

Hence the method is complementary to others that propose measures for these bene® ts, for example cost bene® t analysis.

## Conclusion

In this paper we have described a method that gives answers to the questions posed in the introduction. The information commodity approach is central in the method. In this approach the de® nition of the object of analysis is the information commodity. The value of this object of analysis is assumed to be equal to the price on the marketplace. The value of an information commodity to an organization is in part derived from the costs reduction in production.

The production digraph model is a tool to model production processes and enables the manager to determine the cost of production for different production situations. Concepts from ABC are used to assign the cost to the elements in the production digraph.

The case study shows that the method can be used to determine the cost reduction systematically resulting from the deployment of an knowledge based system. This cost reduction equals reductions in future cash out¯ ows that are input to investment analysis.

A bene® t of the method is that most of the necessary information is already available, like process diagrams and cost information. In our experience the method can be implemented with little effort and commitment of resources. In addition, the method is expected to be useful for business process redesign.

However, the method is not designed to quantify other bene® ts than those that result from increased ef® ciency. Examples are increased revenues, improved marketshare, improved organizational ¯ exibility etc.

There is another shortcoming of the method. It does not involve the concept of uncertainty and risk. Identi® cation and assessment of these risks in combination with a sensitivity analysis of the cost analysis is the solution to this shortcoming.

As a result, the method should be applied in combination with other methods depending on the situation and the type of bene® ts expected.
