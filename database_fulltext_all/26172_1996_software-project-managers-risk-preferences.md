Journal of Information Technology (1996) 11, 287–295

# Software project managers' risk preferences

THOMAS W. LAUER

Decision and Information Sciences Department, School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309-4401, USA

A questionnaire was administered to 68 software project managers (SPMs). Questions were designed to test whether SPMs' risky judgments were more consistent with Expected Utility Theory or Prospect Theory. Although the results were more consistent with Prospect Theory, they differed in important ways showing SPMs' judgments to be less homogeneous than is assumed by either theory. These results highlight the potential importance of SPMs' judgments to the outcome of software development projects.

## Risk and software project management

The failure of software project managers (SPMs) to deliver their projects on time, within budget, and according to specifications has been a source of discomfort to those in the IS profession for over two decades. The size, complexity, and strategic importance of many of the information systems currently being developed makes the understanding of those factors that contribute to software project risk even more important than it has been in the past. A number of factors that contribute to the risk of a project have been discussed in the literature. Generally, these factors relate to structural characteristics of the project such as size and complexity. Although this approach to the problem has led to important insights, very little effort has gone into understanding the project manager's attitudes toward risk.

Modern day technology to support the project manager is vastly superior to what was available one or two decades ago. In spite of this, the tendency of projects to incur both budget and schedule overruns or fail to meet user needs is still a major concern (Rothfeder, 1988; Simmons et al., 1993). Other studies suggest that projects fail managerially, not technologically (Boehm, 1991; Phan et al., 1988). The project manager is always presented with an uncertain flow of resources and is frequently confronted with a set of conflicting objectives. Decisions and actions in this context are significantly influenced by pressures, perceptions, and organizational incentives (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick, 1983). For these reasons, it can be inferred that a major part of the project manager's task involves risk management. Therefore, the study of the project manager's risky choices may reveal important insights into the software project management task.

This paper reports a study designed to examine SPMs' risky choices. In many fields, the dominant paradigm for explaining risky judgments is the expected utility theory (von Neumann and Morganstern, 1947). More recently, a theoretical alternative, Prospect Theory, has been proposed. It is consistent with observed patterns that violate the axioms of expected utility theory. Although accumulated evidence seems to support Prospect Theory (see e.g., Currim and Sarin, 1989), few studies have actually examined practitioners' risky choices with realistic problems. The objective of this study is to determine whether SPMs' choices are in conformance with some of the major results of prospect theory. The SPMs were presented with eight scenarios, each posing a choice of two different risky actions. The scenarios were adaptations of scenarios presented in the original paper on prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews relevant literature on software project risk. Following this is a brief review of utility and prospect theory. Next, the experiment and results are presented. The remainder of the paper is devoted to further analysis and interpretation of the results.

## Software project risk

Risk can be defined in terms of three components, magnitude of loss, probability of loss, and exposure to loss (MacCrimmon and Wehrung, 1986). One way of defining loss for software projects is in terms of the schedule, budget, and system capabilities promised in the system specifications. Negative deviations in any of these three areas may be considered as loss. There is uncertainty associated with each of these deviations.

Furthermore, various stakeholders, including the MIS department, individual managers within the company, the company as a whole, may suffer as a result of their exposure to loss. Most often, studies that concern themselves with SPM risk concentrate on specific risk sources. Risk sources are those factors that potentially increase one of the three risk components.

For example, McFarlan (1981), emphasizes project size, project structure, and the project teams familiarity with the relevant technology as being the primary sources of a project's inherent risk. Davis (1982) argues that the greatest risk is inherent in the information requirements determination process. He goes on to identify four risk sources: size and structure of the project, the ability of the users to specify requirements, and the ability of analysts to elicit requirements. Nidomolu (1995) incorporates both views by defining project uncertainty (risk) as consisting of technological uncertainty and requirements uncertainty. Boehm and Ross (1988) divide risks into generic risk and project-specific risk. Generic risks are common to all projects and may be handled 'by standard development plan techniques'. The most common project-specific risks include personnel shortfalls, unrealistic schedules and budgets, inappropriate requirements, shortfalls in external components and tasks, and technology shortfalls or unknowns.

Boehm and Ross (1988) implicitly point out shortcomings in the state of the art of SPM when they state that ‘. . . search for a single unifying principle to guide software project management has been relatively unrewarding. . .’ Some studies have focused on social and political factors as sources of risk. Waldrop (1984) maintains that political risk and lack of top management support can result in both time pressure and the withdrawal of crucial resources. Scacchi (1984) views software as value laden social artifacts. This accounts for the fact that individuals will not regard the creation and use of software neutrally or dispassionately. He stresses the importance of analysing social interactions as a means of understanding various aberrant events that occur during the development of software.

Although this body of research provides insight into sources of risk, it neglects the judgment of risk on the part of the important actors who make up the software development project. Software projects involve sequences of judgments and choices on the part of, among others, the software project manager. The software project manager's choices in the face of a complex of dynamically changing risk sources can dramatically influence the project's outcome. In contrast to the research on software project management risk, the cumulative collection of risk research in other disciplines emphasizes both sources of risk and judgments of respondents (e.g., Einhorn and Hogarth, 1981).

Risk judgments are influenced by recognition of sources of risk and judgment of potential costs and benefits. Therefore, in order to gain an understanding of software project risk, it is necessary to study risk judgments on the part of software project managers.

Some recent studies have done just that. A laboratory simulation experiment using graduate students in a software engineering class concluded that an anchoring and adjustment strategy was used (Abdel-Hamid et al., 1993). Two studies have examined the phenomenon of escalation of commitment during the life of a software project. Keil's (1995) case study attributed the failure of a large software project to management's escalated commitment in the face of increasingly negative information. In their longitudinal study, Newman and Sabherwal (1996) identified determinants of participants' changing levels of commitment over the course of a 17-year-long software project. Their study discussed both escalation and decline of commitment and found four types of determinants that led to change in the level of commitment: project, psychological, social, and structural. These findings emphasize the important of the SPM's risky decision-making on the outcome of a project, a factor that researchers seeking to understand software project management will have to take into consideration.

## Expected utility theory and prospect theory

Like most fields, engineering disciplines have generally adhered to a set of normative assumptions about risky decision-making that are consistent with expected utility theory. For example, a text on engineering investment decisions (Rose, 1976) asserts that people can be calibrated by a monotonically increasing utility function. Two books on the subject of software engineering (Boehm, 1981; Charette, 1989) explicitly discuss expected utility theory. The rationale advanced for including this discussion is that expected utility is descriptive in that ‘... it is a tool for predicting decisions people will make...’ and prescriptive in that ‘... it establishes how people ought to make decisions ...’ (Charette, 1989). Recently there have been serious challenges to expected utility that undermine its value for understanding risk.

As a preliminary to discussing expected utility theory, it is useful to present the concept of certainty equivalence. If one had an entry to a game of chance that provided a 50% chance of winning \$1000, it would be possible to determine a selling price for that entry. That price would be the certainty equivalent for that individual. A certainty equivalent is the value that would make a decision maker indifferent between an uncertain event and that value. In this example, if a

person's certainty equivalent is equal to \$500, the expected value of the uncertain event, that person is said to be risk-neutral. People who have a certainty equivalent of less than the expected value are risk-averse. Those who have a certainty equivalent greater than the expected value are risk-seeking.

For most people, \$1 000 000 does not have twice the value of \$500 000. Expected utility (EU) theory explains this by asserting that each level of outcome is associated with some level of benefit or utility. The expected utility of an uncertain choice is the sum of the utilities of the outcomes each multiplied by the probability of its occurrence. Some predictions of EU theory are: (i) people are generally risk-averse, (ii) decisions are made based on a final state of wealth, (iii) different representations of the same choice problem will yield the same preference, and (iv) if one option is better than another in one state and at least as good in all others, that option should be taken.

In practice, judgment often deviates from the EU theory. In their original discussion of Prospect Theory, Kahneman and Tversky (1979), presented the following problem that examines prediction (iv) above.

Imagine that you face the following pair of concurrent decisions. First examine both decisions, then indicate the options you prefer.

## Decision I

Choose between:

(a) A sure gain of \$240

(b) A 25% chance to gain \$1000, and a 75% chance to gain nothing

## Decision II

Choose between:

(c) A sure loss of \$750

(d) A 75% chance to lose \$1000, and a 25% chance to lose nothing

Out of 150 subjects, in decision I, 84% chose (a), while 16% chose (b). In decision II, 13% chose (c) and 87% chose (d). Kahneman and Tversky point out that because the two decisions were considered simultaneously, the majority choice constitutes a preference for portfolio (a) and (d) over portfolio (b) and (c). Kahneman and Tversky (1979) proposed Prospect Theory to account for deviations from expected utility theory such as the example above. Prospect theory is ‘unabashedly descriptive’ (Tversky and Kahneman, 1986). Some key features of Prospect theory that offer alternatives to (i)–(iii) for expected utility theory are:

(i $^{p}$ ) The value function for a gamble is s-shaped (Figure 1). The value function is steeper for losses than for gains. These imply that a person is risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses. Furthermore, the pain of a loss is greater than the pleasure derived from a gain of equal value. This has been called the reflectivity principle.

(ii $^{p}$ ) It evaluates gambles relative to deviations from a reference point rather than based on final states of wealth.

(iii $^{p}$ ) A decision concerning a gamble is a two phase process consisting of an editing phase and an evaluation phase. In the editing phase, identical information in competing gambles is edited out. It is during this phase that gambles are edited as gains or losses with respect to a reference point.

The primary challenge to expected utility theory is whether it is adequately descriptive. Prospect theory claims to be a better descriptive theory. Prospect theory makes no claims about how a person should make risky decisions. This study reports data that examine which of the two theories of risk better describes how software project managers make risky decisions.

## Methodology

A questionnaire was designed to examine three predictions from EU theory (i–iii above) and the alternative explanations from PT. The scenarios in the questionnaire were adaptations of questions in the original prospect theory study (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). In their study, the questions were administered to students in introductory Psychology classes. The scenarios in the current study provided a more realistic context for risky decision making, namely software project management. The purpose of the questionnaire was to determine whether software project managers' risky decisions are consistent with EU theory or Prospect theory. The specific research questions addressed in this study are:

(1) Are software project managers generally risk-averse as predicted by EU theory or do they exhibit risk aversion for gains and risk-seeking for losses as predicted by PT?

(2) Are SPM's decisions based on a final state of wealth as predicted by EU theory or do they make judgments of deviations relative to a reference point as predicted by PT?

(3) If SPMs are given different representations of the same choice problem will they make the same preferences as predicted by EU theory?

The questionnaire was administered to 68 volunteer project managers each of whom was employed by one of four computer services companies doing business in southeast Michigan. Each of these companies developed software for profit. This enabled the respondents to relate to the concept of losing money on a project.

The questionnaire was made up of four pairs of questions. Each subject answered all eight questions. The order of the questions was systematically varied to eliminate any biases due to the ordering of the questions.

## Results

## The reflectivity principle

One pair of questions shown in Table 1, examined risky judgments for gains and losses for a software project. It aimed to find out whether SPMs were risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses as predicted by Prospect Theory.

The results were: A and C was chosen by 19 (28%), A and D was chosen by 26 (38%), B and C was chosen by 5 (7%) and B and D was chosen by 18 (27%). The least common choice, B and C, maximizes expected value for both decisions. Those who chose A and C were risk-averse for both gains and losses. Those who chose B and D were risk-seeking for both gains and losses. Prospect theory predicts that A and D will be chosen by most people. This choice indicates risk aversion for gains and risk-seeking to avoid loss. While each of the three other choices can be explained by EU theory, the choice of A and D contradicts it. This is because while Expected Utility can account for a person's risk preference (risk-averse, risk neutral, or risk-seeking), it asserts that a person must be consistent with respect to risk preference. The s-shaped value curve of Prospect theory accounts for the shift from risk aversion for positive outcomes to risk aversion for negative outcomes.

A related phenomenon, the effect of framing (gain versus loss) has been observed in other contexts (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981). The following problem from Bazerman (1983) resulted in a shift from risk aversion for positively framed problems and risk-seeking for negatively framed problems.

A large car manufacturer has recently been hit with a number of economic difficulties, and it appears as if three plants need to be closed and 6 000 employees laid off. The vice president of production has been exploring alternative ways to avoid this crisis. She has developed two plans:

## Positive Frame

Plan A: This plan will save one of three plants and 2 000 jobs.

Plan B: This plan has a 1/3 probability of saving all three plants and all 6 000 jobs, but has a 2/3 probability of saving no plants and no jobs.

Which plan would you select?

## Negative Frame

Plan C: This plan will result in the loss of two of the three plants and 4 000 jobs.

Plan D: This plan has a 2/3 probability of resulting in the loss of all three plants and all 6 000 jobs, but has a 1/3 probability of losing no plants and no jobs.

Which plan would you select?

Although the two problems are mathematically identical, for the negatively framed problem, the majority of subjects chose Plan A (risk-averse) and for the positively framed problems, the majority chose Plan D (risk-seeking).

## The editing effect

The last pair of questions shown in Table 3, examined whether SPMs have the same preferences for different representations of the same problem. These questions test the editing effect of Prospect Theory, namely that identical information for two risky choices is edited out. The results for this pair of questions were: A and C was chosen by 14 (20%), A and D was chosen by 6 (9%), B and C was chosen by 27 (40%), and B and D was chosen by 21 (31%).

Problems 4a and 4b are identical when we consider the 25% chance in problem 4b that the proposal will be accepted. Option C is the probability that the proposal will be accepted and team no.1 is selected, a 25% chance that the project will show a \$30 000 profit. Option D is similar: (0.25)(0.8) chance of a \$40 000 profit which equals a 20% chance of a \$40 000 profit and an 80% chance [75% + (25%)(20%)] to break even.

Those who chose option A and C and option B and D were consistent in their choices for problem 4. Prospect Theory predicts that people will choose option B and C using the rationale for problem 4a that (1) people will reason that 20% and 25% are not significantly different and (2) they will be attracted to the larger potential gain. In problem 4b, they will (1) edit out the information in common to the two options, that there is a 75% chance that the proposal will not be accepted and (2) be risk-averse with respect to the gain of a ‘sure’ \$30 000 versus an ‘80% chance’ of a \$40 000 gain as in problem 1a.

## Comparison to original prospect theory results

The results of this study differ from those reported in the original paper on Prospect Theory. For example, in the original Prospect theory paper (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979), for a problem like problem 1, approximately 80% of the subjects exhibited reflectivity compared to 38% in this study. There are two possible explanations for this. The original study used a between subjects design whereas this study used a within subjects design. Therefore, the original study could only report the number of subjects that were risk-averse for the positive alternative and the number that were risk-seeking for the negative alternative of a pair of questions. They could not determine the actual number of subjects that switched from a risk-averse choice to a risk-seeking choice. As can be seen from this study, there were a sizable number of subjects who were consistently risk-averse or risk-seeking for the two problems. It could be argued that using the within subject design opens the possibility of order effects. However, as noted earlier, the order of the questions was varied to minimize any systematic bias due to these effects. Furthermore, the choice of a within subjects design more closely matches the work of SPMs than does a between subjects design since they repeatedly encounter risky choices as they manage successive projects.

The second difference in the two studies has to do with the subject populations and the context of the problems in the questionnaire. The original study used college sophomores in an introductory psychology class for subjects. The contexts of the risky decisions were purely hypothetical. This study used practicing SPMs making judgments that resembled those that they encounter professionally. This difference in the subjects and the context may have further contributed to the differences in the results. Although this study has greater external validity than the original Prospect Theory studied, it still presented SPMs with an artificial situation. Further research should examine SPMs risky decision making in more realistic situations that capture the uncertainty of resource availability and personnel performance.

## Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of understanding the SPM's stance toward decision-making under risk. Previous research on software project risk has concentrated on risk factors of the software project itself without examining the risky judgments of the SPM. If SPMs behave normatively, as predicted by expected utility theory, fundamental and widespread improvement in software project management could be found by identifying project risk factors and then managing them without reference to the particular SPM. This approach assumes that SPMs will behave homogeneously with respect to risk. However, the results of this study contradict such an assumption.

The results of this study generally favour Prospect Theory over Expected Utility Theory.

(1) SPMs are not generally risk-averse. Most often, they are risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses.

(2) SPMs' decisions are not based on a final state of wealth. They are based on deviations (gains or losses) with respect to a reference point.

(3) SPMs may have different preferences for different representations of the same choice problem.

There are good reasons why the same person may go to great lengths to avoid a loss but be very conservative with regard to gains. The long term effects of losses may be greater than a strictly monetary measure. For example, the SPM who manages a losing project may suffer a long-term loss of credibility that affects future projects and promotions. For gains, a person may find it easy to take credit for gains even if the gain was achieved by failing to take a very reason-able risk.

In the context used in this study, adding people to late projects, the time and effort already invested in the project can be viewed as ‘sunk costs’ that are not recoverable. From a normative standpoint, these costs should not enter into any future decision such as the decision to add people to the project team. However, psychologically, there is a tendency to increase, or at least continue, commitment to a previously selected course of action. The decision to continue with the previous course of action may be made to vindicate previous time and money spent on the project.

Perhaps the most troubling aspect of this study is that the same SPM may have different preferences for different representations of the same problem. This may occur as a result of the editing step in Prospect Theory or the differences between framing the problem negatively or positively. In either case, it suggests that the SPM's risky choice may be susceptible to subtle changes in the project or how aspects of the project are represented.

Newman and Sabherwal (1996) have offered some suggestions for reducing the tendency toward using 'sunk costs' as a justification for escalating projects. They included separating responsibilities for project approval and subsequent project evaluation and reducing penalties for failure. These are useful suggestions. Separation of responsibilities has the effect of removing the emotional attachment the SPM may have for the project from its evaluation. The second suggestions, reducing penalties for failure, has implications for both the problem of escalation and for the tendency of SPMs to be risk averse for positive outcomes. Management may want the SPM to take a reasonable risk where positive outcomes are likely. Explicit policies that reduce penalties for failure are likely to do this.

One additional suggestion for influencing the decision making process of SPMs is through education aimed at elucidating decision making under risk and uncertainty. Such education could include prospect theory results, the escalation phenomenon, and a host of other observations that fall under the appellation 'heuristics and biases'. If the risk preference of the individual SPM is an important factor in determining the outcome for a particular software project, educa-

## End note

It is interesting to note that problem 1b is essentially Brooks' ‘Mythical Man Month’ problem. If SPMs really believed that adding personnel to late projects only makes them later, they would tend to be more risk-averse for problems like 1b than the general population. The fact that a sizable number of the SPMs in this study chose A and D may indicate that the desire to avoid loss reflects more than the money involved.

## Biographical Notes

Thomas W. Lauer is currently the Chair of the Decision and Information Sciences Department and Associate Professor of Management Information Systems at Oakland University where he has been a faculty member since 1985. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from Indiana University.

Lauer has conducted research on a variety of subjects including: the use of questions in the acquisition of information by systems analysts, auditors, and during problem solving; software project managers perception of risk; graphical perception; and decision support systems.
