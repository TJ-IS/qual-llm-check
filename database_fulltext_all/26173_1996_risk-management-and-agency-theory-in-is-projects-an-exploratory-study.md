Journal of Information Technology (1996) 11, 297–307

# Risk management and agency theory in IS projects – an exploratory study

ROBERT PHELPS

Surrey European Management School, University of Surrey, Guildford, GU2 5XH, UK

The way in which project teams respond to problems and manage risks during IT project developments is affected by the organizational context. In particular, it is affected by the implicit ‘contract’ between the team and the organization. This contract is controlled through the adherence to formal methodologies and the use of organizational control system. This paper looks at risk management from the point of view of team behaviour, focusing on behavioural response to risks in terms of effort and risk aversion. Three case studies of project team behaviour are used to illustrate the types of risk management behaviour which may arise under different contracts between project team and organization.

## Introduction

While IS project failures may spring from poor project management practice, or from the inherent difficulty of the project itself, a stream of research (e.g., Beath and Ives, 1989; Kearney, 1990; Scott Morton, 1991; Willcocks and Margetts, 1993) indicates that many reasons for failure stem from the organizational environments of IT systems and the interaction between development processes and the larger organizational system rather than from the development process itself. As noted by Willcocks and Griffiths (1994) risk needs to interpreted operationally as arising from human and organizational patterns of belief and action, leading to the need to develop more process oriented frameworks for risk analysis and management in major IT projects. This paper suggests a framework derived from agency theory in analysing project team risk management behaviour in relation to the project development methodology and control procedures.

Whereas much work has been done on identification of risk factors, essentially for project appraisal purposes, much less attention has been paid to the process of managing risks as they arise during a project. Yet this ‘firefighting’ aspect of project management can be critical. There is a scale of possible risk management responses to problems which arise during development. One extreme is simply to accept that each new problem requires additional time and resource and to add this into a revised project schedule. This is a low effort approach with no risk taking. The other end of the scale is to creatively find ways around problems, possibly completely restructuring the project in the process or increasing workloads in order to minimize the impact of the problem on costs and deadlines. This approach requires considerable effort and involves the acceptance of some additional risk. In general we would expect a more creative project manager to consider more options and arrive at a better (or at least more rational) response to the problem.

The way in which project teams respond to problems and manage risks in real-time is affected by the organizational context. In particular, it is affected by the ‘contract’ between the team and the organization. This contract consists of the expectations about the performance of the project team, both formal as in the case of deadlines and budgets, and informal as in their ability to satisfy clients and to deal with problems of development.

In this paper we look at risk management from the point of view of team behaviour, focusing on behavioural response to risks in terms of effort, opportunism and risk aversion. Three case studies of project team behaviour are used to illustrate the types of behaviour which may arise under different contracts between project team and organization.

## Risk factors and choice of methodology

There have been numerous studies which aim to explain the factors that predict levels of risk in IT developments. Reviews of these are given in Kwon and Zmud (1987), Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987), Bessant (1991). Commonly occurring factors in this literature include size of project; complexity; experience with the technology; project structure; certainty of the requirements (e.g., Parker et al., (1988), Corder (1989), Cash et al. (1992).

It is evident that a number of different viewpoints are possible and this variety may have contributed to the observed fact (Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994) that few companies adopt formal risk analysis (less than 30% in the OTR study (1992) or even investment analysis (less than 40% able to estimate payback periods and only 20% return on investment in the Saarinen and Saaksjarvi study (1989)).

There is more general agreement in the literature, when it comes to using these factors as a basis for the choice of development methodology. Saarinen and Vepsalainen (1993) review four contingency models for choosing IS development strategies: Davis (1982), McFarlan and McKenny (1983), Burns and Dennis (1985) and Mathiassen and Stage (1990). These models exhibit many of the risk factors indicated in the more general risk frameworks reviewed above, including size, structure of project, familiarity with technology, definitional uncertainty and complexity. They indicate that this stream of literature is converging to an agreement on two basic factors which determine choice of an appropriate methodology: complexity and uncertainty. Complexity refers to factors such as project size, the number of functions required, the number of different users and interfaces, the volume of information involved, the amount of processing and the degree of integration with other systems. Uncertainty refers to some degree of ignorance about what the system should do, about user expectations and about the technology to be used.

There is a wide range of methodologies to choose from and unfortunately descriptions and precise meanings of terms used in classifying them differ from author to author. Using a broad brush the basic methodological types can be categorized as:

(1) Structured decomposition: the ‘waterfall’ style of breaking the project down into a series of steps which can each be managed separately. User needs are captured at the outset.

(2) Prototyping: a less structured, more experimental approach in which a ‘quick and dirty’ development is shown to users and their feedback then drives the development of successively improved versions. Users are involved throughout. Spiral (Boehm, 1988) is another term used to describe this approach.

(3) Mixed: where a prototype is used to fix the design requirements which are then developed using structured decomposition, or where prototyping is used to develop certain system components within a structured decomposition project.

Notwithstanding this, it can be argued that for real projects there will always be sufficient uncertainty and complexity for some degree of mixed methodology to be optimal (Mathiassen and Stage, 1990).

Empirical research regarding the validity of these methodological choice guidelines has produced mixed results (Saarinen, 1990). The likely reason for this is that project success is not determined only by appropriate choice of methodology but also by other aspects of the development process and in particular by the way in which the methodology is implemented in behavioural and risk management terms by the project team and the organizations.

Another methodological approach is available to management, which is to contract out the development work to a software house. With the growth of outsourcing and facilities management this has become increasingly popular. In this approach, the specification of the system or the user requirements will be carried out by the organization as the basis for contract bids (or carried out by the contractor but reviewed and agreed by the organization). The design and development work, on the other hand, is done by the contractor. The organization's control processes do not therefore apply to the development work and the organization must rely upon the contractual terms, usually stipulating reviews, milestones, project reports and deliverables but where its role is limited to progress review rather than control action. In contrast to the theory reviewed earlier regarding risks for in-house developments, the literature has little to say specifically on this approach. Adopting the earlier analysis using complexity and uncertainty as the two risk factors, contracting out is only recommendable in conditions of low uncertainty. If uncertainty is high, then no accurate specification will be producable to act as the basis of a contract. Unless the contractor can be perfectly trusted to do his best with an incomplete specification, or unless the contractor is willing for his delivered system to be judged acceptable or not on the whim of the purchaser, there is no basis for a contractual relationship.

## Risk management and agency theory

Adding this form of project management to the two basic methodological types identified previously, a list of representative current project management approaches for software development is structured decomposition, prototyping and contracting out. Other approaches, such as mixed methods or bringing in consultants to manage the project, can be considered as points in the space created by these three dimensions, sharing attributes of these three dimensions in varying degree. Each approach has its own risk profile stemming from the methodology (or mix of methodologies) used. The three dimensional approaches singled out here have been selected for this study in order to examine the representative differences they present in risk management behaviour.

## Dynamic risk behaviour

The basic concept underlying the analysis presented in this paper is the dynamic interaction between methodology and project team risk management behaviour. The appraisal of risk from a basis of contingent factors and the selection of an appropriate methodology can be considered the static aspects of dealing with risk. Decisions are taken once, upfront, and are then adhered to throughout the project. But whatever methodology and risk reduction procedures have been put in place, the project manager must deal with the problems (realized or unforeseen risks) which occur during the development. Tasks take longer than planned; staff go sick; changes to the project are imposed. The role of project management is then to minimize disruption by managing these emergent problems. This is the dynamic aspect of dealing with risk, risk management rather than risk appraisal. However good planning is, within a complex and uncertain environment any plan must be adapted to the changes that impact upon it.

Project teams know from experience that some unforeseen risks are likely to occur, and they make allowance for this in their planning of project tasks. Since the nature of unforeseen risks is by definition unknown, the allowance made for them is a reflection of experience and risk aversion on the part of the team and of the project control structure which must agree the plans. Highly risk averse teams will build large contingencies into their plans to allow for unforeseen risks. This complicates the concept of project success. In terms of the project team, a project estimated to take time 2T and completed in 1.9T is a success. But the same project estimated to take 1T and completed in 1.5T is a failure. Yet for the organization, the second of these scenarios is, in absolute terms, better than the first.

To take account of this problem, the notions of risk aversion and efficiency (in particular the effort applied by the project team) should feature in project evaluations. Risk aversion encourages overlong/overresourced project plans while aversion to effort will similarly give rise to risk management behaviour which increases resource consumption or reduction of functionality in response to problems occurring. Neither of these behaviours are desirable from the point of view of the firm.

The contingent risks which need to be managed are reflected in the choice of methodology. But the choice of methodology may itself influence the behaviour of the project team in the way in which they plan for and deal with the dynamic risk. In particular, it may affect the way in which tasks are planned to allow for some level of unforeseeable risk and it may affect the level of efficiency which the team exhibits. We therefore need to move from a concentration on contingent risk factors to a broader view of development project risk which encompasses the dynamics of risk management. Project success will, in this view, be determined not only by the appropriate choice of methodology for the nature of the project but also by the dynamic interaction between the methodology and its associated control procedures and the self-organizing behaviour of the team with its own goals to aim for. That is, it may affect both the risk aversion behaviour and the effort behaviour of the team. The control of risk averse and effort behaviours in employer-employee relationships is the subject of agency theory. It is this relationship between on the one hand the organizational aspects of methodology and project control and on the other hand the project team risk management behaviour as exhibited in risk aversion and efficiency that this paper investigates.

## Framework for the analysis

## Agency theory

This paper takes an agency theory viewpoint to analyse the behaviour of the project team. Agency theory (Jensen and Meckling, 1976) considers the delegation of work from one person (the principal) to another (the agent) who carries it out. It is concerned with obtaining optimal effort and risk taking behaviour from the agent. The basic issue is the design of an optimal contract (from the point of view of the principal) and this revolves around whether the contract should be based upon evaluation of the agent's behaviour or upon evaluation of the outcome of her work. It can be seen that the basic model of agency theory is very general: in any organization work is delegated from some staff to others. The nature of the contract between principal and agent is implicit in employment situations. There, an employee effectively contracts her entire effort to the firm and accepts to follow occasional instructions as to how that effort should be directed. In this case the question of an optimal contract becomes a question of optimal organizational control, but the underlying options of evaluating performance by behaviour or by outcomes remain the same.

Agency theory is based on two key assumptions about agents: effort aversion and risk aversion. Agents are held to have their own goals which are not necessarily the same as those of the principal. For example taking long lunch breaks. This tendency to pursue their own goals at the expense of those of the principal results in the principal receiving less effort from the agent than he would like. This is effort aversion. Agents are also held to be more risk averse than principals. Economically, this is justified since the principal can diversify his risk by holding a portfolio of tasks and agents. He can afford to take a set of risks in the expectation that the ones that pay off outweigh those that do not. The agent, contracted to one task, cannot afford to take this long run view but needs to ensure that her particular task succeeds. Therefore the agent will be willing to take less risk. On a more intuitive basis it is usually believed that employees seek job security and would not want to accept risks that could put them out of a job, while entrepreneurs are more inclined to take gambles.

The implications of these assumptions for contracts, or equivalently control systems in organizations, is as follows (Levinthal, 1988).

If the agent's behaviour is programmable, (i.e., the steps necessary to achieve the task can be precisely laid out) and observable, then behaviour based control is optimal. Control on the basis of measured behaviour ensures that the agent is committing her full effort to the assigned tasks. In addition, the agent does not have to take any risk since she is ‘just following orders’ and so no conflict between the risk taking preferences of agent and principal occurs.

If the agent's behaviour is not programmable or if it is not observable, then behaviour based control is not feasible. Instead outcome based control must be used. Control on the basis of task outcomes does not ensure that the agent is applying the optimal amount of effort to the task and it also means that the agent is being evaluated on the basis of outcomes which are not completely within her control. For example, if outcome control is applied to a salesperson and sales fall, she is likely to be punished. But the fall in sales may be a result of economic conditions and bear no relation to the effort put in by the salesperson. So outcome based control means that the agent bears some of the risk of the firm: her rewards are tied to those of the firm. In this case a conflict exists between the risk preferences of the principal and those of the agent. The agent will avoid taking risky decisions which the principal (if he were controlling her behaviour) would have taken.

If the expected outcome of the task is not clearly known, then neither behavioural nor outcome based control is feasible. This is the case in many higher level tasks. For example R&D, where it is usually impossible to know when if ever a new drug will be discovered, or exactly what it will do. In particular there are elements of this scenario in IS development under high levels of uncertainty, for example where user requirements are not well understood or an untried technology is involved. Agency theory does not have a direct answer to the question of what optimal contractual form of control applies in this case, as there is nothing to base a contract on. However, with the basic issues of effort and risk aversion in mind, Ouchi (1979) introduced an additional control type: social control. Social control relies on putting in place people or a culture whose goals are convergent with those of the firm. Since neither behaviour nor outcomes can be controlled, the concept of control on the basis of measured feedback is replaced by one of control as shared values and trust. If such a culture can be introduced, then the shared values of the agent and the principal mean that effort and risk aversion do not arise and that the principal is able to trust the agent to carry out the task without formal control measures of performance.

Applying the concepts of agency theory to software development projects, the two roles of principal and agent can be identified as the sponsor (or client) of the system and the project development team. The sponsor is often represented by a project steering committee and the project team by the project manager. In any case, the goals of the two sides are different. The sponsor wishes the project to be completed to a high quality standard at the lowest time and cost possible. The project team, on the other hand, wants to meet agreed targets but with the least strain possible on the team.

Where the sponsor will push for greater effort to solve problems and keep to budget, the team may press for an extension to the schedule or reduced functionality of the system. An information asymmetry exists between the team, with its detailed technical knowledge, and the sponsor. The risk aversion of the team is likely to be expressed by building safety margins into the task estimates and proposed by the sponsor who wants the finished product yesterday. It is this mix of different goals between the two parties which means that project control is necessary.

The different project methodologies which may be chosen imply different types of project control. These control types together with the effort and risk aversion characteristics of the project team affect the behaviour of the team and hence its approach to risk management. We now consider the three methodology choices in terms of the type of control they impose and their agency relations between the team and the organization.

## Structured decomposition

The ‘divide and rule’ approach is used in this methodology. If the project can be broken up into a sufficient number of modules and steps, and each part planned in sufficient detail, then if everything goes to plan there is little room for errors to creep in since little decision making is called for during execution of the plan. This methodology can therefore be seen as an attempt to impose behavioural control on the project. Ideally, each team member simply carries out a pre-programmed task. Effort can be directly monitored.

The effect of this control on risk management is apparent in two stages:

(1) At the project planning (and appraisal) stage, knowledge that the team will be judged on behaviour, i.e., the ability to stick to plan estimates, together with risk aversion and information asymmetry in favour of the team, is expected to lead to ‘safe’ time and resource estimates for the project tasks. That is, the task will be planned with generous safety margins.

(2) At the development stage, effort aversion and behavioural control (where the risks involved are not born by the team) mean that unforeseen problems are expected to be treated by the team as reasons to renegotiate expected behaviour, by adding tasks or resources to the project or by extending deadlines.

## Contracting out

This development methodology gives rise to outcome control. This is the principal agent relationship at its clearest, with a written contract binding the parties. As the development will be taking place under the control of the contractor, behaviour cannot be observed by the firm. At most it can negotiate a sequence of reviews and intermediate deliverables or milestones. The contract will specify the agreed outcomes in terms of functionality, time and cost.

At the project planning stage the risk aversion of the team is balanced by the need to win the contract. Therefore excessive safety margins in the proposal, which would lead to excessive bid costs, are less likely. Further, there is an information asymmetry against the team since the firm may already have investigated the proposed development and be aware of hidden problems. The remaining option to reduce risk for the team is to try to keep the project specification as open as possible by avoiding commitment to details whenever possible.

At the development stage the information asymmetry is reversed in favour of the team as they plan details. Effort aversion can then operate to take advantage of any unspecified details within the development, interpreting loosely drawn requirements in the way which draws the least effort (and risk) from the team. Unforeseen problems are then likely to be dealt with in one of two ways: either by compensating with reduced functionality or by renegotiating the contract on the grounds that the problem constitutes an addition to the task (loosely) contracted.

## Prototyping

In this methodology neither the behaviour nor the outcome can be precisely specified, although a rough idea of both is available. This means that control is exerted through socialization and trust rather than through formal measurement. Although milestones and reviews will typically be adhered to as in more structured projects, there are no precise targets to meet at any of these points; the aim of the methodology is essentially to produce client satisfaction rather than to meet objective targets. At the planning stage risk aversion indicates two possible tactics: one is to add generous contingencies, the other is to keep the definition and scheduling of individual tasks as imprecise as possible.

At the development stage risk aversion implies taking advantage of the imprecision in task schedules to reassign resources from task to task depending upon the difficulties encountered. Effort aversion implies negotiating system details with the client in order to minimize development effort.

## The research approach

An exploratory investigation was carried out to determine whether the behavioural effects proposed from the agency theoretic analysis could be detected in practice, and to further explore the realities of methodology-control-project team interaction and behaviour in an organizational setting. This study comprised a sample of one project from each of the three methodological types. Because of the small sample size no generalizable empirical results are possible from this exploratory study. The cases should be read as illustration of some of the issues discussed and as lending a richer background against which to discuss the theory. They serve as a first step investigating the utility of viewing risk management from an agency perspective and to indicate some of the possibilities and difficulties which are open to empirical research in this area.

To investigate the three styles of project methodology a longitudinal comparative study was carried out of three different development projects within the same organization. Remaining within the same organization offers the advantage that a large number of contingent variables which could affect the approach to risks and their management remain constant over the three studies. A disadvantage is that the organizational setting may not be representative of many firms and so the generalizability of the findings are limited and would benefit from repeat studies in other organizations in the same or different sectors. However, for an exploratory study with a small number of cases this design has the advantage of allowing the focus to be on comparison between the three project types without excessive worries about the impact of other organizational and environmental variables influencing the observed risk management behaviours.

The research was carried out by interviews with members of the project teams and their clients within the organization. Two members of each project team were interviewed at approximate one month intervals over a one year period. Client representatives were interviewed at approximate three month intervals. These core interviews were supplemented by occasional interviews with other project members, steering group participants, etc.

Given the exploratory nature of the investigation, the interview sessions were not structured to avoid imposing any preconceptions on the data gathering process. Instead, interviewees were encouraged to report what they felt were significant events from their own points of view. Some generic issues were prompted by the investigator including project progress, changes to the planning, problems encountered and actions taken. The intention was to build up a rich picture of project evolution from different viewpoints which could then be analysed from different theoretical angles. Limitations of this approach can be summarized as completeness and subjective interpretation. Limited access to a limited number of participants means that the data collected may not be complete: events occurring between interview points may go unreported and events impacting subsets of the participants may also not be noted. The unstructured nature of the interviews and the multiple (often conflicting) views expressed means that the task of deciding the most important events during project developments and the interpretation of group behaviour necessarily involves a high level of subjectivity.

Alternative approaches could have included action research with continual presence in the project team or a more structured form of interview process. The first of these was excluded on pragmatic grounds – time and cost implications, as well as a concern that the project group viewpoint might dominate the data collected. The second was rejected on grounds that we do not have a sufficiently good understanding of the dynamic risk management process to be able to identify in advance the important features to be explored. Future studies building on this initial exploration may prefer to take a more structured approach.

The organization chosen for the study, which will be referred to as European Financial Services (EFS), is a financial sector organization with an important software development activity. In general it has historically relied upon its in-house development capabilities, when it has not bought complete application packages from vendors. There is not one monolithic software development group, rather there are several groups which have arisen historically from geographically dispersed centres and which have expertise in functionally different application software. For example, there is a telecommunications group which buys communication hardware and software and develops software of its own. There is a data processing

The details regarding how each of the steps should be carried out were then filled in by internal discussions. The original intention had been to impose one set of detailed steps throughout the organization. Although this was achieved for the project proposal, appraisal and implementation phases, it was decided to take a pluralistic approach and several different alternative sets of development steps were approved. These included steps for structured decomposition, contracting out and prototyping developments. This situation with other phases of the lifecycle being standardized across projects made EFS attractive as a setting for comparing the differences in behaviour engendered by different methodologies. Over a period of a year three different projects following three different development methodologies were observed. They were carried out by different groups.

## The case studies

## Data conversion

## Choice of methodology

The first project concerned the development of a system to convert data from one format to another in real time as part of a larger integration development.

It was carried out in-house by the data processing group. As a complete and functionally self-contained module intended to fit into another development, it had no external users. Its functionality and performance specifications had been drawn up by another central information systems group which had responsibility for design of the larger system. The requirements were clear and precise. Also there was no especial complexity involved nor any untried technology. This clarity of requirement together with the experience possessed by this group in using structured methodologies naturally led to the choice of the existing in-house structured decomposition methodology. This choice is congruent with the methodology choice guidelines in the literature: low uncertainty and relatively low complexity indicate a structured decomposition methodology.

## Risk appraisal

The project team appeared not to perceive any specific risks during the appraisal stage. Formally noted risks (as required by the framework for project appraisals) included only generic problems such as staff availability and no changes being made to the larger project of which this formed a part. The absence of perceived risks was partly due to the fact that the project team chose development tools and practices which were familiar to them whenever a choice presented itself. Informally, during the development period, the only risk concern which was observed concerned the accuracy of the task time estimates. Although the project was well specified with essentially zero uncertainty, and although complexity was also at a low level, the team had not worked on specifically similar systems before and so the time estimates were indicative rather than precise. To manage this risk time overestimates had been included in the project plan. The degree of overestimation is difficult to estimate, but other software groups with some knowledge of this project informally expressed opinions of about 100% overestimation. Since the project steering committee set up to control this project had no detailed technical knowledge of the system the planned schedule was not seriously challenged at the appraisal stage.

The overestimate approach to risk management was complemented by a team culture self-described as 'professional'. This appeared to mean an emphasis on reliability, thoroughness and testing with concerns about time and cost taking a back seat. The team culture was to measure success both of the project and of individual team members by technical considerations such as elegance of code design, thoroughness of recovery and backup mechanisms and lack of bugs. The project manager considered the insertion of safety allowances in task schedules as a positive sign of good project management.

## Risk management

Only one serious problem materialized during the observation period. This was a change to the larger system which meant that some of the project's interfaces and functionality needed to be changed in turn. The immediate response of the project manager was to consult with team members in order to ascertain what additional tasks now needed to be planned in and then to prepare a new schedule showing how the required changes would cause the project to overrun. This revised schedule was accepted at the next steering group meeting.

## Risk aversion and effort

In an agency theoretic analysis, two aspects stand out. First, the project planning process favoured the project team with an information asymmetry regarding task details so that risk avoiding time overestimates could be inserted. Second, effort aversion took place when risks materialized and had to be managed during development. No attempt was made to restructure the project in order to minimize the impact of the requested changes on the schedule, despite the tacit acknowledgement that comfortable safety margins existed in the plan. Neither was any creative thinking initiated to find other ways around the problem. Task safety margins were protected and fully used: in no case was a task completed significantly before its planned date. In this project the use of a structured composition methodology coupled with a behavioural control style interacted with the existing culture to produce, or at least to allow, severe risk aversion and low effort behaviour.

## Multicurrency position management

## Choice of methodology

This project concerned the development of a package to be sold to customers of EFS to assist them in handling complex multicurrency deals. It was a responsibility of the external projects group. The project was considered to be of medium complexity and, since the concept had been developed as a result of lengthy discussions with its customers, to have a low level of uncertainty. In this case three factors led to a decision to contract out the development. First, EFS had experienced some problems in the past in developing similar types of software in-house; it wanted to avoid repeating those problems. Second, the implementation of EFS' new project management framework which detailed the project management steps to be followed gave the external projects group confidence in its ability to manage a contracted out project by removing some of the risk from the group and attaching it to the prescribed framework. Third, the low level of uncertainty made this a good candidate for third party contracting.

## Risk appraisal

Risks formally noted at the appraisal stage were mainly of a technical nature centring on performance requirements and the ability to meet them on the model of workstation which was envisaged as the delivery vehicle for this system. The only non-technical risk noted was the ability of the selected contractor to deliver the specified system on time.

At the planning stage an information asymmetry acted in favour of EFS and against the contractor, since the in-house EFS group had spent time considering the possible system design in detail and were able to suggest, if not impose, their ideas for the basic system structure including database structure, modularity, backup and other aspects. This was seen as a risk limiting action on the part of EFS, as it would leave less chance of the contractor mishandling the project and affecting deadlines. The contractor verbally accepted these design concepts but they did not form part of the contract which limited itself to requirements, times and costs.

## Risk management

In the development stage, risk aversion of the contractor led to an initial attempt to build the system according to the structure verbally agreed. However it became apparent that the data handling side of the system would seriously underperform using the structure agreed. At this point lengthy negotiations took place with EFS initially taking the position that this was a problem for the contractor to solve, while the contractor argued that the problem was due to EFS' misspecification of the design, and that EFS should accept a delay and increase costs. Eventually EFS accepted increased costs and times.

## Risk aversion and effort

The project was completed but significantly behind the original schedule. In this project use of contractors together with outcome control produced a poor level of effort in the contractor. Effort aversion meant that there was no attempt by the contractor to offer a rethink of the project as a whole in order to accommodate this problem within existing budgets and schedules. Risk aversion added to this since had it suggested a new design which then failed, it would have incurred the rectification costs. The contractor

## Transaction monitoring

## Choice of methodology

This project was to develop real-time monitoring for a major internal transaction processing system. The users were to be a group of technical controllers whose responsibility was to keep the transaction processing in an optimal state. It was evident that in order to help the controllers considerable interaction with them during the project would be necessary. The manner in which information was displayed and how quickly it could be assimilated and acted on were believed to be critical for the real-time control function. Filtering and prioritising the mass of information potentially available was also considered crucial. Further, this system was of a kind outside the previous experience of the decision support group who were charged with its development, and it would involve untried (to the group) technology. Finally the system was regarded as highly complex with a need to interface with several very different systems. The high levels of uncertainty (in both requirements and technology) and complexity indicated a prototyping methodology.

## Risk appraisal

In the planning stage a large number of risks were identified, mainly regarding the performance of the new technology, skills available in the team, the need for high levels of co-operation from other departments especially the controllers, and the undefined nature of the requirements. The project appraisal stage of the EFS framework insisted that fixed time and budget figures were given. The number of stages expected in the spiral development of the modules together with time estimates were mapped out and some contingency for unforeseen risks was built into these estimates. But the need to propose a feasible overall plan meant that the sort of gross overestimate practised by the team in the first of these studies was not possible – the far greater uncertainty in this project would have resulted in an unrealistic schedule if all risks had been suitably covered.

Risk aversion at this stage took the form of insisting that all figures concerning module costs and times were provisional with only the totals fixed. Similarly, the expected functionality was described only in approximate terms. This gave the project team the ability to autonomously change any aspect of the plan within the total time and cost constraints.

## Risk management

In the development stage, great reliance was placed on the team's ability to solve problems in a creative manner. Whereas in the other two projects problems had been met with straightforward requests for more time and resources to overcome them, in this project the spiral methodology made the built-in assumption that problems and changes would be met at each cycle and that these should be dealt with in one of two ways. Either by reassigning resources from modules proceeding more quickly than expected to those facing difficulties or by changing the functionality requirements by discussion with the controllers. It was observed that this process of schedule change, resource reallocation, design and functionality change was a constant feature of this project putting a heavy load on the project manager.

Effort aversion would be expected to make a subteam progressing well on a module reluctant to lose resources to other modules. In practice the team culture of the group seemed to prevent such reactions. The group culture was observed to be of a ‘can-do’ nature. The considerable freedom to change the system design encouraged team involvement and ownership of the project.

Risk aversion might be expected to lead to constant pressure to remove functionality from the system, but this appeared to be balanced by the implicit success criterion: the need to satisfy the controllers with a useful product.

## Risk aversion and effort

In this project, the use of a prototyping methodology with a loose, ‘social’ type of control, appeared to lead to the group considering itself in a principal role rather than an agent role. By making a less specific contract regarding the outcome or behaviour of the group, by enabling the group to set its own system objectives and to design and plan the entire project with little top-down control, an implicit trust relationship seems to have been established. The group adopted goals congruent with those of the organization: to develop the best system for the users within the resources given. Having adopted this role high effort was then applied by the group in dynamic risk management to achieve its own (and therefore the firm’s) goals.

## Discussion

Comparing the expectations of the effect of methodology and control choice on team risk management behaviour with the observation of the three cases, some tentative conclusions can be drawn.

For structured decomposition, the expectations of the agency theoretic analysis were borne out by the case study. Closely monitored behavioural control did lead to a low effort, risk averse from of team behaviour shown in the insertion of large contingencies and in an unwillingness to use them to overcome problems which were instead treated by adding to the schedule.

For contracting out, the expected behavioural effects were partially endorsed by the case. Effort aversion by renegotiation of the contract to deal with emerging problems was observed as expected. The expectation of the contractor wishing to keep the contract loose to enable effort and risk aversion by selective interpretation was not observed in the case, where risk aversion was instead expressed as a willingness to let the firm bear the risk of detailed design.

For prototyping the expectations of risk avoiding behaviour were endorsed by the case but the expectations of effort aversion were not. Although the expected risk averse behaviours of loose specifications and schedules and of effort reassignment during development were observed, the expected effort averse behaviours of large contingency factors and negotiation of reduced functionality were not observed. This appears to have been because the project team adopted a role closer to that of principal rather than that of agent. The goals adopted by the team were congruent with those of the organization in which case, according to agency theory, no effort aversion is possible.

## Conclusions

It has been argued that agency theory provides a basis for analysing the effects of IS methodology choice on team risk management behaviour. Exploratory studies have been presented which give credence to the suggestion that the choice of methodology and corresponding project control affects the behaviour of project teams and specifically the way they dynamically manage risks as they materialize during developments. Different types of risk management behaviour can be expected from different project types and this may be an important factor both in choosing a project methodology and in appraising the chance of success. Perhaps the most interesting and surprising observation from these cases is the high level of effort and creativity put into dynamic risk management under the prototyping approach. Further research is needed to determine how general these results might be and to investigate contingent factors which affect them. The investigation of the self-organization of project teams in response to the controls and expectations put upon them is an underexplored but potentially important element to add to our understanding of IS project risk management.
