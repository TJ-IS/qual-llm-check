# Organizational impacts of multimedia information systems

SCHAHRAM DUSTDAR

University of Art at Linz, Linz, Austria

MARIOS C. ANGELIDES London School of Economics, London, UK

Multimedia information systems present a great challenge to an organization which contemplates their introduction. This paper addresses the impacts that multimedia information systems would pose to an organization. The paper investigates the organizational impacts through a proposed taxonomy of multimedia information systems.

## Introduction

Nowadays, we are witnessing a convergence of information technologies and systems into multimedia information systems which increasingly affect how people and organizations work. The use of multimedia information systems in organization is quite new; therefore we will introduce a conceptual framework (taxonomy) of multimedia information systems and then discuss the organizational impacts by introducing case studies for each category. Rather than viewing the organizational impacts of multimedia information systems as being inherently positive or negative (Markus and Robey, 1988), we view multimedia information systems as neutral with regard to their organizational impacts.

The nature of an organization’s IT use ± and therefore the use of multimedia information systems as well ± emerges through complex interactions between the intentions of key actors, the attributes of the technology involved and the dynamic organizational processes (Pfeffer, 1982). We view multimedia information systems as an enabling factor which, by providing qualitatively new capabilities in storing, presenting and processing information, makes certain organizational changes possible. Hence, we believe that for research on multimedia information systems it is essential to investigate the new capabilities which multimedia information systems offer and then to study the relationship to organizational change.

Research on multimedia information systems is an interdisciplinary research area and consists of several research streams such as communication research (Daft and Lengel, 1984), human± computer interactions (Carroll, 1993), information systems research (Markus, 1994), organization research (Fulk et al.,

1990) and computer science (Furht, 1994). Each of the mentioned research streams of multimedia information systems developed their own categorization system or systems. However, a comprehensive and well-established categorization of multimedia information systems ± which cannot be found in the literature ± is essential in order to generalize research ® ndings across systems and research disciplines.

Historically, communication research, which ± in this context ± investigates mainly computer-mediated communications (CMC), was the ® rst discipline to categorize media systems. Paisley and Chen (1982) characterized computer media systems by presentation features, input and command features and content features. Daft and Lengel (1984) developed a taxonomy for assessing the \`information richness’ of media systems. They conceptualized richness as the \`potential information-carrying capacity of data’ . In Daft and Lengel’s (1984) system, face-to-face communication is the highest in its \`information richness’ , and after faceto-face communication, with regard to its information richness, comes telephone communication. Daft and Lengel’s (1984) taxonomy is often used as a classi® cation system in organization research. Rice and Williams (1984) offered a more general list of constructs which may vary across media systems. They differentiated between dimensions such as stimulus-conveying restrictions, channel redundancy, the potential for interactivity, social presence, privacy and familiarity. Heeter (1989) proposed a classi® cation system based on two traditional communication concepts, namely function and channel. Research on multimedia information systems from a computer science point of view mainly concentrates on technical issues (Furht, 1994; Koegel-Buford, 1994; Minoli and Keinanth, 1994).

Regardless of how successful multimedia is proving to be day by day, multimedia information systems still pose a great challenge for the multimedia information systems manager and the information systems department with respect to their introduction in the realm of traditional information systems for organizational use. The purpose of this paper is to address the impact multimedia information systems pose to the organization considering the introduction of such systems. As it is no longer possible to talk about one class of multimedia information systems and therefore a single set of emerging impacts, the paper proposes a taxonomy of such systems and the investigation of organizational impacts examined through this taxonomy. This yields impacts at the personal, group and corporate levels.

## Taxonomy of multimedia information systems

We believe that ± for two reasons ± it is a challenging task and of paramount importance for information systems research to build a comprehensive classi® cation system for research on multimedia information systems. First, research on multimedia information systems needs to integrate research ® ndings of disciplines such as communication research, information systems research, computer science and organization research. Second, the impacts of multimedia information systems depend on the category of the system investigated. Organizational impacts of multimedia information systems cannot be generalized; moreover, they cannot be generalized over different categories of systems, such as the \`personal, group and corporate’ multimedia information systems as presented in this paper. However, research on multimedia information systems’ organization implications needs close and joint research efforts across research disciplines. Since multimedia information systems consist not only of communication systems enhancing human to human communication, a taxonomy, such as that developed by Johansen (1988), which differentiates between two dimensions, synchronous and asynchronous communication and same place/different place, makes sense only to a limited number of multimedia information systems, termed in the literature as \`computer-supported cooperative work’ (CSCW) systems or, as we call it in this paper, \`group multimedia information systems’.

Therefore we propose a taxonomy of multimedia information systems consisting of three categories: \`personal’, \`group’ and \`corporate’ multimedia information systems. Unfortunately the boundaries between these categories are not always very clean and clear. \`Group multimedia information systems’ , such as point-to-point or multipoint conferencing systems, are currently the driving force in the multimedia information systems arena and researchers in the CSCW domain are and will investigate multimedia elements of collaborative systems (Ahuja et al., 1988; Johansen, 1988, Ishii and Miyake, 1991; Rodden, 1993; Ishii et al., 1994; Koegel-Buford, 1994). Table 1 summarizes a proposed categorization of multimedia information systems, provides system examples in each category and relates them to probable organization implications, which are discussed in the next section. A detailed discussion of every system example used in the table and its main characteristics would go beyond the scope of this paper.

Recent developments in multimedia information systems have shown that research is mainly undertaken in \`group multimedia information systems’ (Ahuja, 1988; Johansen, 1988; Watabe et al., 1990; Ishii and Miyake, 1991; Rodden, 1993; Ishii et al., 1994). \`Personal multimedia information systems’ such as kiosk systems are being widely tested in retailing and services organizations such as Argos, Woolworths, Ladbrokes and Sears, to name a few (IMRG, 1994). Future multimedia technologies, such as interactive television in which the television is turned into a two-way communication device by adding a \`set-top-box’ , will build a possible system by which organizations can focus more on external information systems in their development efforts. The organization can build virtual direct links to customers and can be contacted by (potential) customers via a \`TV’ , which will, in fact, be a computer.

\`Corporate multimedia information systems’ can be differentiated from \`group multimedia information systems’ in the sense that \`corporate multimedia information systems’ build a core structure and backbone of multimedia information systems within an organization, whereas \`group multimedia information systems’ enhance human to human communications. Applications such a corporate-wide multimedia training to the desktop computer (sometimes referred to as just-in-time training), broadcast of, for example, television news programmes or ® nancial information on stock markets on corporate-wide LANs and WANs to the desktop computer and the integration of computer and telephony build the backbone of corporate multimedia information systems and related services. Currently, many commercial organizations are in the phase of exploring the World Wide Web (WWW) as a delivery channel for multimedia information and services and are offering multimedia services on the Internet. A recent case study by Ives and Jarvenpaa (1994) showed that electronic commerce via multimedia information systems on the Internet is becoming a reality.

In the following section, we address those issues that stem from each of the three categories through three case studies: one for personal, one for group and one for corporate multimedia information systems.

## Organizational impacts of multimedia information systems from each category

If we take a historical look at older \`information and communication technologies’ we ® nd that Chandler (1977) argued that the ability of the telegraph to facilitate coordination enabled the emergence of the large, centralized railroad ® rms that became the prototype of the modern industrial organization. The telephone, Pool (1983) argued, made possible the physical separation of management headquarters from ® eld operations. The telephone is also believed to be responsible for the demise of the formerly substantial occupational category of of® ce boy or messenger (Pool, 1983). Yates (1982) and Markus and Yates (1982) argued that the invention of the vertical ® ling cabinet in the late 1800s led to the development of the now institutionalized interof® ce memorandum as well as to the creation of new administrative structures such as centralized ® ling departments. The questions we are interested in are: What role do multimedia information systems play in organizational change processes; and, Do multimedia information systems enable organization changes and do they differ qualitatively from past information systems? These questions are issues for information systems research and organization theory. Lewin and Stephens (1993, p. 404) saw the integration of \`telecommunications, of® ce automation, data processing and video technologies’ as key attributes of \`effective postindustrial organisations’. The results of integration processes in information technologies and systems lead to qualitatively different systems.

Huber and Glick (1993) argued that organizations face two important aspects of change: changes in organizational environments and changes in organizational processes. Thus, they concluded that the level of environmental complexity, the level of turbulence and their absolute growth rate will be signi® cantly greater in the future than in the past. Therefore, decisions will be made more frequently and more rapidly. The increased complexity of organizational environments demands that information acquisition be more wide-ranging. This requires that organizational information distribution is more directed.

## A case study on personal multimedia systems

WORLDRIDE (Angelides and Demosthenous, 1996) is an experimental kiosk system prototype developed at the London School of Economics which helps its user to explore countries and learn about their culture and civilization for the purpose of visitation in these countries as a tourist. Although WORLDRIDE was initially developed for a government department for the purpose of brie® ng both its of® cials and the interested general public about areas of unrest throughout the world, it was then expanded to include all countries of the world.

WORLDRIDE includes all geographical information stored as multimedia objects, i.e. still and full-motion video, audio, animation text and graphics. All objects are linked to those that either they are related to or \`I like these’ . Users may browse through a series of related or \`I like these’ multimedia objects, particularly still and full-motion video images. Just as images bring text to life, audio adds depth. The user may choose to listen to music or \`hear’ text rather than read it or hear people talking in a video clip of their own choice. The use of audio is far more comprehensive and uses speech for conveying information as an alternative to text. A multimedia user interface is used to support the presentation of the various multimedia objects and also to provide the user with a high quality interface and thereby the opportunity of a fascinating interaction.

To the end-user, WORLDRIDE is a collection of interactive electronic books which not only bring images and conversations to life but also allow various pathways through their information \`landscapes’. It uses audio, video, text, animation and graphics to dispatch information about a number of different countries. WORLDRIDE is an encyclopaedia with many pages of written or spoken text, graphs, ¯ ags, world and country maps and video of the history, agriculture, traditional music, industry, main economical resources, fauna, cities and other areas of interest of most countries.

WORLDRIDE has proved to be an ef® cient means of obtaining information for various countries, an acceptable compensation for \`gaps’ arising with traditional information dissemination methods, an introduction to new technology and to the latest advanced methods of information dissemination, and an interesting experience. WORLDRIDE provides a coherent overview by presenting information interactively from various viewpoints (textual, visual, graphical, etc.) by extending current experiences and encouraging different user perspectives. WORLDRIDE allows for the development and exploitation of user understanding of world information and its relation to past experience.

Inevitably, WORLDRIDE brought a lot of changes both internal and external. Long gone is the middleman who was responsible for updating the textual information from various sources. In his place, there is now a competent technologist who is responsible for retrieving information in every possible media, i.e. video, audio, etc. and keeping the system up to date. User expectations have also changed. The user, drawing on their television experience and the dissemination of ® rst class information through television while it happens, which \`is not correct at the time of going to press’ , expects rich information which is not only \`stale’ but adjusted to each individual user. These user expectations put tremendous pressure both on the operator and the system. In addition, such a kiosk system changes information technology from being computation intensive to being communication intensive. WORLDRIDE users who enjoy the material, achieve shorter learning times and much improved retention rates. The reasons for these are largely psychological: people learn better by \`seeing’, \`hearing’ and \`doing’.

## A case study on group multimedia systems on the Internet/MBone

The following section will summarize the experiences we made in some desktop multimedia conferences on the Internet. We draw on our experiences from multiple desktop multimedia conferences on the Internet and in particular we focus on a case study on urban planning using Unix-based desktop multimedia conferencing tools on the Internet. The desktop multimedia conference used the MBone virtual network (Casner, 1994) on top of the Internet and was transmitted on the Austrian part of the Internet, ACOnet. Two active conference sides were involved in this event, which was announced in the MBone-AT mailing list: the Technical University at Vienna and the University of Art at Linz. The peculiarity of the urban planning conference was that in Linz and in Vienna a group of students joined the multimedia conference using their own desktop multimedia workstations in student conference rooms located in Linz and Vienna.

The Institute of Urban Planning at the Technical University in Vienna had prepared digital material on urban planning and wanted to present the plans and digital models to the municipal authority of Linz. These plans and models involved some buildings and streets of Linz. The purpose of the conference was to present the work done at the Institute of Urban Planning and to initiate a decision-making process about the implementation of the presented models in the real world.

After the announcement of the conference, training of the conference participants and preparation of the desktop multimedia conferencing facilities was necessary. Speci® cally the conference participants had to know how to use the multimedia tools, i.e. how to mute the microphone in the audio tool, how to resize windows in one of the video tools or how to load

3-D computer-generated city models into the shared whiteboard. In addition, the surroundings of each participant had to be adjusted, i.e. to avoid background noise, locate microphones, speakers and cameras close to the human interactor and provide natural lighting conditions. Finally, one important aspect was to remind the conference participants to take care on discipline, i.e. not to speak concurrently since audio packets might get lost due to the limited network bandwidth.

During the conference there were several problems. The ® rst problem was that the active conference participants had no experience regarding desktop multimedia conferencing. Hence, the system administrators had to handle the switching between the software used. After 1 hour the Austrian part of the Internet, ACOnet, went down and, hence, no transmission could take place. The time ± 5 minutes ± which was needed to ® x the problem in Vienna, allowed the conference participants in Linz to discuss the issues raised during the conference. Since the conference participants had no time to discuss the issues amongst themselves during the conference, they seemed to be quite happy to have the opportunity to talk without being \`broadcast’ all over Austria!

After the conference we interviewed the conference participants. They liked the idea of multipoint desktop multimedia conferencing on the Internet/MBone because the municipal authority frequently needed to discuss urban planning issues with researchers in Vienna. In the past they had had to travel to Vienna and these journeys slowed down the decision-making process. The second positive point which the conference participants mentioned was the fact that it helped the municipal authority to save travel expenses. Johansen (1988) questioned the assumption that conferencing would reduce travel. We found that it was the subjective feeling of conference participants that they saved a lot of time and hence travel costs and they did not necessarily calculate the real costs and compare them. They did not regard this desktop multimedia conference as a substitute for face-to-face meetings. The participants wanted to use the system for their convenience to be able to hold meetings more often. Apart from the 5 minutes breakdown of the network in Austria there were no technical problems. Since the municipal authority were using conferencing technology for the ® rst time, we asked them how they experienced the audio and video quality. The system administrators decided to limit the video bandwidth to some two frames per second and keep the rest for audio and shared whiteboarding, which they thought would be more important than full motion 25 frames per second \`talking heads’. The reason the system administrators limited the bandwidth for video was that they wanted to keep bandwidth for shared data such as city plans and 3-D computer-generated models. Some tests before the conference had shown that if the transmission of the conference was with top video quality, a lot of data packets would get lost. This was a point of critique by the municipal authority. In spite of the fact that there was no audio packet loss, the conference participants criticized the jitter in the video transmission.

To summarize this case study, it is very important to train the prospective users of desktop multimedia conferencing systems regarding the usage of software and to give usage guidelines for the conferencing tools. In some situations it was not obvious to the participants that if two participants talked at the same time some audio packets would get lost. The second proposition is that prospective users of desktop multimedia conferencing systems should have a clear idea of what quality they can expect. In particular, we witnessed a high degree in user expectation regarding the video quality. The video quality needs to have a minimum frame refresh rate of 15 frames per second to give the impression of real full-motion video. This refresh rate can only be achieved in high bandwidth networks.

The conference participants needed some \`private time’ during the decision-making processes to discuss issues amongst themselves, without the feeling of being \`broadcast’. Hence, we propose the implementation of the functionality to direct video and audio streams to certain users within a decision-making group and to implement features in software which show the remote participant that the local site has switched to mute and therefore is not able to receive audio or video. This would help users to have a feeling of privacy and enhance security. Regarding security and privacy one major problem on the MBone is that it is only possible to con® gure multimedia conferences reaching the site of the organization, a region or the world but it is not possible to con® gure a multimedia conference as a closed conference. Everyone within the scope of the ttl-value (time-to-live) is able to receive and send packets. There is no anonymity, although conference participants can see through the e-mail address who joins a conference. This restricts the use of multimedia conferencing on the MBone. Interested organizations who have full Internet access can use the MBone for their internal multimedia conferences between distributed sites but they will not be private. Research on desktop multimedia conferencing and its application in decision-making processes is interlinked with other information processing, communication and coordination activities. Desktop multimedia conferencing is not a substitute for face-to-face meetings, but enables and ± as we saw in the case study ± forces people to change their decision-making processes and communication patterns. Daft and Lengel’s (1984) theory of information richness states that face-to-face meetings have the highest degree of media richness. We argue that desktop multimedia conferencing enables decisionmaking processes and communication processes in a new dimension. The question is not whether desktop multimedia conferencing is about to substitute face-to-face meetings, but which \`new qualities’ or \`opportunity enhancements’ it can offer. This case study has shown some evidence that desktop multipoint multimedia conferencing allows decision-making processes that are not possible in face-to-face meetings to take place.

Further research needs to be undertaken regarding the design and implementation issues of desktop multimedia conferencing systems in decision-making processes. Desktop multimedia conferencing systems need better integration to work¯ ow systems. We expect new impulses to the emerging ® eld of multimedia systems and propose joint research by the research communities of computer science, communication research, organization design, human± computer interaction and information systems. As Ishii et al. (1994, p. 94) stated \`We are interacting not with computers, but through computers’.

## A case study on corporate multimedia information systems

Barclays PLC, a one-bank-holding company with an enormous network of domestic and international branches, has its origins in the seventeenth century (Watkins, 1982). Barclays Bank is a UK-based leading ® nancial institution and the third largest bank in the UK. Barclays operates on a decentralized basis through regional of® ces throughout the UK, which led to the establishment of 300 business network centres to cater for the needs of small-and medium-sized organizations. In 1990 information technology was decentralized into the business divisions BZW (Investment Banking Division) and SBD (Service Business Division), which deal with the technology issues (Barclays, 1994). During 1992 the group was organized into three main operating divisions: the Banking Division, The BZW Division and the SBD. The Banking Division is the largest business in the group and includes the branch network and card and insurance services, providing corporate and personal banking within the UK and the rest of the world. The BZW Division is an integrated global business, round the world and round the clock. The revenues, consisting of fees and commissions, dealing pro® ts and net interest margins, are derived from securities underwriting, sales and trading, corporate ® nance advisory activities, risk management activities in both the cash and derivatives markets, asset management, structured ® nancing advice, global treasury services and participation in corporate banking. The SBD represents the group’s expertise in information technology and operations intensive business. The SBD consists of three operating units: Barclays Global Services (BGS), which offers information and transaction support services and products, Barclays Network Services (BNS), which provides the group’s network and communications infrastructure and is the vehicle for the promotion of new businesses and alliances based on the innovative use of the latest technologies and Barclays Computer Operations (BCO), which acts as the principal computer service provider.

Barclays history of multimedia involvement goes back to the early 1970s, when a Video Communications Section was set up within the Public Relations Department, which was responsible for one of the ® rst European corporate video networks. The Interactive Video Unit, which was part of the Video Communications Section, is now part of BNS. Barclays has had a \`multimedia unit’ since 1986. Then it was part of the Video Department, producing training programmes for procedural banking business for internal training. The Multimedia Department, operating from the BNS headquarters in Teddington, has been formed by merging the Interactive Video Unit and Divisional Research Unit of the Central Retail Services Division (CRSD). Over the years, this reorganization has shown the changing and evolutionary understanding of multimedia information systems ± from a separated Video Unit to integrated multimedia information systems development ± and the growing need to integrate video and audio technologies and information systems into networked multimedia information systems. The head of the Multimedia Department reports directly to the Director of Alliances at Barclays, since multimedia is seen as a strategic opportunity to broaden the business network and to redesign the business. The main tasks of the Multimedia Department at Barclays are as follows.

(1) Evaluating multimedia information systems through pilot studies.

(2) Developing electronic catalogues for retailers and Barclays Group companies.

(3) Developing a speci® cation for a generic multimedia application framework for retailers.

(4) Developing a software environment for multimedia information systems for retailers.

(5) Developing and testing on-line loan services.

(6) Developing and testing on-line plastic card transactions.

The list of activities of the Multimedia Department shows that Barclays has attributed a crucial role for multimedia information systems to play, both internally and externally.

Barclays Multimedia offers multimedia production and consultancy services which combine knowledge and experience of the bank’s requirements gained over years of producing multimedia information systems for internal clients and a range of skills that comes from working with external organizations. Barclays Multimedia plays an active part as a sponsor of the Interactive Media in Retail Group (IMRG) a platform which was formed in 1990 as a forum for pre-competitive collaboration between suppliers and users of retail multimedia and which has over 130 retail members in the UK.

The main strategic driving force for multimedia information systems is the convergence of the four industries. Recent developments in information technology, which led to the development of multimedia information systems, have brought a new competitive situation to Barclays. First, some digital delivery channels are already available and soon more will be available on a large scale as the government intentions about information highways in Europe, the USA and Japan show. Second, telecommunications and cable television operators are acting as the key service providers for the networked infrastructure on a national and global level. Third, ® nancial services will soon become a commodity.

Barclays’ chief executive formulates the impact and future of multimedia information systems at Barclays: \`As the technology of computer information and telecommunications converge it is possible to envisage a future when many banking services will be delivered direct to the home or business place via television screens’ (Barclays, 1994, p. 9). Since 1993 Barclays has focused on developing new income streams from multimedia. The huge information technology resources and technical skills within BCO and BNS in particular make them well equipped to seek to forge pro® table partnerships, alliances and joint ventures in the external market-place. BCO continues to increase its visibility in the external market and has set up alliances with other companies to provide new services. Summarizing the strategic outlook, some of the main technical problems which Barclays face nowadays are reliance on an existing delivery channel branch infrastructure, complex product information, a poor perception of service level from customers, rapid imitation of innovation by competitors and obsolete systems.

Barclays is exploring the application of multimedia information systems to overcome these barriers. One of the most important opportunities which multimedia information systems offer is the possibility to change the delivery channel ± the mechanism that a service provider uses to deliver services to its customers. The changing nature of delivery channels in ® nancial institutions has drawn the attention of some researchers. Veps„l„ninen and Apte (1987) proposed a framework called a model of service channel strategies (SCS), which recognizes that, in addition to channel systems, the delivery of ® nancial services involves the participation of human intermediaries, such as agents or company employees (Apte and Veps„l„ninen, 1993). Thiemann (1986) developed a 2-D matrix where the \`level of service’ is considered with the potential customer’s \`ability and willingness to pay’ to draw conclusions in conjunction with the positioning of banking services. The issues of service location, customer contact and service process ¯ ows in the electronic delivery of services were discussed by Gordon and Fisk (1987). Huete (1988) developed a matrix to achieve a ® t between the service content and delivery channel. Chase (1978) suggested that a delivery channel consists of both front-room and back-room operations where human efforts and information technology support are combined in appropriate proportions to create the service needed by the customer. One of the main questions for Barclays exploitation of multimedia information systems concerning the structure of delivery channels is how to ® nd the balance between information technology and human intermediation in con® guring the delivery channels for various ® nancial services.

Barclays’ exploitation of multimedia information systems shows a growing need to change the delivery channel from the branch infrastructure to a digital delivery channel. The potential target market served through a digital delivery channel is a mass market although multimedia information systems will be used to customize the ® nancial products. Hence, multimedia services are highly customized but delivered through a mass delivery channel, such as interactive television or multimedia kiosks. The challenge to Barclays regarding the design issues of multimedia services is to ® nd a balance between customized information systems and mass information systems. The commitment to a customized product becomes clear in the following statement: \`We recognise that customers are not alike and we are becoming more focused in order to meet each customer’s needs and desired level of service’ (Barclays, 1994, p. 13).

In the past, the selling of ® nancial services was done solely through bank branches and travelling business bankers. Barclays’ multimedia information systems try to target the potential customers through the redesign of their organizational boundaries. The changing process of a \`store-based shopping’ to a \`shopless shopping’ paradigm is being implemented with multi-media information systems. Financial services can be transmitted to the workplace, to the home and to public-access multimedia kiosks or via the Internet/WWW using basically the same technology and software.

In the case of multimedia information systems, Barclays is pushed to ask itself the main strategic question: which business are we in and what are our core competencies? The answer to these questions is changing, as we can see through the deployment of CD-I, interactive television and the WWW as a delivery channel and the \`information highway’ as a networked infrastructure for the delivery of services and new products, through a multimedia mass information system.

Multimedia information systems play an enabling role at Barclays in the process of redesigning and rethinking core competencies and skills. The most important multimedia information systems used in Barclays exploitation is interactive television. Interactive television and the Internet/WWW-based multimedia systems will redesign Barclays business processes fundamentally. The delivery of customized multimedia services to a mass audience is essential. The delivery channel will be changed from a branch infrastructure to a direct digital customer link into the home, public places and the workplace. The second key multimedia technology involved is the integration of group multimedia systems such as desktop videoconferencing capabilities into kiosks and other applications. They provide a direct link from the point of information or point of sales to Barclays staff, regardless of where the staff are located ± in of® ces, at home or travelling.

Multimedia information systems and particularly networked multimedia information systems can be seen as an enabling technology to move Barclays into the state of a networked organization. The organizational challenge lies in the increasing need to manage the distributed complexity. Through strategic alliances Barclays is redesigning its business scope and developing new services and targeting customers differently than before. The problems of networked multimedia information systems and the development of new services are closely linked with parties other than Barclays. Hence, Barclays is in close discussion with major multimedia companies such as British Telecom, AT&T and Mercury and with computer suppliers. An internal programme is looking at possible alliances and joint ventures. To offer truly global or at least European services Barclays depends heavily on regulation since telecommunications and the associated network infrastructure (information highway) is still more or less regulated. However, to measure and study the scope and degree of their impact, research on multimedia information systems depends on their advanced status in organizational and social realities.

One of the major organizational impacts of multimedia information systems on business processes is the change of delivery channels from a branch infrastructure to direct-digital linkage to the customers. Hence, the way and the procedures in which customers learn about products and new services changes radically. Currently, the shift from one-way communications to two-way, which is enabled by networked multimedia information systems, has and will have strong effects on consumer marketing. Furthermore, remote Barclays staff give advice via desktop videoconferencing links to current customers and potential customers.

The following statement by the chairman of Barclays makes it quite clear what structural consequences multimedia information systems will have on Barclays branch infrastructure: \`I still believe that branches perform a valuable function at the interface between the bank and its customers, but technology and changing shopping habits mean that there will be fewer branches in the future’ (Barclays, 1994, p. 22). Through electronic alliances Barclays is bundling products with their ® nancial services, as Freemans catalogue and Page & Moy travel multimedia information systems illustrate. If the travel catalogue of Page & Moy could be delivered via interactive television or the Internet/WWW to the customer, customers who want to choose a holiday could select a desired continent, see a list of special requirements, dates, etc. ± in fact everything they would normally do in a travel agency. The customer could then call up video presentations of the resorts, including restaurants, hotels and sights. The key services which Barclays would offer are payment services, personal loans, foreign currency exchange, travel insurance, etc. Similarly, the customer would be able to select a car from the visuals, pick the upholstery, book a test drive and arrange ® nancial details.

With regard to the core competencies and skills it is very important to mention that some 30 000 employees at Barclays have been through the multimedia training courses which were delivered at 63 learning centres, but the ultimate goal is to deliver them through networked multimedia PCs to every employees’ desktop. As the chairman puts it \`Changes in work practices, particularly in the UK Domestic Bank, have meant signi® cant extra resources being devoted to training. We also respect the changing nature of our workforce. We allow increased ¯ exibility in working arrangements ± job sharing, a career break scheme for child care, responsibility breaks to care for elderly or sick people’ (Barclays, 1994, p. 26).

To cope with the changing nature of work and the workforce it requires the support of multimedia information systems. It is an evolutionary development of information systems and an enabling technology: ® rst a competitive advantage to some organizations who deploy it and later a necessity for the survival or the organization. Organizations will have to think radically about how multimedia information systems will affect them.

## Concluding discussion and further research needs

Research on the organizational implications of multimedia information systems should have a clear theoretical foundation and state the underlying researcher’s approach regarding the relationship between investigated multimedia technology and organizational change. According to our understanding of the implications of multimedia information systems on the organization, the design, use and implementation of multimedia information systems in organizations need considerable planning. Further research needs be undertaken in each category of multimedia information systems. The organizational implications will differ depending on the investigated systems category. Possible research questions are: What are the main characteristics of and possible changes initiated by multimedia information systems? And, further, Will multimedia information systems help users to achieve new goals and businesses or do they solely lead to an increase in the ef® ciency of work and communication processes? Following the categorization in Table 1 and the discussed case studies of multimedia information systems, we can summarize as follows.

\`Personal multimedia information systems’ enhance the development of a mass information system directed at (potential) users outside the organization. This category of information systems is, with regard to its reach and scope, relatively new to most organizations. A cultural adaptation process of organization members and system developers to the requirements of an implementation process is required. From the perspective of users the amount of available information will increase. The consequences of the retrieval and storage of multimedia information will enhance organizational learning processes. The application of interactive television or the delivery of WWW-based services to residential and organizational users produces more than just productivity enhancements for organizations. It provides opportunities for redesigning the organization, for example through redesigning its delivery channel.

\`Group multimedia information systems’ such as multimedia conferencing systems and multimedia mail have the potential to redesign organizational communication patterns. We suggest that further research needs to be undertaken to investigate what changes, with regard to communications patterns, group multimedia information systems produce. For example, some studies show that the use of desktop multimedia conferencing leads to higher ef® ciency in organizational communications (Hatcher, 1992). Is ef® ciency the only change enabled by desktop multimedia conferencing systems or does it enable qualitatively new communication possibilities? The added value of multimedia information to standard e-mail (Markus, 1994) needs further research on the implications of human communication patterns within organizations, i.e. attached cameras and microphones to desktop computers differ substantially to text-based e-mail systems and, hence, we view group multimedia information systems such as desktop multimedia conferencing using a shared workspace as a qualitatively new communication media, which has no analogue in communication without computers (Culnan and Markus, 1987). It is not only more \`information rich’ (Daft and Lengel, 1984) but it also offers new design possibilities for workgroups in and across organizations. \`Group multimedia information systems’ enhance a \`networked (Rockart and Short, 1991; Miles and Snow, 1992) or \`virtual’ (Dubinskas, 1993) organization structure. The advent of desktop multimedia conferencing systems for collaborative work raises new research questions on the design issues of inter- and intraorganizational communication (Huber, 1990), organizational design (Keen, 1990; Huber and Glick, 1994) and organizational computing (Applegate et al., 1991). Prospective users of group multimedia information systems should take into account the volume and ambiguity of the \`information environments’ (Huber and Daft, 1987) as well as the cultural prerequisites of the organization (Press, 1991). Desktop multimedia conferencing (Vin et al., 1991) is a new form of computer-aided collaboration and is in its early stages. To be successful, an important requirement is a \`critical mass’ (Markus, 1990) of users within an organization and a climate of collaboration (Fulk et al., 1990). Most of the technological problems in group multimedia information systems are due to the lack of a network bandwidth of existing computer networks and a lack of integration with voice networks. The social and organizational implications of desktop multimedia conferencing, e.g. structural and cultural changes in building virtual organizations (Dubinskas, 1993), will be even more challenging for the information systems community.

\`Corporate multimedia information systems’ have a high value in terms of the redesign of an organization’s structure and processes within and across organizations in terms of higher ef® ciency (Huber and McDaniel, 1986). Computer± Telephony integration, which is one application area of corporate multimedia information systems, fundamentally redesigns the responsibilities of corporate information systems departments. It expands the responsibilities of information systems departments with a need to manage and integrate communications infrastructure and will require a thorough integration with organization infrastructures such as LANs and WANs. Besides the technical challenges, the organizational and cultural challenges, with regard to the integration of different communication patterns and the integration of development team members, are crucial for the successful use and implementation of multimedia information systems. Designing post-industrial organizations (Huber, 1984), which use and implement multimedia information systems, is a challenging issue for researchers and practitioners in information systems.
