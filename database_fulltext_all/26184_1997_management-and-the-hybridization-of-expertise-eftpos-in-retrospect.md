Journal of Information Technology (1997) 12, 83± 95

# Management and the hybridization of expertise: EFTPOS in retrospect

JOHN HOWELLS

Department of Management Studies, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

This paper will show how the UK banks learnt the strategic value of retaining IT expertise in-house through their experience of the design of EFTPOS networks. The banks began by attempting to \`buy-in’ IT expertise from other organizations, but they were ® rst compelled to locate expertise in a jointly-controlled organization called EFTPOS UK, then forced to develop the new IT expertise within their existing IT departments. These manipulations of the \`organizational location’ of expertise gave individual banks the ability to create their own technology strategies, which they promptly exploited in an attempt to gain competitive advantage. It will be argued that a greater capacity to form individual technology strategies and exploit competitive advantage will result from the active management of two other dimensions of expertise: these are termed the internal location and the individual partition of expertise. The ® rst term refers to where in the organization technical expertise is located and the second to how technical expertise is divided between individuals. The process by which the individual partition of expertise changes is called the hybridization of expertise and it is argued that although this is to an extent \`natural’ it may certainly be managed. The case for a more aggressive and explicit expertise strategy, including the active management of the process of hybridization, is made by reference to the nature of the network design and practices in the management of technical expertise in other industries.

## Introduction

This paper explores the strategic expertise issues involved in the design and implementation of the UK banks’ EFTPOS (electronic funds transfer at the point of sale) networks. The issues raised by this case are fundamental to network design and are likely to remain relevant for banks and other organizations who become involved in large-scale information technology (IT) network design in the future.

As information processing costs declined from the 1970s to the 1980s, banks became involved in the creation of a series of increasingly complex IT networks that process progressively lower value transactions. By the late 1980s the UK banks were ready to develop EFTPOS, a network that substitutes electronic for paper transactions at the retailer’s point of sale. The result is a network which processes the familiar Visa Delta and Switch debit cards. The UK banks intend that these debit cards and EFTPOS will largely replace the paper cheque as a payment instrument, since to process a cheque costs in the order of 60 pence while electronic transactions may cost as little as 15 pence (Woodman and Diver, 1988).

However, the scale of investment involved in establishing a national EFTPOS network is much larger than for earlier IT-based ® nancial innovations such as the ATM (automated teller machine) networks. One estimate is that the full capital cost of a \`national’ system would be well over £2 billion (Whitehead, 1990). The scale of such a system would be large; it would link the estimated 300 000 retail outlets in the UK to the banks and the banks to each other (Woodman and Diver, 1988). A large proportion of this cost (more than half, according to Woodman and Diver (1988)) would come from the purchase of the computer-processing capacity, which would be suf® - cient to process an equivalent amount of electronic transactions to the more than 2 billion cheque transactions generated annually in the UK (APACS, 1989). From these ® gures, the investment in EFTPOS appears to have been an expensive, but highly remunerative proposition for the banks by the late 1980s.

The enormous capital cost of constructing such a network gave the banks an excellent reason to cooperate over the EFTPOS design and implementation. This was evident when they began to work on the network design in 1986 and assumed that a single national network would be built with the costs shared between all the major banks. However, while every EFTPOS network would substitute electronic transactions for paper, there remained many ways of designing the network. It could process transactions on-line, off-line or a mixture of both and it could have a variety of security systems and hardware that were compatible with the banks’ existing systems to differing degrees. The individual interests of the banks led them to increasingly severe disagreements over these issues between 1986 and 1990

However, the paper further develops the analysis of expertise by arguing that if an in-house location of expertise is important to the ability to form an independent strategy then it must be further strengthened by the management of two other \`dimensions’ of expertise: the internal location of expertise and the individual partition of expertise. ## Expertise and the EFTPOS story

## Out-house network design and location of strategic expertise in IT suppliers

The unity of the banking cartel was reinforced by the tightly-knit social ‚ lite that ran the \`big four’ English clearing banks at the highest managerial levels, by government regulation and by the policies of the Bank of England. Elsewhere some of the published material that describes the banks’ historical development into this cartel between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries has been reviewed (Hine and Howells; 1993). It should not be surprising that when faced with the problem of how to design and implement IT networks, the instinct of the banks favoured a \`collectivist’ approach, one where the design was agreed by all and costs were shared in some \`equitable’ manner. However, just at the time that the banks began work on the EFTPOS network in 1986 it became obvious that the government was intent on introducing more competition into the banking industry, for example through deregulation intended to foster building society and bank competition and through the Bank of England which, in various pronouncements, also appeared to favour increased interbank competition from this time (Hine and Howells, 1993). This introduced uncertainty into the banks initial collectivist approach to EFTPOS ± it was now possible that one bank could break ranks and develop an independent EFTPOS strategy while the Bank of England would not intervene. This is exactly what happened during the EFTPOS network development (see Table 1 for an abbreviated chronology of the EFTPOS events). The essential background for the management of EFTPOS expertise was a rapidly changing competitive relationship between the leading banks.

However, at the beginning of the EFTPOS network development in 1986, in addition to the historical continuity of a collaborative approach to IT service innovation and the cost advantages of close collaboration, senior bankers were aware that the banks’ IT departments did not have the project management and technical expertise necessary to plan and implement such a large project. Outside the banking industry companies such as IBM and BT did have the expertise and it appeared natural to the banks that they should seek help from these companies. The early designs for a national EFTPOS were produced by these two companies acting in a consultancy relationship to the whole banking industry as represented by the CLCB (Committee of London Clearing Banks). The alternative strategy of developing internal expertise housed in the IT department was never explicitly framed and certainly not acted upon at this early stage.

One of the disadvantages of a strategy of collective dependence appeared early. IBM had developed what is called a \`master-session’ security management scheme for ATM networks (Davies and Price, 1989) which used an implementation of the DES (data encryption standard) algorithm for the encryption of PINs (personal identi® cation numbers). IBM, along with BT, proposed to use this scheme in EFTPOS. The banks were not in a position to judge the appropriateness of such a design in an EFTPOS network since they had few security experts and still fewer capable of comparing the security implications of different algorithm implementations in networks. This might not have worried anyone had not a rival consortium appeared (Racal Transcom, Tandem Computers and Logica) and begun lobbying the CLCB to adopt a different EFTPOS design which used the \`transaction key’ scheme for the security of PINs. This scheme also used the DES algorithm but deployed it in such a way that encrypted PIN numbers were managed differently and, according to the consortium, in a manner more appropriate to the security needs of an EFTPOS network. At this point a reasonable suspicion might be that the IBM± BT design was so similar to the design of the ATM networks because of IBM’s close and continuing commercial involvement with the development and sale of ATM networks and not because of the appropriateness of the master-session key management scheme for the EFTPOS network. However, the banks had no internal ability to investigate the issue and to decide between the schemes.

A second disadvantage would become more apparent over time and also had its origin in the strategy of \`collective dependence’ on IBM expertise. It was decided to order IBM \`System 88’ computers to form the central processing centre of the national EFTPOS system, a decision which appeared to the CLCB as a natural complement to the strategy of depending on the IBM project planning and security expertise. This threatened to introduce a new brand of hardware into the banks’ processing systems, a strategy that the banks’ IT departments would naturally oppose if they could, given their historical pattern of purchase of Tandem processors. However, they were not empowered to object, at least not in these early stages.

## Half-in and half-out house ± expertise in EFTPOS UK

Real progress towards a national EFTPOS network was not made until a task force was appointed in early 1986 to review the banks’ position and begin spending money. The decision was taken to form an organization which would eventually be called EFTPOS UK Ltd and which was to be controlled by a \`steering group’ of commercial bankers, one from each interested ® nancial organization. Faced with the problems of the \`out-house’ location of expertise outlined above, the banks began to develop EFTPOS UK as the organizational vehicle which would manage relevant EFTPOS technical expertise. Those forming the steering group represented the banks’ commercial expertise and interest, while those who worked for EFTPOS UK would be IT consultants seconded from organizations such as the Sema Group Plc and Logica.

The idea was that the steering group would ® x the banks’ commercial requirements while the technical consultants developed solutions that met those requirements. At its peak EFTPOS UK employed more than 300 technical consultants working on various aspects of the national system design.

This was technical expertise \`half-in and half-out’ of the banks’ organizations. EFTPOS UK technical consultants were certainly not salaried employees of a non-bank organization with a vested interest in its own network design. The technical consultants were answerable directly to the steering group who had the power and space to interact personally with them. On the other hand, the consultants were paid by the day, they were not loyal to the interests of individual banks and could only relate to the collective interests of the banks expressed by the steering group. The other potential repositories of EFTPOS expertise were the banks’ IT departments and these were supposed to have a subordinate role to EFTPOS UK in the design of the EFTPOS network and to cooperate in implementing the technical solutions the EFTPOS UK consultants decided upon.

## The development of in-house expertise ± Switch and Visa Delta EFTPOS

A further shift in the organizational location of technical expertise occurred as the growing mistrust between the banks encouraged them to begin to develop separate EFTPOS strategies. At ® rst these developments occurred in parallel with the collective EFTPOS UK effort, but by January 1990 the banks recognized that the separate EFTPOS strategies had become the real strategies and the collective EFTPOS UK scheme was formally abandoned. This left the Switch and Visa Delta EFTPOS schemes, each with distinct physical network designs, commercial operating conditions and card designs, as the basis for EFTPOS in the UK.

The mistrust that drove the change in location strategy derived from the uncertain status of Barclay’ s private EFTPOS network. It was unclear whether this was a small-scale \`experiment’ that would aid this bank’ s contributions to the national, collaborative scheme and that would eventually be merged with the national scheme (as the bank claimed in public) or whether it was a fast-developing rival to the national scheme that represented Barclay’ s attempts to subvert the whole collaborative arrangement by stealth and to grab a large EFTPOS market share ahead of its rivals. In an attempt to forestall this latter outcome three banks created the Switch service and network and developed it as rapidly as possible from 1988 onwards. However, this summary account leaves out the full series of twists and shifts in the rival bank strategies (for which see Howells and Hine, (1993a)).

Since the banks maintained the ® ction that the Switch and Barclay’ s debit (PDQ) services would be merged with the different standards of the national system for over 2 years, the collective technical expertise embodied in EFTPOS UK and the steering group continued to exist and to develop \`national’ EFTPOS standards. Once the banks instituted rival, separate EFTPOS strategies, the relocation of the focus for the development of strategic technical expertise was necessarily in-house. Whether Switch or Visa, the banks now relied on their IT departments to support the design and development of EFTPOS. The importance of these departments for both schemes is evident from the decision to process EFTPOS transactions within the IT department rather than in some collectively controlled processing centre.

## Shifting the organizational location of expertise and design decisions

The shifting organizational source of the strategic IT expertise had an important effect on certain key network design choices. The most important, referred to earlier, was the choice of hardware for the SPS in the early phase of reliance on out-house expertise. A decision had been taken to order IBM System 88 hardware for the EFTPOS UK central processors which represented a break in the banking industry IT department tradition of ordering only Tandem on-line processing computers.

This was one of the \`unforeseen consequences’ of increasing bank competition and moving to reliance on the IT department technical expertise ± these departments had their own perspective on systems development and their own views on earlier EFTPOS decisions. The hardware incompatibility problem was made acute by an early decision within EFTPOS UK to abandon the idea of an SPS in the projected national scheme for what they called a strategy of \`devolution’. This meant that EFTPOS UK’s pilot service would be run as an SPS network, but the national scheme would devolve the control of the terminal placements and transaction processing to individual banks (see the terminal-bank connection represented in Figure 2). The policy was of® cially adopted to increase the ability of banks to differentiate the EFTPOS services and it was a step in the breakdown of the cooperative approach to the EFTPOS design. It implied that at some future time the IT departments would have to buy system 88 computers; if the banks did not, it implied a lack of commitment to EFTPOS UK and the increasing power of the IT department perspective on incompatible hardware. In the event, none of the big four clearers installed system 88 equipment to interface with the EFTPOS UK processing centre (some smaller banks did so). Nor did these large banks second their own IT department people into EFTPOS UK as they were supposed to ± they were far more valuable acting on behalf of their bank’s separate EFTPOS strategy, located within their banks.

Barclays was prominent in pressing the case for design features which would allow the bank to continue developing its proprietary EFTPOS network, called PDQ. Internal to Barclays, the IT department responsible for designing and operating PDQ was an important actor in the argument for preserving the expertise and investment made in the PDQ design. For Barclays, devolution was necessary to prevent PDQ becoming obsolete because, as described already, under devolution it was possible to have a proprietary network where the transactions were processed by the individual bank (Figure 2). By pressing the steering group to make such design changes Barclays raised suspicions about its real EFTPOS strategy that led ultimately to the breakdown of the EFTPOS UK arrangement.

## Management and the hybridization of expertise

With devolution planned there remained the question of how to clear EFTPOS transactions between banks. Some banks chose to use the existing Visa network (Visanet), but the Switch banks refused to do so on the grounds that Visa was too closely associated with certain banks. That stance left them with the option of using what would have been the EFTPOS UK SPS as a simple \`switch’ to link the processing centres in the banks or to develop some other solution. In 1989 one of the steering group members had argued for the development of what was called the Tandem TAN (terminal access node) that would allow the System 88s already purchased to be used as a simple switch linking all the banks and allowing the clearance of all banks’ transactions through one centre (see the interbank connection in Figure 2). This would have required the development of two logically distinct pieces of software that could run on the banks’ own Tandem processors and interface with the System 88 switch. The System 88s were eventually scrapped because of the cost of this proposal, the dislike of IT departments of having to use the System 88s and the intensity of the dislike on the part of some banks of sharing any infrastructure with their new rivals. The Switch banks would build bilateral links between member banks, an expensive option in the long term, while the other banks would continue to use the Visa organization’s physical infrastructure.

In essence, as the banks became more competitive there was a progressive empowerment of the technical expertise embodied within the individual IT departments. This was demonstrated by the quality of debates between and within the steering group and EFTPOS UK. The bank representatives tended to be in¯ uenced by their own IT departments as well as EFTPOS UK consultants. The interviewees agreed that there was a widespread desire to see the pilot project trial each bank’s preferred design ± the result was to delay and complicate the development of the EFTPOS UK design and so ultimately to undermine it.

## Expertise issues

## The competitive logic for in-house expertise

In the course of the EFTPOS design period the banks changed the organizational location of the EFTPOS expertise twice. Each time it was relocated more inhouse in an attempt to develop an individual capacity for strategic action in an increasingly competitive and uncertain EFTPOS environment. The greatest control over expertise was obtained when it was located within the IT departments.

If the collective approach to EFTPOS had been attempted a few years earlier than 1986 it might have succeeded (as it did in many European countries, for details of which see Howells (1996)). At this time Barclay’s proprietary PDQ network would not have existed in its developed form and the mutual absence of proprietary technological interests would have served to reassure the banks of their common commitment to a national scheme. Ironically, the pre-existence of proprietary EFTPOS interests stimulated the mistrust that eventually led to the destruction of the collective EFTPOS project and resulted in each of the leading banks fostering in-house expertise.

The advantages of developing technical expertise inhouse were clearly demonstrated in the EFTPOS case and it would seem likely that this \`lesson’ is the one that is most likely to be permanent. As an example, at present (1996) most large clearing banks have inhouse smart card development teams. If the preceding argument is valid, the very existence of such teams must now tend to undermine the possibility of a collective approach to designing a smart card network.

## The organizational location of expertise

It is clear from the last section that the EFTPOS case is an excellent demonstration of how the increasing need for individual competitive action drove expertise in-house. However, whether expertise should be located internally or externally to the organization is only one dimension of what may be called the \`organizational location’ of expertise.

There are two other dimensions that are as subject to the same strategic imperatives as the in-house ± outhouse dimension: the internal location and the individual partition of expertise. As an example of the ® rst, individuals may be grouped within a department or selected expert individuals positioned at a high level within the organization. The individual partition of expertise concerns how expertise is divided between individuals; business expertise may reside in one set of individuals and \`IT expertise’ in others.

In the EFTPOS case, although management moved the expertise in-house they did it without explicit consideration of the \`dimensions’ outlined here. There was no explicit consideration of the dimensions of internal location or the individual partition of individual expertise. However, the same competitive rationale applies to all these dimensions; just as the movement of expertise in-house gave banks greater control over expertise and the ability to form individual EFTPOS strategies, these same strategic objectives may be furthered by the deliberate management of the internal location and the individual partition of expertise. Similar arguments have become relatively common within the IT management literature with respect to other cases. The next section will examine the individual partition of expertise in the EFTPOS case and the process of \`hybridization’ , which whether \`natural’ or managed leads to a degree of breakdown in the pre-existing partitions of expertise.

## Hybridization of expertise as a \`natural’ process

In the organizational design for EFTPOS UK it was assumed that the steering group bankers had power and commercial expertise and that they would stipulate the system requirements for which the technical experts would provide solutions. In this design it is evident that there is a major partition of individual expertise between the bankers and the technology consultants, although clearly the organizational arrangement of having groups of these different individuals in one setting is an acknowledgement that EFTPOS could not be completed by one group alone. The EFTPOS UK design was eventually abandoned but its debates fostered what may be called a \`natural’ process of hybridization of the two divided forms of expertise. For EFTPOS, many design choices were interconnected, a characteristic which promoted the hybridization of expertise as a means of understanding the commercial implications of the interconnections. To an extent, simple participation in the debates enabled some breakdown of the division between \`commercial’ and \`technical’ expertise as each side learnt from the other.

As an example, PINs were originally chosen to authenticate the customer at the point of sale in EFTPOS because PINs had proven reliable in ATM networks. PINs were thought to be more secure than signature veri® cation, which depended on the vigilance of retail operatives. As the project progressed this basic assumption was increasingly quali® ed by a more developed understanding of the security implications of PINs in an EFTPOS environment. It became accepted that it was more dif® cult to keep the PIN itself safe at the retailer’s point of sale than in the physical environment of the ATM. The EFTPOS terminals themselves were necessarily cheaper and almost certainly less physically secure than an ATM and so more prone to attacks in search of PINs. The use of PINs implied encryption should be used to safeguard PINs in the network, which increased the costs and created the possibility of choosing between encryption algorithms and different security implementations of the same algorithm. In addition to all this, retailers had their own views on PINs and special encryption terminals being positioned at their point of sale. They were suspected of being deliberately obstructive by some bankers, but were eventually understood as having a distinct point of view on the technology by others. The attempt to resolve the uncertainty intrinsic to such choices required members of the steering group to acquire some understanding of the relevant \`technical’ expertise.

Not only were the technical± commercial issues interlinked and complex, but there was a highly uncertain political environment which put a further premium on the possession of hybrid expertise. This was because the positions of the parties in what appeared to be disputes over \`technical’ issues were sometimes the result of a political position rather than their sincere belief in the \`technical’ merits of their case. For example, there was a debate within the steering group over the choice of encryption algorithm that would be used to secure a PIN transfer within the national service. Barclays PDQ service used an algorithm called DES, but EFTPOS UK decided that an alternative algorithm called RSA was a better choice. Barclays was perceived (almost certainly correctly) as hostile to RSA because the choice would again threaten to make PDQ incompatible with the national system; equally, one leading bank was perceived by some steering group members as a supporter of RSA precisely because it would damage Barclay’ s interests. In this environment there was a premium on the capability to read the political and organizational positions revealed only by declared preferences for \`technical’ choices. In other words there was a premium on the possession of \`hybrid’ expertise ± individuals with an understanding of both the commercial and technical ® elds which would enable the most rapid establishment of a good interpretation of the technopolitical pitches made by other banks.

It should not be thought that what has been called natural hybridization proceeds smoothly and effortlessly and that all participants gain equally from being present at debates. The technical consultants at EFTPOS UK had no reason to treat their role as other than temporary; for them, there was no long-term career interest in the acquisition of commercial banking knowledge. Nor were the commercial bankers in the same position with respect to each other, as the following quote from one who regarded himself as having hybrid expertise makes clear.

I was unique, I think, in my technical and business background and I contributed very forcibly to the steering group debates and tended to raise hares running where I had found a particular technical solution a bit strange . . . but few other people round that table debated with me. They were in no position to do so and it came over as a personal con¯ ict between myself and the technicians in EFTPOS UK . . . (steering group member).

The commercial± technical split in expertise affected the quality of the debate between the steering group and the technical consultants of EFTPOS UK. Hybridization may in part be enabled by participation in projects, but on such accounts it also contributes to their successful management. This prompts the idea that it is possible to promote the hybridization of expertise to aid design and the implementation of networks actively, an idea that will be more fully discussed below, while the existence of a \`natural’ process of hybridization may be more a signal to management that the existing individual partition of expertise has become inadequate.

## Networks as con® gurational technologies

These has been widespread interest in the hybridization issue in the management of IT literature since the early 1990s. This increased interest in hybridization occurs, as many authors (for example, Earl and Skyrme, 1990; Fincham et al., 1994) have observed, when \`users’ become necessarily more involved in the design and implementation of IT systems ± whether that system is an interorganizational network like EFTPOS or a system such as a management information system deployed internally to an organization. Of course the two are related, for as IT networks become capable of extension to more parts of an organization, \`contingent knowledge’ (Fleck, 1996), distributed, organizational context-speci® c knowledge, becomes more important to implementation. Those who possess such knowledge are unlikely to be IT experts, but most collaborate with the experts to enable the successful design and implementation of networks. With increased user involvement comes the natural process of hybridization, experience with which increases the management interest in anticipating and fostering hybridization.

A useful way of conceptualizing this change in the nature of IT systems is to think of IT networks as \`con® gurational technologies’ (Howells, 1994) an idea developed from the study of robotics technology (Fleck, 1991). To say networks are \`con® gurational technologies’ is to say that there is not \`one best way’ but many ways to design and implement the technology and retain its basic functionality; con® gurational technologies are highly \`plastic’ in implementation. Neither modern robots nor IT networks can be considered to have a stable design outside their particular \`production’ or organizational contexts. The precise differences in design depend on the precise nature of the interests involved in the implementation and has much to do with the contingent circumstances of the organization or group of organizations which will operate the technology. This is highly apparent when the EFTPOS design is compared across European nations; almost every possible design variation has been constructed, dependent upon the early strategies and the relative power of the banks, retailers and consumer groups (Howells, 1996). To describe network technologies as con® gurational is to make it explicit that the attainment of any particular design is likely to depend on various forms of hybrid expertise. There should be great value attached to the ability to distinguish between what is genuinely technically possible and impossible and what is a result of other constituents (retailers, other banks and suppliers) and political± technical positions.

It is perhaps not so surprising that with these new developments in IT it has been argued that the IT department can be instructively compared to the established R&D departments in other industries (Earl, 1989; Howells, 1994). The move of IT expertise inhouse can be compared to the process of institutionalization of the R&D department in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Then, the in-house R&D department spread through US industry following an earlier wave of merger which produced oligopolistic structures in many industries (Mowery and Rosenberg, 1989). According to Mowery and Rosenberg (1989), because R&D was in-house, ® rms had con® dence that the bene® t of the new technologies would remain appropriable by the organization and so were willing to risk large sums in speculative projects. Only relatively routine research was ever contracted out to other organizations. R&D departments also take on the tasks of systematic search activity and \`uncertainty reduction’ , i.e. they act as repositories of expertise that aid analysis of the competitive environment. As the banks moved to rely more on in-house IT expertise they were looking for a similar competitive advantage.

Extending the argument in a speculative direction, it should become possible for some banks to become increasingly deliberate in the management of IT expertise as a means of creating the ability to act as technological leaders to the banking industry (again, Earl (1989) argued that this is the future promise of IT in general). Although it would constitute a break with the inherited banking tradition and strategy, that tradition and strategy was evidently strained in the design and implementation of EFTPOS and one can expect it to be tested again in the future. Part of this new strategic approach is the deliberate management of the hybridization of expertise.

## Managing the hybridization of expertise

## The arguments for the management of hybridization

The problem with the natural generation of hybrid expertise is that even if it happens, it is during and after the events for which it is most relevant. If it could be generated before individual projects it could aid in their implementation. How to manage the hybridization process and how to produce \`hybrid’ managers has been debated and to an extent researched in the management of IT literature in recent years. There was an early enthusiastic interest in hybrid managers which was led by the British Computer Society when it organized a Task Group on Hybrids, which reported that hybrids were likely to be of increasing importance in the 1990s (Palmer and Ottley, 1990). The Oxford Institute for Information Management (OXIIM) began a programme of research into hybrid managers which sought to de® ne what was known about hybrids (Earl and Skyrme, 1990) and then to develop advice for their development and successful use (Skyrme, 1992).

Skyrme’s (1992) work focused on the \`bridging mechanisms’ that foster the hybridization process, i.e. the organizational devices that senior management might employ to promote the transfer of IT expertise into the business organization (Earl and Skyrme, 1990: p. 4; Skyrme, 1992). Skyrme (1992) ordered these devices (such as job rotation, secondment and steering groups) by the degree to which they are \`organizational and structural’ and concern \`groups’ or \`individuals’ (a breakdown which resembles the types of organizational location of expertise outlined earlier). Part of the debate (in particular in Palmer and Otley (1990)) focuses on the professional development of IT experts and argues for a change in formal educational curricula.

Skyrme (1992) concluded from his ® eld research ± 29 interviews in eight organizations and 60 questionnaires ± that many managers (as well as hybrids themselves) believe hybrids are already improving the management of IT and that many of the \`bridging mechanisms’ are being deployed by organizations.

In the banks too, there are organizational experiments in managing the hybridization process. Fincham et al. (1994) referred to attempts to place IT specialists at a high level in company hierarchies ± an experiment in the internal location of expertise. At least one (Scottish) bank attempted organizational innovation of this type as early as the late 1980s, for the purpose of promoting hybridization of expertise (Scarborough and Lannon, 1988; Fincham et al., 1994). Other banks have by now engaged some of the devices listed in Skyrme (1992) since that date.

Although the argument for managing hybridization has so far depended on an analysis of changes in the nature of IT itself, one can derive powerful arguments for the direct management of the hybridization of expertise from other industries. Analysis of Japanese car manufacturers points to the management of the hybridization process as one of the sources of productivity and quality advantages. Thus, it has been reported that approximately half the Japanese productivity advantage in car production comes from \`design for manufacturability’ (Womack et al., 1993). These advances in design for manufacturability are partly based on organizational arrangements that foster the hybridization of expertise between individuals. The practices include some of those listed by Skyrme (1992) such as the systematic job rotation of technical staff, but also include other examples such as the award of great status and power to new model project leaders who manage multidisciplinary teams (they have tended to be promoted to company president in Toyota). This gives them a greater ability than their Western counterparts to marshal the resources for innovation and to override the tendency of functional departments to try to retain the best people for their own purposes. Although the term is not to my knowledge used in the total quality literature, the object of these practices is the creation of hybrid managers. Engineers begin with a technical specialization but through systematic job rotation become hybrid managers who may eventually reach the senior levels of the company. In contrast, the excessive \`professional’ and \`technical’ specialization of engineers is often seen as a source of Western rigidity and competitive disadvantage in the total quality literature and by Japanese commentators (see, for example, Hutchins, 1992).

Comparisons between the banking and car industries are interesting, for just as Western car manufacturers proved complacent with respect to their existing individual partition of expertise, one can argue that the banks will remain equally complacent in the absence of a powerful new industry entrant, their current experiments with bridging mechanisms hardly bearing a comparison to the practices apparently embedded in some of the ‚ lite Japanese car ® rms.

In the EFTPOS case and in the cases presented in Fincham et al (1994), it is clear that the current individual partition of expertise is itself actively maintained and in that sense is as \`arti® cial’ as an effort to impose a new partition of expertise. Within banks, technical people are seen as a socially distinct breed; they have separate career structures and they tend to be prevented from rising to senior levels in the banks because of their lack of commercial expertise. Hence, comments on technical people such as the following, from the EFTPOS case.

Very, very much boys in the back room. I wouldn’t say \`techies’ , I wouldn’t necessarily say that, but they’ re not recognized as potential contributors to the full running of the company (steering group member).

Research which showed in more detail how attitudes and practices such as recruitment policies, promotion blocks and work management procedures intertwine to maintain the technical± commercial split would be welcome, but it is not just internal practice that maintains the split. An important step in instituting the deliberate management of hybridization is to recognize that the very de® nition of \`technical’ people is forged by professional and educational institutions outside the banks ± some of the internal attitudes and practices can be seen as no more than \`coping mechanisms’ for the different types of expertise that are recruited raw into the organizations. As part of this argument it is worth pointing to a pertinent critique by German industrial sociologists of what may be termed \`Anglo-Saxon’ skill practices (see Altmann et al., 1992). Authors from this perspective have argued that the relative strength of large versus small ® rms and the numbers of hierarchical managerial layers within the ® rms are in¯ uenced by the degree of dependence of the ® rms on formal academic education rather than in-house vocational education. The banks relatively high dependence on recruitment from the labour market, rather than in-house skill development, suggests they may suffer in such a way, while their tendency to accept the de® nition of \`technical’ competence delivered to them by higher education in the form of quali® ed graduates is certainly reinforced by the lack of in-house education and training across their organizations. This argument further increases the case for a vigorous policy for the hybridization of expertise, one inclusive of education curricula reform as an essential part of the strategy.

A ® nal argument in favour of instituting an active hybridization strategy concerns estimates of how long and how many major bank IT projects will occur in the future for the banks. There is little doubt that the banks are faced with successive future \`waves’ of IT network development as it becomes economic to transform more of their paper-based services. Such networks include ® nancial electronic data interchange (FEDI), smart card payment services and the possible provision of payment services into Europe. These will all involve major tasks of design and implementation, similar in complexity to those involved in the EFTPOS design. There will be differences from EFTPOS, but the knowledge that such projects will one day occur strongly supports the development of long-term and active strategies for the management of the organizational location and hybridization of expertise.

## Hybridization policy grounded in past experience

Even if there were not some direct evidence from authors such as Skyrme (1992) that hybrids are seen to help the design and implementation of IT systems, the arguments derived from the car± bank industry cross-comparisons and evidence about higher education’s in¯ uence on the organizational and industrial structure support the view that hybridization can be vigorously supported in advance of the IT projects and advantage gained.

However, major organizational change is not a simple affair. It is notable that both the Japanese and the German vocational education systems are expensive and are supported by \`collectivist’ structures: in Japan it is the \`keiretsu’ , while in Germany statesanctioned coercive legal power is devolved to industrial associations (Howells, 1995). Hence, intensive multiple-skilling and hybridization policies can be supported, as well as a degree of skill \`redundancy’, without ® rms being free to evade the costs of creating skills while poaching skills from those who paid to develop and use them. The UK banks, as an oligopoly, happily retain the ability to absorb the expense of hybridization policies on a large scale, should they choose to advance the experiments they are currently engaged in (and referred to above). They have an industrial structural advantage that they have perhaps never had the chance or will to exploit fully in this way before. To the extent that the arguments of the last section are convincing, banks could err on the side of overskilling, by attempting to exploit the industry’s oligopolistic structure to ® nance this policy and building a corpus of hybrid managers as a strategic resource.

There remain the problems of what expertise to hybridize and the extent of hybridization, both in terms of the numbers of individuals within the organization and the expertise of the individuals. De® nitive answers to these questions do not seem likely and it is dif® - cult to think of an alternative approach to an incremental hybridization strategy, one which builds on the experience of past IT projects. This still leaves many options. In particular Skyrme (1992) noted of some senior hybrid interviewees that they were uninterested in detailed technical knowledge in itself, but had

. . . a passionate interest in information ± where it resided, who used it, new sources of it, how it could be extricated and used. This focus then determined the level of technical detail that they needed . . . (p. 19).

Would-be hybrids are selective about the knowledge they acquire, but it cannot be assumed there will be a tight, organizational consensus on what knowledge constitutes hybrid knowledge. Since the precise nature of this knowledge is de® ned through their experience of projects (for example, as described in the EFTPOS case with PINs and encryption algorithms), the experience of past projects is the only guide to \`managing’ hybridization in advance of future projects. This suggests that in addition to Earl and Skyrme’s (1990) bridging mechanisms, primarily intended to facilitate the person to person diffusion of knowledge, some attempt to formalize project experiences could prove invaluable in the retention and diffusion of experience that otherwise would be haphazardly located within those individuals who participated in projects. More developed internal project reports that approached academic texts in form might provide the unique and detailed case studies that could be used to create or to sensitize many managers to hybridization in advance of its being needed.

Besides the development of nascent in-house training activities it makes sense to work simultaneously on the source of the severe social division between technical and commercial expertise ± higher education and formal professional training. Faced with two separately institutionalized, elaborate curricula intended to create distinctive types of expertise the problem is again what to hybridize and what to leave out of the hybridized curricula. Again, past projects are an obvious source of criteria. However, reference could usefully be made to the history of the institutionalization of formal, technical knowledge teaching in different countries for clues as to how to proceed. A basic starting point would be the active involvement of \`consumer’ organizations such as the banks in the curriculum design in cooperation with educational institutions and an end to the passive acceptance of their output.

## Power and hybridization

More radical organizational change than an active management of the organizational location of expertise and a rethinking of the role of the IT department may be necessary because of the problem of the organizational location of power and its divorce from operational responsibility in an uncertain environment. On the EFTPOS project, one steering group manager described how in seeking authority for some technical decision, his request had to be passed upwards through so many layers of management that there was no chance the senior banker would understand the significance of authorizing his request. Several iterative returns were necessary to clarify the meaning of the request and the context of the decision. In this view, the hierarchical structure of the banks was dysfunctional ± in the rapidly changing environment for EFTPOS decisions ± because it divorced those with power from those with freshly acquired \`operational’ knowledge. If such problems repeat through time, some devolution of power to those with hybrid expertise or some contraction of the hierarchy may be necessary to improve the organization’s ability to act on its environment. It is relevant to turn to Burns and Stalker’s (1959) classic study of the management of innovation in which the terms mechanistic and organic are associated with ® rms that deal with relatively low and high rates of technical advance. With their many-layered hierarchies and tightly de® ned jobs the banks meet the criteria for classic, mechanistic bureaucracies, organized around the control of individuals and routine process rather than innovation. On this analysis the banks’ organizational \`style’ is dependent for its stability on the slow rate of technological change in paper-based information processing, the technology which underpins the banks’ core businesses. The problems of EFTPOS and the management of expertise can be seen as the problems of mechanistic organizations facing a leap in the complexity of technological change. This analysis and the analysis of organizational location and hybridization of expertise suggest the banks’ strategy should be to \`reorganize for innovation’, to prepare for the uncertainty that a future IT network design will bring them once again.

## Conclusions

When the banks began to look for an individual competitive advantage in the design and implementation of an EFTPOS network, they were driven to bring new forms of IT expertise in-house. They did not explicitly manage the other dimensions of the organizational location of expertise ± the internal location and individual partition of expertise ± that could have provided even greater sources of competitive advantage in the design of IT networks. The value of explicit strategies for the management of these aspects of expertise is likely to remain as the banks’ core business of information processing shifts from paper to electronic technology through a series of network implementations.

The natural process of the hybridization of expertise observed on these projects is itself a poor source of hybrid managers and its occurrence may be better perceived as a symptom of the inappropriateness of the actual organizational location of expertise. With an uncertain technological future, experience of past projects is an insuf® cient basis for strategy, although making more innovative use of that past experience is important. Other inputs should be the management of the organizational location of expertise in other industries and for other forms of technical expertise, where much more aggressive forms of the management of hybridization are found.

## Management and the hybridization of expertise

Although organizational \`bridging’ mechanisms that facilitate hybridization through personal contact are important, the more aggressive strategies will include moves towards establishing or upgrading in-house training schemes while simultaneously working to reform formal educational programmes (outside the ® rm) that otherwise maintain the commercial± technical partition of expertise. However, while the banks have an industrial structure that gives them these options, it is not certain they yet have the social incentive to develop such individual organizational sources of competitive advantage aggressively.
