# Knowledge for software maintenance

MARK TAYLOR and EDDIE MOYNIHAN

School of Computing and Mathematical Sciences, Liverpool John Moores University, Liverpool L3 3AF, UK

TREVOR WOOD-HARPER

Information Systems Research Centre, Salford University, Salford M5 4WT, UK

Studies have shown that maintenance is up to 80% of the life-cycle effort of developing and maintaining a business information technology (IT) system. There are several factors which signi® cantly affect maintenance work. This paper concentrates on one of these factors which is the knowledge staff require for effective and ef® cient systems maintenance. The knowledge required is split into two categories. Firstly, there is the knowledge of the business environment within which an IT system operates. Secondly, there is the knowledge of the technical IT system, particularly the details of the software structure. It is argued that both categories of knowledge are equally important and dif® cult to acquire. Examples of techniques for improving this knowledge are discussed. The article is partly based on case-study research into the actua practice of systems development and maintenance in 31 UK IT departments.

## Introduction

## Systems and software maintenance

Most publications on the maintenance of information (IT) systems (e.g. Swanson and Beath, 1989) concentrate on software maintenance. However, the wider view of maintenance was discussed in Moynihan (1993) where the maintenance of the whole business system was considered. Broadly, a business system is seen as everything that is required to perform a function or set of related functions such as sales invoicing and customer accounting. The wider perspective of a business system includes, for instance, hardware, software, of® ce procedures, business stationery such as invoices and services such as electronic data interchange (EDI). The maintenance of the whole business system and its non-software components such as hardware and of® ce procedures is a matter of considerable importance. However, in this paper we primarily examine the maintenance of the software-related aspects of a business system.

## The nature of maintenance

Maintenance is any deliberate change to an existing business system, e.g. repairing faults or keeping it up-todate. The various types of system and software maintenance have been considered in publications such as Pressman (1992). Both the literature and research examined below suggest that maintenance is very variable and frequently poorly de® ned and there is no consistent terminology found amongst IT practitioners. Some maintenance-related terms used by IT staff include bug ® xing, corrections, amendments, changes, improvements, upgrades, enhancements, new requirements and conversion. Indeed, practitioners often make no clear distinction between development (producing new systems) and maintenance (amending old systems).

An example of business systems maintenance is a change in payroll requirements. A UK illustration of this is the introduction in 1987 of statutory maternity pay (SMP) under Sections 46± 50 and Schedule 4 of the Social Security act 1986. In brief, the legislation imposed on employers a new mandatory minimum amount of pay for female staff who were absent due to the impending or recent birth of a child. This legislation required, for instance, changes in payroll software, business stationery and of® ce procedures (see Tolley (1995) for details). Frequently it is not initially clear how such changes affect the business system. Indeed there may be a choice. For example, if some requirements are simple and infrequent then it may be more economic to handle them by amended manual of® ce procedures, instead of changing the payroll system software. This sort of maintenance decision presupposes some understanding of the existing business system in its entirety, as well as the new requirements.

## Software maintenance tasks

Assuming that the decision it taken to amend the software then the maintenance staff (observed in the research explained below) typically carry out a set of steps similar to the traditional software development life cycle. In outline this consists of the following steps. Firstly, there is an investigation and analysis of the requirements. The IT staff then identify how the requirements can be met by amending the existing IT system design, i.e. what changes are required to the existing ® les or database and the program code. They then make the changes, e.g. by amending the COBOL program code. The changed computer system is then tested to ensure that it meets the new requirements. Another key task is regression testing, i.e. checking that the system features which should remain unchanged have not been inadvertently modi® ed. In theory and to some extent in practice, the detailed nature of this work depends on the information systems methodologies and local standards used in the IT department.

It is often stated that maintenance is up to 80% of business IT work (see, for instance, Swanson and Beath (1989)). Thus, maintenance work is of major economic importance, but this is not re¯ ected in either publications or established methodologies. In most cases maintenance receives a brief mention and little more (see, for instance, Avison and Fitzgerald (1995) and SSADM (1995)). The emphasis in most textbooks and methodology handbooks is usually on development new systems ab initio.

## Maintenance knowledge

As explained below a systems maintenance project requires that the IT staff possess or acquire knowledge prior to starting their work. For instance this knowledge could include a general appreciation of how the IT department operates, e.g. the correct procedure for a maintenance speci® cation to be authorized. Staff often need a detailed knowledge of relevant business operations, e.g. life assurance administration. They also need knowledge of the existing computer system design and coding. All this may seem mundane, but the examples below illustrate that inadequate prior knowledge can clearly lead to expensive, poor quality and delayed maintenance work. In some cases this could seriously jeopardize business operations dependent on IT.

Most commercial information systems methodologies such as SSADM (1995) do not appear to concern themselves particularly with prior knowledge of an existing system. It is either assumed that the documentation produced during a systems development will act as the source of all systems knowledge or that every software maintenance project will have to include an investigation of the current system as part of the analysis or design phase. This lack of interest in maintenance appears to be quite common. For example, Kaminski (1988), Li (1989) and others have noted that higher education does little to prepare computing graduates with the skills necessary for software maintenance work. In many instances computer science degrees barely even mention software maintenance, let alone prepare students for such future work.

## Literature review

There are only a few books that make IT maintenance a major topic, e.g. Takang and Grubb (1996). There is also a limited journal literature related to IT maintenance. Previous research has highlighted a number of aspects of the activity of software maintenance.

## Undocumented systems

A major problem facing many maintenance staff is the large amount of source code with little or no systems documentation (Warren, 1982).

## Understanding

Even if a system is reasonably well documented, then understanding the system is still a problem. Wilde and Maly (1988) and Conklin (1989) estimated that as much as half of the effort during software maintenance is understanding the system in order to make effective corrections or enhancements. McClure (1992) found that software maintainers spend approximately half of their time in trying to gain an understanding of the software. Saiedian and Henderson (1994) pointed out that since maintainers spend so much of their time attempting to understand software then any improvements in aiding that understanding will yield signi® - cant savings in time. In the process of understanding a maintenance job, computer personnel develop a \`mental model’ of the application (see Hage and Van der Meulen, 1989).

## Staff experience

The experience of maintenance staff with a given system is a factor in the effectiveness of the maintenance of that system. This has been discussed by Lientz and Swanson (1981), Boehm-Davis et al. (1992), Tamanujan and Cooper (1994) and Moore (1988). Banker et al. (1991) and Jeffery and Lawrence (1985) quanti® ed the amount of application experience necessary to improve signi® cantly the effectiveness of maintenance personnel engaged in the maintenance of a given system as being 2 years or greater. Parikh (1985)

stated that in many large companies it takes approximately 6 months for a programmer to be productive in software maintenance work.

## Skills, training and familiarization

Duncan (1989) expressed the view that IT managers should acknowledge that there are such things as maintenance skills and that these skills are not necessarily the same as development skills. Martin and Osborne (1984) identi® ed a number of characteristics that organizations required of their maintenance staff. In particular they identi® ed analytic, business and technical skills as being important qualities of the ideal maintainer. Harmer (1987) identi® ed analytical and social skills as being important skills for maintenance programmers. Chapin (1987) concluded that the job of software maintenance included social, analytical and technical skills. McGill (1989) and Rzevski (1982) also noted the large range of skills required for maintenance. Figure 1 provides a summary of the skill sets required for software maintenance. Briand et al. (1994) noted that there is a large communication cost overhead between maintainers and users. They suggested that communication may represent up to 40% of the software maintenance effort and, hence, there is a need for the participants of the maintenance process to communicate in a non-technical, more businessoriented manner. Cousin and Collofello (1992) advocated that companies should provide more education and training for maintenance staff in the skills and techniques required for software maintenance work.

## Knowledge and maintenance

We argue below that the knowledge required for IT maintenance can be divided into two areas. Firstly there is the \`business’ knowledge. This includes, for example, the National Insurance requirements of a payroll IT system. Secondly, there is the more familiar \`physical system’ knowledge which includes, for instance, the details of how the software is designed and coded.

Layzell and Macaulay (1994) found that business (or domain) knowledge and IT system knowledge can be essential to the success of the software maintenance function. A limited survey by Layzell and Macaulay (1990) of ® ve UK organizations concluded that there is an established need for successful software maintenance to use and transfer knowledge about its systems. A similar conclusion was reached by Keller and Over (1989). Despite this, Younger and Bennett (1993) noted that the understanding gained by maintenance staff is rarely recorded for the bene® t of their successors. Haziza et al. (1992) concluded that maintainers need to understand better the way the system is used in the operational environment and that they must share a common knowledge of the operational context with the users. Chapin (1987) concluded that maintainers need to learn about the users’ functions and the interaction between the software and the ways the users operate. Davies and Olson (1985) and Vitalari and Dickson (1983) suggested that IT staff gain domain knowledge over time and that this is a vital factor in successful IT projects. Duncan (1989) advocated that IT management should formally allocate time for software maintenance staff to master the existing applications.

## Business knowledge

A basic premise is that all software maintenance activities require an understanding of the application under maintenance, in terms of the application’s design and the rationale behind it (Martin and Osborne, 1984; Georges, 1992). Haziza et al. (1992) and Calow (1991) identi® ed the maintainer’s knowledge of the maintained system and its environment as being a major organizational issue. Dekleva (1992b) identi® ed adaptation to the rapidly changing business environment and understanding and responding to business needs as being two of the major problems in software maintenance. Layzell and Macaulay (1994) stated that staff would integrate more easily into the software maintenance function if they could have a wider vision of the business before dealing with the software maintenance problems, which typically related to the minutiae of the business. Boldyreff et al. (1994) commented that maintainers need a better understanding of the vocabulary of the domain, of the nature and purpose of the objects handled by the system and also a better knowledge of how the maintained system is used within its operational environment. A limited survey by Layzell and Macauley (1990) of ® ve UK organizations concluded that there is an essential need for successful maintenance to use and transfer knowledge about the business domain.

Most information systems methodologies appear not to concern themselves with providing techniques for developers or maintainers with which to assimilate business knowledge (Fitzgerald, 1994). One notable exception, however, is Information Engineering (1988), which takes a very corporate view of information systems and includes such techniques as business area analysis, which aims to aid IT staff in their understanding of the nature of the business that the information system is intended to support.

## The transfer of knowledge

For systems undergoing the transition from development to maintenance, one management strategy to ensure that a member of staff who is knowledgeable regarding the system is available for maintenance projects is to have a member of the development team (usually a junior member) accompany the system into support (Lientz and Swanson, 1980; Ball, 1985). This is known as the \`escort’ approach. This ensures some contiguity between development and support and that at least one development person will have to live with the system they have produced.

Pigoski and Cowden (1992) described an alternative approach to the transfer of systems knowledge from development to maintenance, whereby software maintenance staff were seconded to the development team for weeks at a time. During this period the maintainers became familiar with the code and attempted to assist the developers by ® xing low priority problems. The developers still retained responsibility for the system as it had not yet been transferred to maintenance. The seconded maintenance staff were heavily involved in the ® nal stages of testing in order to prepare them further for the system’ s handover.

## Research method

Researchers have criticized the lack of empirical research on information systems in real organizational contexts (Jenkins et al. 1984; Hamilton and Ives, 1992). More research is needed into the actual practice of information systems development and maintenance in organizations. This is justi® able on the basis that practice has often preceded theory in the ® eld. Programming style, compiler writing and user interface design are all areas where practice led theory (Glass, 1991).

This paper is partly based on a research project into business systems development and maintenance practice in 31 UK IT departments. This involved preparing case studies of variable depths based on organizations involved in manufacturing, commerce and government, mainly in the north west of England. Table 1 gives details of the types of organizations studied. The research is continuing using further case studies.

The size of the IT departments chosen ranged from two IT staff to over 300 IT staff. The case studies were built up using standard techniques such as interviewing, document collection and program code inspection. Where possible, several staff were interviewed in each of the organizations researched. The interviews included questions on the skills and knowledge deemed necessary for software maintenance projects, how such knowledge and skills were used in actual practice and how their organization sought to manage and improve such knowledge and skills. Appendix 1 shows the questions applied in the case studies. It should be noted that since this research was very much exploratory in nature, the questions asked were correspondingly of an open nature. The IT staff interviewed were often from different levels and occupations within the IT departments and included, for instance, a programmer, an analyst programmer, a systems analyst, an auditor and a project manager. Of course the job titles and terminology varied between the organizations studied. The limitations of time and resources meant that the results elicited from the organizations were mainly qualitative.

The authors believe that case studies based on personal contact are particularly appropriate for investigating IT practice. It is a holistic approach and helps overcome the problems of terminology and veri® cation which bedevil the use of questionnaires. A primary weakness is the time-consuming nature of a case-study investigation for the researchers. In addition, it is disruptive for the organization studied and this makes it dif® cult to obtain the necessary cooperation. Another outcome is the variable level of access permitted to the development and maintenance process. The case-study approach has been fully justi® ed by Gummesson (1991) and Benbasat et al. (1987).

## Research ® ndings

Previous research has indicated that maintenance staff require knowledge regarding the IT systems and business activities they support and that there is a need to manage such knowledge (Layzell and Macaulay, 1994). However, previous research has not investigated the nature of such knowledge, nor how such knowledge is actually used in practice, nor ways in which such knowledge could be managed. These areas were the target of the research exercise reported in this paper.

## Classi® cation of the knowledge required for software maintenance

When the practitioners were interviewed regarding how they would classify the skills and knowledge required for maintenance, the majority of those interviewed across the 31 organizations researched identi® ed two overlapping knowledge sets of physical system knowledge and business knowledge, although different terminologies were of course encountered in the different organizations studied. The physical (IT) system knowledge primarily concerns the software and data items together with their structure.

Other aspects such as the hardware or pre-printed stationery such as invoices were sometimes signi® cant. The business knowledge was a mixture of relevant general theory, e.g. double entry bookkeeping for accounts systems and a knowledge of local circumstances, e.g. accounts of® ce procedures and the jobs of individual user staff.

One example came from research into an insurance policy system within a large insurance company. The practitioners interviewed indicated that without an understanding of the calling chain of the programs within the insurance policy system they maintained and a knowledge of how speci® c records were used within the programs, systems amendments would be dif® cult. In addition, without at least a basic understanding of insurance business terminology, even discussing maintenance requests with users was dif® cult, e.g. what was the difference between earned and unearned insurance policy premiums?

All of the practitioners in the 31 organizations researched that were interviewed stated that the staff working on IT systems maintenance required a detailed knowledge of the physical system they are to maintain. Practitioners in all but eight of the 31 organizations researched indicated that maintenance staff should also have some degree of knowledge concerning the business that the IT systems supported. The eight organizations that did not recognize the need for all the maintainers to have business knowledge operated separate pools of programmers that were distinct from the analyst and designer roles. In these organizations only the analysts and designers were deemed to require business knowledge. The eight organizations that operated separate analyst and programmer roles are shown in Table 1. In general, these were organizations with medium-sized IT departments (i.e. between ten and 50 IT staff).

In the 23 organizations where both physical system and business knowledge were required, the practitioners interviewed indicated that an important overlap between these two knowledge sets was how the physical system was used in actual business practice. This overlap we shall term business system knowledge. Figure 2 shows the relationship between physical system knowledge and business knowledge.

## The use of physical system and business knowledge for software maintenance

The practitioners interviewed were questioned with regard to how physical system and business knowledge were used for software maintenance projects. Overall this research indicated that practitioners in 23 of the organizations studied considered both physical system and business knowledge were necessary for effective system maintenance, with the other eight organizations concentrating more on physical system knowledge.

Physical system and business knowledge were stated by the majority of the practitioners interviewed as being useful for various activities in the life cycle of a given maintenance project. For example, physical system knowledge made the necessary changes to the systems design and coding required easier to identify. Business knowledge made the generation of test plans and test cases for a project easier to de® ne. However, there were found to be particular activities in maintenance projects where physical system and business knowledge appeared to be of greatest use amongst the organizations researched.

The researchers examined a number of software maintenance projects where problems occurred which were a result of the maintainers involved having insuf-® cient physical system knowledge. The problems were perceived as being due to a lack of understanding of how the actual system code worked and how the information system operated within the wider organizational context.

## Knowledge for software maintenance

A lack of physical system knowledge gave rise to the dif® culty of performing impact analysis for a given maintenance project. In one ® nancial services organization researched, impact analysis had been identi® ed by the IT manager as the major problem area for IT systems maintenance projects. In this particular organization, the analyst programmers interviewed stated that the only \`reliable’ method of performing impact analysis was to consult the relevant application \`gurus’, who had suf® cient knowledge of the systems to be able to guide the maintenance staff towards the areas that from experience were the most likely to be affected by a given maintenance project.

Impact analysis should not be underestimated as an activity. An incomplete impact analysis can lead to serious problems when implementation occurs, as unforeseen \`knock-on’ or \`ripple’ effects may corrupt other parts of the system or even other systems. Impact analysis for IT maintenance projects was stated as being a problem for ten of the 31 organizations researched. In general these ten organizations supported the larger and more complex IT systems of the 31 organizations studied. Table 1 shows those organizations that reported problems with impact analysis.

A lack of business knowledge gave rise to requirements elicitation problems in software maintenance projects. Understanding business terminology was quoted as being a dif® culty in 13 of the 31 organizations researched. Unless the maintainers were suf® - ciently knowledgeable as to how the information system was used within the wider organizational context, then it was dif® cult for them to cater effectively for the requirements of a given maintenance project. Table 1 indicates the organizations quoting understanding business terminology as a problem.

One example of such a problem was witnessed by the researchers in an insurance company. A software maintenance project failed because the maintainer involved, an analyst programmer, was unaware that a simple change to an insurance agent ® eld on a certain data input screen should have been applied to a number of other data input screens. This failure was partly due to the maintainer not being aware of how these screens were used in actual business practice. In addition, the omission was not detected in the impact analysis because the datamanager and library source code scans had failed to identify these ® elds on the other screens due to the weakness in the programming standards with regard to ® eld names.

It should be noted that it is not necessarily just IT staff who have a lack of business knowledge (Arfa et al. 1991). This research indicated that for companies having a high turnover of user staff, the business knowledge of the user community may signi® cantly diminish. For example, in the eight ® nancial service organizations researched, all of which sold insurance policies, the IT practitioners interviewed indicated that few of the user staff were aware of how the policy premiums were calculated as the IT systems performed this activity for them. In a case study reported by Abran and Nguyenkim (1993) in a Canadian ® nancial institution, the users had requested details of computerized algorithms based on either the system’s documentation or on the program code. They could not obtain this through their own records. In other words the users did not know the details of their own \`business logic’.

The two types of knowledge required for effective computer systems’ maintenance were found to be complementary. The majority of the practitioners interviewed across the organizations studied indicated that business knowledge helped the maintainer understand why a physical IT system performs its numerous functions and physical system knowledge helped the maintainer to understand the business data and the detailed business processes undertaken.

## Managing physical system and business knowledge

With regard to both physical system and business knowledge, there is a need to ensure that such knowledge is properly managed and conveyed to both maintainers and users. During the course of this research, it was found that in the majority of the organizations studied, the physical system knowledge was frequently accessed via the experts on the system. Paper or electronic media, where they were adequate, were mainly used as an aide-m‚ moire for detail, e.g. lists of valid user codes for a numeric sales area ® eld. Practitioners in virtually all of the organizations researched stated that to understand how the systems worked purely from the available documentation would be almost impossible. In a survey of American organizations, Dekleva (1992a) also found that approximately three-quarters of the organizations depended on speci® c individuals because no-one else could maintain particular systems.

In the majority of the organizations studied the acquisition of relevant maintenance knowledge was therefore more a case of using the physical system experts as consultants. In six of the organizations studied (which all had very large complex systems to support) there were a handful of physical system experts, the majority of whose time was spent purely providing consultancy for the maintenance staff. The IT management in these organizations recognized that without the assistance of these experts, software maintenance projects would be extremely dif® cult and the costs and time scales would greatly increase. These six organizations are indicated in Table 1. Moore (1988) also noted the existence of systems that had to be maintained by \`certain’ people because no-one else understood the logic they contained.

During the course of this research, the researchers identi® ed a number of techniques that could be used in an attempt to improve the knowledge required for maintenance by IT staff. Some of the techniques below can be regarded as working hypotheses for improvement based on our research, which is an initial survey and reports of craft experience. Most of these techniques were in occasional use and no organization used them all. If they had been used these techniques were considered reasonably effective. If not used, they were usually considered a useful idea for future work. Some techniques were used for improving business knowledge and some for improving physical systems knowledge. Some were applied to both.

## Maintenance documentation

The documentation situation was dif® cult to quantify and in the main qualitative descriptions of the state of the documentation was all that could be obtained. The quantity and quality of the documentation varied by organization and within an organization by system and even by software module. In some cases, virtually no documentation was available and the staff worked almost entirely from the source code. In the majority of the 31 cases the systems documentation researched was declared to be out-of-date. As an example, in a large ® nancial services organization studied, a database analyst con® rmed that the data dictionary in use in the IT department was less than half populated, as the IT staff did not bother to update it properly during maintenance projects.

It was stated by approximately half of the practitioners interviewed that the documentation work had, in their experience, been reduced or eliminated to boost the short-term productivity, although this varied within individual organizations. Examination suggested that most systems documentation is more development oriented than maintenance oriented. The production of maintenance-oriented documentation was occasionally found, but was incomplete. Maintenance-oriented documentation can, for instance, specify known \`hot spots’ which are frequently amended. Skimpy documentation can be tolerated in the infrequently amended parts of a system. Another example of maintenance-oriented documentation is the provision of written guidance on recurring similar maintenance tasks.

Documentation can cover the business aspects as well as the physical system design. However, during the research, business-oriented documentation was seen in only nine of the organizations studied. Table 1 shows those organizations that used businessoriented documentation. Most documentation, when it existed, concerned the physical system.

Boosting the amount of good quality business and physical documentation seems like a sensible idea. It could ease maintenance problems, particularly if specif ically tailored for maintenance tasks. All the organizations accepted the principle to varying degrees, but worried about the costs of creating documentation. In addition, creating extensive maintenance documentation was sometimes resisted on the grounds that it would provide little bene® t to the current generation of maintenance staff who already had adequate and business physical knowledge.

## CASE tools

Computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools speci® cally aimed at the maintenance environment such as MAP (Warren, 1982) or those from Viasoft can in principle help. For example, CASE tools can develop physical system knowledge, e.g. by making the systems structure, control ¯ ow and data usage more visible to the maintainer. However, reservations about CASE tools were expressed in all the organizations. Only six of the organizations studied used any form of CASE tool for software maintenance and then only in a limited way, as shown in Table 1.

## Transferring knowledge from experienced staff

This research found that maintenance staff (analysts and programmers) often needed to consult \`experts within the IT or business departments, usually on an ad hoc basis.

More formal methods of transferring general pknowledge about business and IT systems can be used. These are listed below with comments on actual practice.

1 Presentations, e.g. a software designer explaining the physical design of a large IT banking system. Surprisingly this was only used for proposing future projects and not explaining existing systems.

2 Discussions, e.g. a payroll manager discussing how a computer payroll system is used within his or her of® ce. This approach was rarely used in any of the 31 organizations studied.

3 Tours, e.g. with a large order processing system, programmers can be given a tour of the sales of® ce and warehouse to obtain an overview of the whole business system. Business tours were provided for new IT staff in eight of the 31 organizations studied. We did not meet any recent examples of tours being given to established staff.

Presentations and discussions can also be used for speci® c maintenance projects, although this approach was rarely used by any of the organizations studied.

## Knowledge for software maintenance

## Secondment to user departments

A systems analyst can be seconded to a business department, perhaps for a few days. During this experience the analyst can absorb many practical details by actually performing the same tasks as the IT users. This provides a background in business operations such as sales accounting. It should improve the understanding of maintenance requests from IT users. This approach can also uncover both underlying problems and opportunities to enhance systems (Tayntor, 1989). In some cases systems analysts can do normal IT work mostly within the business department rather than the IT department. Only two of the 31 organizations researched had any formalized secondment policy for IT staff, as shown in Table 1. In one medium-sized distribution company researched, approximately onequarter of the systems analysts employed by the organization were permanently resident in the business user department.

## Expert systems

For very large and complex systems with a long life expectancy it could in theory be economically viable to construct expert systems to contain the systems knowledge of the given systems (Cousin and Collofello, 1992). Layzell and Macaulay (1994) concluded that such systems would be particularly useful in organizations with a rapid staff turnover or where there is an inability to attract staff. Such expert systems could contain the rules and heuristics that are in present practice contained in the minds of business and physical system experts, as well as what is contained in the documentation. The Maintenance Assistance Capability for Software (MACS) tool set (Georges, 1992) is one example of such an expert system. However, none of the 31 organizations studied had such expert systems and only one of the organizations, a large ® nancial services company, expressed any interest in such expert systems.

## Business documentation

The collection of internal business documentation, e.g. company brochures or procedures manuals, is a standard systems analysis technique. External documentation, e.g. legal regulations, can also be important. An example for payroll IT systems is CA29 (the manual for employers on statutory maternity pay published by DSS Contributions Agency). All the organizations studied seemed to be poor at utilizing business documentation for software maintenance projects on a regular basis. Systems analysts in effect seemed to rely on user staff to identify and translate the business literature into maintenance requirements.

## Business quali® cations

This involves IT staff in obtaining business quali® cations in areas relevant to their employer. This approach to developing business knowledge was encouraged in only three of the organizations studied and all were in the ® nancial services sector. The few IT staff involved were encouraged to obtain ® nancially oriented quali-® cations offered by professional bodies (see Moynihan (1993) for a further discussion of this). Chapin (1987) suggested that maintainers should learn some of the theory and practice associated with the area of the organization that the software serves, but had not investigated mechanisms for achieving this. Less demanding approaches than obtaining professional quali® cations can be used, e.g. attending 1-day business seminars a couple of times per year.

## Recruiting experienced staff

Recruiting IT staff with the relevant business and technical experience may appear to be a common personnel selection technique which can help both the development and maintenance of IT systems. For example, recruitment can be restricted to only those programmers with COBOL experience on IBM mainframes in ® nancial services. Indeed many job adverts, e.g. in Computing or Computer Weekly, are more speci® c than this. However, only one ® nancial services company encountered in this research project had a stated recruitment policy that only staff with previous knowledge of the particular application areas related to the vacancy would be employed.

## Conclusions

In this paper we have explained the need for both business and physical system knowledge for systems maintenance projects. Our general conclusions, based on both the research and existing literature, are as follows.

(1) The importance of the maintenance function is ignored or underestimated in many publications, which tend to emphasize development. IT and business practitioners, whilst admitting the importance of maintenance, display some reticence about tackling its problems. This has been noted in the past, but it constitutes a continuing psychological bias against maintenance work.

(2) Business and physical system knowledge are both essential and complementary to the maintenance process.

(3) Improved staff knowledge should contribute towards better systems maintenance. This can be achieved in many ways, some of which are outlined above.

(4) The importance and development of the supporting knowledge required for maintenance is only occasionally recognized and at best receives half-hearted attention.

(5) This paper has concentrated on the role of IT staff. However, a similar analysis could be applied to the role of business staff in maintenance and this is worthy of separate investigation. A holistic approach would be ambitious, but even better. This would consider the role of both business and IT staff in maintaining the whole business system.

(6) This paper has been based in a preliminary but relatively time-consuming investigation into actual system maintenance practice. Further investigation could try to assess more precisely and quantitatively some of the factors involved, e.g. the effect of formal business training on maintenance performance.
