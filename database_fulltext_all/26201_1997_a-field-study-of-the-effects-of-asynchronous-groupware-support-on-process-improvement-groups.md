# A ® eld study of the effects of asynchronous groupware support on process improvement groups

NED KOCK

Department of Computer and Information Science, Temple University, 1805 North Broad Street, Comp B uilding, P. Box 38-24, Philadelphia, PA 19122, USA

ROBERT McQUEEN

Department of Management Systems, University of Waikato, Private Bag 3105, Hamilton, New Zealand

Process improvement (PI) groups have been among the main change instruments of widely adopted and publicized organizational development approaches such as total quality management and business process re-engineering. Asynchronous groupware tools, such as electronic messaging systems, have found widespread use in organizations yet very little ® eld research exists on how PI groups are affected by such tools. We try to ® ll this gap with a ® eld study of the effects of asynchronous groupware support on seven PI groups in two New Zealand organizations. Our study indicates that, while not having perceived negative effects on group effectiveness, asynchronous groupware support was perceived as increasing process adoption, hierarchy suppression, departmental heterogeneity and contribution length and decreasing discussion duration, cost and interaction in PI groups. We argue that, based on these ® ndings, the use of asynchronous groupware tools is likely to be bene® cial in PI projects, particularly where a large number of PI groups proposing incremental process changes is conducted.

## Introduction

The concept of business process has been the basis of several organizational development movements, in particular the total quality management and business process re-engineering movements, whose peaks of worldwide attention occurred respectively in the 1980s and 1990s. In addition to their focus on business processes, these movements also share some other characteristics. One of particular interest in this study is their reliance on small groups, i.e. with three to 15 members, with well-de® ned member roles to generate and coordinate the implementation of process improvement proposals (Deming; 1986; Ishikawa, 1986; Walton, 1991; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Soles, 1994). In this paper we refer to these small groups as process improvement (PI) groups.

The general literature on empirical studies of groupware support for groups is ® lled with examples of effects, at both the group and individual levels, that indicate the potential bene® ts from the use of groupware as a tool for PI groups (Nunamaker et al., 1991; Sproull and Kiesler, 1991; Wilson, 1991; Brothers et al., 1992; Chidambaram and Kautz, 1993; Shef® eld and Gallupe, 1993; Clement, 1994). In spite of this, related empirical research has been limited. There have been some representative examples of research studies where groupware was used to support PI groups, such as Pietro’s (1992) study of quality improvement groups, Dennis et al.’ s (1993) study of one business process reengineering group and Dennis et al.’s (1994) study of business process modelling groups. These studies, however, have focused on synchronous groupware tools, particularly group decision support systems (GDSSs), in experimental or quasi-experimental settings.

The focus on GDSSs in particular is curious since such systems have had very modest commercial success when compared with some instances of asynchronous groupware systems, such as e-mail and are thus less likely to be found in organizations (Grudin, 1994). In consequence, one could quite reasonably argue that research on GDSSs is less likely to lead to ® ndings that are of direct interest to a wide range of organizations than research on asynchronous tools, particularly e-mail, which calls for at least a better balancing of research focus.

This unbalanced focus has been compounded by what appears to be a bias in groupware research in general towards experimental studies (Davison, 1995), perhaps a consequence of the predominance of positivist research approaches in the information systems (IS) ® eld in general (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991) and a lack of consistency in the research frameworks used (Pervan, 1994). The bias towards experimental studies may be one of the causes of the large number of contradictory research ® ndings regarding groupware effects on group process and task attributes pointed out by DeSanctis et al. (1993). While there is no clear indication as to whether this is true, this research bias has almost certainly led to dif® culties in the replication of ® ndings in organizations. We believe that one of the main reasons for these dif® culties is the suppression (or arti® cial inclusion), even in ® eld experiments where little control is applied, of the effects of the variables present (or absent) in actual organizational contexts, which can lead to startling discrepancies in technology impact ® ndings, e.g. Orlikowski’s (1992) study of the in¯ uence of organizational culture on the adoption of groupware technology and the contrast of its ® ndings with those of previous experimental studies on groupware adoption and use (Lillemor and Hjelmquist, 1991; Shef® eld and Gallupe, 1993).

We try to provide a shift of focus in both the groupware technology and research approach used by focusing on the effects of asynchronous groupware support on PI groups and using a research approach which tries to take into account the full richness of organizational interactions and yet exerts no arti® cial control on the environment being studied. The asynchronous groupware tool used was e-mail conferencing (EC) and the main research approach used was action research (Winter, 1989; Checkland, 1991).

In our search for prospective organizations in which to conduct the research, we visited a number of organizations in New Zealand, where we found no organizations using asynchronous groupware to support group-based PI efforts. This virtually eliminated the possibility of using non-interventionist research approaches, such as case or survey research and steered us towards two alternative research approaches: (1) experimental research, which is characterized by arti® - cial control and (2) action research, which is by nature low in arti® cial control (Elden and Levin, 1991; Francis, 1991; Elden and Chisholm, 1993). Given the previous research bias towards experimental research and its likely negative consequences, we naturally opted for the action research approach.

## Research method

Organizational action research studies are characterized by the researcher applying a positive intervention to the client organization, while collecting ® eld data about the organization and the effects of the intervention (Lewin, 1946; Peters and Robinson, 1984; Grif® ths, 1990; Jonsonn, 1991; Karlsen, 1991; Heller, 1993; Ledford and Mohrman, 1993). The client organizations of this study were School (pseudonym), a school of studies of a New Zealand university and MAF Quality Management (MQM), a branch of the Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries of New Zealand, with of® ces spread throughout the country. Staff from 18 different MQM of® ces and two departments in School participated in the research study.

The speci® c action research approach followed is detailed in Kock et al. (1995a,b). The approach is centred on the action research cycle proposed by Susman and Evered (1978), which comprises ® ve stages: diagnosing, action planning, action taking, evaluating and specifying learning, as shown in Figure 1.

In the diagnosing stage the researcher and client organization identify and specify an opportunity for improvement in the client organization and match it with the research goals. In the action planning stage the researcher and client organization consider alternative courses of action to attain the improvement identi® ed and devise a plan to implement one of these alternative courses of action. In the action taking stage the researcher and client organization implement the plan devised. In the evaluating stage the researcher and client organization assess the outcomes of the plan implementation. Finally, in the specifying learning stage, the researcher structures the information generated in the previous stage as general ® ndings.

The study consisted of facilitating and studying seven EC-supported PI groups in the client organizations. In this paper these groups are referred to as G0 (at School) and G1± G6 (at MQM) and their main features are summarized in Table 1. Table 1 shows the number of members in each group, the number of electronic postings contributed by group members, the duration of the group in days, the number of departments and of® ces (or physical sites) represented in each group and the group’s scope of change. The study of G0 at School was conducted as a ® rst iteration in the action research cycle, after which the researcher moved into the second iteration with the study of G1± G6 at MQM. Such a move across the iterations provided the background for an interorganizational and longitudinal comparison of the research ® ndings and as a consequence, for a reduction in the impact of potential threats to the internal (and, likely, external) validity of the research ® ndings.

Departments are de® ned here as organizational areas comprising interrelated functions performed by staff who share the knowledge to perform interrelated activities and may comprise staff based at different locations. The interrelation between functions referred to was de® ned by the common knowledge required from staff to perform the functions, rather than by the functions being part of the same business process. For example, even though the interrelated functions \`meat handling inspector’ and \`meat processing consultant’ require common bodies of knowledge to be performed, only the latter is involved in the process of providing quality systems consulting.

This departmental structure mirrors the one used by MQM to classify staff according to the service supplied and the basis for MQM’s management structure, where departments are part of a core business or a support division. For example, a dairy and food training manager in an of® ce on the bottom tip of New Zealand’s South Island ultimately reports to the national dairy/food manager based in Hamilton, in the North Island.

This research study involved two main core businesses at MQM: (1) food, which comprises departments supplying dairy- and meat-related services and (2) plant, which comprises departments supplying plant-related services. The research also involved two support divisions at MQM: (1) information technology regional support, which comprises departments supplying hardware and software installation and support services, and (2) communications support, which comprises departments supplying public relations, media liaison, document design and business communication consulting services. School was considered as a business itself, from which one academic department and one support department participated in the study. Of® ces are de® ned here as physical aggregations of staff in the same building, typically in different cities.

The PI groups consisted of members from one or more departments, located in one or more of® ces. The scope of change of a PI group was said to be departmental when processes comprised by only one department were redesigned, even though the changes might have affected processes in different of® ces. The scope of change was called interdepartmental when the processes redesigned involved more than one department, but not a whole business (or support division). Finally, the scope of change was said to be business when the processes redesigned involved the whole business (or support division), even though the PI group involved members of only one department (as in G3).

The groups followed a group methodology called MetaProi (short for meta-process for process improvement), which is discussed in detail in Kock (1995a). MetaProi was devised by a researcher (the ® rst author of this paper), based on normative frameworks for PI (Harrington, 1991; Davenport, 1993; Guha et al., 1993; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Wastell et al., 1994; Kock, 1995b), case studies (Caron, et al., 1994; Dingle, 1994) and a previous action research project in which the general effects of EC support on PI groups were examined (Kock and McQueen, 1995).

All of the PI groups had a leader, a facilitator and ordinary members. The facilitator in all of the groups was the researcher. All of the group leaders and ordinary members were staff of the client organization. The researcher provided technical and methodological support for the PI groups by interacting mostly with group leaders. Only the structure of the PI discussions was in¯ uenced by the researcher, who helped the group leaders to build messages where the individual contributions of the group members were summarized and group stages were initiated and completed. No in¯ uence on the content of the group discussions was exerted by the researcher.

A typical PI group was formed by a self-appointed leader after a few face to face meetings with the researcher. In these meetings, both MetaProi and its integrated use with the EC tool were discussed. The initial meeting between the researcher and prospective group leader occurred typically on the researcher’s initiative; group leaders were selected for this initial contact based on suggestions from other members of the organization such as top managers (the ® rst to be contacted in the two client organizations of this study) and members of previous PI groups. In some cases group leaders voluntarily approached the researcher with the intention of becoming a PI group leader. The group leaders typically de® ned a general problem to be discussed by their PI groups and invited other group members at their discretion. The general problem was de® ned on the expectation that it would lead to the identi® cation, during the PI group discussion, of one or a few business processes to be analysed and redesigned by the group.

The computer tool used to support the PI groups was a simple EC system, implemented using Groupwise functions (Groupwise is an asynchronous group support system commercialized by the Novell Corporation). Group e-mail distribution lists comprising the PI group members were set up for each of the PI groups using Groupwise rules (simple programming structures that enable users to associate actions, such as distributing a message to a list of users, to certain events such as an incoming message with a speci® c subject). The group distribution lists used in the research were similar to Internet list server’s electronic mail distribution lists, e.g. BPR-L and ISWorld (BPR-L, administered by the University of Delft in The Netherlands, is a forum for the discussion of issues related to business process redesign, while ISWorld, administered by the University College Dublin in Ireland, is a forum for the discussion of issues related to information systems research). The EC system allowed the PI group members to exchange one to one electronic messages and replies to the whole group. Spreadsheets, ¯ ow charts, presentations and graphs could be attached as ® les to electronic messages and read by the recipients. Attachments could be read by clicking on icons on the computer screen.

## Research framework and data sources

A set of eight group-related research variables provided a basic framework for the data collection ± process adoption, hierarchy suppression, departmental heterogeneity, duration, cost, effectiveness, interaction and mean contribution length. Research variables were selected based on the research questions associated with the unit of the analysis PI group, which in turn were derived from a literature review of small group theory (e.g. Shaw, 1981; McGrath, 1984) and empirical research on the group effects of groupware support for work groups (e.g. Ellis et al., 1991; Nunamaker et al., 1991; Markus, 1992; Pietro, 1992; Alavi, 1993, 1994; DeSanctis et al., 1993; Shef® eld and Gallupe, 1993; Gallupe et al., 1994). Each of the research variables is de® ned and discussed separately later in this paper, together with related EC support effects.

The data were collected through participant observations, interviews, questionnaires and the compilation of transcripts of electronic messages from PI group members. Complementary data about the organization and its main processes were obtained from internal archival records, such as service records and memoranda. Another source of complementary data was documents published by the organization, such as advertising material and the organization’ s internal newsletter.

Participant observation notes were generated based on the facilitation of the PI groups and formal and informal interactions with staff. These notes were the result of perceptions and re¯ ections about the researcher’s interactions with the client organization and were intentionally framework free, that is they were not necessarily related to the set of research variables initially de® ned. This approach was adopted to provide a balance between framework-free sources of data (e.g. transcripts of electronic discussions and participant observation notes) and framework-bound ones (e.g. structured interviews), which, in turn, was expected to prevent the researcher from leaving out relevant effects unrelated to the initial set of research variables.

The interviews were of two types, structured and unstructured. Both types of interviews were based on open-ended questions (i.e. not restricted to a set of pre-de® ned alternatives). The structured interviews were based on a pre-de® ned list of questions and were taped and later transcribed. Three to six unstructured interviews per group were performed with group leaders and staff. These interviews lasted from 1 to 3 hours and were not taped. The data obtained through unstructured interviews were merged with the participant observation notes for later analysis.

The structured interview respondents were PI group members who were based in the city of Hamilton (where the researcher was based). Questionnaires with open-ended questions were sent via e-mail to PI group members based in of® ces outside Hamilton and followed up by phone. The questions in the questionnaires were the same as those used in the structured interviews. PI group members were interviewed or received questionnaires within 2 weeks of the completion of their groups. Transcripts of 11 structured interviews and seven questionnaires were obtained.

The participant observation notes were indexed by date and topic. These topics typically described a person or speci® c PI group and an event observed (e.g. John ignored other member’s requests or Trish’s group has had a low degree of interaction so far). The transcripts of the interviews were indexed by the respondents’ name. The transcripts of electronic messages from PI group members were indexed by PI group and data. Overall, approximately 135 000 words of descriptive qualitative data were generated. Most of it was in the form of interview and questionnaire transcripts (59% of the total), followed by participant observation notes (23%) and transcripts of electronic group discussions (18%).

## Process improvement methodology

The process improvement methodology followed by the PI groups was comprised of three main stages, namely process de® nition, analysis and redesign (see Kock (1995a) for a detailed description of this methodology). A description of the above stages for each of the PI groups is provided in Table 2. In the ® rst column, the table shows the proportions of time calculated as spent by group members on interactions through the EC system (EC) and orally, totalled based on the interaction times reported by the members in the structured interviews ± the members were asked how much time they had spent on EC-mediated and oral interactions with other group members. Although some groups conducted a few face to face group meetings, almost all oral interactions were one to one conversations (face to face or over the phone).

In the de® nition stage the group leader posted an electronic message to the group discussion containing a description of a general problem, a list of narrowly de® ned problems that were believed to be components of the general problem, a list of processes that were believed to be at the source of the problems and a request for comments from group members re® ning this information and indicating which process (or few processes) the group should target. This ® rst message was written by the group leader with the support of the facilitator.

In the analysis stage the group leader and the facilitator summarized the group discussion following the ® rst message in a new electronic message posted to the group discussion. This message typically contained acknowledgements to individual contributions, a ¯ owchart or textual description of the process that the group decided to target, performance-related information about this process (e.g. the number and types of users complaints, leading time and overall cost), a list of proposed changes to the process with an indication of those who would be responsible for these changes and a request for comments from group members re® ning the information provided and the proposed changes.

In the redesign stage the group leader and the facilitator summarized the member contributions in the analysis stage in a ® nal electronic message posted to the group discussion. This message typically contained acknowledgements to individual contributions and a brief plan with activities to be performed to implement the process changes agreed on by the group members. This brief plan included a description of the activities, deadlines for their implementation and a list of those who were responsible for carrying out the activities.

## A brief description of the groups

G0 targeted two processes related to the teaching of a university course on business computing. The course was one of the most successful optional courses at School, with approximately 100 enrolments per semester. The need for process improvement was motivated by a disproportionate number of software and hardware complaints from students regarding the computer laboratories used during the course. All of the process redesign proposals generated by the group were implemented within a month of the group completion. Student perceptions surveys conducted in the middle and at the end of every semester indicated a \`dramatic improvement’ (in the words of one of the lecturers who routinely conducted the surveys) in the perceived quality of the course.

G1 targeted the process of providing software applications support to MQM’s internal users. Three main problems triggered this group: (1) a perceived slow turnaround of software repair jobs, (2) a high number of complaints from users about not being noti® ed about the \`status’ of their jobs and (3) the lack of time to carry out local area network (LAN) software maintenance. Six out of the eight process changes proposed by the group were implemented within 6 months of the group completion. A qualitative evaluation of the changes conducted based on departmental staff perceptions indicated a slight increase in the perceived quality and ef® ciency of the redesigned process.

G2 targeted the process of editing MQM’s internal newsletter. Three main problems motivated this group: (1) the lack of internal staff contributions to the newsletter, (2) several complaints about distortions in the articles provided by staff when they were ® nally published in the newsletter and (3) the common delay in the publication of contributed articles. All of the process changes proposed by the group were implemented within 3 months of the group completion. The problems that originated the group were reported by the editor as having been \`completely eliminated’ as a result of the process changes.

G3 targeted the process of MQM’s media liaison during pest and disease outbreaks threatening New Zealand’s agriculture, which typically occurred no more than three times a year. This group arose from the need to improve the coordination of the several teams reporting outbreaks to the media (TV and radio stations and newspapers) in different parts of the country and, as a consequence, the need to avoid the release of misleading and inconsistent information about outbreak status. Four out of the seven proposed process changes were implemented within 4 months of the group completion. An increase in both the quality and ef® ciency of the process was reported by members of MQM’s media liaison team as a result of the process changes when the team was brought into action during a fruit-¯ y outbreak approximately 7 months after the PI group was completed.

G4 targeted the process of providing quality systems consulting for MQM’s external customers. This group arose from the need to adopt a new quality systems standard as the basis for one of the consulting products supplied by MQM and, as a consequence, the need to redesign some other consulting products, particularly in the food industry. After nearly a month of heated debate no agreement was achieved. No process changes were proposed and the group discussion was seen as a failure by the group leader. A month later the same set of issues were discussed in a face to face group, but again no agreement was achieved.

G5 targeted the process of providing information technology (IT) support for MQM’s internal users. This group was an extension of the discussion in G1 to deal with the more general problems faced by the IT support team, but involved fewer IT support staff in the group ± most of its members were process customers. Four out of the ® ve process changes proposed by the group were implemented within 6 months of the group completion. A survey of customer perceptions indicated an increase in perceived process quality, while the staff ’ s perceptions suggested an increase in the ef® - ciency of the process. During the implementation of the process changes the leader of the IT team was promoted to the position of regional IT support manager.

G6 targeted the process of coordinating staff training and development at MQM. Assuming that most group members previously agreed that the key point to improving the target process was to develop a computer system, the leader of this group tried to steer the group members into specifying the requirements of the computer system. The group members reacted to this attempt by posting some messages which were perceived by the group leader as \`trivial’ and which suggested a lack of group members’ enthusiasm about the group discussion. After several phone and face to face contacts with group members, where they were asked to contribute more and better structured information, the leader grew increasingly frustrated and ® nally decided to use the group discussion as a forum for a permanent exchange of information about the software requirements rather than process improvement. The goal of this forum was to complement face to face meetings. This group was seen as a failure by the group leader, from a process improvement perspective.

## The effect of asynchronous groupware on PI groups

A quantitative analysis of the perceptions of the PI group members gathered in the structured interviews allows us to form a basic picture of the EC support effects on the previously identi® ed group-related variables. The frequencies of the responses were interpreted based on the assumption that if most respondents (i.e. more than 50% of the respondents) had the same perception, this would be an indication of a possible causal link (the only exception relates to group effectiveness, which is discussed regardless of the majority of the respondent perceptions). Whenever this happened we tried to gather more quantitative or qualitative evidence to support the existence of that link and also looked for discon® rmatory perceptions and related evidence. In addition, data on the group interactions and contribution lengths of the EC-supported discussions were collected and analysed and are presented at the end of this section. Consistently with this approach, the analysis of the response frequencies pointed to six main effects of EC support on PI groups, as shown in Table 3.

The respondents consistently indicated that EC support increased group process adoption, hierarchy suppression and departmental heterogeneity. Moreover, no respondent indicated a perceived decrease in these three variables as a result of EC support. The respondents also consistently indicated a decrease in group duration and cost as a result of the EC support. In the case of these two variables, however, there were a few dissenting responses. Finally, although there was no majority consensus about the effect of EC support on group effectiveness, perceived as directly linked to the quality of the redesign proposals generated by the group, most respondents perceived no negative effects on this variable. These perceptions are discussed individually next, followed by a discussion of the evidence regarding the effects on the variables group duration and mean contribution length. The following discussion draws on other sources of research data to supplement the perceptions summarized in Table 3.

## Group process adoption and hierarchy suppression

The perceived increase in group process adoption fostered by EC support was strongly supported by analysis of the interview transcripts and participant observations during the face to face facilitation sessions with the group leaders. For example, almost all of the replies from the members referred to messages posted by the group leaders (this can also be observed in Table 2), a behaviour not commonly seen in the face to face PI groups we have facilitated in previous studies (Kock and McQueen, 1995; Kock et al., 1996). In those face to face groups the leaders typically had to put a lot of effort into preventing the group discussions from veering away from the main subject into new threads brought up by group members. In contrast, in the EC-supported PI groups in our study, this happened only once, in G4 and most group leaders indicated that it appeared to be much easier to lead EC-supported than face to face PI group discussions. Moreover, two group leaders admitted to having severely limited leadership skills and, hence, not being able to lead a face to face group discussion with the members that were in their EC-supported PI groups. In one of these cases the group leader was one of the most junior staff in his group and his national business manager was a member of this group. In the other case the group leader was the most junior staff in his group. These cases also provide support for the perception that EC support decreases the in¯ uence that status differences in the organizational hierarchy have on how individual contributions are perceived by members, an effect referred to in this study as group hierarchy suppression.

It is important to explain the reason why we use the term EC support in this study, rather than EC mediation. An analysis of EC mediation effects would typically require us to run some PI groups where interaction was through the EC system only and some PI groups where interaction was exclusively face to face (control groups). However, we opted to offer EC support optionally to the PI groups.

Requesting PI groups to interact through the EC support system only would allow us to make assumptions about EC mediation effects, but would at the same time apply arti® cial control on the PI groups being studied and probably bias the results, i.e. the PI groups would tend to see their work as part of a ® eld experiment, which could reduce their willingness to contribute to the group discussion and their commitment to the process redesign proposals generated by the group. Since this was an action research study and therefore one of our goals was to actually improve the client organizations through the PI groups rather than merely use those organizations as laboratories, we offered both the group methodology MetaProi and the EC system as alternative tools to be used by the PI groups, which would, in our view, likely increase those groups’ ef® ciency and effectiveness. We made it clear that we had no objection to a group running the whole group discussion or most of it through face to face meetings, which was what group G3 did. Even so, all of the PI groups except G3 ran all their group discussions through the EC system, using face to face phone and ordinary e-mail messages (as opposed to group postings) only for one to one conversations.

## Group departmental heterogeneity

Perhaps the preference of the PI groups for the EC system over face to face meetings as the main communication medium was motivated by the higher departmental heterogeneity found in the groups, which may also suggest a higher group site heterogeneity, as organizations often group together staff from the same department and separate departments by walls and distance (e.g. departmental staff are grouped on different ¯ oors or in different buildings). We found support for this hypothesis when we looked at the main reasons given by the respondents for their perception that EC support increases departmental heterogeneity in PI groups. These reasons were (1) that EC support enables group discussion to be carried out without affecting individual timetables (eight respondents) and (2) that EC support reduces the in¯ uence of distance (® ve respondents).

The ® rst reason can be interpreted as a reduction, caused by EC support, in the perceived disruption that PI group discussions can have on members’ routine (or functional) activities. However, the second reason can only be explained by an underlying correlation in the organizations studied between departmental heterogeneity and site heterogeneity. This underlying correlation was con® rmed by a correlation test between the numbers of departments and sites involved in the PI groups shown in Table 1. The Pearson correlation coef® cient obtained from this test was 0.70 (P , 0.05, one-tailed test), which indicates a strong correlation between the number of departments and the number of sites in the PI groups studied.

In addition, only one group, G1, involved staff from only one department and who were located in the same site. While the members of this group found EC support useful, particularly because some of them worked on different shifts and the majority spent most of their work time outside the of® ce, other prospective leaders of similar groups did not show much interest in using the approach proposed by the researcher. A comment of one of these prospective leaders illustrates this lack of interest: \`Why would we interact through (the EC system) when we can talk to each other at any time? . . . there is no use for this system here . . .’ .

These pieces of evidence suggest that the usefulness of EC support may increase with the number of departments involved in PI groups, even though it may also be useful to departmental PI groups provided that there is some sort of time or distance constraint that prevents group members from meeting face to face (e.g. different shifts).

## Group duration

A high percentage of the structured interview respondents (72%) perceived EC support as decreasing PI group duration. The main reason given by these respondents was a reduction in the group set-up time, which was perceived as the time spent inviting prospective members to participate in the group discussion, choosing and booking a venue for group meetings, negotiating a meeting schedule and reminding members to attend meetings. Obviously, the need for the three last activities would be totally eliminated in ECsupported PI groups where all interactions take place through the EC system.

However, a few respondents (two out of 18, or 11%) were of the opinion that EC support actually increases PI group duration. These respondents were unanimous in the reason given for this ± a perceived increase in the time that each member takes to contribute. In fact, several of the structured and unstructured interview respondents implicitly supported this view by noting that it was much more time-consuming to post a message to the group discussion than make a spoken contribution and that the low control over individual participation in the EC-supported PI groups allowed them to give a low priority to posting messages to group discussions. We also compared the time required to create and post an electronic message with that required to make a spoken contribution and to read a posting in a PI group discussion. This comparison was based on estimates of the spoken word rate in taped interviews (3.33 words per second), the posting creation and typing rate in EC contributions (6.93 words per minute or 0.12 words per second) and the word reading rate for electronic messages (2.89 words per second). We assumed here, based on our previous facilitation of face to face PI groups, that the mean word input rate would be almost the same in taped interviews and face to face PI group meetings. The comparison indicates that it is severely more time consuming (3.33/0.12 = 27.75 times) to contribute through the EC system than it is to contribute in a face to face meeting. On the other hand, reading is only 1.15 times faster than listening.

An analysis of the transcripts of the electronic discussions also shows that there was a considerable lag between the leaders’ messages and replies from the group members. While the minimum lag was slightly less than 1 hour, the maximum lag was 193 hours, that is over 8 days. The mean lag was 73 hours or approximately 3 days. This means that group leaders would typically have to wait 3 days for replies to their main messages before they could proceed any further in the group discussion.

Finally, while the duration of ® ve out of the seven PI groups studied (G0± G2 and G4± G5) ranged from 25 to 33 days, G3 was completed in only 14 days. Since the narrow scope of change of G3 (business according to Table 1) cannot be used as a valid explanation for the fact that this group was completed faster than the others, it seems plausible to say that G3 lasted less than the other groups because its analysis stage had been run through a face to face meeting.

While the evidence presented in this section points to EC support factors that may cause an increase in the duration of PI groups, this evidence also suggests that the combination of EC-supported and face to face group discussions is likely to decrease PI group duration, as the interview respondents’ perceptions indicate. That is, while a purely EC-mediated PI group may not be completed in less time than a PI group in which interaction was purely face to face, the support provided by the EC system appears likely to reduce PI group duration.

## Group cost

A high proportion of the structured interview respondents (78%) were of the opinion that EC support decreases the cost of running PI groups, which involved transportation and communication as well as member participation time costs. The respondents explained this reduction in group costs as resulting from three main factors.

(1) A reduction in the disruption of member functional activities (i.e. the routine activities associated with their functional position in the organization) and, therefore, of the costs generated by this disruption (e.g. quality costs).

(2) A drastic reduction in transportation and communication expenses in groups involving members based in different cities, since PI group members would not have to travel to participate in the discussions nor call their of® ces to keep up to date with local developments.

(3) A considerable reduction in member participation times in the case of ordinary members and a slight reduction in the case of group leaders.

While factor (1) is dif® cult to measure, and factor (2) is too trivial to warrant further analysis, we thought that a simple quanti® cation of factor (3) would lend more weight to our ® ndings. In the structured interviews, the respondents were asked how much time they spent on the group discussion. The time averaged 1.5 hours for ordinary members and 7 hours for group leaders. Some members who declared having participated in face to face PI groups before were asked to estimate the amount of time they would have spent on their PI group discussions, had those discussions been conducted only through face to face meetings. These estimates averaged approximately 20 hours. This suggests that ordinary members’ participation time was reduced for approximately 92% $( 1 - 1 . 5 / 2 0 = 0 . 9 2 5 )$ due to the EC support, whereas the reduction for group leaders was 65% $( 1 { - } 7 / 2 0 = 0 . 6 5 )$

## Group effectiveness

A third of the structured interview respondents (33%) saw EC support as having increased group effectiveness. In addition, although 17% were of the opinion that EC support decreased group effectiveness, another 33% believed that EC support had no effect on this variable, that is 66% of the respondents were of the opinion that EC support had no negative effect on group effectiveness.

All but one of the respondents who thought EC support increased group effectiveness explained this effect by an improvement in the quality of individual contributions fostered by the asynchronous and written communication medium. According to the respondents an asynchronous discussion allowed them enough time to re¯ ect on other members’ contributions, whereas the written mode of communication led them into carefully structuring their ideas and preparing better thought out contributions. One of the respondents, on the other hand, assigned the increase in group effectiveness to a better distribution of contributions fostered by EC support.

Those who saw EC support as neither increasing nor decreasing group effectiveness explained their answer by a balance of positive and negative effects. According to these respondents EC support increased the quality of individual contributions but in turn decreased member participation, which led to a neutral effect on group effectiveness. These perceptions, to a certain extent, were consistent with the perceptions of those respondents who thought that EC support decreased group effectiveness. According to these, the main reason is a decrease in member participation induced by EC being the primary means of communication.

The higher perceived quality of individual contributions is consistent with two primary EC support effects on groups. One of these is an increase in the length of individual contributions, which is discussed in the next section. The other effect, observed during the facilitation sessions with the group leaders and reinforced by the participants’ comments in the unstructured interviews, is the particular care that members take when preparing electronic postings. Several group leaders, for example, read their messages several times before posting them to the group, often clarifying ideas, itemizing main points and using terms perceived as less \`ambiguous’. On a number of occasions the group leaders were, after carefully preparing their contributions, still reluctant to click on the \`send’ button to post their messages to the group, often looking at the researcher and asking \` . . . are you sure (the message) is ready? Maybe I should read through it again a couple more times and post it to the group in a few days . . .’ , to which the researcher sometimes responded by taking over the mouse and clicking on the \`send’ button for them.

On the other hand, the negative EC support effect of reducing member participation and, thus, perceived group interaction was also con® rmed by our analysis of electronic discussion transcripts, as discussed in the next section.

## Group interaction and member contribution length

Group interaction was de® ned as the number of individual contributions per active group member (i.e. a group member who made at least one contribution to the group discussion). Member contribution length was de® ned as the number of words per contribution.

The interaction in face to face meetings can be rapid, e.g. McQueen’s (1991, Chapter 4) ® eld study of business meetings reported a mean duration of 12 seconds for individual contributions. In a group with seven members that met for 5 hours, for example, this rate would account for approximately 214 contributions per member $( 5 \times 6 0 \times 6 0 / 1 2 / 7 = 2 1 4 . 2 9 )$ . Given our previous estimate of 3.33 words per second for spoken contributions in face to face meetings, a typical individual contribution in a face to face meeting would have 40 words $( 3 . 3 3 \times 1 2 = 3 9 . 9 6 )$

In the PI groups studied, however, the EC support was perceived by the members as having led to a considerable decrease in group interaction. Almost invariably we noticed that the group leaders expected to see more interaction in their groups, with some group leaders having the expectation that group interaction was going to be higher through the EC system than in face to face meetings. The frustration of these expectations led a few group leaders to feel affronted and ignored by the other members and in one of the cases (G6) this contributed to the group leader discontinuing the group discussion.

In order to quantify the impact of EC support on group interaction and contribution length, we calculated the number of words of individual postings in the PI group discussions, as well as the number of messages per active member in each of the groups. The smallest posting was three words long and the largest was 784 words long, with a mean of 260 words per posting. In addition, the number of postings per active group member varied from 1 (G6) to 4.2 (G0), with a mean of 2.25 contributions per group member. These estimates, when compared with those for face to face discussions, indicate that while EC support can increase the length of individual contributions, it can also drastically decrease group interaction. We are disregarding here the one to one oral interactions that accounted for 11± 33% of the interactions in six of the groups. Nevertheless, the research results lead us to believe that the more the EC medium is used for communication in PI groups, the more pronounced is the decrease in the group interaction.

## Discussion

The research ® ndings support the general assumption that EC support is more likely to be bene® cial than detrimental to PI groups and warrant the recommendation for organizations to try and use EC and other asynchronous groupware technologies to support PI projects. The research ® ndings are also consistent with previous ® ndings regarding GDSS effects on PI groups and, in fact, with most of the empirical literature on synchronous groupware, which indicates major productivity and slight quality gains from a group task perspective (Dennis et al., 1994; Reinig et al., 1995).

The reasons why these gains are achieved, however, appear to differ considerably for synchronous and asynchronous groupware. While GDSS support effects on group productivity and outcome quality are often associated with an increase in the quantity of ideas generated by the group (Nagasundaram and Bostrom, 1994), our ® ndings suggest other factors related to EC support. The main factors affecting group productivity appear to be an increase in group process adoption and discussion focus, which apparently decreases the losses caused by parallel discussion and a drastic decrease in group set-up costs. In the same vein, the main factor affecting group outcome quality and, thus, effectiveness is an increase in the quality of individual contributions, rather than in the number of contributions as in GDSSsupported groups (in fact, in EC-supported groups the number of individual contributions appears to be drastically reduced). Moreover, EC support appears to allow PI group discussions to be conducted over a longer period of time and more re¯ ection from the part of the members, even though it is perceived as reducing the duration of PI groups when compared with no groupware support at all.

The analysis of the effectiveness of some of the PI groups raises serious questions as to the current focus, as pointed out by Nagasundaram and Bostrom (1994), on the analysis of the number of ideas as a measure of group effectiveness. Even though no direct quality assessments were conducted on the process redesign proposals, the analysis of some groups indicated that group success is not always related to the number of individual contributions or ideas. In G1, for example, the number of individual contributions (and ideas) was considerably lower than in G4. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of G1, which succeed in improving a process, was higher than in G4, which failed.

At this point it is interesting to analyse some of the research ® ndings in the light of relevant theoretical approaches. Of particular interest in this respect is the media adoption theory widely known as media richness theory (also known as information richness theory), because of the implications of some of our ® ndings to the interpretation of the validity of this theory. The media richness theory (Daft and Lengel, 1986) is one of the most in¯ uential theories of media choice in organizations. It is interesting to note that while some of our research ® ndings are inconsistent with predictions based on this theory, some other ® ndings support the theory to some extent. The media richness theory states, for example, that the ® ltering of non-verbal cues and the lack of an immediate feedback characteristic of asynchronous groupware interaction tends to increase the \`equivocality’ (and, in consequence, the ambiguity) in the group discussion. One could quite reasonably predict, based on this hypothesis, that group members would perceive a decrease in the quality of individual postings as a result of increased use of the EC system for interaction. However, what happened was exactly the opposite ± the majority of the structured interview respondents perceived an increase in the quality of individual contributions. The reason for this increase appears to be, as a number of previous studies (Lee, 1994; Markus, 1992, 1994; Orlikowski et al., 1995) and emergent theoretical approaches (DeSanctis et al., 1993) suggest, that users can adapt their use of the technology in ways that allow groups to overcome the limitations believed as being inherent in the speci® c communication medium used.

On the other hand, in groups where the complexity or abstraction of the issues being discussed by the group was perceived as high, the reaction was consistent with the media richness theory. In G4, for example, the high level of abstraction in the discussion was perceived by some members as contributing to the group failure. By the same token, G6 tried to undertake a task, software requirement speci® cation, that was, in the view of the group leader and some members, \`too complex’ to be conducted through the EC system. The members in this group had agreed on neither the technical language to be used in the group discussion nor the structure of the speci® cations ± some used software engineering terms and data structure representations to convey their ideas, while others used speci® c terms of their respective business jargon ± before the discussion was begun. Several members pointed out that the combination of a lack of immediate feedback and higher contribution effort in the EC-mediated portions of the discussion led them to refrain from asking other members for clari® cations about the idiosyncratic terms and language used and eventually put them off the discussion.

We could speculate, based on the discussion above, that the consideration of the adaptive power of social groups to overcome equivocality is a fundamental ingredient in explaining the interaction between asynchronous technology and PI groups, but that, on the other hand, in extreme situations (e.g. very high task complexity) the predictions of the media richness theory can hold. In this sense, theoretical perspectives that take into account this adaptive power, such as the adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis et al., 1993) for example, could be seen as analogous to the Newtonian Theory of Mechanics, which explains physical phenomena reasonably well at low speeds (low group task complexity), but which must give way to Einstein’s Theory of Relativity to explain physical phenomena when the relative speed between physical systems approaches the speed of light (high group task complexity). Both types of theoretical frameworks are ingenious and relevant, but have their scope of application de® ned by a threshold ± a certain speed (task complexity).

## Potential sources of bias

The research ® ndings in this study may have been biased by the high involvement of the researcher with the groups and organizations studied and by the small sample examined of some units of analysis.

Our facilitation of PI groups may have led those groups to behave in an arti® cial way, exactly what we wanted to avoid by using action research. We tried to minimize this source of bias by refraining from in¯ uencing the content of the group discussion and proposing the group methodology and groupware technology as supporting tools for the groups while not trying to impose them on the groups. While the former strategy appeared to be successful, as several group members declared being unaware of the involvement of the researcher until they were asked to be interviewed, the latter was partially unsuccessful to the extent that most of the researcher’s suggestions regarding group process structure were adopted by the groups, e.g. most of the groups made heavy use of both the group methodology (MetaProi) and EC tool proposed by the researcher. This factor, along with the fact that the facilitator role performed by the researcher may not be available in a number of organizations, must be taken into consideration in the assessment of the likelihood of replication of our ® ndings in other organizations.

The small sample of some units of analysis may have distorted some conclusions. It should be noted, however, that the ® ndings based on the comparisons across the groups were based on a smaller sample (n = 7) than those based on the comparisons of the respondent perceptions (n = 18) and electronic messages (n = 88). Moreover, all of the ® ndings were supported by multiple sources of data, notably participation observations, unstructured and structured interviews and electronic discussion transcripts. Nevertheless, we believe that the ® ndings in this study should be seen as descriptive and preliminary.

## Conclusion

This research has addressed the effects of asynchronous groupware support on groups engaged in process improvement activities. From the view of the participants in the study, asynchronous groupware was perceived as having increased group process adoption, hierarchy suppression, departmental heterogeneity and contribution length and having decreased duration and cost. The participant perceptions also suggested no negative effects on group effectiveness. The combination of these effects is seen as a positive improvement in the operation of these groups.

However, asynchronous groupware as a medium for discussion appeared to have been responsible for a decreased interaction (turn sharing in the discussion) below that which might be expected to be typical of similar face to face meetings. This ® nding implies that while asynchronous groupware shows promise as a medium to support process improvement activities, it may not be suited to support process improvement activities in which a high level of interaction is needed in order to, for example, clarify points about a complex process quickly or build commitment towards a risky decision. We believe that the likelihood that these characteristics will be present in improvement groups increases as groups move from gradual and localized towards radical and broad process change proposals. We therefore believe that as this move occurs so does the need for a combination between groupware-mediated and face to face discussions in process improvement groups.
