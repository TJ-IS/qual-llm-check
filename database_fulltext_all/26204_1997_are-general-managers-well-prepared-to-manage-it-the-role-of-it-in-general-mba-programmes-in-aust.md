# Are general managers well prepared to manage IT? The role of IT in general MBA programmes in Australia

CHRIS SAUER, RAJEEV SHARMA AND BARBARA POTTER Fujitsu Centre for Managing Information Technology in Organizations, Australian Graduate School of Management, The University of New South Wales, Sydney NSW 2052, Australia

This paper reports on the results from a survey of the provision of information technology (IT) course units in general MBA programmes in Australia. The results establish a baseline for future studies. They show widespread penetration of IT courses in general MBAs, but the amount and type of content they provide is only suf® cient to give students a partial preparation for the general management task with respect to IT. There is an internal expectation gap between what IT academics view as core and the time and space available in the Master of Business Administration (MBA) curriculum. There is an external expectation gap between the issues currently of concern to business and the topics taught. At present the gap is widest in respect of business managers’ concerns about information infrastructure and organizational learning. The evidence suggests that neither expectation gap will decline over the next 5 years and may increase. We conclude that there is a need for increased provision in the curriculum. Recommendations are made for two core courses, with more attention to be given to topics of current concern to business managers.

## Introduction

Management education is widely recognized as an important input to national economic well-being (Karpin, 1995). To continue to meet national needs it is necessary that educational programmes prepare managers for the challenges of today’ s and tomorrow’s workplace. Information technology (IT) management is an important area for such preparation.

The quality of IT management has important consequences at the level of the national economy. The costs of IT alone are signi® cant. The Australian IT industry accounts for more than 3% of gross domestic product (GDP) at \$A15 billion. The costs of poor IT management can be macroeconomically damaging ± a recent US survey projected the 1995 cost of cancelled IT projects in that country as \$US81 billion, equivalent to 1% of US GDP (Johnson, 1995). Better IT management can help reduce these costs. The growth potential the technology presents for national economies is probably even greater than the cost savings.

Managing IT is a relatively new challenge for most general managers. In the 1960s and 1970s ® rms were often content to leave IT management to the chief information of® cer (CIO) or equivalent. The result has been widespread dissatisfaction among line business managers who now feel that IT, managed by the functional specialists, has not provided the expected pay-offs (Strassmann, 1985). Today, managers with general business accountability who do not choose to outsource are more likely to opt for an active role in managing IT because of the business opportunities it presents, its high costs and the level of risk involved (Rockart, 1988). IT is strategically important as a means of gaining competitive advantage, improving productivity, enabling new ways of managing and organizing, and of developing new businesses (Earl, 1989). IT is increasingly the major item of a ® rm’s capital expenditure ± 50% of all new business capital investment is in IT (Keen, 1991). However, IT is high risk with high failure rates reported (Sauer, 1993; Willcocks and Grif® ths, 1994). To gain IT’ s advantages and avoid its pitfalls, general managers need a thorough understanding of how to manage it. For management students, it is therefore increasingly important to master the challenge of IT.

IT specialists will welcome high-quality IT management education because they have found general managers to have narrow perspectives leading to a lack of understanding and involvement, with a consequential lack of management support (Lederer and Mendelow, 1988; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993). Support from business managers has been a critical issue for the IT function for the last 10 years because of its in¯ uence on IT performance (Ginzberg, 1975; Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Parker and Idundun, 1988; Buchanan, 1991; Niederman et al., 1991; Galliers et al., 1994; Pervan, 1994; Broadbent et al., 1995). Its continuing contemporary relevance has been con® rmed by a recent Korn/Ferry international survey in Australia (Hanks, 1996).

The provision Master of Business Administration (MBA) programmes make for IT management has the potential to be competitively important for business schools. The scope and intensity of competition has increased over recent years as new schools have entered the market and is further increasing with globalization. For provider institutions, how their management programme treats IT may be seen by potential students as an indicator of its current relevance and, hence, it may be an important element in the competition for fee-paying students.

In view of the importance of IT management education, there is surprisingly little information available about appropriate provision ± in Australia, the two-volume Karpin Committee report on management education (Karpin, 1995) had almost nothing to say about IT. This paper makes three contributions: as the ® rst study to investigate IT in general MBAs in Australia, it establishes a baseline for future studies, it identi® es de® ciencies in the current provision and it develops recommendations for what needs to be done to rectify the de® - ciencies and to prevent future deterioration. In making these contributions, the paper provides MBA directors and IT academics with data and arguments to help them improve existing provision and thereby improve our ability to gain the business bene® ts IT has to offer.

We start by formulating the research question. We describe the study and the data collection method. We then use the data to show how Australian MBA programmes have responded to the need to include some form of IT course. We make comparisons between what academics currently consider important, what they currently teach, what they think will be important in the future and what practising managers think important now and in the future. We ® nd gaps between what is needed and what is provided. We expect the gaps to widen unless our recommendations for strengthening the existing provision of IT courses are adopted.

## Research question

There are several kinds of programmes offering relevant IT management education including executive, undergraduate and graduate programmes. The MBA quali® cation is considered by many to be the pinnacle of management education. It is designed to prepare students for senior management positions, in which they might expect to have major business responsibility for IT during their career. It is therefore the most appropriate quali® cation to investigate in respect of its treatment of IT in that other programmes cannot be expected to take IT seriously if MBAs do not.

Prior research on IT in the MBA has largely been conducted in the USA. It investigated the educational opportunities IT affords (Alavi et al., 1995; Leidner and Jarvenpaa, 1995; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1996) and curriculum design issues (Silver et al., 1995; Slater et al., 1995). Trauth and colleagues (Trauth et al., 1993, Lee et al. 1995) investigated the adequacy of current provision. They carried out a joint academic/industry study of the knowledge and skill requirements for IT professionals. They highlighted an expectation gap between the two parties. Their work is limited in that it does not consider the needs of business managers. Ramakrishna et al. (1995) did address the expectations of business managers. They reported gaps between the IT-based knowledge and skills senior managers want in MBA graduates and what business schools are providing. Their study is limited in being based entirely on business perceptions and being reported in little detail. It does however con® rm that there is some limited evidence of what we shall call an external expectation gap between what business wants and what MBA programmes provide in the USA.

There is concern within institutions, particularly among IT academics, that, despite IT’s apparent importance to business, it is not accorded a proportionate position in the curriculum (Targett, 1991). This suggests what we call an internal expectation gap between what the education academics think should be provided and what MBA programmes provide. Stohr (1995) noted that some US business schools are actually moving IT out of the core curriculum despite the need for managers who understand the technology.

There are both general and specialist MBA programmes. The specialist programmes lead to a quali-® cation that emphasizes some functional specialism. The general programmes provide graduates with a rounded education in all the principal elements of business without specialization. They are directed at preparing graduates for senior roles in general management.

Given the increasing importance of IT to business and the indications that there are de® ciencies or expectation gaps in the current MBA provision in the USA, it is important to determine whether a similar problem exists elsewhere. The research question we ask is how well do general MBAs in Australia prepare their graduates for the business management of IT.

## Research method

The principal objective of this study is to investigate the match of IT content with the demands the increased importance of IT places on the general manager. The research is essentially exploratory, with the aim of establishing a baseline picture. This paper reports ® ndings from the Australian part of the study as they relate to the research question. A complete summary of our results can be found in Sauer et al. (1996).

## Data collection method

We conducted the research using a survey instrument designed by the Information Systems Research Group at Warwick Business School both as an ef® cient method of data collection and for possible future comparison with other surveys using the same instrument. Minor changes were made to the wordings to re¯ ect Australian terminology and the academic calendar.

Possible alternative methods, including eliciting the data from programme brochures and telephone interviews were rejected. Programme brochures were not considered an adequate data source because of the high risk with a dynamic subject such as IT that published information might have been incomplete or superseded. Telephone interviews were rejected because we wanted the respondents to be under no time pressure to make their responses on the free format comment sections of the instrument. These sections permitted respondents to expand on issues arising from their structured responses and to highlight matters worth investigation in future studies. They also provided the researchers with helpful information in interpreting the ® ndings. The absence of negative comment about the questions or the structuring of the survey indicates that the respondents found that it gave them adequate scope to report on their particular arrangements.

## Identifying the survey population

To identify all institutions in Australia delivering a fulltime, general MBA programme we used the Good Universities Guide to Management Education (Ashenden et al., 1994). Part-time and specialist programmes were excluded from further involvement. All qualifying institutions were contacted by telephone to verify that their programmes were full-time, general MBAs.

## Administering the questionnaire

For those programmes that quali® ed as full-time and general, the name of the academic IT coordinator (or equivalent) was requested during the initial telephone contact. In several institutions where no such role was de® ned, an MBA programme coordinator or similar person was named as an initial contact. IT coordinators (or subject leaders as appropriate) were then contacted by telephone and, following a brief explanation of the survey, invited to participate. MBA coordinators, who were contacted where there was no designated IT coordinator, were asked to pass the questionnaire to the most appropriate person. In one institution responsibility for IT teaching was divided among several academics, each responsible for their own courses, who therefore responded separately to the survey.

The survey was self-administered in two parts. Part A collected data about the characteristics of the various IT courses available in general MBA programmes ± each institution returned as many copies of part A as there were core and elective IT courses in their programme. Part B collected data about the general MBA programme and the position of IT course material and courses within that ± each institution returned just one copy of part B. Two institutions, whose MBA programmes did not offer any IT courses either as core or electives, were sent part B only and asked to complete those questions relevant to their situation. Although their current IT content was nil, what they saw as the ideal IT content and what changes they believed should or would take place were germane to the study. All responses related to academic arrangements for 1994.

In total, survey kits were mailed to 31 institutions, including the two receiving only part B. Follow-up calls were made when returns were not received after 4 weeks and, where necessary, at regular intervals after that. A cut-off was declared 11 weeks after the ® rst mailing.

## Response rate

In total 21 institutions returned both parts A and B, two returned part B only, one returned part A only and one of the 21 returned an unusable part B. Consequently, although we used 22 institutions’ returns, there is a small difference in the sample in respect of parts A and B data. For the institution in which several academics undertook to complete the survey, we received two separate responses to part B (in addition to several valid part A responses). As only one part B response could be used for each institution and in the absence of inconsistencies in objective data, we used the return which had been more fully completed.

The ® nal response rates were as follows.

(1) Part A: of the 29 institutions surveyed, 22 made returns of which all were usable (76% usable response).

(2) Part B: of the 31 institutions surveyed, 23 made returns of which 22 were usable (71% usable response).

## Terminology

The survey instrument uses the terminology of information systems (IS). In describing and discussing the results, we use the term IT because it accords more with general management usage (Baker et al., 1995). In this generic usage, IT covers computer awareness and literacy, systems development and programming, use of computer applications, management of projects and the IT function and business management of IT. In the survey, it was made explicit to respondents that IS and IS/IT were being used to cover all these possibilities. We therefore believe that the generic use of IT in our reporting adequately represents the responses received.

## Approach to analysis

Ideally, to tackle the question of how well general MBAs prepare their graduates for the business management of IT, researchers would start with some agreed standard of adequate provision. There are some standard curriculum guidelines (e.g. Nunamaker et al., 1992) but there is much diversity of opinion and practice regarding what is appropriate (Silver et al., 1995). This study therefore investigated whether or not there was consensus among Australian IT management academics regarding what should be taught. We found considerable agreement about what was ideal but much less agreement about priorities under the constraints of scarce space and time in the curriculum. We therefore compared the current provision against both ideal and constrained choices to identify the internal expectation gap. Lacking any objective data about what business needs, we used an existing study of critical IT issues for business managers to compare the current provision with what is salient for business so as to investigate any external expectation gap (Galliers et al., 1995). The study thus provides an answer to the research question by addressing the following questions.

(1) Is there an agreed academic core?

(2) What is the level of current provision of IT courses?

(3) How do curriculum topics compare with academics’ expectations?

(4) What are academics’ expectations for the future?

(5) Do IT courses cover the currently critical issues for business managers?

(6) How do business managers’ and academics’ expectations for the future compare?

## Results and analysis

Is there an agreed academic core?

In order to investigate a possible internal expectation gap our ® rst task was to determine whether there was consensus among academics as to the essential curriculum for IT. We looked at two survey questions. The ® rst asked which of 22 topics were, under ideal circumstances, core, elective or to be excluded. The second asked for a constrained choice of just ® ve core topics. We checked both the relevance of the IT topics and the completeness of the list of topics. We found that views about the six computer skills topics were divided evenly between core, elective and exclude. However, only one of the 16 non-skills topics was excluded by more than one person ± social implications and responsibilities was excluded by three. We conclude that the list of topics was considered relevant. Under the twenty-third category (other topics ± please specify) no other single topic was named by more than two respondents. We conclude that the list was also considered substantially complete.

When asked to identify core topics under ideal circumstances, seven were named by two-thirds or more course coordinators (see the topics in bold in Table 1). Apart from overview and future developments, all the topics were managerial, either relating to business management or functional IT management. These constitute an uncontroversial set of core topics. A further ® ve received the support of between 50 and 64%. These include information requirements analysis, storage and retrieval of data and management of systems development suggesting that there is a reasonably strong school of thought that topics which are semi-technical are core. This interpretation is further supported by the fact that ten respondents (45%) included developing and implementing systems as core. However, the data also indicate that a slightly weaker but still strong school of thought sees these topics as elective. Their position in the curriculum is plainly debatable.

Seven to ten topics cannot be covered in depth in a single MBA course, yet in our survey no programme contained more than one core IT course. It was therefore thought to be more realistic to consider the responses for a constrained choice of only ® ve topics. We found that the top seven topics were the same as when the choice was unconstrained (Table 1).

However, Table 1 demonstrates that there was less agreement under constrained choice. Apart from the overview which was included by all the respondents, no topic was chosen by more than 12 of the respondents (55%). The topics jointly ranked as sixth were included by only seven respondents (32%). The indications are that within an agreed outer boundary of ideal core topics, there is a considerable diversity of opinion about what are the core topics under conditions of scarce curriculum space and time.

We were concerned that the data for constrained choice might hide distinct schools of thought, that is that some topics might be highly correlated. We therefore constructed a correlation matrix of all the respondents’ constrained choices for the topics ranked second to ninth under constrained choice. We found that none of the intercorrelations was signi® cant (p < 0.1) suggesting that these topics do not cluster together systematically. This strongly suggests that there is no consensus among IT academics as to what should be part of a single core course. However, there is considerable agreement among IT academics that skills topics (the last six topics in Table 1) are not core.

## What is the level of current provision?

n = 22.

In identifying the population, we found 31 general MBA programmes of which only two did not teach IT courses. This suggests that there is IT coverage in 93.5% of programmes. In terms of our sample, only two out of 24 responding institutions did not teach any IT courses. Even on this more conservative calculation, we ® nd 91.6% of programmes having IT courses.

Table 2 summarizes the existing provision of core and elective courses. Of the 22 programmes offering at least one course in IT, six offered both core and elective courses, nine offered only core courses and seven offered only electives. Six of the 13 programmes offering IT electives offered more than one. No programme offered more than one core course. (One return included a second core unit, research methods, but this was discarded as not IT speci® c.) In total, it was possible to study more than one IT course in nine programmes. These ® gures show that in 15 out of 22 programmes (68%), some IT study is compulsory, but in none is more than a single course compulsory.

We sought to identify the types of IT course offered. The survey response contained usable data on a total of 34 courses. As this is a very small sample on which to conduct a statistical analysis such as factor analysis we relied on expert judgement to develop a classi® cation scheme. To classify the courses into categories one of the authors examined the course titles and their contents and developed a simple classi® cation scheme based on the data. The classi® cation scheme and the labelling of courses according to the scheme was then checked by a second author. Our classi® cation identi® ed three distinct types plus a fourth catchall category. They are summarized in Table 3.

As our classi® cation scheme is based on expert judgement we sought to verify it using other data from the survey. For this we used the responses to a question which asked for a rating (1± 5) of the current relevance of each topic on our list of 22. If the respondents had different conceptions of the courses which should be taught, this would be evident in the clustering of the responses. For instance, we reasoned that a respondent who was teaching a computing skills course would highly rate the relevance of most or all the topics related to computing skills. To identify patterns of this type we conducted a factor analysis with varimax rotation. If the factors matched our classi® cation scheme we considered this a validation. However, our sample size, both in absolute terms and also relative to the number of variables, is very small meaning that the results from our factor analysis should be treated with caution. The analysis resulted in six factors of which the ® rst three were clearly interpretable and are reported below. Table 4 presents the results for those ® rst three factors. We label the three factors loosely as systems fundamentals (factor 1), skills (factor 2) and management (factor 3). Broadly speaking, these three factors correspond, respectively, to the introduction MIS, computing skills and managing IT course types in our classi® cation scheme. We did not ® nd a factor to match special topics because it is a catch-all category.

## How do curriculum topics compare with academics’ expectations?

We asked how much each topic is taught measured on a four-point scale (\`not taught’ , \`taught a little’ , \`taught some’ and \`taught a lot’ ). Figure 1 summarizes the taught a lot and taught some responses for all topics. It also classi® es the topics using a taxonomy derived from Galliers et al. (1994). This serves two purposes: it provide a ® ner classi® cation than course type and it permits a comparison between course content and the issues business managers consider critical. The taxonomy identi® es four broad areas: IS which covers both the technical and managerial activities of the IT function, organizational development (OD) which relates to organizational structure and change, strategy (SS) which encompasses business strategy and competitive advantage and information infrastructure (II) which includes hardware, software, communications, data, methods, methodologies and standards.

Three are unclassi® ed because there is scope for very different approaches to teaching them which would warrant different classi® cations. Inspection indicates that strategy is apparently taught less than the other topic classes and that fewer courses teach infrastructure a lot. However, while there are these differences, overall the variation is not great. In view of the numerical dominance of IS topics, this con® rms our earlier assessment that MBA IT courses are dominated by the introduction to MIS type of content.

While Figure 1 presents a picture of how much each topic is currently taught, it provides no standard by which to assess provision. Given our ® nding that there is disagreement about what should be core and in order to discover whether business schools are in fact teaching what they believe to be important, we compared each programme’s current curriculum against its own IT coordinator’s constrained choice of ® ve core topics. For each of the ® ve, we identi® ed the extent to which it is included in the principal course (where a particular school offered only one course we used that and where a school offered more than one we used the course identi® ed as core). Of the 110 comparisons made (® ve topics for each of 22 respondents), in only three instances did we ® nd a topic not currently taught included in the ideal core course. Nearly 50% of the topics included in the ideal core course are currently being taught a lot, another 25% are currently being taught some and the rest are taught a little. We conclude that principal IT courses substantially meet their academic coordinators’ expectations in respect of a single core course.

## What are academics’ expectations for the future?

We investigated IT coordinators’ views about the future so as to identify emerging expectation gaps by examining the perceived relevance of the 22 topics in 5 years time compared with their relevance at the time of the survey. Table 7 reports the mean values and correlations for the 22 topics measured on a scale of 1± 5 ranging from no relevance to very relevant. The topics for which there is a signi® cant difference between the two means (at the 0.05 level) are shown in bold.

Table 7 reports the correlation between the responses for \`now’ and \`in 5 years’ to be very high. The correlation for the 22 topics taken individually ranged from 0.36 to 0.86, with a mean correlation of 0.68. ## Do IT courses cover the currently critical issues for business managers?

In this section, we compare those issues identi® ed as currently critical by business managers with the topics currently taught more than a little. There have been several relevant studies of managers’ perceptions of critical issues. We use the Galliers et al. (1994) study because it reports the views of business managers. Other studies, such as Niederman et al. (1991) and Pervan (1994), are limited in reporting only the views of IT managers. Pervan’s (1994) study has the advantage of reporting Australian data whereas the Galliers et al. (1994) data relate only to the UK. An informal comparison between Galliers et al.’ s (1994) and Pervan’ s (1994) results for IT managers ® nds a strong similarity between the Australians and the British. We anticipate that the views of Australian business managers are suf-® ciently similar to those of their British counterparts to justify using Galliers et al.’ s (1994) results.

One possible limitation of this approach is the disparity of 2 years between the administration of the business managers’ survey and our own. From our contacts with business managers we think that this is not problematic inasmuch as we ® nd the same concerns are still high on business managers’ agendas today.

All the critical issues studied use issue labels that differ from the topics in our survey because they address different questions. In listing critical issues, managers are typically revealing what is salient for them. In contrast, academics’ judgements about core topics incorporate an assessment of what is fundamental and necessary for the successful management of IT. Where possible, we make a direct comparison of academics’ topics and managers’ critical issues; where it is not possible we map issues into an aggregated classi® cation to achieve a higher level comparison.

Table 8 shows where direct comparisons are possible between the topic descriptions from this study and the issues in Galliers et al.’ s (1994) study. The academic topics we use for comparison are selected because we believe they are the place in the curriculum in which the speci® c critical issue is most likely to be addressed.

Table 8 reveals an apparently satisfactory concurrence on priorities between academics and managers for business process redesign and IS for competitive advantage which would be covered by management of change and IS/IT planning (strategy), respectively. However, appearances are deceptive. Despite academics’ ranking both topics ahead of managers, IS/IT planning (strategy) is taught a lot in only 47% of core courses and management of change is taught a lot in only 7%. This suggests that most MBA graduates are not being well prepared for dealing with either issue.

Table 8 also shows up some obvious divergences on priorities between managers and academics. The issues ranked ® rst and second by business managers, improving IS strategic planning and quality of software development, are ranked fourth and seventh by academics. The top issue for business managers, improving IS strategic planning, is covered by the IS/IT planning (strategy) topic which is taught a lot by only 47% of core courses while the second issue, quality of software development, is covered by the management of systems development topic which is taught a lot in only 20% of core courses. Quality assurance, ranked eighth by business managers, is covered by the ® nancing of IS, quality assurance and evaluation of IS topic which is taught a lot in no core courses. Thus, an expectation gap is revealed in some programmes in relation to topics such as IS/IT strategic planning and in all programmes in relation to topics such as ® nancing of IS, quality assurance and evaluation.

At the aggregated level of issue and topic classes, Table 8 indicates that IS issues do not dominate business managers’ concerns as they appear to dominate MBA course content. Of the top ten issues for business managers in Table 8, four relate to information infrastructure. One (managing distributed systems) re¯ ects the increasing efforts of businesses to make use of the best available information throughout the organization. The other three issues (developing the information architecture, managing data as a corporate resource and security and control) re¯ ect a growing business view of the information infrastructure as an asset to be managed and protected because of its value in facilitating the integration of increasingly distributed business activities. In contrast to business managers concerns, the two information infrastructure topics included in this study are ranked tenth and thirteenth equal by academics (Table 1). Moreover, these two information infrastructure topics are taught in depth in only seven and ® ve of the 34 courses in the sample and in only three and two core courses, respectively. It is also worth noting that organizational learning appears as a critical issue but is not obviously covered by any academic topics. We interpret these differences as a serious external expectation gap.

## Discussion

This study has examined the provision of IT courses in MBA programmes against the standards of academics and business managers in respect of the present and 5 years hence. We assess the provision in respect of fundamentals by comparing course content with academics’ views of topic relevance (internal expectation gap). We assess the provision in respect of managerial relevance by comparing course content with managers’ critical issues (external expectation gap). We thus expect to be able to answer the research question through the ® ndings in respect of both these kinds of comparison.

We have found considerable agreement among academics about what are ideally core topics, but under constrained choice there is no consensus. The ideal is best thought of as an indicator of how much material academics believe should be taught as core. Our interpretation is that this amounts to at least two courses. We have also found widespread agreement that computing skills topics are not core.

Our study has found a signi® cant internal expectation gap in respect of both the limited amount of curriculum space available and the type of content offered. This has serious effects. For example, because only 50% of general MBA programmes have core courses of the introduction to MIS or managing IT types, in the other 50% of programmes students can graduate with no formal introduction to the managerial aspects of IT. Moreover, since only one programme offered a core course of one of these two types and an elective of the other type, there is only that one programme in Australia that would permit students to choose something approaching what academics regard as an ideal core. In summary, there is too much opportunity for graduates to avoid being prepared for the IT dimension of the general management role and too little opportunity for them to achieve a satisfactory preparation.

It is appropriate to question whether Australian IT management academics’ expectations in respect of curriculum space are reasonable. One approach is to compare them with the provision in other countries. The data are as yet scrappy; however an early study showed that in leading North American business

Our own ® gure of 50% of programmes having either introduction to MIS or managing IT as core suggests that Australian IT management academics’ apparent desire to increase core IT management is not out of line internationally. Further international survey research would clearly help make better comparisons.

So, how have MBA programmes responded to the curriculum constraints they face? First, not surprisingly, we found that having no more than one core course available to them, academic coordinators chose to teach the topics they believed should be included in a constrained choice core. Second and less obviously, we found that although 12 programmes offered more than one course in IT, only one offered both a managing IT and an introduction to MIS course. As a secondary analysis we therefore investigated the kinds of choices academics make by examining the data relating to the amount each topic is taught in designated core courses. We found that on average twice as many topics are taught some (mean 8.8 per core course, 97 topics in 11 courses) as are taught a lot (mean 4.2 per core course, 46 topics in 11 courses), suggesting that, under conditions of scarce curriculum space and time, academics are compromising by offering breadth of coverage rather than depth. This may re¯ ect a wider problem affecting other disciplines as well ± that general MBAs try to cover too much ground in total. Although we did not investigate this in our survey research, an inspection of Australian universities’ MBA handbooks shows that most programmes include at least one core course and several electives in established disciplines such as marketing, organizational behaviour, accounting, ® nance and strategic management. Thus, other disciplines have the opportunity to achieve a better balance of depth and breadth. We suspect that the choice IT academics make in favour of breadth over depth re¯ ects a belief that many students are unlikely to select more than one IT course and that therefore it is better to ensure that students are given some familiarity with a wider range of topics than to risk putting important topics in a second course which few will opt to study. This reasoning could explain why coordinators with a second course available to them are not typically offering both managing IT and introduction to MIS courses.

Table 4 shows that the course types are dominated by introduction to MIS (50%). By contrast managing IT courses make up only 14.7% of the total. Only one programme in Australia was found to have a core course of this type. This suggests that existing IT provision concentrates on delivering an understanding of the tasks and issues facing the IT functional manager rather than those facing the general manager.

The appropriateness of the current emphasis can be debated. The key argument in its favour is that the fundamentals provided by an introduction to MIS are a necessary foundation for managing IT. It is better therefore to cover the fundamentals than to launch into managing IT which will be insuf® ciently comprehensible without them.

While this argument is not proven, the approach has the added advantage that knowledge of the IT function is valuable in the ® rst years after graduation when line managers have to interact with IT functional managers on operational matters. It is also valuable for those who choose to move into the IT function. The argument against the current emphasis is that the continued acceptance by general managers of separate functional management for IT causes many of the dif® culties they face. Thus, IS strategic planning, business process re-engineering and IT for competitive advantage are all much easier issues to deal with if they are managed as business problems not as an IT problem to be solved separately. Thus, the current emphasis reinforces the damaging separation between business and IT and does not equip general managers to make IT integral to the business. While we cannot resolve here whether introduction to MIS is a necessary precursor to managing IT, it is clear that a greater emphasis on the managing IT topics is warranted.

In our comparison of what is currently taught against what are the critical issues for business managers we found differences in priorities. Arguably, it is an appropriate role for academia to assess today’ s business issues as to whether they are substantial and durable or merely fashionable and ephemeral. Nevertheless, while some difference in priorities may be acceptable, the market for MBAs requires that graduates be up to date. Where there is a substantial external expectation gap such as that relating to information infrastructure, it is important that it be considered for inclusion in the curriculum. Our own experience suggests that information infrastructure is a substantial issue which will continue to worry managers for some years. It is an important issue because it typically requires large investment without precisely identi® able returns. It is a challenging issue for business managers because, although in principle it offers them the opportunity to integrate increasingly distributed business activities, they often feel unable to determine how investment in technology will help them achieve their business objectives. While they may accept that the architecture and data represent an asset, they have little idea about how to develop that asset to increase its business value to the ® rm. For these reasons, it is important that IT courses address information infrastructure issues.

We can see no sign that the external expectation gap arising from business managers’ concern about organizational learning is being accommodated by academics. Galliers et al. (1994) suggested that the increased concern with this issue re¯ ects the trend towards greater business exploitation of established IT systems. Experience suggests to us that there are several issues for managers. One is to determine what competencies must be acquired to make IT investment successful. A second is how to manage change so as to acquire new competencies which can exploit new and existing technology. A third is how to manage relationships with business partners including those supplying IT services so as to learn from them and help them learn. The increasing importance of competencies, alliance and sourcing makes it essential that organizational learning appear in the IT curriculum.

We did not ® nd much change expected in either the academic topics or the business issues that will be relevant in 5 years time. This indicates that academics do not yet have in mind changes which might reduce the existing expectation gap in respect of information infrastructure. Moreover, we found an increasing difference in priorities, fuelling concern that the external expectation gap will widen rather than narrow in the next 5 years.

In order to manage the external expectation gap so that it does not widen, IT academics can bene® t from reviewing existing course contents in the light of surveys which address business concerns such as Galliers et al. (1994). Unfortunately, hitherto these have been infrequent. There are more frequent surveys of IT managers’ concerns but they are a poor substitute ± had we used Galliers et al.’ s (1994) ® ndings in respect of IT managers’ concerns instead of their ® ndings for business managers we should have overlooked the expectation gap in relation to information infrastructure issues. We therefore urge those who conduct regular surveys of IT managers to follow Galliers et al.’ s (1994) example and survey business managers as well. We also encourage IT coordinators to maintain their industry contacts and to take every opportunity to gather informal feedback on emerging critical issues.

## Recommendations

This study indicates the need for change in the way general MBAs currently prepare managers for managing IT. We make recommendations which combine what IT academics believe should be taught with what business managers appear to need. We vary the topic contents from those used in the survey to achieve what we believe is a coherent curriculum. We then review these recommendations against the political realities of changing MBA curricula.

Two core courses are required to prepare students for the general management task with respect to IT (Table 11). We recommend an introduction to MIS course emphasizing management of the IT function followed by a managing IT course concentrating on the business perspective. The courses should be presented in consecutive terms or semesters so as to achieve continuity rather than separation between the two. The introduction to MIS course should comprise the overview, an introduction to the different kinds of technology used in MIS, along with future developments, IS/IT management and control and organization and management of the IT function. Thus, students will gain familiarity with the key resources and processes required to deliver IT systems and knowledge of how they should be managed.

The managing IT course should link to its predecessor through the study of IS/IT strategy and planning which itself links the activities of the IT function and the achievement of business value by the ® rm. Organizational structure and management concepts will cover the discussion of the structural positioning of IT within the organization and the question of sourcing. Internationalization of IT should be covered here. Information infrastructure should be included as a distinct strategic approach to gaining business value. This course should also include management of change comprising basic issues such as politics, people management and skill changes as well as more up to the minute issues such as business process re-engineering and organizational learning.

While it is straightforward to prescribe an enhanced IT content for general MBAs, the political reality is that it will be dif® cult to achieve that enhanced provision. Only half our respondents were optimistic that more space and time would be made available. Plainly, all IT academics in MBA programmes need to lobby MBA directors. Given the academic expectation that the importance of many topics will increase over the next 5 years, the failure to lobby for change will mean that for many programmes the current shortfall in provision will worsen. This will be bad for business where IT management skills are still in short supply. We are concerned that it will be demotivating for IT academics and could lead to some losses to academia as staff make career changes. In a ® eld where it is already dif® cult to hire good teachers, any losses of this kind will be damaging, both to the programme in question and the discipline.

And, more generally, IT academics should be responsive to the critical issues for business. At the current time, this means attending to concerns about information infrastructure, quality and organizational learning.

## Conclusions

Our study has found coverage of IT topics in most Australian general MBA programmes, but there is no cause for complacency among academics as to the preparation they provide for the business management of IT. This is partial at best. We have found serious gaps between both academics’ and managers’ expectations with respect to current educational provision. Although we have made recommendations for how MBA programmes can improve their offerings, in the light of IT coordinators’ expectations about the future we doubt it is realistic to expect our recommendations to be widely adopted. In view of the business importance of IT this is a depressing outcome.

For businesses employing MBA graduates, it is clear that they cannot take a strong preparation in IT for granted. For them, it will pay to quiz prospective employees. There is no reason to believe that all or even most programmes will provide graduates with a strong preparation for managing IT even in 5 years time. Realistically, we expect that employers will have to continue to develop this area of competence for themselves.

The implication for prospective students is that is pays to shop around. There is currently no guarantee

## Are general managers well prepared to manage IT

that any given programme will include adequate preparation for the general manager to manage IT. For students wishing to have the options to study IT management in greater depth, they should look for programmes with at least one core and one elective course covering as much as possible of the content we have recommended.
