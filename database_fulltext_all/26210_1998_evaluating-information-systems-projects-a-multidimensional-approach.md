# Evaluating information systems projects: a multidimensional approach

GUY FITZGERALD

Department of Computer Science, Birkbeck College, University of London, Malet Street, London WC1E 7HX, UK

Evaluation of IS/IT investments is a notoriously dif® cult area. Some doubt that IT investments are ever really productive; others point to mismeasurement as a major reason for such a conclusion and for the so-called IT \`productivity paradox’ . The paper reviews traditional approaches to IS/IT evaluation, and discusses their limitations. An eight-stage, multidimensional approach is then put forward to address those limitations.

## Introduction

The evaluation of investments in information technology and information systems (IT/IS) by organizations is not a new endeavour, indeed attempts have been made since the earliest systems were proposed (Peltu, 1981), but despite the long history the results of such evaluations have not generally been regarded as totally satisfactory nor convincing (Strassmann, 1997). However the ability to be able to evaluate IT/IS investments effectively is probably of greater importance today than at any time in the past, due to the large amounts being spent on IT/IS by organizations, the increasing focus on value for money in all areas of business activity, prompted by the recent economic recession, and the continually changing and developing character of information technology (IT).

Expenditure on IT/IS has risen inexorably over the years and most organizations have seen this become an increasingly important category of total organizational expenditure. A research study by the Oxford Institute of Information Management suggested that expenditure on IT/IS in large organizations continued to grow even between 1990 and 1992, years which were generally regarded as the height of the recession when it might have been expected that expenditure would decline. The study showed that expenditure patterns varied between sectors and that in banking/insurance/® nance the increase was signi® cant and IT/IS expenditure continued to consume an increasing proportion of revenue. In the retail/distribution/engineering sectors increases were more modest, and in the government sector a decline was registered. The study also indicated that the overall patterns of expenditure were changing, with hardware accounting for a declining proportion and external services and outsourcing increasing (Rands, 1993).

The same survey also attempted to collect data on information systems (IS) performance indicators such as the proportion of projects installed on target, the number of defects discovered, the ratio of repair work to the installed base, and the maintenance workloads. Whilst almost all companies could relatively easily provide data concerning use, expenditure and the allocation of resources, the response on IS performance indicators was considerably more patchy. It might be argued that companies regarded such data as con® dential or competitively and politically sensitive but this was not suggested by any of the companies. It appears that they simply did not collect much information concerning IS performance. The area that provided most data, although even here the ® ndings were based on only 15 respondents, was in connection with the number of projects delivered on target which indicated that there was little change between 1989 and 1990 with an average of 60% being installed on target. One interesting feature was that there were wide variations in these averages, ranging from 10 ± 100%, which indicates perhaps that for some there is great progress still to be made in the management of IS.

This lack of IS performance data is signi® cant and re¯ ects the more general results of studies suggesting that performance measures were not taken as seriously in the IT/IS area compared with, for example, manufacturing or services. Subsequently we have seen much greater concern to measure IT, but even recent surveys suggest that evaluation practices remain patchy (Willcocks and Lester, 1997). Equally there is evidence that investment in IT/IS often is based on beliefs in the bene® ts that will accrue rather than any very great attempts to measure such bene® ts. As one example, according to a study by Child and Loveridge (1990) banks had invested in IT/IS with the expectation that other costs, particularly staff, would be contained, and that the quality of customer service would be improved. However most banks did not carry out any formal or systematic attempts to prove that these bene® ts actually accrued nor to discover what impacts and effects they were having. A number of arguments as to why this was not done were advanced, for example the feeling that it was obvious what the bene® ts were and that any study would justify the IT/IS \`umpteen times over’ , that any study would be spurious because it could only be done in broad brush terms, that it is dif® cult to separate any one project from the others, etc. Despite the absence of formal measures two banks felt able to estimate staff productivity gains of 11% two years after installing counter terminals. However, staff productivity is a rather crude basis of measurement without some associated measures of capital productivity (Strassmann, 1997). In terms of quality of service Child and Loveridge report a survey of customer opinion in which 43 people thought that new IT/IS in a branch was positive and only 3 negative. But they then say that this has to be quali® ed \`by the fact that almost half of the survey reported that they had not really noticed the technological changes that had taken place’.

## Evaluation of IT/IS

These ® ndings, relating to the effects and bene® ts of IT/IS investments, are supported by a number of other more general studies, for example Ballantine et al., (1996), Willcocks (1992; 1996), Hochstrasser and Grif® ths (1991), Vowler (1990), PA Consulting (1990), Kearney (1990). These studies indicate that organizations ® nd it very dif® cult to evaluate IT/IS investments; they signi® cantly underestimate costs and use faith and notional ® gures for assessing bene® ts. Hochstrasser and Grif® ths (1991) found that only 16% of their study used rigorous methods to evaluate bene-® ts, and that over a quarter of managers did not know if IT/IS investments were better or worse than non-IT/IS investments in their organizations. Vowler (1990) reports that 66% of his study categorized their organization as being poor at measuring the bene® ts of IT/IS to the business.

The general picture of evaluation of IT/IS thus is still a rather sorry one. The question is does it matter? There are some people, as we have seen, who suggest that the bene® ts are so dramatic that it does not need to be proven, it is obvious simply by casual observation and that sophisticated studies would be manipulations to con® rm what everybody intuitively already feels.

This view is by no means satisfactory or obvious to everyone, especially non-IT/IS people, and increasingly to worried Chief Executive Of® cers and Finance Directors as IT/IS costs continue to grow at a faster rate than company revenues. There is still a de® nite questioning of the role and value of IT/IS, as

Strassmann (1997) suggests by the very title of his recent book. This questioning is supported by the research that shows there is no linear relationship between IT/IS expenditure and business success (Earl, 1989 and Strassman, 1990) and by studies that indicate wide differences in success within organizations operating in the same sector and also by the evidence of the number of IT/IS projects that appear to realize no net business bene® ts. Willcocks (1992) suggested that 30± 40% of projects fall into this category and that at least 20% of IT/IS expenditure is wasted, a result that looks just as plausible ® ve years on.

The implications are that organizations need to be much more careful about which IT/IS projects they implement, rather than seeking to invest in IT/IS across the board, an approach that has frequently been adopted in the past. Organizations have tended to respond to this need to be more selective in their IT/IS investments in a number of ways. For some this has meant that they will invest only in those IT/IS projects that can be shown to have clear links with the overall business strategy (Lederer and Sethi, 1992), others have sought to outsource some or all of their IT/IS (Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1994), whilst others have adopted a strict ® nancial evaluation of IT/IS proposals and will only implement those projects that can clearly be shown to produce ® nancial bene® ts. The problem with this latter response is that, by the nature of the justi® cation process, only IT/IS investments that lead to cost savings tend to be approved. The implication is that such organization will develop a number of IT/IS investments leading to measurable improvements in ef® ciency but they will potentially miss out on what might be more worthwhile projects that cannot be justi-® ed on ef® ciency criteria alone. (The term project is used to denote an IT/IS application, or group of applications, that are under consideration or in development. It is not restricted to just the IT/IS elements but also includes the associated manual, management and business procedures.)

## Ef® ciency projects

In the context of this paper an ef® ciency IT/IS project is de® ned as one that seeks to reduce the cost of performing a particular process or task by utilizing information technology. It does not seek to radically change the nature of the objectives that those tasks and processes were devised to ful® l, it simply seeks to achieve the same objectives at lower cost, i.e. to perform existing tasks more ef® ciently.

In the past most IT/IS projects have been of the ef® ciency type. In particular the bene® ts or ef® ciencies generally have come from the reduction or displace-

ment of labour costs. The original information systems applications, such as payroll, sales order processing, and administrative computing in general, were justi® ed in this way. The typical justi® cation was based on a Cost/Bene® t Analysis (CBA) which identi® ed the costs of computerization and then evaluated these costs against the bene® ts. The bene® ts were usually savings in labour which could be quanti® ed, e.g. the number of jobs saved multiplied by the average cost of those jobs, discounted over a particular period. It has been argued (Silk, 1990) that these cost displacement savings are relatively easy to quantify and a clear ® nancial case for investment demonstrated, using any of the available ® nancial analysis techniques, such as Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Net Present Value (NPV), Return on Investment (ROI), or Payback Period. This does not mean that the cost/bene® t analysis was an effective evaluation method, indeed it has been argued that its predictions were rarely achieved, often many important elements were ignored, often in the early days alternative costs, such as the costs of new computer staff, were underestimated. Nevertheless the mechanical evaluation process itself was relatively straightforward. IBM developed a method known as SESAME which used a cost/bene® t technique to assess a computer project in comparison with a non-computerized system (Lincoln and Shorrock, 1992). Historically the cost saving via labour displacement case has been easiest to make when a computerized system is used to replace a manual system because of the large number of staff traditionally involved in manual systems, which the new system eliminated or reduced. Relatively recently CBA and the displacement of labour argument has been used to justify, for example, the introduction of OCR (Optional Character Recognition) technology and in some organizations the introduction of word processors (Hochstrasser, 1990). Cost/Bene® t Analysis continues to be popular for the evaluation of IT/IS projects and Willcocks (1994) found that 62% of organizations used CBA as the predominant criterion in their evaluation process. The labour displacement justi® cation also continues to be used and has justi® ed the transfer from an existing computerized system to a new and improved computerized system, the new system being argued to require fewer people to run it whilst offering improved functionality. However the case is becoming more dif® cult to make because the propensity for reducing traditional labour costs via information systems is declining, organizations no longer have rooms full of payroll or invoicing clerks. Most large organizations have already replaced the majority of their manual systems and recently have been through periods of substantial labour reductions and there is very little potential for further reductions. Organizations that have few remaining opportunities to reduce their labour force consequently have little opportunity to justify new systems using the labour displacement justi® cation, although there is still some potential for this in the managerial sector, and recently there have been a number of justi® cations of IT/IS projects based on cost displacement relating to a reduction in managerial numbers and levels. It is this area that probably will provide any future labour saving cost displacement IT/IS projects.

Although labour costs have formed the largest and easiest costs to displace using IT/IS, there are examples of other costs that also have been amenable to displacement. For example, paper and postage costs have been displaced by EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) and electronic mail, property costs have been displaced, or reduced, where organizations have used IT/IS to enable the move of back of® ce functions away from town centres to cheaper areas, inventory costs have been displaced by IT/IS enabled JIT (Just In Time) systems, etc.

Thus in the past the majority of IT/IS projects have been justi® ed on the basis of cost displacement, whether labour or other costs. These projects essentially have been ef® ciency projects, they seek to perform the same functions as before but to perform them at lower cost. However, in many organizations the situation is changing as the propensity for further IT/IS investments based on cost displacement criteria are limited. First, the number of opportunities are reduced as more and more projects are implemented, and secondly the returns are declining as the most clear cut ef® ciency improving opportunities have already been addressed. Nevertheless, many organizations continue to apply ef® ciency (cost displacement) criteria in their evaluation processes and as indicated above a high proportion still utilize CBA techniques. The problem for these organizations is that an increasing number of IT/IS projects are of a type that will never be able to demonstrate ef® ciency cost savings and are thus very unlikely to pass ef® ciency based evaluation hurdles. The danger is that the organizations that continue to rely on traditional cost displacement evaluation approaches may miss signi® cant opportunities and even, as Wiseman (1985) suggested more than 12 years ago, jeopardize the future survival of the organization. Thus, there are different types of IT/IS investment that require different approaches to evaluation.

## Effectiveness projects

The basic objectives of effectiveness projects are not simply to reduce the costs of performing existing tasks but to identify ways of doing different things which better achieve the required results, leading to increased revenues, better service, or whatever. Effectiveness projects are not addressing ef® ciency criteria but seek to improve organizational effectiveness. The distinction between ef® ciency and effectiveness has been made many times (for example, Hill, 1993) and hinges on the notion of objectives. It is perfectly possible to perform some set of tasks very well and ef® ciently, i.e. in the cheapest possible way, but this does not mean that performing that particular set of tasks is the best, or even an optimum way of achieving the objectives associated with those tasks. Ef® ciency is broadly concerned with how we do things whereas effectiveness is concerned with what we do.

As identi® ed above, IT/IS investments that seek to address effectiveness cannot be justi® ed on the basis of cost displacement arguments. The justi® cation for effectiveness projects must be based upon effectiveness criteria, for example, increased functionality, better products and services, improved presentation or image, enhanced competitive positioning, etc.

Making such effectiveness justi® cations is not impossible but it is a much more dif® cult process than making a ® nancial case on the basis of ef® ciency improvements. The main reason for this increased dif-® culty is because there is an extra stage of proof to be gone through. In ef® ciency projects the bene® t is reduced cost and this is relatively easily identi® ed and quanti® ed. This does not mean the bene® ts necessarily accrue in practice but for the purpose of the investment evaluation it is relatively easy to identify and illustrate, e.g. salary and associated savings, from displacing order processing clerks. For effectiveness projects it is not just necessary to identify the bene® ts, for example, better service, but also that the recipient of those bene® ts will recognize and value the improvement and change their behaviour in some positive way as a result, e.g. by existing customers buying more services, or by not transferring their allegiances elsewhere, or for the improved service to attract new customers. Customers or potential customers must ® rst be aware of the change and secondly perceive it as an improvement over the service they currently use, which may be no service at all, or a competitors service. Thus the bene® t realization process comprises at least two stages, ® rst the provision or implementation of the project to provide bene® t and secondly the effect of that bene® t on the wider environment and any resultant behaviour change. Figure 1 illustrates this notion; for ef® ciency projects the identi-® ed ef® ciency concept is implemented and the bene® t accrues (or possibly not), usually as reduced costs, and that is the end of the process. It is internally controllable by the organization and usually does not depend on any interaction with the outside environment.

Effectiveness projects have a similar ® rst stage process which may result in some stage 1 bene® ts. However it is not necessary for any cost bene® ts to accrue at this stage. The second stage of the process is the effect of the implementation of stage 1 on the environment and any resultant change in behaviour leading to stage 2 bene® ts. Stage 2 bene® ts may be, for example, improved sales resulting in improved revenues.

The fact that there are two stages to the process unfortunately provides greater opportunities for the potential bene® ts to get lost or become dissipated and makes the evaluation of such effectiveness projects so dif® cult. In the case of cost displacement the organization is more in control of the realization of bene® ts whereas in the improved services case (i.e. effectiveness justi® cations) the organization is in control of achieving the ® rst stage but not the second stage which is the impact on others, for example suppliers, customers and potential customers. This second stage has been the area where miscalculations are frequently made, for example it is often assumed that the move from stage one to stage two is a logical and deterministic process, e.g. that an improved service will lead to more people buying that service, or that better information will lead to better decision making.

These dif® culties have caused some organizations to abandon any attempt at formal justi® cation and rely on intuition or blind faith. This is not necessarily a totally bad thing because the fact that bene® ts cannot easily be proven does not mean that they will not occur. Without it many effective systems would probably never have been embarked upon. A study by Earl and Runge (1987), for example, found that 80% of successful competitive edge (i.e. effectiveness) applications bypassed the standard management approval processes in organizations and relied on managerial hunch or what they term \`strategic insight’ . However, although this approach to justi® cation may on occasions lead to success, it is more likely to lead to failure, in other words it is a highly risky strategy. Effectiveness projects are thus dif® cult to evaluate but for organizations simply to use this as an excuse not to evaluate such projects at all, and rely on intuition or faith, is not good enough because the potential for failure is so high. What is needed is an approach to the evaluation of effectiveness projects which simply does not use the traditional ef® - ciency criteria. However before examining such an approach some further types of IT/IS projects that frequently pose evaluation problems will be discussed.

## Other types of IT/IS project

This paper has made a distinction between ef® ciency and effectiveness IT/IS projects. However, some authors (Willcocks, 1994, 1996, Hochstrasser, 1990) argue that there are additional categories of IT/IS projects, that are neither ef® ciency not effectiveness, which require special forms of evaluation. For example, Butler Cox (1990) identify three extra types of IT/IS investments. First \`mandatory’ investments, these are investments that the organization is compelled or forced to undertake. There is no option but to proceed, so, it is argued, there is no requirement for any justi-® cation because an investment of some kind simpl cannot be avoided. A mandatory investment may be caused by some new legal requirement, for example legislation to monitor environmental factors which necessitate a new system, or it may be as a result of changes demanded by a powerful external customer or supplier, for example a bank may require payments to be made in a particular way which small organizations are forced to comply with. A mandatory investment might also be driven by competitive pressures, for example the use of ATMs (Automated Teller Machines) have become a necessity for those involved in retail banking. The second investment type is what they term \`infrastructure’ investments. These are investments in technological platforms, communication systems, networks, etc. to enable some future systems or services. It is argued that infrastructure investments present problems of evaluation because the return, per se, on the infrastructure is usually very limited, usually they simply enable other investments. The third investment type is the \`research’ investments. These are investments concerned with developing and learning about the next generation of technologies or systems, and establishing their potentials. Also, they are argued to present evaluation problems as the objec tives of such investments are unclear and the direct returns, if any, are usually long term.

Whether the above really are different types of IT/IS investment is debatable. The author believes that rarel are there any situations which do not require some form of evaluation. For example, in the case of mandatory investments, if the organization is being forced down a particular path, it is still important to be clear about the costs and bene® ts, and in particular, the relative costs and bene® ts between alternative \`solutions’ to the mandatory investment. Even where the overal approach or response is being dictated by external forces there are almost always alternatives and variations in the nature of the particular IT/IS system that need to be considered. There are usually a variety of different ways in which what is being forced on the organization can be done and therefore an evaluation process should be used to ensure that the most ef® cient and effective option is selected. For example if a smal manufacturer is being forced to establish EDI connections with a powerful retailer, there are still options about whether to develop such a system in-house, buy a package, or even accept a solution provided by the retailer. The options still need to be evaluated carefully, their relative ef® ciency needs to be assessed as well as their effectiveness. For example it might be that the most cost-effective and ef® cient option is to adopt the system provided by the retailer but in strategic terms this may be ineffective as it might be judged as unhealthy to be tied so close to the retailer. Thus even where there is a situation of a mandatory investment there are always likely to be options which must be evaluated on ef® ciency, and possibly also effectiveness, criteria. So it is argued that a mandatory investment is not so different that it requires some special evaluation approach to be adopted, it requires assessment for ef® - ciency and effectiveness like any other IT/IS project.

The infrastructure investment is also argued not to be a special case. The fact that an infrastructure investment rarely generates any bene® ts itself does not mean it should not be subject to the usual evaluation process. The evaluation should not be performed on the infrastructure alone but on the ® rst, or ® rst few, applications that are enabled by the infrastructure. If this cannot be done then the infrastructure investment should not proceed, it should wait until the organization is clear what bene® ts are going to be enabled. If they cannot be identi® ed then it is probable that they do not exist. This is not to say the justi® cations have to be purely ® nancial or quantitative but they must be arguable and able to stand up to keen questioning. The applications enabled by infrastructure investments are projects like any other and need to be evaluated. Their costs simply will include all or some of the costs of the infrastructure that has enabled them. They may be ef® ciency or effectiveness projects. Probably most will involve new and different ways of doing things (enabled by the infrastructure), i.e. they are probably effectiveness projects, and need to be justi® ed on effectiveness criteria. Thus the infrastructure investment should not be viewed as a special case that somehow does not require evaluation.

However, the research investment is perhaps a special case. It is not really a speci® c type of IT/IS investment in the normal sense but should be treated as part of an organizations research budget which by its nature does not normally have to return speci® c bene-® ts on an individual project basis. Nevertheless even research investments should have some evaluation criteria applied and it is suggested that this should be related to organizational learning and development.

Therefore, with the exception of research investments, there are not really any special cases of IT/IS investments that do not require evaluation, and that do not ® t into either the ef® ciency or effectiveness categories, or both.

## Towards a multidimensional approach

As a result of the above discussion it is suggested that an enhanced approach to IT/IS evaluation is required which addresses particularly effectiveness as well as ef® - ciency criteria and such an approach is now proposed.

The approach comprises eight speci® c tasks in the evaluation process. These tasks are discussed separately for illustrative purposes, although it is not critical that they are performed in any particular order or that some of them should not, in practice, be combined together or performed in parallel. For the purposes of this paper the approach focuses on the initial evaluation of an IT/IS project proposal, however it is recognized that evaluation is an on-going process that continues throughout the life of the project and that the evaluations need to be revisited. The results of evaluation at subsequent stages may well result in changes to various aspects of the project, the approach is thus, in practice, iterative. Some of the tasks and evaluations that are suggested are likely already to be undertaken in organizations in other forms or as part of some existing evaluation approach. This does not matter, the important thing is that the tasks and evaluations are seriously undertaken at some stage and in some way, by relevant people in the organization.

## Identifying costs

The ® rst task, as in most evaluations, is the identi® - cation of costs and, as indicated above, this is a relatively well understood area, so it will not be discussed in great detail. The costs should, as far as possible, be quanti® ed and the traditional ® nancial evaluation techniques are likely to be appropriate. The objective is to identify fully the costs associated with the project, including any cost displacement that may occur. However one of the different emphases in this approach is a recognition that costs are often signi® - cantly underestimated and that in practice many projects suffer from serious cost overruns. One of the main reasons for this is the hidden nature of many IT/IS costs and in particular the approach emphasises that the identi® cation of costs should account fully for those costs incurred by non-IT departments. These costs are frequently underestimated or ignored, up to 40% of relevant costs have been found to be outside of traditional IT/IS budgets (Willcocks, 1994). It is particularly important to fully assess the costs associated with end-users and user management in a project. It should be recognized that people usually cannot continue to do all their current tasks as well as playing a major role in the de® nition and development of a new project. Signi® cant extra staff costs therefore may be incurred when people are seconded to a new project.

## Evaluating IS projects

Even if speci® c costs do not occur there is often a degree of opportunity cost that needs to be calculated, for example where end-users and end-user management are not able to devote so much time to their existing tasks as a result of their involvement in the new project.

Costs that cannot be quanti® ed in the traditional way also need to be identi® ed and evaluated in qualitative terms. For example a project that changes the nature of peoples roles might have costs associated with retraining etc. that can be quanti® ed but there may well also be costs, or drawbacks, in terms of changes and a possible reduction in job satisfaction. It is very dif® cult to assign costs to this but they should nevertheless be evaluated and discussed. The most important part of this process is the documentation of the evaluations and discussions such that they can be input to later stages of the approach. This ensures that, unlike most cases, they do not get lost or ignored.

## The contribution to business strategy

The second task is to identify to what extent the project contributes to the overall business strategy of the organization. This is a key element in the justi® cation of effectiveness projects and a contribution needs to be established if the project is to be approved. For example, if an element of an organization’s strategy is the decentralization of various functions and the empowerment of line management, how does the system under consideration support or enable that strategy. The linking of IT/IS applications to business strategy has for a number of years been identi® ed as an essential element for successful organizations (Lucas and Turner, 1982) and thus needs to be closely addressed in the evaluation of effectiveness projects. Indeed, it is possible that effectiveness projects will have evolved from a strategic planning exercise, and that business strategy formulation will have resulted in the identi® cation of a number of effective IT/IS projects. In such cases the assessment of the contribution to business strategy will be clear and already documented; unfortunately not all organizations initiate their IT/IS projects in this way. Irrespective of the origins of the project it needs to be evaluated as to its contribution and alignment with the business strategy. Any effectiveness project should make a contribution to business strategy, the degree of contribution is always dif® cult to assess and usually there is little point on trying to assess it in quantitative terms, although a relative assessment on a one to ten scale can be useful, or rather the process, including the arguments and discussion, of making the relative assessment is usually rich and worthwhile. A useful question to ask is what the effect on the strategy would be if the system were not implemented?

This task also needs to be performed for ef® ciency projects, not to establish any contribution to business strategy, because it is not necessary that they make any, but it is essential to establish that there is no negative contribution, or unhelpful impact, on the strategy. This is a much neglected assessment but it needs to be performed because it is possible for an ef® ciency project, justi® ed on ef® ciency criteria, to con¯ ict with the longer term strategy, or constrain some future avenue in relation to strategy. For example, on one organization a costly investment in a project to provide better pricing information for the central planning function, which was cost justi® ed on ef® ciency criteria over a ® ve year period in terms of improved product pricing decisions, con¯ icted with the overall business strategy which was to devolve decision making, including price setting, to the business units in the regions. The central pricing system was not readily compatible with the devolved regional requirements which were scheduled to be introduced over a two year timeframe. In retrospect it was suggested that if an evaluation of this system had been made in relation to the business strategy it is probable that the central pricing system would never have obtained approval.

## Analysis of bene® ts

The third task in the approach is the identi® cation and analysis of actual bene® ts associated with the project. If some of these bene® ts are quanti® able then this should be done but in addition the approach recommends that the assumptions that underlie the quanti® cation of the bene® ts also should be clearly identi® ed and subjected to discussion and analysis by, and with, the relevant participants. So if it is suggested that a particular project will result in quanti® able savings the source of these savings should be discussed and reviewed, particularly with people in the area where the bene® ts are expected to accrue, to establish that the basis for the calculation of the bene® ts are realistic. In particular the dif® culties of the two stage bene® t realization process must be addressed. One organization predicted signi® cant bene® ts from a new IT/IS investment based on a take up rate of 100% by their existing customers, in the event less than 20% of their customers changed to the new offering. The assumption was clearly unrealistic and the predicted bene® ts were never likely to be achieved. The assumption was never challenged because it was hidden in a very sophisticated statistical analysis and was glossed over in the general excitement that everybody felt for the project. There are thus two elements to the assessment of such bene® ts, ® rst to recognize the two stage bene® ts realization process and secondly to make realistic assumptions concerning its impact.

Another area that should be examined closely is where savings, even savings that actually accrue, are in reality negated by increased costs in other areas or where costs are simply shifted from one budget to another. One company reduced its labour costs as a result of a change to an existing system but later found that another area, further along the business chain, was employing an increased number of contract staff to compensate. These costs were accounted for under different budgets and so the real costs of the system was not fully assessed. Another example was the introduction of word processing in an organization which was justi® ed on savings in secretarial staff; however they discovered that in practice that they were simply moving costs from relatively inexpensive secretaries to relatively expensive managers. A further example was a construction company that embarked on a decentralization project moving from a large centralized data centre to a decentralized client-server system in order to reduce costs. Unfortunately a post-implementation review concluded that the costs saved from the data centre were largely absorbed by increased personnel costs by end-users and others, which were disguised by appearing in other budgets.

This approach also recommends that sensitivity analysis be used in the assessment of bene® ts, something which appears to be used relatively infrequently in practice. Assumptions should be tested against the proposed bene® ts and those that are most critical need to be identi® ed. These should then be subjected to reevaluation and more detailed examination and questioning. No attempt should be made to quantify those elements that are clearly not amenable to quanti® cation, and for most effectiveness projects there are liable to be many of this type. Figures should not be derived simply to ® t the available techniques. A bene® t such as the improvement to the image of a company by the use of state-of-the-art technology is unlikely to be quanti® able and yet it might be a relevant and important bene® t.

In the analysis of bene® ts the discussion and debate concerning the quanti® able versus the nonquanti® able (or intangible) bene® ts, the assumptions, and the justi-® cations, are again an extremely rich part of the evaluation process and should not be lost. The process serves to improve understanding of the system and its proposed role by all those involved. In addition, such factors need to be recorded, including the assumptions underlying the bene® ts. The recording of the debates and justi® cations is very important, ® rst to help ensure that the bene® ts do not get lost or forgotten during development as is so often the case, secondly to enable the bene® ts to be fully monitored and tracked, and thirdly to help organizational learning in any postimplementation review.

## Second order effects

The fourth task concerns the identi® cation of what are termed \`second order’ effects (Hochstrasser, 1990); these often appear as surprises when they occur (Willcocks, 1994). Whilst not all such effects can be predicted many can, and it is worth attempting to do so. It is a case of trying to assess the potential impact of the system, particularly in terms of the social, political and organization context. It did not take much skill to predict that the introduction of word processing would have an impact on secretarial roles and yet many organizations appeared to recognize this only when it happened. Some organizations found that it had dramatic effects on the number of drafts that were produced before a document was ® nished and that rather than producing savings it actually increased costs, and that the bene® ts of word processing were more to do with improvements in presentation quality and consistency of documents rather than actual savings.

Any project that impacts roles, relationships, sources of power, or organizational structures is likely to have second order effects. These effects if predicted can be addressed and if necessary mitigated, turned to advan tage, or exploited. One approach to the prediction and assessment of second order effects is to involve those people most directly affected by the system in the task and provide them with the opportunity to in¯ uence events. Such people are often very good at predicting second order effects because of their intimate knowledge of the work processes. The assessment and control of second order effects are critical to the overal success of the evaluation process. Another approach is to conduct a pilot study of the system with the express objective of exploring second order effects.

An important second order effect is the information that is collected as a result, or biproduct, of a system. Some transaction processing applications have more than justi® ed themselves on the basis of their secondary information. For example, a manufacturer that set up a system to deal with servicing and warranty produced for the ® rst time global information on product defects and customer preferences that they were able to feed into new product development very effectively. It was this aspect that produced the major bene® ts rather than the ef® ciency savings on the warranty system itself. Another example of second order effects is that of a high street retailer which implemented a system to link computers and tills in their stores to their head of® ce via an X.25 private network. The system polled the shops every 10 seconds and had a sophisticated system for locating and diagnosing faults. The incidental posi tive effect of the system was its effect on security. When burglars cut the telephone lines in raids on the shops the system raised an alarm at head of® ce and the police were noti® ed. In one weekend out of 12 attempted break-ins at its branches, nine were foiled by the system. The security bene® ts of the system were unanticipated but substantial and could easily have been used in the evaluation and justi® cation process. The security department had been considering various solutions to the security problems including just such a diagnosis system on the traditional phone lines but they were unable to justify it as a security measure alone. It was a case of one part of the organization not knowing what the other was doing which an active consideration of second order effects involving the right balance of people could well have revealed.

## Flexibility

The ® fth task concerns the evaluation of ¯ exibility. It has long been recognized that the degree of ¯ exibility and adaptability of any project is very important to its long term success (Land, 1982) and therefore any evaluation of a project should include an assessment of its ¯ exibility. Flexibility is a much neglected area but of great importance, therefore this approach to evaluation suggests an analysis of ¯ exibility as one of its key tasks. The evaluation of ¯ exibility is a recognition that the success of a project is not just about the bene® ts that accrue when it is implemented but also about how well it continues to perform over the lifetime of the system. The focus of development tends to be a snapshot approach, typically dealing with problems of an existing system and the immediate requirements of the business. Equally important is that the system continues to perform well when the requirements change in the future. Most systems are not designed to accommodate such future changes and that because the business requirements change on a different, and faster, cycle to that of systems development many of our systems are out of date, and sub-optimal, shortly after development. The problems that most organizations are having with legacy systems provides support for this case, as does the continuing, and increasing role of maintenance. Systems are continually having to be changed and enhanced as new and different business conditions occur.

Flexibility does not just occur, it has to be designed for and planned. There are two aspects to the evaluation of ¯ exibility. First, it is necessary to think through the kinds of changes that the business might be subjected to in the future and ensure that such change easily can be accommodated. The period for which future changes are analysed should ideally be the proposed life of the project, but obviously it is easier to predict future changes for the near future rather than the long term. Obviously, some changes are impossible to predict but there is evidence that many can be. One study (Fitzgerald, 1990) indicated that a large proportion of changes and enhancements made to existing systems were predictable at the development stage (over 90%) and that the consequences of such changes could easily and cheaply have been accommodated. These range from trivial examples of not allowing enough space in records for growth, or the change of the century not being indicated in dates, through to the more fundamental, such as a complete change in reporting structures within an organization that meant an information system had to be completely rewritten just a few months after implementation. An evaluation for ¯ exibility would ascertain whether such accommodation had been made and if not would ensure that it was undertaken.

The second element of ¯ exibility that should be examined is whether any proposed system is actually building in constraints to ¯ exibility, and it is surprising how often this happens. The pressures to develop systems quickly are leading to short cuts being taken that can result in constraints to ¯ exibility. The construction of a system in one company developed a system that served its main purpose in one area quite well and successfully but it was written in such a way that it constrained other systems in the organization from operating in the way that was best from an overall perspective.

Flexibility is regarded as such an important and neglected issue that it justi® es a stage itself in the evaluation process on the basis that the more ¯ exible the system the more effective the system will be over its life time.

## Implementability

The sixth stage of evaluation is an assessment of how practical or implementable the project is. Many projects are only evaluated on the potential costs and bene® ts that they provide, but an equally important factor is whether those bene® ts will actually ever occur. Most evaluations ignore the fact that some projects are considerably more dif® cult to implement than others, in fact some may never get off the ground at all. The potentially most bene® cial effectiveness projects are likely to be the most dif® cult to implement because of the degree of change that they require, not just technically, but also organizationally and politically. Some organizations have recognized the importance of business champions and sponsors in the successful implementation of systems, sometimes to the extent that they will not even consider implementing a project unless such people exist. Questions such as, \`will there be resistance to the implementation of the system by entrenched power bases’ , or \`is the technology untried state of the art’ need to be asked and evaluated. For example, the early implementations of laser barcode reader point of sale systems in retailing were unsuccessful, despite the large predicted bene® ts, due to the unproven nature of the technology in practice. Such features all contribute to the degree of implementability of the project and need to be assessed. There is some evidence (Earl, 1989) that building on existing strengths and existing systems to develop enhanced functionality and effectiveness is an important contributor to success rather than adopting a \`big-bang’ approach, due to the problems experienced in the implementation of such systems (Willcocks et al., 1997). Again the implementability of a project is generally a neglected area in evaluation and yet it is critical to whether any of the projected bene® ts actually accrue. The assessment of the degree of implementability is essentially an assessment of risk, which in a wider context is the topic of the next stage of the approach.

## Risk

The seventh stage is an evaluation of risk. We have already seen that risk is under assessed in IT/IS evaluations but it is nevertheless an important factor. What is meant by risk? Risk is a measure of the degree of possible variation in the outcome or bene® ts of the project. If the variation is large then the risk is high. It is essential to analyse the factors that contribute to risk, projects with high risk are only worth considering if the potential returns are also high. In general a project with high risk but low potential returns should not be contemplated. Effectiveness projects are typically of higher risk than ef® ciency projects due to the two stage bene® ts realization process discussed above. Once areas of risk have been identi® ed they may be susceptible to measures that will control or minimize them. For example, if some of the potential bene® ts are discovered to be highly dependent on the attitude and behaviour of suppliers then, although suppliers may not be directly controllable perhaps some form of incentive for appropriate supplier behaviour could be established. For example, Thompson Holidays initially paid a bonus to travel agents for every booking made through their new reservation system in an attempt to encourage travel agents to change their behaviour and use that system rather than continuing to use the more costly (to Thompsons) old telephone booking system (Feeny, 1988). The risk to Thompson’s was that they would provide an expensive new reservation system that travel agents would not use due to inertia. Attempts to address aspects of risk is a much neglected pursuit, that can be critical to the achievement of objectives. Incentives to encourage particular behaviour and attitudes need not necessarily be ® nancial, indeed the most effective are often to do with personal perceptions of worth and value. Managing the incentive systems are critical elements of IT/IS projects that involve organizational change. Sometimes it is suggested that such issues are not the province of IT/IS personnel, which may be true, but they certainly need to be addressed by someone in the organization and perhaps those involved in the evaluation process are in the best position to do this. The issue of who needs to be involved in the evaluation process is addressed later.

## Testing the business idea

The eighth and ® nal phase is an attempt to test and clarify some of the assessments, assumptions and stated bene® ts made earlier in a practical way. This is necessary particularly if the project is risky. In the estab lishment of a new product no organization would go ahead without undertaking some kind of market research to establish whether the product is likely to sell or not, to ® nd out what the market thinks, what market sectors are attracted, etc. and yet that is exactly what we do with IT/IS projects. IT/IS projects need to be tested out in terms of the business bene® ts to establish if they are going to work prior to implementation. Apart from market and behaviour testing, for example, by the use of surveys consideration might be given to prototyping the business idea.

Prototyping is now accepted in systems development as an important way of enhancing the understanding of user requirements but it is usually seen as technical prototyping. Why not extend the concept to test if the business bene® ts are actually going to accrue? For example, if a retail bank proposes to invest in a system that increases the speed of transactions at its ATMs and they argue that this improvement to their service will lead to business bene® ts, let them try it out and see what effect it has. It is relatively easy to simulate faster response at a few terminals and see what effect it has, either by observing the effect on the behaviour of customers, or by interviewing them about it. If customers perceive the difference and like it then the argument that it is likely to have some effect on business would be stronger than if the customers did not even notice any difference. If tests revealed that customers did not notice a difference it would be a brave manager that would then base the justi® cation of the system on an improved contribution to the bottom line, although there may well be other aspects on which the system could be justi® ed. Such testing helps us to be clear about exactly what the basis of any justi® cation is.

This type of testing or prototyping is not far fetched or impractical, some organizations have done just this.

## Evaluating IS projects

The classic case of the American Hospital Supply Company is frequently cited as one of the ® rst examples of using IT/IS to gain competitive advantage. They tried out the ideas on one or two of their large customers as a kind of prototype to test their reaction before committing to the development of the full system. Thompson Holidays tested out their proposed systems on a small number of travel agents before implementation and changed the systems at least twice as a result of experience and the reactions of travel agents (Feeny, 1988). Another example concerned a system to reduce queuing which was expensively implemented and did on average reduce queues by two minutes from 11 to 9 minutes but postimplementation evaluations revealed that people did not perceive any reduction, they still thought they had been kept waiting 10 minutes. For the system to be effective it would have to have reduced queuing by about 5 minutes so that people perceived the difference and felt they had been queuing for 5 minutes only. This kind of perception behaviour could easily and cheaply have been assessed by experimenting before the commitment to the system was made and perhaps a decision made not to implement unless signi® cant reductions were achievable.

## The decision point

Once the eight phases of the evaluation process have been completed then a decision needs to be taken as to whether to proceed with the project or not. This is not a ® nal irrevocable decision because evaluation should, in reality, be an on-going process as mentioned above. However in practice there is usually a major decision-making point after evaluation of the potential of the project before further signi® cant commitment of resources is made for development. At this point the objectives of the system should have been clari® ed and the type of system, in terms of ef® ciency or effectiveness, identi® ed. Whatever decision is made, and it will involve a process of weighting the various evaluation criteria and perhaps applying some quantitative analysis of the weightings, the key factor is that the basis for the decision making should be identi® ed and documented, i.e. the expectations for the system and the basis of the justi® cation for the system should be recorded. This will enable the expectations for the system to be kept in the forefront of peoples minds during development and implementation, it will help to ensure that the objectives do not get lost, and it will help at subsequent decision points in the project to make the decisions that will help ensure success. A further important reason for recording this evaluation information is to keep track of how well the expectations are being met during the life of the system. It is important that part of the evaluation is the establishment and monitoring of appropriate criteria for measuring the success, or otherwise, of the project in achieving the objectives. This is of equal importance whether the bene® ts are quanti® able or not. It is sometimes assumed that nonquanti® able bene® ts cannot be effectively measured but management accounting recently has become quite sophisticated at measuring and evaluating non® nancial criteria such as service levels (Fitzgerald et al., 1992), and IT/IS projects need to adopt similar methods.

Also, it is important to assess user perceptions of system performance as well as the more usual quantitative assessments. Unfortunately it has been observed that this is a particularly weak area of IT/IS evaluation. Willcocks (1994), for example, discovered that 44% of organizations did not include the user departments in the evaluation process which helps to explain why the risks are so high if the users are not involved. As Willcocks states \`This cuts off a vital source of information and critique on the degree to which an IT/IS proposal is organizationally feasible and will deliver on user requirements’.

## Who should perform the evaluation process?

The question inevitably arises as to who should perform this proposed evaluation process. Due to the wide ranging nature of the evaluations proposed it is impossible for this to be purely an IT department task and responsibility, furthermore they often have a vested interest in the outcome. Equally the ® nance function should not be responsible. It is often assumed that ® nance has the necessary skills for evaluation and although they may well contribute, particularly in the use of the various ® nancial evaluation techniques, and as is the case with the IT department, they do not have suf® cient business or line responsibility. It is preferable for the area of the business with prime interest in the successful outcome of the project to be responsible and have the major role in undertaking the evaluations. Exactly who should be involved wil depend on the nature of the project but for strategic systems that cross departmental and organizationa boundaries the process should contain a senior executive who has a responsibility for business strategy and should involve people from all areas of the business that are affected. The IT/IS and ® nance functions should be involved in an advisory rather than a controlling capacity. The overall responsibility for the evaluation has to be with the people who are going to make it happen in a business sense if the project is approved. This also results in greater commitment to the success of the project and is one of the bene® ts of true evaluation and this particular approach. In this approach it is advised that there be a small group within the organization who’ s role is to facilitate any evaluation process. They would not be responsible but they would ensure that the team is set up in the ® rst place, ensure the on-going process, and guide the team in the approach. They would also be the repository of evaluation experience and organizational learning.

The nature of the project approval process, the \`go/no go’ decision, will depend on the organization but ideally it should not be a question of someone on high sitting in judgement, although this can be useful as a process to check that all the necessary tasks have been undertaken. Ideally the project is approved or not by a consensus of those in the evaluation team, that is they are empowered to commit to the project and are responsible for ensuring its success in the terms they have de® ned in the evaluation. The idea is to link closely the tasks and responsibilities of evaluation, development, and implementation together, to ensure success. If they are separated, responsibility for success is dissipated and goals and objectives become diluted or lost.

It should be recognized, despite rigorous evaluation, that some decisions ultimately boil down to acts of faith in the sense that they are based on someone’s judgement or feel concerning what is right and what is likely to succeed. Such judgement needs to be based on an understanding of the business and the business environment as well as risk and probabilities, rather than guesswork. This evaluation process should help in achieving that understanding.

In practice such an evaluation approach takes time, energy, and resources but if it helps to ensure projects that contribute to business success, or eliminates the development of projects that would not be successful, it will be worthwhile. The process is itself an up front investment for subsequent long term gains.

This paper has focused on the early stages of the evaluation of IT/IS projects with a view to making better investment decisions in this dif® cult area. Clearly the process does not ® nish with the an approval decision, and apart from on-going evaluation over the project development there are a number of other important evaluation points. Of particular signi® cance is the post implementation review. This is the time at which the actual results of the project can be compared with those of the initial evaluation. Apart from the usual bene® ts this presents the opportunity for the evaluation team, and the organization, to learn from the results. This learning, especially after a number of projects have been evaluated, may lead to signi® cant improvements in subsequent evaluation projects. For example, it may be found that particular elements are always underestimated, that training for users takes longer than anticipated, that staff turnover increases during change periods, etc. It even may be that certain metrics, speci® c to the organization, can be determined, e.g. that on average the ratio of IT staff time to user department time is 1:2.3. Whatever the outcomes this should be absorbed in the evaluation culture and so engender con® dence in future evalua tions. Too many post implementation reviews, where they are performed at all, are treated as witch hunts to identify guilty parties where things have gone wrong, rather than as a valuable learning opportunity. For this to occur the evaluation culture needs to be made open and nonthreatening. The establishment of the evaluation facilitator role in the organization is important in this respect.

## Conclusion

This paper has attempted to examine the dif® cult area of evaluation of IT/IS investments. It has discussed the various problems of traditional approaches to IT/IS investment and made a distinction between investments for ef® ciency and effectiveness, and it has suggested that most evaluations are de® cient in analysing investments with effectiveness objectives. As a result a proposal of an approach that particularly attempts to address the evaluation of effectiveness projects is made. This consists of eight evaluation stages, some of which are not usually found in traditional evaluation processes. The emphasis is on the process of evaluation, i.e. the serious consideration of the topics of the stages, rather than the use of particular techniques. The serious consideration and discussion implied by the stage leads to the explicit identi® cation of assumptions, understandings and justi® cations. These in turn lead to organizational learning for IT/IS evaluation.

Although the proposed approach is described as a number of stages it is not necessarily suggested as a methodology which should be followed step by step, indeed not enough detail is provided to do this. Rather the stages are used to separate and illustrate the ideas of the approach, in practice it might be more appro priate for an organization to understand the ideas and incorporate them into their existing evaluations. Thus, it might be used in an organization as a discussion prompt, to identify any criteria that might be relevant, but currently de® cient. These elements might then be integrated into their current approach. However, it is important that the evaluation culture required for this approach to succeed also be included, and this may be harder to achieve.
