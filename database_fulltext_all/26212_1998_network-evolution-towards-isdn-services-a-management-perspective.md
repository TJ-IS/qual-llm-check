# Network evolution towards ISDN services: a management perspective

VINCENT S. LAI

Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, Faculty of Business Administration, The Chines University of Hong Kong, Shatin, N.T., Hong Kong

JAN GUYNES CLARK

School of Business Administration, Al Akhawayn University in Ifrane, Box 104, Avenue Hassan II, Ifrane, Morocco 53000

ISDN (Integrated Services Digital Network) has been recognized by technologists as a major step in the evolution of public network architecture. However, a large percentage of network users do not recognize any bottom line improvements that can result from ISDN nor do they know how to exploit the bene® ts of the technology. This study evaluates and summarizes the ISDN implementation experiences of nine organizations that are currently using ISDN. Along with their experiences, the study also investigated potentia problems and critical success factors associated with ISDN implementation. Understanding these critical issues provides IS management with new insight, enabling them to strategically expedite their ISDN implementation and diffusion.

## Introduction

Management of telecommunications is a complex, complicated endeavour, especially when organizations invest in incompatible networks which need to be operated, managed, and maintained separately (Streeter et al., 1996). The objective of ISDN is to provide a means of controlling the increasingly diversi® ed, complex communications technologies while simplifying and reducing the installation and maintenance costs (Sapien and Piedmo, 1997). This is achieved by providing voice, video, and data on a single digital network, allowing users to enjoy the virtually error-free performance that digital technology provides (Durr, 1995; Srinivasan, 1995; Fitzgerald, 1996). Since ISDN is a public network that is operated by service carriers, the vast majority of network management, maintenance, wiring, and installation are the responsibility of the carrier.

With ISDN, a company’ s information islands, which were at one time isolated by means of multiple data networks, can now be integrated at reduced cost and technical complexity (Railing and Housel, 1990). Although ISDN services have been widespread in many European (such as Germany, England, and France) and Asian (Hong Kong, Singapore, and Japan) countries, they have been slow in emerging within the United States (Lai and Guynes, 1994; Lai et al., 1996). There are several reasons for this, the most obvious of which is the lack of an ubiquitous nationwide ISDN service (Staal et al., 1995; Fitzgerald, 1996). The practical experience or early adopters, as well as their insight in ISDN implementation and use, can greatly assist other companies who are considering migrating toward this network technology.

In addition to the strategic importance of ISDN and the international competitive pressure for ISDN technology, MIS researchers are stressing exploration of interorganizational IT innovations that could alter business operations and competitive patterns. As suggested by Swanson (1994, p.1069), \`innovation in its employment among organizations is increasingly crucial to competitive survival and success . . . Yet, current theory explains little about IS innovation and its role in organizational innovation in general’. Swanson classi-® ed IS innovations into three types, based on their ability to support IS core (Type I), administrative core (Type II), or the technical core (Type III). Type I innovations, such as data administration, are con® ned to the IS task, Type II innovations, such as an information centre, apply IS products and services toward supporting business administration, and Type III innovations, such as ISDN, typically impact the business as a whole by integrating IS products and services within the core business technology. Swanson stated that studies of Type III innovations are especially important to address the contributions of IS to the larger organization. Prescott and Conger (1995) share the same perspective, stating that study of the role of interorganizational innovations, such as ISDN, is needed, due to the limited explanatory power of traditional diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory to this type of innovation. These arguments reinforced the urge for researching ISDN, which is an important type III interorganizational innovation, and its diffusion in organizations.

Based on the above rationale, the objective of this study was to evaluate and summarize the ISDN implementation experiences of nine organizations that are currently using this technology. Along with their experiences, the study also investigated potential problems and critical success factors associated with ISDN implementation. Understanding these critical issues provides IS management with new insight, enabling them to strategically expedite their ISDN implementation and diffusion.

## The companies studied

This study employed a multiple case study methodology to understand the process of ISDN implementation and capture the dynamics of the situation during implementation. Nine organizations participated in the study. At the time of data collection, each company had at least 150 employees on site, and had been utilizing ISDN technologies for at least three years. The demographic and implementation information of these organizations are depicted in Tables 1, 2, and 3.

Company A is a medium size manufacturing company which started their business operation in Dallas, Texas in 1975. The company has grown rapidly in the last ten years and is now a major supplier of of® ce equipment in Asia and South America. The company has installed ® ve different networks to accommodate the rapidly growing computing demand and to support their communication activities. However, escalating processing and communication costs resulted in management’s desire to develop a more advanced, ¯ exible computing platform at a reduced cost. After consulting with the local common carrier, they decided to build an overlay ISDN network, with the goal of replacing their existing network services within one year.

Company B is a growing computer company which distributes hardware such as modems and hard disks. It has ten sales of® ces, with its headquarters located in San Jose, California. The company is well-known for its innovativeness, especially in the application of information technologies in support of their business operations. It was one of the ® rst companies to explore the opportunities associated with ISDN technology. Management believed digital networks would be the mainstream communications in the future, and in order to gain a competitive edge among its competitors, the company invested in ISDN technology in 1988.

Company C is one of the oldest computer manufacturers specializing in the production of mid-range computers. Since its acquisition of two computer vision companies in the late 1980s, it has moved into the computer-aid manufacturing arena. With the assistance of its local ISDN vendor, the company evolved into a digital communications platform. This strategic decision bene® ted the company tremendously, since ISDN supports multimedia services, which are of prime importance for computer-aided design, engineering, and manufacturing.

Company D is a regional bank holding company with headquarters in Atlanta, Georgia. The holding company owns and operates 34 full service commercial banks in Georgia, Tennessee, and Alabama. The company already had invested heavily in advanced computer and communications technologies to support the daily ® nancial transactions of the banks, which are distributed in more than 15 cities, in three states. Since the bank’ s management was convinced that ISDN provides more services and applications that would help their business operations and improve their customer relationships, the evolution of their networks to ISDN was a logical decision.

Company E is a well-known consulting company performing a range of services in auditing, tax, and computing. Management discussed the major shift in the consulting business in which clients are no longer content to stay with a consulting ® rm because of tradition or customer loyalty. Instead, clients are more demanding of services provided and will only invest in the consulting company which delivers the services of greatest value to them. In order to survive in this increasingly competitive environment, the consulting company needed more innovative ways to deliver value-added service to its clients at a reasonable cost. Based on this rationale, company E implemented a national information network that would link all their of® ces to each other, as well as to a wealth of databases and information services. ISDN network technology was chosen for its pilot of® ce.

Company F is an electronic distribution company with its own manufacturing factory in China. The of® ce in Atlanta, Georgia, is a distribution and sales centre for its line of merchandise. It also has several smaller sales of® ces in other cities in the United States. In the past, the company relied heavily on postal services and fax machines to exchange product information across the country and continent, re¯ ecting the fact that information technology had not played an important role in their business operations. Their computers, according to the IS manager, were standalone machines used only to support basic clerical and database activities. In 1990, the company decided it needed a major change in its computing practices in order to be more competitive in their industry. The company established some strategic policies to guide the infusion and diffusion of information technology within the company and hired a new IS manager to design the company’s computing architecture with the objectives of cost reduction and ef® ciency improvement. ISDN was one of the technologies the company adopted to support its new communications platform.

Company G is a medium size automobile parts manufacturer. The company carries a large line of products ordered by different automobile manufacturers in the United States and overseas. Information technology investment has accounted for a large percentage of the company’ s business operations budget. To support business transactions with its customers, the company has implemented a sophisticated interorganizational system. However its information processing platform was maturing, and the company was selected as one of the ISDN trial sites by its local ISDN service supplier.

Company H is a construction company with businesses in real estate, construction, and retail. Its headquarters consists of two major buildings separated by a third building in a busy street in downtown Dallas, Texas. To support collaboration and construction design, both local and metropolitan area networks were implemented. Unfortunately, the transmission speed and quality of such networks were inef® cient and the architects requested the IS manager to explore other alternatives to support their high bandwidth communication requirements. The company was also exploring a new technology to replace their existing telephone switching services, opting for a service that possessed Automatic Number Identi® cation capability. Eventually, the company selected ISDN, which matched all the requirements speci® ed.

Company I is a medium size investment bank with its headquarters in Dallas, Texas. Due to the continued adverse effect of the recession and declining real estate values, the bank made little pro® t in 1993. Anticipating no change in environmental conditions, it was expected that revenues for 1994 also would be decreased. Therefore the bank announced a major cost reduction programme for the technology and operations areas. To achieve this objective, the bank decided to downsize their computing platform to PCs and interconnected these PCs and other branch of® ces with digital networks. They believed ISDN provides higher quality, more services, and enough processing ¯ exibility to support their communication requirements.

## Research methodology

The MIS directors or persons in charge of the Information Systems department of the nine companies were initially contacted by phone. We explained to them the objectives and importance of our study and requested that they share their valuable ISDN experiences with us. We also requested the MIS director to provide us with the name of other IS professionals within the company who had been involved extensively with the ISDN implementation. Based on their suggestions, we contacted 31 IS personnel to determine their ® tness for our study. During the initial telephone interview, we discussed with them their ISDN implementation involvement and willingness to participate in our study. A total of 23 employees agreed to a more detailed personal interview. These personnel included seven senior network specialists, six MIS directors, three project managers, three network managers, two MIS managers, one VP of Information systems, and one network engineer. All were experienced IS professionals with at least six years of network experience. They were also senior IS executives extensively involved in ISDN implementation and knowledgeable in both the managerial and technical aspects of ISDN. All the interviews took place in the interviewee’s company and lasted about one and a half hours.

We adopted a multi-step ® eld research approach to guide the data collection. The ® rst step involved a series of open discussion for implementation issues facing the IS profession. As a result of these discussions, we proceeded to a series of focus study to generate more speci® c questions for subsequent data. During this second step, the subjects were asked to: (1) describe their involvement in ISDN implementation; (2) explain the ISDN planning activities; (3) identify any potential causes of implementation problems, and (4) suggest potential means of improving implementation for potential ISDN adopters.

Immediately after each interview, the notes were transcribed. The subjects were contacted again if either clari® cation or supplementary information was needed. Upon completion of these interviews, all the subjects’ answers were encoded and summarized. In addition to interviews, management reports and data related to ISDN implementation were obtained and reviewed. Such information can be extremely helpful in linking missing concepts during interviews.

## Discussion of ISDN implementation

In this study, Stamper’s product life cycle framework (Stamper, 1994) was adapted to guide the evaluation of ISDN migration processes. Within this framework, ISDN is recognized as a new product evolving through several well-de® ned stages, from the point of conceptualization through implementation. These stages include investigation, analysis, design, implementation, and operation and maintenance.

## Investigation

This ® rst step entails a concerted effort toward obtaining a complete understanding of the problem and the conceptualization of ISDN. This includes not only the obvious consideration of whether the organization should adopt ISDN to satisfy the communication need or problem, but also a more strategic analysis of how the organization would, or could, utilize this technology as a source of competitive advantage.

Each company within the study investigated ISDN with the intention of reducing communication costs and providing ¯ exibility for fast response. Spiralling communication costs were perceived as a potential obstacle in making the companies more effective, and they believed that integrated digital networks, such as ISDN, would allow them to reduce communication costs, yet remain pro® table within their highly competitive markets.

In order to develop a better understanding of ISDN technology, each company sent teams, comprised of both technical and managerial members, to visit other ISDN premises. They were all impressed by the capabilities and prospects of ISDN and believed this was the network technology which would best meet their needs in solving their communication problems.

Representatives from each company were asked to describe the characteristics of an organization which would best bene® t from ISDN implementation. They indicated that an organization should consider implementing ISDN if they possess one or more of the following key attributes:

(1) multiple campuses spread out over different locations, served by the same Central Of® ces (CO), with the need for frequent voice, data, and image communications;

(2) frequent and simultaneous use of PCs and telephones to support the daily work of a large number of employees;

(3) the need to support high-speed transmission and multi-media communications;

(4) the existence of both steady and bursty transmission characteristics in the PC± PC, PC± Host networking environments;

(5) the need for using electronic directory services (ISDN offers the Automatic Number Identi-® cation (ANI) feature that supports database queries based on the caller’s telephone number);

(6) the need for frequent multiplexing and protocol conversion in the communication process (PRI provides multiplexing, submultiplexing, and protocol conversion services to its subscribers);

(7) the need for dynamic station movement (i.e. swapping of of® ces among users without rewiring the terminals). ISDN services are immediately available by connecting the system to the phone outlet without any rewiring.

## Analysis

This step focuses on identifying the current information processing (especially data communications)

environment, investigating future expansion plans, and analysing new applications of hardware, software, and communications within an organization. This evaluation is critical in that it offers management a chance to scrutinize their existing information practices and future information expectations. It also enables them to develop a sense of what is needed in regard to technology to support the organization.

All companies within the study had adopted a structured approach to perform analysis activities. With the exception of companies A and F, all companies formed a project team to assist in identifying current and future needs. Members of each team were composed of representatives from general management, systems engineering, software development, and telecommunications. The major responsibilities of these teams were to determine data requirements, match the organizational communication needs to ISDN features and capabilities, and to provide suggestions for future planning activities (such as changeover and migration plans).

Companies A and F did not use a project team because they had already established telecommunications steering committees. These committees, composed of top management, users and service personnel, were responsible for setting communications standards, stating a subset of data processing policies, and managing telecommunications resources. Therefore, the role of investigating ISDN was delegated to this steering committee.

All companies indicated that a thorough investigation was conducted to identify the requirements necessary to support the installation of ISDN. Overall, the companies indicated that the issues illustrated in Table 4 were investigated, but with varied degree of emphasis. According to the interviews, some companies were more concerned with performance issues, devoting their efforts toward the investigation of network con-® guration, bandwidth requirements, and performancerelated issues. Other companies focused on securing the maximum possible competitive advantage ISDN technology could provide, exploring the issues of data usage, bandwidth requirements, applications support, and service improvement.

In order to ensure better communications, understanding, and support among top management, end users, and IS professionals, all nine companies performed various planning activities and generated network planning reports. These efforts included capacity management, security management, and development of the architecture and implementation steps necessary to evolve toward ISDN. Interviewees stated that these planning processes were implemented and maintained by the project teams, steering committee, or those involved in network operations. Overall, the greatest impetus in their planning processes was the unfamiliarity of this technology. This problem was intensi® ed when they had to decide upon the need and potential value of the myriad ISDN products and services that are available.

## Design

Two strategies can be used to evolve toward ISDN: overlay and replacement (Lai and Guynes, 1994). If the proposed ISDN network is not capable of handling all existing network functionalities (because of cost, incompatibility, etc.), the overlay strategy is proposed. This entails the installation of an ISDN network in parallel with the existing networks. The replacement strategy entails the replacement of the existing network(s) with the ISDN network. This strategy is employed when the capabilities of both networks are compatible.

All studied companies, with the exception of G and I, adopted the overlay strategy to evolve toward ISDN. They indicated that this strategy was less expensive and more prudent. Utilizing this strategy, much of their existing equipment was kept, no expensive rewiring of the premises was necessary, and migration was facilitated through adapters (if devices were incompatible).

All companies agreed that designing a suitable ISDN infrastructure to support the gradual deployment of ISDN services is critical in achieving successful evolution toward ISDN. However, they also stated that many problems were encountered in the network design process because of the vast variety of devices and applications required to keep the current system operational. Some interviewees stated that this was compounded because their organization already had advanced, established networks. In order to ensure that their existing hardware and software could be utilized within the ISDN network, their IS personnel had to perform numerous compatibility tests.

During the design process, each company indicated the use of different design strategies to derive their ISDN network. However, their design concerns were mostly con® ned to ISDN system con® guration, equipment analysis, and cable reinforcement. Throughout the design process, the studied companies were guided and assisted by the marketing, sales, and central of® ces of their ISDN service providers. Summarizing the design issues expressed by the interviewees, it was found that the following activities were focused upon:

(1) PC-to-ISDN connection: All interviewees agreed that this was the easiest aspect of their design. PCs can be networked into ISDN via a direct connection between the PC serial port and the RS-232C asynchronous interface on the ISDN telephone set.

Among the nine companies studied, Companies A, C, D, E, G, and I were using Group IV facsimile and Companies B and F using Group III (all expecting to upgrade to Group IV in the near future). Since these two groups of facsimile systems are digital oriented, integrating them into ISDN network was not dif® cult.

(4) LAN-to-ISDN connection: Some interviewees stated that they had dif® culties identifying standard devices to tie their LANs directly to ISDN. Normally, the solution is to use a PC-based ISDN interface card (terminal adapter), a bridging or routing software, and a standard LAN interface card. However, they mentioned that a terminal adapter, when used to connect an existing router or bridge to an ISDN, would have limited capability of controlling their ISDN lines automatically.

(5) PBX-to-ISDN connection: Some of our interviewees commented that ISDN performed much the same role as an analogue PBX. They are both capable of handling and switching voice messages. However, these analogue PBXs are not quite compatible to ISDN. In addition, analogue PBXs are slower and less ef® cient in digital data manipulation. Companies A, G, and H had analogue PBXs prior to their ISDN installation, but they decided to evolve to digital

PBX to take full advantage of ISDN. Their new PBX were installed as a hub at the central processing of® ce, providing access to the local ISDN and supporting ISDN terminals and services.

(6) Building-to-building-to-ISDN connection: Two options were found to be available for building connection, based on the geographical distances between buildings. First, an organization can choose to install a single ISDN line from the Central Of® ce to the main distribution frame (MDF) on campus if the buildings are fairly close together. With this architecture, individual distribution frames have to be split from the MDF to individual buildings. Second, if the buildings are at distant locations, the connections can be set up at the switching of® ce. All the companies we studied utilized the MDF approach.

## Implementation

Virtually all Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs) and long distance companies provide installation services for ISDN. Companies which do not provide these services often refer their potential customers to third party data agents, such as Telcom Service (Durr, 1994, 1995). All the companies in this study reported that the carriers had assigned some of their network specialists to assist the RBOCs during ISDN implementation. All companies expressed great satisfaction with the installation services offered by the RBOCs. Company C even mentioned that its RBOC had made a great commitment of resources from the beginning of the project to the maintenance of ISDN, including stationing over 20 RBOC employees on the premises during cutover to ISDN. In order to permit a smooth transition to ISDN, the studied organizations indicated they had some form of implementation plan to guide the installation and acceptance of the technology. These included cable and equipment installation plans, as well as a performance acceptance plan. Table 5 depicts the major installation activities undertaken by the studied companies.

Our studied companies, all of which had no staff trained in the use and maintenance of ISDN prior to implementation, were initially concerned about the quality and quantity of training. They had to rely totally upon their ISDN providers to train their ® rst group of ISDN network personnel and users. However, the servic providers alleviated their concerns and the interviewees indicated great satisfaction of the high quality training programmes they received. As expressed, a variety of training services were offered, including coordination of training classes, provision of trainers, and supply of al training documentation. They also indicated that their

ISDN providers still provide them with follow-up training/documentation and frequent follow-up calls/visits.

After the initial groups of employees were trained, all interviewed companies provided training programmes to their other ISDN users. The commonly used training sources included formal training programmes and documentation (such as ISDN news updates, user manuals, and interest group ¯ yers). Among all the training sources, the interviewees stated that the ISDN provider training was the most effective source, trailed by formal training programmes and informal expert consultation.

The implementation process, as relayed by the interviewees, was termed successful at all companies. This was due, in part, to the active involvement of management, resource commitment of the ISDN providers, and frequent executive level coordination between the company and the provider. Interviewees from each company stated that they learned a lot from their implementation experience and that their level of ISDN expertise has been greatly improved. Based upon their implementation experiences, the following are their suggested recommendations for future ISDN adopters.

(1) Consult with as many department managers as possible prior to the ® nal network con® guration.

(2) Keep management and potential users informed about the status of ISDN before and after its implementation.

(3) Provide adequate training on hardware, software, and applications.

(4) Provide voice mail training separate from ISDN telephone training.

(5) \`Calling ID’ when used with voice-mail creates an opportunity for abuse of the system. Therefore, it must be managed carefully.

(6) Migrate users to ISDN in phases to allow for transparent evolution of applications and data services.

(7) Do not convert all CPE stations to ISDN simultaneously. Flash cutting makes troubleshooting very dif® cult. Phased implementation by building or department is preferred.

(8) Do not convert the phones, voice mail, appli cations processor, and any other new technology simultaneously.

(9) Provide a \`help desk’ to provide assistance to ISDN users on a continuing basis.

(10) Establish problem resolution procedures.

All companies attributed their implementation success to the guidance of the ISDN providers.

## Lessons learned

## Politics

It was observed that the evolution process to ISDN was not only the result of extensive planning, a considerable amount of bargaining and mutual adjustment among selected personnel and groups was also involved. Implementation and use of new information technologies may shatter the status quo and cause con¯ ict of interest among the political systems within the organizations (Keen, 1981; Pfeffer, 1981). Con-¯ icting objectives of different personnel or groups may cause them to use their power to determine the outcome of the decision process of evolving to ISDN. Hence, planning for ISDN implementation is not merely a simple, rational process that has identi® able goals with a prescribed means of achieving them. Before migrating to ISDN, the organization must understand the project participants and where they stand on this issue. Recognizing these factors may avoid implementation failures caused by personal human dislikes and political disputes.

## Network management

A shift to ISDN indicates a switch from private PBXs to public digital centrex services. The consequence of such a change results in a higher level of dependence upon the telephone company and a loss of control over premise-based traf® c switching and service con® guring. A good example of this is the ability to manage bandwidth. In order to ensure optimal network performance, network management should have the ability to aggregate and redirect bandwidth instantaneously, especially during network failures. It is imperative for organizations to monitor, control, and obtain performance and capacity information for all intraand interorganization communication environments. Evolving to ISDN could severely hamper this ability if one invests in a carrier which does not provide the necessary managerial and maintenance tools. Conversely, performance and capacity could be greatly enhanced by a common carrier who provides these tools. Therefore, it is wise to thoroughly investigate all potential carriers.

## Standards

Until recently, one of the major obstacles to ubiquitous ISDN within the United States was the lack of a uniform industry agreement on ISDN standards for products and services. This resulted in the formation of \`islands’ of ISDN which could not communicate with each other without relying upon complex protocol converters. All the companies involved in this study have implemented their own de facto ISDN. With thi type of communications environment, organizations will not be able to bene® t, at least initially, from commercial ISDN for interorganizational communications. The organizations, however, can enjoy ISDN rapidly on an intraorganizational basis. Although Bellcore, the Corporation for Open Systems (COS), and the North American ISDN Users Forum (NIUF) have joined forces to develop standard interfaces and messaging for future ISDN development and implementation, management must still consider this standards issue and determine whether or not ISDN wil ® t into their communications strategy.

## Economic value

Research in network diffusion (Gurbaxani, 1990) con® rmed that the value of a communications network depends on the size and content of the set of existing subscribers. From an economic perspective, the consumption externalities were slight with a small user base, but signi® cant when the \`critical mass’ has been cumulated. Although the United States has been working on ISDN standards for several years, few are in concrete form. Additionally, the subscription base of this technology is still small. If critical mass is not cumulated and ISDN service is isolated in \`islands’ , the full bene® ts of ISDN cannot be exploited. Some of our interviewees expressed that network users are still skeptical of ISDN because there is no clearlyfocused, widely-shared vision of the applicability, bene-® ts, and realization of this technology.

## Service level determination

BRI service (two 64-Kbps voice or data lines) is most bene® cial to small and mid-sized companies that do not have sophisticated data communications requirements. Large companies need PRI service (24 64-Kbps voice or data lines) to satisfy their broadband communications between intelligent workstations and mainframes. However, how can management determine how many ISDN lines are required? Additionally, how can management determine the trade-off or the equivalence between different lines, such as PRI lines and a corresponding number of BRI lines and other types of private network services? Clearly, the construction of such a guideline by management prior to ISDN implementation would be of signi® cant importance.

Many interviewees mentioned that implementing ISDN service can be dif® cult, given that their phone company representatives did not have adequate knowledge about ISDN, data communications equipment, or data applications. One company mentioned that their phone company had misspeci® ed the parameters for the initial setup of their ISDN service. Another company complained about the ignorance of the installer in switching some equipment in their central of® ce, causing them tremendous loss in identifying the problem. They mentioned that all these problems could be avoided if the ISDN providers had provided more in-depth ISDN training to their representatives.

## LAN-ISDN Trade-offs

LANs have grown rapidly in the past decade and have emerged as a front-running network solution. Although ISDN is basically considered a wide area network (WAN), it also inherently offers the capabilities of LAN-like services, via a Centrex system. To avoid the potential redundancy of services offered by these networks, the need and role of LANs in the ISDN environment must be addressed. If LANs are interconnected with ISDN, the immediate advantages will be lower cost in network operation, greater ¯ exibility in user relocation, more consistent and predictable throughput, and greater connectivity ¯ exibility with the use of existing telephone lines. However, one disadvantage to this solution will be allowing a company’s sensitive data to leave the premises, which is a serious data security issue that needs to be addressed before ISDN implementation.

## Management support

The magnitude of an ISDN implementation project is company-wide; it affects all personnel and its impact is immense. Top management support is de® nitely required for the success of such a project. All companies studied indicated their senior executives had a healthy appreciation for the value of advanced technology in the conduct of business. In the past, the companies had already invested heavily in high information technology innovations. ISDN was just another step toward revolutionizing their communications behaviour to stay competitive. Company B and F even had a Vice President chairing the ISDN project team. Interview responses indicated strong interaction and close working relationships among the senior executives in the general and MIS management staffs throughout the entire implementation project.

## Vendor commitment

There was a consensus among the interviewees that without the total commitment from the ISDN provider, their ISDN migration would have been much more dif-® cult. The support solutions contributed by the providers included visits to the ISDN laboratories and other premises, technical support from the central and regional of® ces, training class coordination, ISDN trainer provision, training documentation offerings, and ISDN experts stationed on premises, among others. This support has been consistent since the beginning of the project. Their commitment continues even after the implementation. Follow-up visits and calls from all areas of ISDN providers, additional training, and enhancement documentation were rendered to update the companies with the latest ISDN developments.

## Proper planning

All companies had established ISDN implementation plans. The extent of planning activities varied across the companies and appeared to be a function of the number of lines installed and the amount of time available for installation. The companies agreed that their planning processes were effective, mainly because al vested interests were taken into consideration. The companies also indicated that their carriers were also involved, either directly or indirectly, in their planning for ISDN. They believed the carrier involvement in their planning process was bene® cial because they were able to make their short- and long-term needs clear to the carrier, thus in¯ uencing the product line planning of the carrier. In such instances, their needs were given some degree of priority, enabling them to suffer less risk and disruption during the implementation and operational stages. All companies indicated that their planning activities allowed them to design and optimize their ISDN to be cost effective, maintainable, and geared to meet their own company’s objectives.

## MIS support

Evolution to ISDN demands a dedicated staff of MIS professionals. The studied companies had either ISDN project teams or network support to deal with all MIS and network activities during the transformation process. According to the interviewees, members that were selected to the ISDN team were the most experienced and capable MIS management staff within their company. Because of the magnitude of the implementation project, the MIS departments of all the studied companies had the total commitment to planning and installing ISDN. ISDN implementation had been their ® rst priority since the idea to evolve to ISDN was initiated and approved. With the exception of those maintaining the basic operations of MIS functions, all computer professionals were totally devoted to ISDN implementation activities.

## Implications

This study represents a signi® cant step in going beyond the anecdotal literature on ISDN by capturing data from organizations that have already implemented ISDN. It conceptualizes the life cycle of this network product and provides preliminary evidence to examine the critical implementation issues and success factors for its installation. However, it should be pointed out that some IS professionals are still wondering about the capability and applicability of this technology. Others are waiting until the ready availability of B-ISDN before investing in this technology. As such, this study is re¯ ective of the stage of evolution of research of ISDN implementation and its applications in organizations. It is our assessment that BRI or PRI can meet the needs of most organizations. Although B-ISDN can be very bene® cial to those organizations with a need for extremely large, variable bandwidth, most organizations either cannot afford, or do not have this great need for B-ISDN technology.

## Implications for practice

Several implications can be derived for practicing managers. First, planning was vital to successful ISDN implementation. Planning allows the project team a chance to review the current network infrastructure and projected implementation strategy. However, management must realize that the key to successful network planning is threefold. They must review set plans on an ongoing basis for their appropriateness in light of current strategies and other plans in development. Then, they must review these plans during the implementation phase to identify any shortfalls not previously anticipated. Finally, they must review these plans to evaluate the effectiveness of installed networks. Strict adherence to these steps is of utmost importance to ensure smooth implementation of networks.

Second, management support and participation in the planning, designing, and implementation of ISDN is necessary for the success of ISDN implementation. Perhaps the most important contribution from management involvement is the provision of more accurate management expectations, establishing a ® rm commitment and understanding of project plans and needs, development of mutually agreed upon project development priorities, narrowing the communication gap between management and members of the implementation team, and committing available resources to the completion of ISDN system. Additionally, management involvement would also provide support to arbitrate political issues resulting from a change of power structure, normally caused by the implementation of an IS system (such as ISDN) that cuts across political boundaries. Thus, practicing managers should try to include management in network projects to enhance the chance of success.

Third, the vendor relationship and participation are critical to success of an ISDN implementation. Prior to implementation, management must identify the responsibilities of both vendor and the organization in regard to ISDN implementation. Management also needs to identify project plans, including schedules for completion of milestones, and available resources that would be allocated to support their ISDN implementation. These elements of planning are critical in understanding the vendors’ roles.

Fourth, ISDN is an evolving technology. There are still many concerns with the implementation and application of this technology. Therefore, it is important that practicing managers do not grasp ISDN as the one true technology, but retain a balanced perspective. As technology development proceeds in cycles that include distinct exploratory and maturation phases, it is wise for practicing managers to adopt ISDN slowly, in an evolutionary manner.

## Implications for research

This study, although it represents a signi® cant step in exploring the implementation details of ISDN, still represents an exploratory approach to data collection and analysis. While only nine successful ISDN implemen tations were investigated, many of the implementation issues and \`lessons learned’ during this study should be further validated through more rigorous empirical investigation.

Further research is also needed to investigate the role of ISDN in gaining and sustaining organizational competitive advantage. For instance, how can organizations strategically use ISDN in a dynamic and volatile business environment to improve their competitive advantage, which may range from improving the ef® ciency of their existing operations to innovating products and services? Although we recognize that overlay and replacement strategies could be adopted to evolve to ISDN, we also discovered that no guidelines were available to steer this evolution process, due to the complexity and heterogeneity of the existing network infrastructure. Research is needed to investigate frameworks of planning, managing, and integrating network utility that would provide easy adaptability to growth and change.

The evolution process to ISDN is not only a result of extensive planning, but also a result of considerable bargaining and mutual adjustment among the involved personnel and groups. Such motive-driven behaviour in IS context needs to be investigated. Several research questions can be raised to address this issue: (1) How can organizations handle motive-driven behaviour of con¯ icting political parties to achieve network or IS planning? (2) What are the impacts of motive-driven behaviour on network or IS planning? (3) How can IS be used to mitigate political issues surrounding the organization’s strategic plan?

ISDN has already been introduced around the world with various degrees of success in its development and implementation. Europe and the Paci® c Rim countries are deploying national ISDN-based networks and have well-delineated plans to evolve ISDN into its subsequent technologies (such as Broadband ISDN). In the United States, the adoption rate and implementation extent of ISDN remain low. Lack of government support, inconsistent implementation standards, absence of tariffs, and ineffective marketing strategy have been proposed as the prohibiting factors for the universal acceptance of ISDN in the United States (Lai et al., 1996). However, from a theory building perspective, further research should be conducted to explore and validate the postulated propositions. Additionally, a more comprehensive model must be developed to capture the variations of diffusion of ISDN among countries and to measure accurately the effects of variables, such as government policy and cultural factors on diffusion processes.
