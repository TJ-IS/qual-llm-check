# Examining information systems outsourcing: a case study from the United Kingdom

ALAN FOWLER and BEN JEFFS

School of Management, University of Newcastle, Newcastle upon Tyne NE1 7RU, UK

This paper examines the issue of outsourcing information services (IS) through the framework of case studybased, grounded research, located in a major UK company. The impact of outsourcing drivers including ef® ciency, IS alignment and the human resource dimension are explored and balanced against the perceived outsourcing risks. It is also postulated that although managers claim rational economic bene® ts, when making outsourcing decisions, they may, in fact, be bounded in their rationality by their perception of the qualit experienced as users of IS/IT. Hence, it is suggested that the real justi® cation for outsourcing may be tha it provides a de® nitive, albeit drastic, vehicle for change.

## Introduction

It has been estimated that up to 40% of corporate capital may typically be invested in information technology (IT) and information systems (IS) expenditure (Benko, 1992). However, it appears that many executives are increasingly dissatis® ed with the perceived returns obtained from their investment. Internal IS costs are perceived to be rising too rapidly, technology is changing too quickly for their companies to remain state-of-the-art and customers often perceive themselves to be inadequately served by existing systems. As a result, many are opting for outsourcing. Consequently, the outsourcing market has grown rapidly both in the USA and in the UK (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992; Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1995; Willcocks et al., 1995; Antonucci and Tucker, 1998).

It therefore appears that many managers conclude that IS is ® rst and foremost a commodity service that should be obtained at the lowest possible price (Benko, 1992). However, it is also recognized that outsourcing is a radical option and its associated decision process is a critical one, especially when viewed from a strategic perspective (Robson, 1997). It implies an ongoing, long-term relationship with considerable complexity and business risk. For example, computing requirements may change radically over the life of the outsourcing contract and the future environment may bear little resemblance to today’ s. Technological advances may also nullify several of the bene® ts of the outsourcing arrangement and reversing course, after the event, is likely to prove very complex and costly.

There is also a growing concern that too many contracts are signed for the wrong reasons, by the wrong people and without competitive tendering, proper service level agreements or monitoring of the contract once it is in place (Kaye, 1993). Finally, there are questions about the prices charged by outsourcing suppliers, and their ability to meet customers’ aims. In short, it appears that the more popular outsourcing becomes then the more the accepted bene® ts are coming to be questioned (Byrne, 1996; Glass, 1996).

Hence, senior executives and those responsible for the management of IS are currently faced with an apparent paradox. Since the 1980s, the strategic importance of IS has been increasingly emphasized as organizations have shifted their focus from the administrative capabilities of IT to a wider recognition of its strategic dimension (McFarlan, 1984; Porter and Millar, 1985; Earl, 1989; Galliers, 1994; Alexander and Young, 1996). However, as noted above, the role of outsourcing in its various forms has, in recent years, achieved considerable prominence in the search for cost savings and solutions to IS problems (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993; Rosenberg and Macaulay, 1993; Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993; Evans, 1994; Frost, 1994; Grover and Teng, 1994). The question therefore arises as to how these two apparently con¯ icting themes can be reconciled? If IS is truly of strategic signi® cance should it not, therefore, be woven inextricably into the strategic fabric of the organization? If so, how justi® able is wholesale outsourcing, which arguably implies that the role of IS is relegated to the status of a commodity?

## Objectives

This paper addresses the paradox outlined above and questions the real reasons which direct organizations towards outsourcing. Are these based on purely rational economic decisions or do other factors impact on, and possibly dominate, the decision-making process? The analysis extends into an evaluation of the longer term implications of outsourcing with respect to an organization’s core competencies, its ability to adapt to change and its cost-effectiveness.

The study explores and develops these questions through the medium of a case study featuring qualitative research undertaken in a major UK company. The research is grounded in theory, drawing reference to published literature and linking this to observations arising in the ® eld research. By this means it supplements existing case material covering experiences both in Europe and in the USA (Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993; Corbett, 1994; Lacity et al., 1994, 1995; Cross, 1995; Willcocks, 1995; Collins and Miller, 1995).

In embarking on the study, the researchers were particularly mindful of the suggestion by Lacity and Hirschheim (1995) that much of the literature tends to be overly optimistic because evaluation is often based on experiences during the \`honeymoon period’ when customer and vendor ® rst sign a contract. In addition, it was noted that outsourcing failures may be under-represented in the literature, probably due to the fact that it is rare, in practice, for any organization to offer a failure for public scrutiny. Also, notably, a number of the articles appearing have been sponsored by outsourcing vendors and it is therefore dif® cult to ascertain the level of objectivity in these particular studies. Hence, the intention in this particular case was to present a balanced view of the issues, as perceived by key individuals within a particular organization, during consideration of the outsourcing option. The paper may therefore be summarized as a review and analysis of observations arising from case research, presented within the context of existing literature, with the aim of substantiating the research base and contributing to the outsourcing debate.

## Research methodology

In addressing the objectives outlined above it was considered necessary to explore the level of motivation, degree of understanding and enthusiasm for outsourcing amongst those who were responsible for the assessment and decision-making processes within a particular organization. Structured interviews were used, enabling a degree of spontaneous probing to uncover deeper issues of concern which might not readily be surfaced by other methods. Individuals were selected for interview primarily on the basis that they were involved, knowledgeable about or otherwise in¯ uential as stakeholders in the outsourcing decision.

A set of open questions re¯ ecting the objectives of the research was drawn up and the interviews were tailored to suit the role of the respondents within the organization. Three of those interviewed were senior business managers with direct experience of IS and two others were primarily senior IS managers with some busines experience. Tape recordings, transcribed and subsequently validated by the interviewees, featured in the process, thereby re¯ ecting the grounded theory principles of Glaser and Strauss (1967) in which the simultaneous objectives of problem solving and knowledge contribution are to be jointly served. Such a methodology is considered to be highly appropriate as a means of generating models and hypotheses (Gummerson, 1991).

The interviews speci® cally focused on managerial perceptions of IS/IT services and facilities including their strategic dimensions, quality and susceptibility to outsourcing. Hence, interviews were structured around a number of subject areas and designed to converge systematically on the core outstanding issue including the following.

(1) The role of the interviewee.

(2) General issues of IS/IT, thereby establishing a framework in which key concepts, major frustrations and strategic insights could be surfaced.

(3) Perceptions of the potential advantages and drivers of outsourcing.

(4) Perceptions of the potential dangers of out sourcing.

(5) Business alignment issues.

(6) Human resource issues with respect to IS/IT provision.

(7) Implementation factors.

(8) The outsourcing decision process.

Hence, the research progressed by an iterative approach as depicted in Figure 1.

The transcripts from the interviews were sifted and the contents collected into appropriate categories. Many of the points raised were considered to be spe ci® c to the company but others which appeared to be of more generic interest or which were reiterated prominently by different managers during the research were speci® cally noted, categorized and quoted where appro priate. No attempt was made to quantify the responses obtained as this appeared somewhat impractical given the qualitative nature of the research. However, it was possible to present a clear, qualitative evaluation and assessment of the responses obtained.

## Research observations

In conducting the interviews, a range of speci® c issues were targeted for consideration, as re¯ ected in the questions outlined in the methodology section above. The individuals selected for interview had been identi® ed as key ® gures in the outsourcing debate within the organization, their respective roles being:

(1) Manager 1: a senior IS manager responsible for the provision of information services to the distribution network.

(2) Manager 2: a senior distribution operations manager.

(3) Manager 3: a procurement manager responsible for purchasing outsourced IS (particularly knowledgeable and experienced in outsourcing).

(4) Manager 4: a former IS account manager responsible for the interface between IS and business needs.

(5) Manager 5: a data centre manager.

The transcribed and validated notes from the interviews were coordinated under a number of headings, as presented below, broadly corresponding to the key issues to be explored.

(1) IS outsourcing drivers.

(2) IS alignment issues.

(3) HRM (human resource management) issues.

(4) Implementation and general concerns.

These were then considered in turn, in the light of comments appearing in the literature, in accordance with the original research objectives.

## IS outsourcing drivers

A number of the interview questions were intended to explore the perceptions of the drivers of the outsourcing momentum within the company. Manager 1 commented that \`Outsourcing deals are basically about improvements in ef® ciency, a more manageable way of running an operation that is not core to your business and can actually work to everyone’s bene® t. Catering deals, OR (operations research) deals seem to have worked very well’.

In representing a customer and user perspective, this manager claimed that the IS value debate is based on

Frustration over the failure of IS to deliver on cost, within time, to deliver tangible bene® ts, to quantify those bene® ts, to be actually seen to realize the change and to see the improvements in the business that it says it can deliver. It needs to avoid getting a reputation of just front ending and developing the IS philosophy without necessarily being in tune with the business direction. I think overall, from my point of view, it is almost as though IS has always been there delivering as it sees ® t, not necessarily listening to what the customer is actually asking for.

## From an IS position manager 1 claimed that

We have, maybe, over-engineered solutions; taken too long, gold plated. We have always looked for leading edge, maybe even bleeding edge, which is good to a degree but not always the right approach. We have failed to bridge the gap between the user and the technician so we have hidden away behind jargon. We have hidden away behind methodologies. We have made it confusing. It has become mystical. I think, all in all, the business has become frustrated with how IS genuinely contributes to effectiveness. I do not think there is a dispute that it does contribute but it is quantifying it, it is rationalizing it. It is presenting it in such a way that it is an effective means to deliver solutions. It is not an end in itself; it is a tool. The frustration over time is the key point.

Asked whether all non-core activities should be outsourced manager 1 replied

Yes, in reality the only argument is why should they not be. What would justify them being inside the business. From a purely business perspective, any business would say yes. If it genuinely does not need to retain all of its non-core activities, there are others better placed to manage them.

Outsourcing was also seen as a response to rapidly changing business circumstances: \`This organization has got nine data centres it does not need. They just do not ® gure in the plans. Why would the business want to maintain an asset just gathering dust? It wants the legacy split out.’

## According to manager 2

The major reason for outsourcing has got to be value for money. I am not convinced that there are people out in the market-place who can build much better systems. I think that the company has had a very high quality of applications within IS but I think we have had to pay for it.

He also recognized the need to introduce a change in culture and attitude to IS development within the organization.

We have to get people in the habit that just because they work for this business, they will not always have bespoke systems which have been developed just for them. Maybe there are systems out there which have been developed for someone else which will give us everything that we need.

He also claimed that outsourcing generally provided better access to \`marketing type skills’. \`Outsourcing will also give us new ideas. Outsourcing would give us the option on a regular basis to dip into the market, creating a dynamic environment.’

Manager 3 similarly claimed that outsourcing could provide leverage to new technologies. He was also of the opinion that outsourcing would provide ® nancial bene® ts claiming that \`It would help reduce the company’s head count, increase the responsiveness of IS and improve the turnaround of requests. Outsourcing would provide a lever to ® nancial bene-® ts and help to changing existing processes.’

When asked which activities should be outsourced and why, manager 3 stated that the question was \`not why should activities be outsourced but why should they not be’.

He also thought that outsourcing may be increasing in popularity because of the challenge it now places higher in the management structure. In the past, senior IT managers and directors had been prompted from within and were generally technical specialists. However, there was traditionally very little interest at board level in IS/IT and there was a credibility gap between IT and the rest of the business. Authorization papers for expenditure were not suf® ciently challenged and IT had accrued a large amount of power. He also claimed that within IS/IT there was too much emphasis on doing and not enough on planning. Finally, he suggested that the emergency of these issues, coupled with the prevailing 1980s macho management culture, had resulted in senior management turning on IS/IT so that it subsequently became popular to contemplate getting rid of it altogether. He added that the marginalization of IS/IT is not the only case where this has happened and a similar backlash was occurring in personnel, legal services and ® nance.

## Examining information systems outsourcing

Manager 4 was of the opinion that IS was one of the limited number of ways of helping the company to reduce its costs. However, he believed that \`IT has a reputation, some of it deserved, for being very ©ivory towerishº ; wrapped in mystique, being over-technica or over-specialist and, frankly, not delivering’.

He argued that a historical problem had been that information services used to give the users what they thought they needed, rather than what they really needed. He argued that the people who supported the service provision, the network and the of® ce systems could, quite easily, be outsourced. \`The code generation is something you could send off to Bombay’.

Manager 4 also claimed that the company should consider insourcing other people’s outsourced IS/IT: \`There is a major opportunity in the British Isles at the moment, because of legislation over government sites and the need to operate their IS activities on a lowest cost tendering basis’ .

Finally, this manager also acknowledged that outsourcing could be used as a threat to the internal organization to ensure that change actually happens.

Manager 5 also perceived cost reduction to be a major driver but also emphasized the importance of freeing up time to concentrate on core business:

Outsourcing IS is seen as the possibility of reducing costs and an opportunity not to have to concentrate senior managers’ time on that activity so they can spend more time on other things; hopefully making better decisions. This is certainly what is driving the managing director.

He was also particularly sceptical about the role of external consultants in promoting outsourcing.

This country and the US, more than any others, are driven by consultants. In Japan there is no consultancy at all and in Japanese companies they actually have the skills within the company. If they need to do a major project, they do not spend a lot of money bringing in people from outside who do not even know the business. I think that one of the worst things this company does is spend so much money on consultants.

## Alignment issues

The issue of alignment with the business strategy and the need to move from a functional to a process perspective were recurrent themes in the interviews. Manager 1 stated that

The problem with IS is that it has had to drive its solutions on a functional basis; it has driven them down very functionalized silos. It has seen payroll as part of the ® nance application which ® nance have driven; the engineering side drive theirs and the service side drive theirs, with very little interaction or interfacing going on. We need to hold the information once, bring it in once, utilize and present a client± server sort of relationship so that you have things tied together and build the systems along a process. Then you are completely driven from start to end of that process, all the way, cutting through all that functionality.

Manager 1 also referred to the problem of de® ning user needs, particularly at the executive level: \`IS, as an industry, is not good at truly rationalizing its information into some form of executive need, because the executive do not always know what they need’.

He also noted that the users did not know what they wanted because they did not truly understand the ful process:

The bit in marketing did not know what the bit in ® nance had or what the bit in purchasing had. If you have got a process, everybody has to know everything otherwise the process collapses. If you strip away function, you strip away the old \`we only know out bit’ attitude. Then you can put in place systems which support the whole activity.

Manager 2 emphasized the need for simplicity, ¯ exibility and user focus in order to promote alignment:

Things change. You can bet that what you want today will change and in 6 months time you will want something else. So there is no point producing systems which are hard coded and all those things that make it dif® cult to change. I would like to see systems where the users are more IS literate; that have some ¯ exible query language where the user can go in and manipulate the data to suit themselves. I think we should keep things simple. We have spent too long trying to satisfy everybody’s requirements. In fact, that is what we should do, design much simpler systems, certainly more ¯ exible systems. Above all, I would keep it simple because I think that our systems have been far too complex. Systems often seem to be developed as an intellectual exercise for people in IS.

Manager 2 cited the example of a Management Information System which was beautifully designed but which was, for the majority, of no real use.

IS wraps things up in technicalities that you cannot understand and gives users less respect than they should have. In my experience IS people tell you what you need rather than asking you what you want. I would not say there is a good cultural ® t. I guess for the vast majority of managers, most people in IS speak a different language. But I think it is the same for engineers and accountants. I think, for every profession I have ever worked with, it is the same. I do not think IS is any different.

Commenting on the issue of control over the IS function, manager 2 commented that

I guess IS is probably one area of the business that you never really have control of. I have got a lot of in¯ uence in personnel; we can even get accountants to change their actions sometimes, but with IS there is no overall control. It is like sailing a catamaran when the two hulls are not joined together. The technical jargon can bamboozle you and they think we do not understand it. Why should we? It is not our business. It is all very technical. They have got their rules and the whole scenario is one of a closed shop. I am surprised we have taken it for so long.

Manager 3 also claimed that although there were problems on both sides, internal IS should see its primary role as interfacing with the business like an external supplier.

Manager 4 also argued that a historical problem existed, in that information services used to give the users what they thought the users needed rather than what they actually needed. However, he claimed the business had now moved away from this practice and that \`Information services are now doing exactly what they are told to do as far as meeting business requirements are concerned. Unfortunately the business has not got its act together, to be able to actually describe, in any sensible way, what its overall needs are.’

In fact he felt that IS was now aligned with the business to the extent that it was almost showing the business the way forward in some cases.

He also stated that business managers in the past have been \`very insular’ and have tended to duplicate their IS activities and IS costs because they have the power to specify and implement their own systems.

Asked whether IS/IT could market itself better, manager 4 claimed that one way to overcome the ivory tower image and the mystique of IT was by transferring the responsibility for some of the more minor but traditional IS tasks, such as resetting passwords or the movement of desktop hardware, to the end-user community.

Finally, manager 4 argued that with regard to IS, the company should make sure that it \`Knows what it wants, speci® es what it wants, has the discipline to stay with what it wants or is prepared to take the consequences of changing. It should also know when it’ s got something which it asked for.

Manager 5 commented that one of the problems is that business does not set clear targets each year: \`They do not say IS, here is your targets. At the top level, up until recently, IT has not been seen as a major player: they are out on the side of the business; marginalized.

This was attributed to the fact that IS is not represented at the board level.

Asked whether IS staff had a suf® cient business focus, manager 5 opined that 95% of the IS staff hardly think about the business. They obviously understand their own little niche but in terms of the business in general, they show little interest.

Asked whether IT has been effectively managed, manager 5 stated that

No, certainly in the past we did not concentrate on business requirements in the way that I think we should have done. We were driven by technology rather than business requirements. It took IT a long time to understand that it was there to serve the business. I think it does that to a large degree now.

Manager 5 also claimed that at certain levels there is still a reluctance to accept IS. He also stated that the company had not been very successful in implementing EIS (Executive Information System) and MIS systems.

There is loads of information. There have been many attempts at it and we have never really got that act together. It seems as if there are different people at senior levels who have different views on what they would like. There has not really been an acceptance of the need for it. We do not have a good EIS system which sits there and helps managers make good decisions. I think that it is easy for them to get certain information quickly, but I do not think they have got a smart system which really helps people.

He quoted e-mail as an example that was over-used for trivial \`do not need to know purposes’, commenting that it was too easy to push information around and that, consequently, information overload was likely to be a continuing source of dif® culty.

## Human resources issues

This group of questions was intended to elicit perceptions about the role and attributes of IS professionals within the organization. Manager 1 strongly empha sized the need for IS professionals to be deeply inter ested in the core business: \`You have to have people who truly understand the business need and, where an executive is not always as clear as he could be, someone who can go in and can rationalize it. That is where I focus quite strongly on the new sort of business process re-engineering laboratories.’

This raised the rhetorical question concerning how many IS people have actually worked in the business proper.

## Manager 1 noted that

Once you have identi® ed with the other side of the business you have got a better feel for how you manage systems and what the system needs are. I think that a major failing in our business is that we do not encourage or develop good, complementary, business-trained, technical people. It is the same with engineers; they are all engineers, they have never seen another facet of the business. That is being narrow.

Manager 1 was also aware of the need to maintain and extend awareness.

You need to be outward looking. If you are going to be the best you have to know what is going on amongst the best. Every now and again we get some third party interest coming in when we are looking at these issues and, all of a sudden, you are asking God, why did we not think about that? You have these ideas bombarding you and they spin you off into a different arena. So from a technological point of view, I look at bringing in people, when I have got an issue, with a view to introducing external perspectives on how to move it forward.

Manager 2 claimed that in the past IS attracted certain types of people who were not generally well rounded in business or were not aware of business: \`If you recruit from within, as IS tends to do, then your ©techiesº become managers and so you never really break that cycle’.

Manager 3 also claimed that internal IS managers and personnel generally lacked commercial skills and that there was insuf® cient investment in training: \`Few possess general business quali® cations such as DSM, MBAs, even BTEC quali® cations in business. Many technical specialists become managers, through internal promotion.

Manager 3 also suggested the possible existence of, what he termed, a conspiracy theory in that hardware and software vendors invest signi® cant amounts of time and effort in supporting their customer’s technical personnel, knowing that they will one day become the IS managers responsible for purchasing decisions.

Manager 4 was also of the opinion that IS staff did not have an appropriate level of business knowledge and that business users would like IS personnel to understand their business better. He thought that the culture in the company had been shaped by its dominant market position and its historical industry status. He claimed that because of this culture, IS staff were still relatively complacent despite all the changes that were occurring. He also mentioned that, in future, failure might be more expensive at the personal level.

Manager 5 noted that \`The theory is that it (IS/IT) ought to facilitate change, but I am sure that in many cases we are actually seen to prevent change. IT should always be business driven or business led but we certainly were not in the past.

Quoting a recent major centralization project, manager 5 commented tha

With something as big as this there was not anyone at the right level who was fully IT literate (you are talking almost director level). I believe they were sold on a solution which the vast majority of IT people at a middle manager level, down within the company, thought was ¯ awed.

Manager 5 also commented that IS traditionally has not produced good managers.

There are too many people who, and this is not just true of IS, like to get their hands dirty and get into too much detail. We have some clever IT people who perhaps do not have their strength in management. It is a shame really because the company is not getting the best from these people.

He also commented on the professional status of IS professionals: \`I do not think they are regarded the same as quali® ed people like accountants. Certainly in this business engineers have always been dominant up till now, though this is possibly going to change.’

## Outsourcing implementation and general groups

The interviews led managers to contemplate the strategic and operational factors associated with implementing outsourcing arrangements, which in turn led to a discussion of general concerns.

Manager 1 emphasized that \`I would never, ever, advocate outsourcing the strategic decision making of IS. The control of our strategic approach to IS must never be relinquished.’

He added that

Systems are only core if there are elements of the data which make your business unique. If you relinquish that information you are then compromising your business operation. If you let it go too far, you may ® nd that they (the vendor) are ultimately determining your technical platform, your technical strategy, your capability to change. They can determine your response to business needs.

Asked about the vulnerability of the company, should it outsource, only later to encounter an inevitable economic change as the business environment cycle changes, manager 1 replied

I am very conscious of these fads. I think that we are at the fad stage with outsourcing, because, again, everyone is in a cost reduction stage. The view was, have you cost reduced? To get your numbers down you simply throw it to another service supplier. But what happens when you go on the reverse cycle of the business and you suddenly realize well, I need a development doing tomorrow. Sorry we cannot do it, the business is stretched with all these other customers.

He also expressed reservations about outsourcing during a period of great instability, stressing that \`you obviously do not outsource unstable products’.

Manager 2 suggested that outsourcing should be evaluated on a matrix of the availability of contractors against the criticality of the system (see Figure 3) commenting that

Given the high level of specialist skills required by the company’s core business, there is not much experience outside, so it is all in house. It is the same with running the computer network. The computer network is critical to the running of this business, therefore it should be kept in-house, to make sure it is operating properly and is available when required.

He noted that outsourcing simply to avoid problems was unlikely to succeed: \`ideally you should know your current operation before you outsource; otherwise you may spend even more money, in the long run’.

He was also of the opinion that IS is often used as something of a \`scapegoat’ for wider problems: \`people have always used IS as an excuse and suggested it is a change inhibitor’.

Manager 3 believed that outsourcing often failed because the scope of services is badly de® ned, contracts are badly written and there are poorly de® ned longterm plans: \`The decision is often dictated by chairmen or other senior executives, without any in-house technical/commercial IS/IT advice. There is bad outset planning and a lack of an effective benchmarking strategy or no effective selection criteria for the vendor.

He was of the opinion that in-house should be given the opportunity to demonstrate that it can meet the company’s needs ® rst, before resorting to outsourcing.

Manager 3 claimed that it was important to enter into a contract which allowed all parties, the business, the vendor and the staff, to win. Any savings above those projected should be shared between the company and the vendor on an equal basis, although failure to achieve the projected savings would be at the vendor’s expense. He also stated that the contract should include clear exit points.

Manager 4 claimed that although most of IS/IT could probably be outsourced, \`Total outsourcing is unwise due to the criticality of the service to the business. Therefore, you have to consciously outsource only the parts that you agree are most cost-effective. You then have to decide on the correct interface.’

However, he too was concerned about the reasons behind outsourcing: \`If outsourcing is less expensive then you probably do not understand the business that you are in and the person you are outsourcing it to is going to start to know that very soon. The result of that is that they will start upping the costs, fairly quickly.

He also argued that, because IS is a major part of the business, efforts should be concentrated on integrating it within the business and providing a greater level of self-suf® ciency in the user community. He felt that this should improve the service to the user and reduce costs.

You would actually keep the core people who are specialists in-house designing what you want your network to be and how you want it to operate. Building it, operating it and servicing it are somebody else’s job, but on your terms. This is what you will provide for me, is it not, now tomorrow, the next day.

Manager 5 claimed that outsourcing would be acceptable

If you could do it all contractually and know exactly what things are going to cost you. For example, if it were like a house, you can de® ne a house very easily; architects do it. ## Discussion synthesis and analysis

Based on the iterative process of data collection and analysis, as originally identi® ed in Figure 1, the following salient points emerged as qualitative outcomes of the research process.

## Participants’ perceptions of IS value for money

One of the bene® ts of outsourcing, most frequently quoted in the literature, is that it reduces IS/IT costs with the majority of companies who have outsourced having apparently done so with the expectation of achieving signi® cant reductions (Eggleton and Otter, 1991; James, 1993; Ketler and Walstrom, 1993; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993; Little, 1993; Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993). These savings are typically realized through economies of scale, head count reductions and improved cost-control mechanisms such as resource usage-based costing (Benko, 1992). It is also claimed that outsourcing helps to get a ® rmer grip on IT/IS costs by making them more transparent, thereby encouraging business users to become clearer about what facilities and services they really require.

From the results of the case study interviews, it was also clearly apparent that the managers believed the projected economic bene® ts to be one of the key drivers behind the outsourcing agenda. All of the managers interviewed supported this notion with varying degrees of emphasis. However, a number of other observations were noted, re¯ ective of those cited by Galliers (1992). For example, the in-house development of applications was often seen as taking too long, being too costly, exceeding its budget and often producing systems which were mismatched to the real requirements. IS was also criticized for being too technology led and adopting leading-edge solutions while failing to quantify the bene® ts properly.

It was also stated that information services had failed to provide an adequate recharge mechanism. This meant that business managers did not possess an adequate degree of control over their IS/IT spending which, in turn, prevented them from managing their IS/IT costs effectively.

## Alignment issues and strategic capability

When the provision of IT/IS facilities and services is not part of the core business, outsourcing is often promoted in order to remove day to day operational responsibility and control from senior management, thereby allowing them to focus on core business activities (Quin et al., 1990). Quin (1992) extended this argument to claim that any internal activity that cannot be demon strated to be world class should be outsourced. Outsourcing IS/IT may therefore look very attractive to busy managers as evidenced by the PA Consulting Group Survey (1994) which found that freeing up management time to focus on core business activities was the third most popular bene® t quoted.

These considerations were also clearly evident during the case study interviews, with the issue of strategic capability being seen as vitally important within the outsourcing context. One manager emphasized the positive contribution arising from the current emphasis upon business process orientation as contrasted to prevailing \`functional siloism’. Another emphasized the problems of providing executive decision support and poor IS representation at the board level.

The issue of controlling IS and the perceived tendency of IS to produce products that they thought the users needed, rather than what they really wanted, was also addressed although it was acknowledged that the source of this problem lay on both sides.

This supports the notion that the accurate assessment of IS’s strategic dimension is central to the outsourcing debate. Although IS was not seen as a core business activity it was regarded as a core support activity. There was a clear inclination towards the outsourcing of many IS activities although there was also a substantive consensus that the company should not relinquish its control over IS or outsource its strategic planning activities. It was therefore apparent that management favoured a form of \`bounded outsourcing’ in preference to the wholesale divestment of IS activities with a hierarchy of outsourcing appropriateness being proposed, as depicted in Figure 4. The categories of activities nearest to the base should be regarded as being the most susceptible to outsourcing whilst those nearest to the top are more likely to be retained in-house.

Deciding on the appropriateness of the outsourcing option is obviously related to assessment of the associated level of perceived risk, which in turn is contingent upon the role which IS/IT plays within a particular business. Does IS, as Porter and Millar (1985) suggested, truly provide the organization with a competitive advantage by building barriers to entry, increasing switching costs, differentiating the product, spawning new businesses and lowering the ® rms costs, thereby enhancing its value chain? If so, questions arise concerning the long-term strategic consequences of sacri® cing direct control over these key levers of competitive advantage? This is not only a question of whether IT provides these facilities at present but will it need $\mathbf { t o } ,$ and be able $\mathbf { t o } _ { \pmb { \imath } }$ do so in the future?

This is obviously a key issue facing individual organizations, depending on their particular location in McFarlan’s (1984) \`strategic grid’. Some may view IS/IT as being fundamental to their business and therefore wish to retain it in-house. Others may view it as a commodity service which can be outsourced with impunity. Finally, there are those who regard outsourcing as a suitable means of processing their routine operational activities in a way that does not result in them completely relinquishing strategic control.

It is therefore apparent that the perception of the strategic role of IS is a key to deciding whether or not to outsource. However, it was also notable that there apparently existed an inconsistent de® nition, within the organization, in terms of differentiation between the core and non-core activities. This uncertainty re¯ ects observations in the literature and points towards caution with respect to the possible dangers of outsourcing components of the organization which may be vital to the process of future strategy formulation.

## The human resource dimension

In response to the issue of perceived shortfalls in internal provision, it is often claimed that outsourcing vendors can offer a broad range of state-of-the-art skills and technical knowledge to their clients as they can recruit, hire, train and consistently and economically engage specialized IS personnel on a continuum of projects. The PA Consulting Group UK (1994) found in their survey that this factor was ranked as the most popular reason for outsourcing. Possibly this result re¯ ects the growing popularity of new technologies such as client± server architecture, computer aided software engineering (CASE), fourth generation languages and object-orientation, since some companies are now ® nding that they do not necessarily have the correct mix of skills and knowledge to make the best use of these technologies.

Perceptions of shortfalls in IS management, in terms of inappropriate business focus and commercial awareness, were also apparent in this case, although these were mainly associated with the past. These problems were thought to arise, at least in part, as a consequence of the common practice of promoting IS/IT managers from within. It was frequently commented that good technicians did not necessarily make good managers and that IS/IT staff were too technical and did not have a suf® cient understanding of business issues. In other words, it was perceived that there was a general shortage of \`hybrid management’ skills. Consequently, IS was sometimes seen as being inadequately aligned with the business and this was likened by one manager to \`sailing a catamaran when the two hulls are not joined together’.

It was also suggested that action was needed to change, what was sometimes perceived to be the prevailing culture of complacency and that a more outward looking, marketing-oriented attitude should be engendered. In fact, rather than being seen as a change enabler, IS was sometimes seen as a change inhibitor. The role of business education and training also emerged as issues of concern within this context and outsourcing was seen as a potentially useful mechanism for ensuring that a more outward looking, up to date perspective was maintained. These issue appear to highlight a culture gap typical of that reported by Grindley (1992).

## Reservations and concerns

Many of the factors discussed above broadly support the arguments in the literature promoting the outsourcing solution to perceived IS problems. However, in reviewing the data assembled here, the validity of these arguments may also be challenged on several accounts. Indeed, in sharp contrast to the bene® ts of outsourcing, many writers have identi® ed several shortcomings and potential dangers (Judenburg, 1994; Meyer, 1994). For example, loss of control is potentially seen as an important disadvantage and is cited as the main reason for rejection of this option (Ketler and Walstrom, 1993: PA Consulting Group UK 1994). Other disadvantages include issues of quality control, security and disaster recovery, prompting the question of what guarantee does the IS manager have that the outsourcing vendor will apportion the same of amount of time and effort on an urgent problem as would have accrued if the

Willcocks and Fitzgerald (1993) also found that the actual savings are frequently less than those claimed and are highly dependent on the contract itself and on the approach taken to managing the contract. For example, outsourcing vendors, aware that requirements may change over the life of the contract, may take the initial business as a \`loss leader’ , hoping later to pick up additional business at a higher pro® t margin. Vendors also exploit and retain the bene® ts of price-performance improvements over time. Evans (1994) and King (1994) also warned of the longterm strategic dangers that can arise from a relatively short-term decision to outsource, suggesting that the management of the outsourcing vendors may be more problematic than is initially expected. Finally, it is noted that renegotiation can be a costly exercise (Yates, 1993) and yet, how many companies signing long-term contracts today are certain about planning prospects or know exactly where their business will be in 10 years hence? These were points which were clearly endorsed as concerns expressed during the case study interviews.

Evans (1994) also pointed out that IT can provide a window on the fast evolving world of technology providing advanced warning of signi® cant and imminent changes in the business environment. However, the bene® ts of such insights are less likely to arise in a situation where the IS facility has been outsourced. Interestingly, a rather different view was revealed by one of the managers in this case, as evidenced by his comment that one of the bene® ts of outsourcing was to engender a more outward looking perspective in order to exploit the potential bene® ts in technological developments.

Other potential strategic \`disbene® ts’ appearing in the literature and broadly supported by the case research include (1) a reduction in long-term ¯ exibility to meet changing business requirements, (2) irreversibility potentially inherent in outsourcing contracts and (3) dif® culty and expense associated with bringing IS back in-house if required at a later date.

Closely related to these concerns is the contribution of Lacity et al. (1995) which offers additional advice in terms of questions such as the following.

(1) Is this system truly strategic?

(2) Are we certain that IT requirements will not change?

(3) Can a commodity system actually be \`broken off ?’

(4) Do we have the expertise to negotiate and maintain a suitable outsourcing contract?

## Outsourcing orthodoxy challenged

In reviewing the comments and observations arising in the research a number of points may be raised, challenging the current enthusiasm for outsourcing. Firstly, it appears that the principle of maximizing ¯ exibility and strategic control should be generally endorsed rather than simply emphasizing cost factors in the decision analysis. Indeed, it may be argued that these bene-® ts are not automatically or uniquely achievable through outsourcing. Organizations may be able to achieve similar cost ef® ciencies in-house and, if so, they may be in a position to outperform the outsourcing providers since all of the associated added value is then retained rather than being distributed as the outsourcer’s pro® t margin. Furthermore, there is evidence of contracts having to be renegotiated or brought back in-house, both outcomes requiring a signi® cant amount of management time and expenditure.

The concept that a company can build and maintain a strategic partnership, based on a relatively loose contractual arrangement, may also be disputed. Since the company and its vendor do not share a discrete pro® t motive it is unlikely that they can exist as \`true partners’ and it then becomes necessary to look for other means of contractual control. This leads to a recognition of one of the fundamental problems of outsourcing contracts, namely that it is necessary to work out the details in advance and to plan for the unexpected to a signi® cant and often unrealistic leve of re® nement. However, in practice, companies often have dif® culties in de® ning and quantifying the services provided in-house and are therefore unable to specify their requirements adequately within an external contract. This point was clearly echoed in the case interviews. Unfortunately, the failure to do so often results in the need to secure additional services at a later date. These are often charged at a premium fee thereby increasing the overall cost. It is thereby evident that outsourcing arrangements are heavily dependent on the quality of the contract and the approach taken to managing and monitoring it.

The argument that outsourcing frees up managers time to concentrate on the attainment of strategic focus is also challenged since, in practice, the monitoring and controlling of outsourcing contracts can require a signi® cant amount of management time. Improved access to an expanded knowledge and skills base may also be viewed as a potential fallacy as evidence exists to suggest that companies who outsource are generally supported by the same personnel as before.

Finally, the proposition that outsourcing promotes a strategic focus appears to be potentially outweighed by the dangers inherent in sacri® cing strategic control and the associated ¯ exibility to adapt in times of change.

Arguably IS/IT strategy should therefore be based on combining and honing technical and commercial skills thereby enabling the organization with the capability to exploit market anomalies and new opportunities quickly, as they arise. This would seem to require particular attention to the HR-related issues featured in the case interviews. IS strategy should therefore be about identifying which activities are true commodities and can therefore be safely contracted out, as contrasted with those activities which are core value adders that should be developed internally to provide a superior competitive advantage.

This ambiguity, clearly expressed in the literature concerning the bene® ts attributable to outsourcing, was also evident amongst the managers interviewed here. There was concern at the possible danger of outsourcing strategic components of the business, possibly without even realizing that this was occurring. It was felt that this could too easily happen particularly if those making the ® nal decision were not fully aware of IT issues and were therefore susceptible to signing unfavourable contracts. In particular, problems were envisioned with specifying practical and suf® - ciently detailed contracts and monitoring the service subsequently provided. It was felt this could end up by consuming more rather than less managerial time, hence defeating the original objective.

There was also concern that core IS design skills could be lost and future strategies jeopardized. This would be particularly problematic if, in future, it was decided to reintegrate IS as an in-house activity once again. One manager thought that managing the IT network was so critical to the success of the business that loss of control through outsourcing could be particularly damaging.

It was also felt that attempting to outsource unstable products or attempting to \`outsource away’ problems was impractical and that short-term ® nancial pressure on the cost focus might mask the longer term perspective. It was also suggested that if an outsourcing vendor could do the job and make a pro® t, then why could the same not be achieved internally?

## The implications of imperfect rationality

In reviewing the prevailing attitudes towards IS, as evidenced in the interviews and in the literature, the comments of Ryan (1991) appear particularly apposite when he drew analogies between the potential demise of IS, in part through outsourcing, with the decline of the medieval Craft Guilds of Europe. These institutions, originally established in order to foster and protect the skills of their members, eventually frustrated their clients (the merchant class) to the point where they were ® nally bypassed and eclipsed by more directly controllable sources of production.

Upon analysing the results of the interviews and framing these within the context of the outsourcing literature, it appeared that the pro® le and growth of the popularity of outsourcing may indeed re¯ ect more subjective factors relating to the perceived quality, responsiveness and controllability of the in-house IS/IT service provision. Another important factor may derive from the dif® culties and frustrations experienced when attempting to align IS/IT functions with the main business which they support. However, the research found that although managers identi® ed problems and frus trations with the provision of their in-house services, they did not feel that these would unduly in¯ uence the outsourcing decision and they emphasized that such decisions would be based purely on assessed, economically rational bene® ts.

However, the work of Simon (1978), who postulated the concepts of \`bounded rationality’ and \`satis-® cing’, has shown that managerial decision making is not usually scienti® cally logical and rigorous, but is truncated in order to accommodate cognitive, temporal and informational constraints. The parallel concept of imperfect rationality (Harrison, 1987; Carter and Jackson, 1990), introduces the additional dimensions of power and subjective preference which suggests that perceptions based on these are almost certain to in¯ uence the decision-making process in practice. In this sense, the term bounded or imperfect rationality is used to convey the notion that managers make decisions which do not simply invoke the purely rational economic paradigm, but may be based on highly subjective, possibly even subliminal perceptions of key factors.

This does not necessarily imply that they are deliberately reducing the number of alternatives considered in order simply to reduce mental overload. Neither doe it represent an attempt to reduce uncertainty. In fact, the intrinsically high uncertainty associated with out sourcing cannot be reduced simply through more data collection, since future technology changes, business changes and organizational requirements are inherently and unavoidably unpredictable. However, it does imply that the real reasons for selecting the outsourcing option may be partially obscured. Consequently, claims of purely rational economic behaviour may not be entirely convincing as it appears questionable that managers would remain dispassionately unaffected by the array of perceived shortcomings identi® ed during the interviews.

It is therefore suggested that companies may too often seek to make structural changes in order to compensate for the strategy failures of the past or to correct existing perceptions of dysfunction. It follows that outsourcing may emerge as one such structural change which can too easily occur without adequate analysis of the full strategic implications. However, the difference between outsourcing and some other forms of structural change is that it is far more radical and the costs and risks of failure are potentially far more signi® cant. It therefore follows that companies may not always fully appreciate the degree to which outsourcing may impact upon their strategic capability and their ability to react to changes in their external environment.

The full impact of these concerns may be placed within the context de® ned by Mintzberg and Quin (1991), who argued that it is the organizational structure which houses the organization’s capabilities and it is this structure which plays a major role in determining strategy by constraining and conditioning it, as well as guiding it. Mintzberg (1990) concluded that both strategy and structure contribute to supporting the organization and that strategy formulation should be part of an integrated system which must include structure. If this is accepted then it follows that outsourcing potentially diminishes an organization’s IS/IT strategic capability as it moves a large part of the organizational structure outside the company to the outsourcing vendor.

These considerations rekindle the \`design school debate’ which has featured in the business strategy literature for some time (Ansoff, 1991; Mintzberg, 1991). This concerns the issue of whether strategy can be planned and set in advance from the top of the organization, or whether it emerges or is crafted, often at the operational level, from within the organization. Under the \`crafting’ or \`emergent strategy paradigm’, the strategy process is integrated into the framework of the business so that there is a sense of universal ownership which helps foster the necessary commitment required at the implementation stage. An emergent strategy also recognizes that not all the facts will be known in advance and, given that the future is unpredictable, it therefore enables the organization to develop the requisite skills to adapt and survive.

Clearly the outcome of this debate, which at present appears to be far from resolution, potentially produces a signi® cant impact on the IS outsourcing decisionmaking process. It is apparent that if the logic of the \`strategy crafting school’ is upheld, then even the bounded form of outsourcing, as generally favoured by the managers featured in this case study, will have a signi® cant impact on the organization’s strategic capability. Consequently, this and other organizations should be particularly wary of large-scale outsourcing, even when retaining some \`strategic’ activities in-house. It is therefore suggested that each activity should, in turn, be carefully considered, to establish that it really does have little or no strategic content, now or in the foreseeable future and is therefore, by de® nition, truly a commodity item which can be regarded as a safe candidate for outsourcing. Even then, it may be argued that the strategic control of these activities should be kept ® rmly in-house.

In summary, it appears that the current popularity of outsourcing may, at least in part, be a response to senior executives’ frustration with their IS professionals which in turn, paradoxically, comes from a lack of strategic attention given to information management. Galliers (1995) interestingly postulated that, with respect to outsourcing, there exists the possibility of history repeating itself, citing an analogy with the user frustration which helped spawn the PC revolution in the 1980s. This, in turn, led to problems of disintegration and \`islands of information’ which in due course prompted attempts at reintegration through networking and client± server architecture. He summarized by asking whether the outsourcing of today wil inevitably lead to parallel problems and a search for corresponding solutions in the future, as the unrecog nized de® cits of an apparently attractive solution mature with time. This observation is highly reminiscent of Senge’s (1990) \`® x that fails’ archetype which presented a generic model of the dynamics through which initially attractive outcomes, which result from decisions, based on bounded or imperfect rationality, are often superseded by very unwelcome long-term consequences.

## Conclusions

In presenting this case study the objective has been to review the outsourcing debate in the context of investigations performed in an organization which was actively involved in its consideration, at a speci® c point in time. Hence, \`rightsourcing’ issues have been examined through the perceptions of a number of managers who were currently confronted by the \`outsourcing paradox’. Although such a case study methodology cannot claim to offer prescriptive solutions or de® nitive statements of best practice, it does potentially present a useful linkage between theory and practice, allowing a number of points to be raised and suggestions made.

Hence, it is con® rmed that outsourcing may, in some cases be represented by an over-simpli® ed view in which analysis focuses too readily on the rationa weighing of costs, bene® ts and risks whilst ignoring the role of other factors, often internal and hidden, which leads managers to make outsourcing decisions. It is also suggested that although managers make outsourcing decisions claiming economically rational bene® ts, a danger exists in that they may in fact be paying insuf® cient attention to the full array of more subtle costs and risks which may be involved.

They may thereby miss the point that many of the bene® ts, allegedly attributable to outsourcing, can often be achieved internally, with a much lower degree of risk. This suggests that they are not always behaving with rigorous rationality, but in making outsourcing decisions are, in fact, bounded in their rationality by a number of factors, not least the fact, that they themselves are also users of the IS/IT service.

It has also been suggested that it is unlikely that problems can be consistently and effectively \`outsourced away’ without substantial internal efforts. Organizations need to address the problems and issues relating to the provision of the in-house service, at source, prior to implementing outsourcing. Not least, they will need to perform this exercise in order to be in a position to effectively benchmark outsourced facilities against their current in-house provision, bearing in mind that without adequate benchmarking the organization would be unable to quantify the bene® ts achieved effectively, anyway. It can therefore be argued that the failure to address these internal dif® culties will inevitably impact on the successful negotiation and management of an outsourcing contract and that this, in turn, may lead to disappointing results.

However, given that this degree of analysis should be conducted as a preliminary to signing an outsourcing deal, it is argued that it may be worthwhile considering again alternative strategies, based on improving and retaining in-house capabilities. Such alternatives include mechanisms such as a closer alignment between IS and its parent business, the development of hybrid management skills and the implementation of adequate recharge mechanisms.

The study also suggests that recourse to outsourcing may re¯ ect a response to the problems generally experienced in managing and integrating the IS/IT function. Frustration at the user level, combined with cultural alienation between IT and senior management, can result in an impatience to change the relationship between IT and the business radically. Hence, outsourcing comes to be seen as the ® nal solution in the change management itinerary which cuts through the smokescreens, prevarication and defence initiatives mounted by the internal IS function. It is therefore suggested that it is this factor, rather than IS/IT having come to be perceived as a commodity item, which has led to the current preference for outsourcing.

It is further suggested that should an organization prove capable of resolving these internal dif® culties, then outsourcing need not necessarily appear as such an attractive option, since the costs and risks involved might outweigh the advantages. Conversely, it is argued that a concerted attempt to integrate IS fully in order to exploit its full strategic potential should be carefully considered, including a full appraisal of the implications for corporate vision and strategy.

The research concludes that the current rush towards wholesale outsourcing should be viewed with some circumspection. Each activity should be individually scrutinized and evaluated to ascertain its strategic signi® cance and to assess whether or not it could be provided more effectively and cheaply in the marketplace. This may be referred to as \`rightsourcing’ or \`smartsourcing’. These appear to be merely acronyms for the traditional business process of contracting out, although an important difference must be acknowledged in that with contracting out control is retained de® nitively in-house, whereas with outsourcing this outcome may be uncertain.

It is noteworthy that the organization featured in this study is, at the time of writing, currently undertaking another major strategic review and although many of the drivers identi® ed in the paper remain extant, there is now evidence of a general move away from the previously accepted preference for the pure outsourcing of IS/IT. This change in perspective derives, in the main, from recognition of the relative immaturity of the new business structure and the uncertainty in its commercial environment, which implies that a need exists to retain a greater degree of control internally in order to manage the associated risks effectively. However, the drive for increased productivity and ef® ciency is also recognized and remains a powerful driving factor. The company is therefore currently in the process of creating a wider strategic relationship with a major IT services provider with the aim of supplying IT-enabled business solutions on a more commercial basis. This is a two-stage process, the ® rst step of which comprises an internal phase associated with \`getting the house in order’ and creating a commercially viable entity. Following review, this process may eventually lead to the creation of a formal (external) joint venture.

In summary, it is contested that if IS is seen as a major strategic factor within an organization then its strategic and operational facets must be clearly delineated and appropriately enshrined within the organization. Only when it is clear that this can be achieved within an outsourcing context should the outsourcing option ® nally be adopted.

## Biographical notes

Alan Fowler is senior lecturer in operations and information-systems management in the Department of Management Studies, Newcastle University. A chartered mechanical and marine engineer by background he has previously held a number of industrial posts in the engineering and marine industries and was a lecturer and consultant in engineering for several years before extending his academic interests into the management area. He holds BSc and MSc degrees from Edinburgh and Newcastle Universities, respectively, a PhD from Newcastle University and an MBA from Durham University.

Ben Jeffs is an internal consultant to a major UK company and has worked on a number of major change projects. Having started his IT career as a capacity planner he has held several positions in IS manage ment having direct experience of knowledge management, account management and benchmarking. More recently, he has been involved in the establishment of a strategic partnership with a major IS/IT company for the supply of IT-enabled business solutions. He holds a BA degree from Northumbria University and an MBA from Newcastle University.
