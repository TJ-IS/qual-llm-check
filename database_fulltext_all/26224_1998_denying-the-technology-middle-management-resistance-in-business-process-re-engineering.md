# Denying the technology: middle management resistance in business process re-engineering

KYLIE SAYER

School of Computing Sciences, University of Technology, Sydney (UTS), PO Box 123 Broadway, NSW 2007 Australia

Business Process Re-engineering (BPR) as a methodology for organizational transformation has been around since the early 1990s. There are however, some serious issues of concern surrounding the success of BPR as a methodology for organizational change. This paper attempts to highlight the issues surrounding the BPR approach to change management that remain implicit within the re-engineering literature. The research presented in this paper specifically investigates BPR in practice and concentrates on the issues of power and control that underlie many of the re-engineering methodologies and how these conflict with the prerequisites of the flattened, empowered workforce that BPR envisions. The research presented here was conducted using the interpretive approach of ethnography to investigate how middle management were able to deny the technology of BPR and disable a change project. In order to maintain their hold on power in the organization they needed to revolt against the technology of re-engineering imposed on them by senior management. In using the clinical metaphor, middle management were able to create a reality around technology that portrayed it almost as a function of biological warfare, denying it being seen as a positive social tool. This paper highlights how they were able to manipulate the technology and turn its power around to reinforce their position and power in the process.

## Introduction

This paper investigates one of the many organizations that have gone through Business Process Re-engineering (BPR) since its widespread uptake in the early 1990s. The organization sought to re-engineer their communication processes with the introduction of computer mediated communications. As it stands today the organization does not have a process focus, does not have an empowered workforce and has used information technology (IT) only as a tool for automation. In this organization middle management became the key figures in constraining the re-engineering effort. This paper seeks to highlight why the BPR attempt was unsuccessful and by what process the organization came to negate re-engineered communication. In doing this it is important to look at the way in which BPR, and particularly IT, were put into the organizational discourse and how they were manipulated by middle management in order to deny them.

Middle management believed the empowerment enabled through re-engineered communication threatened their position and power within the organization. This paper looks at how organizational members are able to shape an object in order to deny it. In using the clinical metaphor, middle management were able to create a reality around the technology of re-engineering that portrayed it almost as a function of biological warfare. Management were able to deny it being seen as a positive social tool, the clinicalization of IT establishing its unnaturalness to the culture. In order to maintain their hold on power they needed to revolt against BPR and IT. This paper looks at how they were able to do this, how they were able to manipulate the discourse on BPR and IT and turn it around to reinforce their position and power to prevent re-engineering taking place.

## BPR, empowerment and middle management

The concept of re-engineering first entered the management literature in the middle of 1990 and has been put forward as a revolutionary, radical change approach to increasing organizational performance. BPR focuses on the customer, working to the goal of radically changing existing business processes so as to serve the customers' needs more effectively. BPR promotes a flatter organizational structure, filled with empowered process workers who use IT in radically new ways to carry out operations.

In the re-engineered organization where the hierarchy is flattened and information is decentralized empowerment of the individual is both possible and necessary. Empowerment is one of the guiding principles of BPR and often put forward as a precondition for its success (Rai and Paper, 1994; Bashein et al., 1994). Competitive advantage can be secured by ‘empowering employees to take responsibility for tasks and processes that previously were in the domain of supervisors and managers’ (Willmott, 1994, p. 91).

The re-engineering approach to empowerment allows for responsibility and authority to be devolved to lower levels of the organization. This has the potential benefit of stripping out redundant levels of management, while at the same time giving the employees working at the customer coalface opportunities for job satisfaction through decision-taking and genuine involvement in a process. This concept of empowerment is often offered in conjunction with the use of IT to maintain control over empowered workers. Hammer (1990) suggests that IT can be used as a means of controlling the operations of empowered workers. Empowerment, then, changes both the structure and the culture of the organization. The implicit approach of cultural change underlying the BPR approach to empowerment needs further explanation.

Re-engineering takes a shift in culture. Within the re-engineered organization, management are put forward to determine and set that new culture, they seek to promote a re-engineered corporate culture. A large amount of literature on BPR emphasizes the importance of the new re-engineered values. Hammer and Stanton make the case for re-engineering with disdaining directness when referring to the use of values to govern employee behaviour. 'It is doubly important that people internalise the new values before they represent a key management tool for a reengineered environment' (1995, p. 158).

Re-engineering cannot be accomplished if the values of organizational members are not realigned. If the traditional values of the organization prevail then the transformation of the organization cannot be made. Trying to overlay new work practices on old style process workers is impossible. It is for this reason that cultural change plays such an important role in re-engineering, for it is the key social element that can either enable or constrain change. It is this cultural change that cannot be added on after the re-engineering event. Getting the context right is the first thing involved in a re-engineering effort. It is the struggles associated with contextual change that are the focus of this research paper.

The cultural change required of re-engineering, has been approached as a top-down management initiative. Leaders dictate a new set of values, employees internalize these values and management can be confident that employees will behave as required. The dictated values become the new organizational norm. However, people always have and always will resist dramatic change. To overcome this, the re-engineering principle suggests that those who conform are rewarded and those who do not are punished. It is assumed though that once employees are informed of the gains to be made, the benefits that will result, then they too will conform. Organization-wide cultural consensus is sought. Dissent is not permitted, and while negotiation and increased communication are put forward as means of convincing people of the need to change, 'At the end of the day, if all else fails, the leader can simply demand that people contribute to the reengineering effort, that they subordinate their own domains to the needs of the new processes' (Hammer and Stanton, 1995, p. 36). The BPR literature correlates to the view that we can produce a society by design which requires control, unobtrusive and obtrusive, to change what we are. This reliance on an engineered corporate culture (through the re-engineering of meaning), changing people's information and meaning bases, provides only for an objective or formalized cultural environment that attempts to override the existing corporate culture, that is more subjective and informal. Again, the social side is treated as an objective element.

BPR takes a prescriptive, dictatorial approach to change. However, it is the outcomes promised through cultural change that, while they sound enticing, are often very difficult to achieve as they bring about a number of difficulties in their implementation. The responsibility for the implementation of the empowered workforce is left with those in the management positions that will, as a result, have less power. 'At the middle of the organisation, we find the stakeholder group that is probably the most crucial and, at the same time, most at risk of any group in today's organizations: Middle management is at the crossroads of the forces for change: the drive and the leadership that must come from the top of the organisation and the involvement and initiative that must come from the bottom' (McKersie and Walton, 1991, p. 263). This quote highlights the competing forces of control and empowerment that must be reconciled to bring about change. Empowerment of lower echelon staff challenges the power of middle management. The problem with this is that symbolic issues cannot be solved by mechanistic means, they require negotiation not dictation.

It is proposed in this paper that this stance that leads to many of the re-engineering problems encountered when organizations typically attempt to undertake a re-engineering transformation. In trying to understand these issues surrounding empowerment, it is important to explore the concepts of organizational power.

## Foucault, power and resistance

Generally speaking, the distribution of power forms the basis of any organization. The view of power in the re-engineering literature is reflected in the approach it takes to empowerment. Power is a commodity to be exchanged and utilized, its source discerned and its distribution controlled. It is treated as though it has an objective reality. This is a notion that is rejected by many. As Boland discusses it, 'power is not an entity. The dynamics of power are dialectic and found in human interaction. Power does not emanate from some location, but like meaning, is produced and reproduced through action and sense-making dialogue' (1987, p. 373).

Power is socially constructed within the organization, where one member is decided to have greater power over other members. Power is not a commodity that can be taken from one and given to another. Power accrues through organizational discourse where a member is discussed to be powerful and where those that control the text have the power. Power is a relationship that is held between those with and those without power, it is symbolic. The re-engineering approach glosses over the depth of value placed upon power, 'BPR is adopted and deployed as if office politics either do not, should not or will not exist in the re-engineered corporation' (Grint and Willcocks, 1995, p. 102). The concept does nothing to address the inevitable struggle that will ensue when attempts are made to change traditional power structures through the flattening of the hierarchy and empowerment of lower-echelon staff.

I argue that a Foucauldian approach to power is a more appropriate means of understanding the effects of BPR on the organization as it promotes the view of power as a relationship. Michel Foucault argues that power is not an object, but a relationship among individuals. Power is a social construction, created through discourse among organizational members. 'Power, as Foucault has shown us, operates through a set of technologies and mechanisms rather than through a social class: it is diffused throughout society rather than imposed by one class upon another' (Fiske, 1993, p. 11). Foucault (1982) argues for the importance of the study of power relations. Society is viewed as being made up of the functioning of power relations. Analysing these is vitally important. It highlights their historical development, their strengths and their weaknesses. This is a required precondition for their transformation and one that is missing from BPR.

As with any form of power there is always resistance. BPR assumes this resistance can be controlled and requires it to be overcome to enable success. However, there is never one overwhelming form of power held over others, for example those at the top of an organizational hierarchy have a relationship with those at other levels of the hierarchy, that keeps them in this position, it is a ‘relationship of mutual support and conditioning, a mutual hold’ (Foucault, 1980, p. 159). The organization is made up of different forces at work. Rejection and resistance comes as this mutual support dissipates in the face of attempts to shift the traditional power relations in the organization.

## Research methods

The particular approach to the research taken here comes mainly from a gap in the existing research into BPR. The case studies that were reviewed (Hammer, 1990; Buday, 1993; Hall et al., 1993; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Kennedy, 1994; Wastell et al., 1994; Watson, 1994) provide only a brief snapshot of time of the end result of successful re-engineering projects. The case study descriptions presented do not reflect the transformation process the organization goes through, they describe the situation as it was before, in terms of problems within the organization and then they describe the resolved ‘re-engineered’ outcome. These case studies are lacking in their contextual and historical view of the organizations. The research methodology selected for this research was motivated by the limitations seen in the existing studies on BPR.

Ethnography has been adopted as it allows for the study of the organizational culture under investigation (Van Maanen, 1979; Rosen, 1991). Organizational ethnography is important as a research approach in IS (Orlikowski, 1988; Zuboff, 1988; Lee, 1993; Harvey and Myers, 1995) as it can help explain how people deal with issues in their everyday life. Taking an ethnographic approach in this research allows for the study of the organizational culture under investigation. By highlighting the cultural and symbolic processes within the organization, a greater understanding of the issues surrounding cultural change can be gained.

Ethnography provides means of digging beneath the surface of the object of study, by focusing on the meaning of events for those involved. It constantly questions reality construction and interpretation within the context. This is consistent with the goal of ethnography, to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the situation under study that is meaningful to those within the context of the study. This is particularly relevant to this study which attempts to highlight the differences between the principles and the practice of the BPR transformation.

Within the ethnographic approach itself, there are differing schools of thought stemming from the debates in sociology and anthropology (between modernism and postmodernism). These have been described as the different genres within the field of ethnography (Clifford and Marcus, 1986; Van Maanen, 1988; Atkinson, 1992). They focus on the changing relationship between the interaction of the text, the author and the reader of the text. A postmodern ethnographic approach has been adopted in this research.

The purpose of the postmodern ethnography is to read into the text and to create from it. Thus the postmodern ethnography rejects the notion of the objective reality. Instead the author invites the reader into a discourse on possible interpretations. What is highlighted in this method of research is that knowledge is never neutral. As a result, the postmodern ethnography is often concerned with the political nature of knowledge as discourse takes place in a world that is politically, historically and contextually dependent. Postmodern ethnography recognizes that there are multiple realities of the world, which allow for multiple interpretations of a text. In doing this, the nature of the research produced does not reach a concluding interpretation of the world. What it does is open itself up to a continual discourse between the reader, writer and the text, rather than making a claim to a definitive representation of reality.

## Field site

The ideal site sought for this research was restricted to one that was undertaking a BPR project. The organization chosen for the study also had to be large enough to provide the depth required to achieve the results desired, that is, to be exposed to the transformation taking place within the organization and observe the process of empowerment.

The site chosen was one of the Divisions of a large Australian public sector organization. The Division had approximately 350 employees dispersed across various sites. With existing problems with noncomputer based communication identified, and the physical separation of staff, IT was identified as the communication basis needed.

The project they were undertaking was to re-engineer the communication process within the division enabled through the implementation of electronic communications. The two main functions of the service were to provide facilities for electronic document interchange between the division's participants as well as an interpersonal messaging system. The system was seen as a prerequisite for the future introduction of electronic trading and its service delivery improvements. The greatest benefit of the electronic mail initiative were seen in the area of improved intra-divisional communication, identified as a significant source of competitive advantage.

The overriding benefit of choosing the site was the introduction of communication technology as the IT enabler within BPR. This choice of medium allowed for the study of meaning within the social context as it is how people view it, and use it as a symbolic representation of power that provides the most insight into the potential difficulties with empowerment within a highly structured, role-dominated organization. As the BPR approach is based on flatter organizational structures, centred on empowerment and lateral work flows, then the site chosen is typical of that which is in need of re-engineering.

The e-mail system was put forth by the IT Working Group as a means to improve the flow of information between and among units, to free up communications and let people get around the constraints imposed by hierarchy. There was an intense belief throughout the culture that hierarchy was the dominant force. As this was not dealt with first, there was the implicit expectation that the e-mail tool would address this issue. This highlights the fact that people within the organization were ready to say 'give me the power of process', supporting the devolution of the hierarchy and the skipping of management levels.

The drive and push for the project came from the Principal Manager (PM) of one of the branches within the Division (he was also my project sponsor). He became the project champion for the installation of electronic communications (cc:Mail was the chosen package). PM saw himself as the most senior and experienced in the Division with IT, having set up his own network within a previous branch. PM also faced the most immediate problems with communication since his staff were fragmented over three separate locations across the city. The PM was the type of person that believed in accessing resources directly and saw great benefits in having direct access to anyone in the branch. He found the traditional means of communication frustrating and was strongly pushing for the branch-wide introduction of an electronic communications system. Many others in the branch were also eagerly awaiting its introduction. The organization was already trying to force a transition in that the hierarchy was dominant and people were trying to bypass it. The environment seemed perfect for change. The question is then ‘why was the change constrained?’ The argument put forward in this paper is that the issue of contextual change (required in order to empower) was not managed and it was the political connotations of this that constrained the ability to reengineer.

## Field study

The investigation was conducted in 1994 over a ten-month period within the head office of the Division.

## Denying the technology

The principal used in conducting the field research was participant observation. This provides the researcher with close, long-term exposure to the phenomenon, to enable the observation and analysis of situations relevant to the object of study. This can only be achieved through participation with those involved in the social setting (Burgess, 1982). The primary techniques of data collection used in this ethnography included: interviews (unstructured and semistructured); a review of organizational and project documentation (this helped in developing a history of the context in which I was entering), and key informant collaboration. Taping in conjunction with handwritten notes were used to record the interview sessions. These interviews lasted between half an hour to one and a half hours. Interaction with organizational members on a daily basis as well as observation of the activity over the new communication medium was used to understand the situation. My own diary recordings also provided a valuable source of information on the factors affecting my interpretation of the findings and was a useful source of self-reflection.

My introduction to the project was in the role of an unpaid consultant investigating the issues related to the re-engineering project within the Division. In this role I worked as an internal adviser, giving direct feedback to those concerned as my understanding of the issues grew. During my two to three day a week visits to the Division, I was there during all stages of the project and so was able to observe all phases of the change process. Although all planning sessions had been held prior to my arrival on the site, I was given access to all decision making material and background information. I was also given access to all levels of the organization within the sites, in order to conduct my research. The organization did reserve the right of confidentiality of its and the participants' identities. Additionally, all research results were presented back to the organization for comment.

Within the Division, middle management became the key figures in constraining the re-engineering effort. This next section attempts to highlight why the BPR attempt was restrained and by what process the branch came to negate the enabling technology. In doing this it is important to look at the way in which BPR and IT were put into the organizational discourse and how they were manipulated and shaped by middle management in order to deny them as a positive organizational change.

In discussing the organization, I have used the genre of creative artists when looking at the organizational text. Observable events of what happened in the organization are not simply retold and presented as the analysis of the re-engineering attempt. I am instead creating a story from what I perceive to be the reality of the situation I was part of. This is my perception and my interpretation of events. In taking this stance, it becomes clear that there is not just one tale to tell as different views, or a different focus will result in different interpretations and representations of the situation. The perspective taken in this paper focuses on the event, the impact of cultural change and the denial of the technology.

## Social construction of the object

After an object is introduced into discourse, it undergoes shaping by the organizational members in order to determine its meaning as a socially constructed object within the context. As discussed by Davies and Harvey (1994), it is the perception of something as a knowledge-object that influences how it is defined as a power element. Thus it can 'be a prime political tool depending upon who has the knowledge to define it as a particular form of knowledge object' (Davies and Harvey, 1994). Knowledge is a political tool. By producing knowledge of an object you can exert power over that object, thus discourse is never neutral. By being objects of knowledge, BPR and cc:Mail are also technologies of power and hence power-knowledge objects. The shaping of BPR and cc:Mail as power-knowledge objects will therefore reflect 'the power relationship history in which it has been formed as a meaningful object' (Davies and Mitchell, 1994, p. 248).

Electronic communications (BPR and cc:Mail) became the subject of debate within the Division. Many of the lower echelon staff I spoke to within the Division welcomed the introduction of the new technology and the changes they perceived it would bring. They saw the ability to free up the information flow within the organization, enabling freedom from the restrictions of hierarchy and the formal flow of information. This was welcomed as it was at these levels of the organization that the strictness of hierarchy provided the most frustrations. The installation of e-mail along with re-engineering was hoped to provide the means to discourse directly with senior management.

## Acceptance

Once becoming an object, once it is created through discourse, a position has to be taken as to whether it will be viewed as acceptable or unacceptable within the organizational context. The branch members had to choose between re-engineered communications and the traditional communications, between the electronic and the personal.

When speaking with members of the middle management team, I discovered that IT was acceptable as a tool but became unacceptable when it took the role as an enabler of a re-engineered communication process. While members of middle management welcomed the technology, when it was spoken within the context of a communication medium, the rhetoric of people values surfaced and were continually impressed upon me. They viewed it as an efficient means of speeding up the 'existing' flow of information but refused to view it as an enabler of a re-engineered process. This was the consequence of viewing e-mail only as a tool. The tool is machine-like as opposed to face-to-face communications that are personal. Communication was equated with personal contact and so electronic communications was a foreign concept.

I consider myself a people person and e-mail brings an impersonal touch to communication.

It's people that matter not the technology. Technology doesn't replace people.

The people's opinion has, on something like e-mail, to say 'hey here's the silver bullet, this is going to fix our communication' is bullshit because it's people that communicate not, machines.

Behind this rhetoric, it was the people values so highly espoused that were often not displayed. While people were important in the organization, as is true with most service providers, the continuing ramifications of the old machine bureaucracy style of operation viewed them as ‘troops’ performing a designated function in accordance with orders from above. There was also little in the way of open information to lower echelon staff as middle management controlled the information flow within the organization, often filtering or hiding information coming through their office. Staff were deemed only to need certain information to perform their function and it was up to the discretion of the manager as to what information that would be.

There was a fear that re-engineered communications would provide what one manager described as 'the means for the ultimate egalitarianism'. This comment recognized the existing inequalities within the organization and saw the opportunity for greater equality among employees with the introduction of the re-engineered communication process. Like most egalitarian ideals show, the removal of one kind of inequality can almost certainly bring about another. So while the differences in access to information may decrease, there was the growing possibility that there would be dramatic changes to power, placing the threat of disempowerment on the levels of middle management.

Many of the lower-echelon staff were eager to use the technology to enable a new way to work as they were the ones most frustrated with the existing communication system. The re-engineered communications were greatly encouraged by the PM who insisted all communication and documentation to him to be via cc:Mail. Middle management however, only acknowledged cc:Mail as a new tool for the organization along the lines of the telephone and fax machine, not an enabler of a new way to work. This attitude towards e-mail is portrayed in the following comments by management before the implementation occurred:

E-mail is only a tool, it is only as good as the people who use it . . . I suspect it won't be the answer to all our prayers.

I don't see it as solving a particular problem but being a useful tool.

Don't misunderstand me, I see the need for that sort of thing, but I see it as a tool rather than as the be all and end all. It's just another way to work and my view is, it can't be enforced on people who have a totally different way of working.

I think it's a very useful tool for a lot of routine business but, I think we've got to be careful we don't take it to the extremes.

The emphasis is to see it and use it as a tool rather than the way of doing things.

There was an obvious attempt by middle management to downplay the effects and possible uses of the technology. By continually reinforcing the tool-like attributes of e-mail, management were shaping e-mail to be used only in this manner. That is, the tool is an inanimate object with the single purpose of performing a designated function.

This has been discussed by Orlikowski in her ethnographic study of the computer mediation of production work. 'Use of the terms "tools" suggests they are simple, tangible, value-free instruments that aid humans in performing some well-defined task. Humans are implied to have direct control over the tools, whose effects are thought to be well-known, predictable and bounded ... The metaphor prohibits consideration of tools as anything other than discrete, static, and socially neutral' (1988, p. 203). Management were trying to shape cc:Mail within the confines of the attributes of a tool. This view places restrictions on the potential use of the technology as it is perceived as functional rather than social. Management refused to delve into the social ramifications of the technology, preferring to view it along the lines of the telephone and fax

Management felt threatened by the new object of power (BPR and cc:Mail). To them there was a danger in allowing electronic communications to pervade the organization as it would have a direct impact on a larger part of their role. Zuboff discusses this centrality of information and communication with respect to the traditional management role: 'Traditionally, the middle manager's role has been largely defined by the collection, analysis and dissemination of information. The most skilled aspects of the job involved nonroutine information, usually culled in face-to-face interactions and through personal network' (Zuboff, 1988, p. 358). Managers had now discovered that the power of BPR and e-mail had the potential to undercut this dimension of their job. The move towards a technical dominance in the discourse was evident as some managers commented:

It intrigues me how we can go to a management meeting and spend half of our time talking about the latest wizardry...I just think it's rubbish and that happens...and in the meantime Rome's burning and that's happening more and more.

It's like taking an Olympic athlete under your wing and saying 'irrespective of what you think, this is your training programme and you will produce that result . . . you know human nature's not that strict, it's not that predictable.

The danger I see is that over the next ten years say, that the culture will dictate that unless you do that you're an idiot, that you're not a performer.

In terms of group dynamics you can add another clique. That's the electronic zealot versus the not quite so married to it zealot.

Once an object is pulled into the discourse within an organization it gains power. By introducing BPR and IT into the discourse it gained power. In order to stop an object being knowledge you need to counter power. By redefining cc:Mail as a tool only was an attempt by management to counter the power of it to change the status quo within the organization before it was even implemented. This was a management effort to influence the ability of the technology to re-engineer and to reassign their role in the communication process.

## Denying the object

As an object emerges, organizational members have to ask if they want it. If they do then the culture must change to accept the changes. If members of the organization do not accept the change then they need to put a discourse forward to deny the object. This can only be done through being in a dominant position within the organization to allow influence and this depends on the social groups you belong to. This effect of re-engineering can be more effectively undermined by the dominant members of the organization. As Spears and Lea discuss, this 'power differential makes it likely that the powerful will use or revise features of the medium to reassert their position over the powerless' (1994, p. 436). In the same way, using their formal position in the hierarchy, middle management were in an advantageous position to redefine the technology to reassert their power. Middle management were able to influence the discourse on BPR and cc:Mail through the reality they created about them.

It was the importance they placed on face-to-face communications and personal contact that they were using to undermine the threat of the power placed on them by re-engineered communications. Also critical were the values that the history of the organization had determined as meaningful, the perception of the importance of face-to-face communications and the role middle management played in this.

Management sought to deny it, to prevent it from becoming a positive social tool. In doing so, they reconstructed cc:Mail as a clinical, sterile tool. This ‘clinical’ view of cc:Mail was an expression of their recognition of the potential power of the technology to control.

The personal contact side of it I feel is a very important part of the work environment. If it's not there, the environment lacks, it's probably a very sterile environment. Maybe that's one way it is heading.

I don't want a situation where people come to work and lock themselves in a germ-free cubicle and come out at the end of the day, I think that'd be a sad day.

It's people that matter not the technology. Technology doesn't replace people ... I think it's important to get out and rub shoulders otherwise it all becomes clinical and you know, three cc's of this and ten cc's of that and a triple antigen there.

The introduction of IT into the communication process was viewed by middle management as an attempt to sterilize the work environment and consequently the way they worked it. This was the rhetoric that was promoted within the branch, this is how they exposed it within the organization. There was a fear that the introduction of e-mail would take importance off them as sources of information by opening access to the rest of the organization. To look at the text literally, they were facing a type of biological warfare. Introducing e-mail was correlated to the injection of a vaccine – a triple antigen used to wipe out the germs that reproduced and grew under their realm of ‘personality management’. This would wipe out the germinated, face-to-face networking environment that they flourished in, that they wielded power in. The introduction of e-mail would refocus importance on the germ-free environment and those that could dominate communication within that context would then wield the power. The seriousness of the situation grew increasingly apparent. Some comments from management included:

We've got to be very wary and make sure things like cc:Mail don't rule the day.

I just think you have to be aware of all threats and manage them as best as possible.

I just think there's a danger in allowing the system to become too impersonal.

There is a danger in relying totally on it as a means of communicating.

There was no real danger in the physical sense, no one was exposed to harm or risk. What the danger involved was to the power wielded by management when staff had the same information as management. The danger however was not directed individually at staff access, it was focused on the technology. It was cc:Mail not people that had the potential to 'rule the day'. It was the danger in the system as a means of communication. One manager even likened the situation to the Darwinian notion of the survival of the fittest where those that could operate and dominate the work environment would guide the future of the organization.

It's like the survival of the fittest and the strongest, and that doesn't necessarily mean physically the strongest but mentally the tougher . . . that they are the people generally that the drive for the future will evolve from, the direction of these people.

There was a threat that was felt to them as a species, the management species in the evolution of the organization. Their disempowerment severely curtailed the role they traditionally held in the communication process. Communication and information was the means by which the organization ran and to have little influence on the process gave little power and so threatened their existence.

This prevailing attitude justified in the minds of management the serious demand for control to be placed on the use of cc:Mail. An inability to control information means an inability to derive power based on that information. Information was used as a means of political control within the branch. Once the possibility of open information access became a reality with the implementation of electronic mail, the control management held over information would be destroyed. One way seen to curb the threat is to place barriers in the way of information access and on many occasions managers expressed this as a necessary feature they would like to see added to the communication system.

Categories of access need to be set. Some protocols need to be put in place and the appropriate level of dispatch has to be determined. I think it would be useful to put some parameters on its use.

Basically anything that goes on the computer can be accessed by anybody. Now that's one of the things that I have difficulty with.

I think it's a question of who gets access I suppose ... Some use it usefully and some abuse it ... there are some who manipulate the fact that it's on the general net and try to circumvent protocol.

Of course to introduce protocols is a means of controlling information. While this conflicted with the BPR concept of empowerment, it was consistent with the BPR recommendation to continue to have controls in place and so was not denied by the PM.

## Revolt

Power never truly comes from one particular point, such as the top of the hierarchy. Foucault explains 'this summit doesn't form the "source" or "principle" from which all power derives as though from a luminous focus . . . The summit and the lower elements of the hierarchy stand in a relationship of mutual support and conditioning, a mutual "hold" (power as a mutual and indefinite "blackmail") (1980, p. 159). Power is not in individuals, it is in relationships and when it becomes more important than individuals then the relationship breaks down. The power relationship that had existed to previously support the PM's introduction of re-engineered communications had broken down as individual managers were threatened by the potential outcome of re-engineering. The power relationship between middle management and the PM broke down and was strained for some time. Many of the informal communication channels had been downgraded or replaced by electronic communications. Management now had to re-establish their power within the confines of the technology.

## Denying the technology

After the re-engineered process had been operating for a short period of time, management brought their concerns to the next management meeting with the PM behind closed doors. The middle management ‘decision makers’ banded together to stop cc:Mail being used as a means to deny them in the discourse. What came out of the management meeting was a protocol to be placed on cc:Mail. A message was sent around the organization to all staff, both electronically and through paper memo from the PM. An excerpt of that message is presented below:

One concern expressed by some managers is the potential impact on their responsibility to monitor activities and the allocation of resources within their section or unit. You are therefore asked to exercise judgement in directing cc:Mail messages to me and other senior officers (as you would with paper based communications). cc:Mail provides a very easy facility for you to provide your manager with a carbon copy of any message you might send me and vice-versa – please use this facility.

This message clarified and reinforced the position of management in the communication process. This was their revolt against the culture change, the revolt against the re-engineered communications. This action of cc'ing middle management served to reinforce their power back into the communication process.

Most employees reverted back to the traditional flow of communication, which included their immediate management level. E-mail slowly became the 'tool' middle managers had envisioned. This had the effect of automating the traditional flow of information rather than re-engineering it. A couple of middle managers commented on what had happened.

I think there were some fears of the sort of communication structure. Because it would have been a new way of thinking and people thought it would flatten the structure of communication. But I think we saw to it and with the discussion with [PM] ...we took it up at our management meeting and discussed that and tried to establish some sort of protocol in the way of communication to make sure we don't have any breakdown, that we haven't been bypassed and that sort of thing.

Initially people were bypassing but we had to bring that back into line because it didn't complement the hierarchy. A lot of advantages of it really couldn't be used to the best until a protocol was put in place and even that protocol, people aren't as confident with it now because essentially you've got to 'cc' a copy of everything you do to the manager . . . I think initially we had good ideas but the old power game or politics comes in and people want to make sure they're not left out of a lot of the links.

cc:Mail became another means of transferring data, rather than for the communication or dissemination of information. Instead of transforming the way the organization communicated, cc:Mail simply automated the information flow, allowing middle management to retain their control over information. The re-engineered model now included middle management as a legitimate point of contact, a legitimate control, so bypassing was not acceptable. The communication system of empowered workers was a rhetorical concept. Apart from the introduction of a new 'tool', nothing was perceived to have changed within the organization. As one employee commented:

I defy you to find any evidence in this Division of where the new principles [of more open communication] are being acted out – we don't talk to each other.

If anything, the attempted re-engineering project strengthened the role of middle management within the organization. It undermined the authority of the PM and reified their positions of power.

## Discussion

In this section, I summarize the ability of a technology to empower. The re-engineering project required that management give up their positions of control in organizational communication and empower lower echelon staff. In this paper, I have focused on the resistance displayed by middle management in this process and how they sought to deny the object any relevance in the discourse. Middle management were fearful of the empowerment enabled through the technology. They instigated a power play to counteract the transformative nature of re-engineering. The sociopolitical nature of re-engineering prompted the power games that ensued.

It is the power of what is valued within the organization that influences change. The outcome of change will most likely depend on who has the vested power to make change. This is historically based and not representative of formal authoritative power which will play a minor role in change if it does not represent actual power. Within the Division it was middle management who historically held the power, this enabled them to band together against the re-engineering vision of the PM.

The project was never officially labelled a failure. In terms of its original objectives however, the goals of re-engineering were never achieved. There was little IT and employees could do within the branch except automating the traditional communications process. This meant that while process improvement was possible, re-engineering and transformation were not, as this required contextual change.

It is important to note here that this study is limited to the degree that it is not a large-scale investigation but a localized study specific to one project within one Division within one government department. As the study is context specific, generalized truths are not sought but context-specific truths can be gained as the research leads to in-depth knowledge of a single situation only. However, the depth in the research approach has enabled greater insight into the use of a BPR methodology.

## Reflections on BPR

Within BPR the emphasis lies on changing the formal and using mechanisms of control to change the informal. This is approached through cultural change. However, BPR takes a prescriptive, engineered view of culture. Cultural change is nondeterministic and so it is the managerial and the tribal powers of the organization that are likely to reject the new ‘re-engineered’ management symbols as powerful. Empowering workers, getting management to change is anathema to the traditional views of managing and organizations.

It is the historical (socially constructed) context of power that can constrain empowerment. In order to be truly effective, the re-engineered process should match the sociopolitical processes. While BPR acts on the visible (formal) structures of power, it is the invisible links of people that hold power – the webs of invisible authority. Who wins in the inevitable power struggle is likely to be based on whoever has more power – the authoritative power of reality. While there are always the notions of formal and informal structures of power, changing these depends on where the emphasis lies. BPR emphasizes changing the formal and uses mechanisms of control to try to align the informal. This cultural control has shown itself to be ineffective as it takes a view of cultural change that is outdated and ineffective. In fact it actually instigates resistance.

An organization is a product of its history and the history is part of its cultural development. It is the history of the organization that will influence the impact of change. History must play an important role in any transformative effort. Authority is held in formal positions of power yet real power is derived through social relations. This concept of power is not addressed in BPR but is crucial to the outcome of BPR as it is only the true power holders that can influence the change process.

Power relations can only change through discourse. The only way employees can be empowered is to be a part of that discourse. There can be no true empowerment until those who are to be empowered are able to participate in the re-engineering discourse. As it presently stands in the re-engineering literature, only those with the power will continue to be empowered. Until the implementation of empowerment is taken out of the hands of those that are to be disempowered (middle management) then the notion of empowerment within BPR will remain a rhetorical concept.

## Reflections on research method

The postmodernist approach to research that I have adopted in this investigation is an important contribution to the IS field as it departs from the more representative accounts of organization culture. Postmodernism takes the finality out of a piece of work and promotes instead the search for multiple issues. In this way, by providing a forum for voices within the context to be heard, a multiplicity of issues can be considered without having to draw closure through making a judgement of correctness which is then imposed upon the voices, effectively silencing them.

This research approach has allowed for a multiplicity of interpretations whilst avoiding the conclusion that all applications of BPR will result in failure. Instead it has highlighted an instance of where failure occurred and why. It is important to note here that there are multiple interpretations on what constitutes the failure of a project. I have interpreted the project presented here as a failure. It is not important whether others view it that way or not, what we do know is the transformation was stopped. This paper has highlighted some of the issues associated with changes in power relationships in BPR projects.
