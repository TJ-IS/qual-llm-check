# Managing electronic channels: the KLM cargo

ELLEN CHRISTIAANSE

University of Amsterdam, 1018 WB Amsterdam, The Netherlands

ROBERT-JAN ZIMMERMAN

The Venture Factory BV, 3332 RA Zwijndrecht, The Netherlands

The adoption and management of electronic channels provide critical challenges for managers and researchers in the ® eld of information systems. With the recent advent of electronic commerce, new business models have emerged to capture the value of on-line electronic channels. In electronic channels, channel functions in particular can be combined creatively to reduce costs and improve responsiveness and can be dispersed among several different players. We argue that buying processes and channel functions in electronic channels are of a key strategic nature. This paper presents an analysis of the challenges and requirements of electronic channels and illustrates these with the KLM Cargo Cyberpets case. Cyberpets refers to Pets Travel Service, KLM Cargo’s recent addition to its electronic commerce portfolio which organizes and facilitates air transportation plus ancillary services for cats and dogs. The existence of \`Channel silos’ and channel coordination problems were found to be one of the main inhibitors for channel effectiveness and success. The paper concludes with suggestions for managers as well as for researchers.

## Introduction

Strategic channel design is an important issue in today’ s competitive markets. Channel theorists (Frazier, 1983; Stern et al., 1996; Anderson et al., 1997) have been addressing the changes in channel design and providing valuable insights into new channel structures. A channel can be an independent intermediary, such as an agent in the insurance industry, a retail outlet or a ® rm’s in-house sales force. These strategies have not been linked explicitly to changes due to the role of information technology (IT). IT is rapidly changing traditional marketing, distribution and sales strategies and practices. Assessment of current channels and the identi® cation of alternatives, based on creative combinations of value-adding channel functions, are major decisions enabled by the availability of new technologies to alter, redirect or monitor channel behaviour (Venkatraman and Christiaanse, 1996; Anghern and Meyer, 1997).

Our main premise is that electronic delivery channels require a new way of looking at channel con® guration options and design. This paper describes the new challenges involved in multichannel management and presents important insights into the link between channel functions and electronic delivery channel con® gurations. In addition, it illustrates the role of ITs in the execution of composite electronic channel strategies. We argue that with the new IT applications, new delivery and monitoring mechanisms are made possible which enable organizations to separate the (physical or tangible) distribution of goods from other channel functions, such as marketing/sales and service activities.

The case study of a new KLM Cargo electronic commerce project presents a set of challenges which confront many multichannel initiatives. We will ® rst discuss relevant marketing channel literature and present a process perspective on marketing channels. Electronic channels and their speci® c challenges and bene® ts will then be discussed in the second section. We will follow with a case description and analysis. We will conclude with the speci® c multichannel bottlenecks and capabilities we believe to be crucial to the successful strategic management of electronic channels. The paper ends with suggestions for future research as well as for managers faced with decisions in the area of electronic channel management.

## A marketing channel perspective

## Strategic channel design

How channels can be managed and con® gured to enhance channel outcomes and performance, continues to be of key interest to the business and academic community (Anderson et al., 1987; Anderson and Narus, 1990; Frazier and Rody, 1991). A ® rm that has invested in the wrong channel con® guration can ® nd itself trapped in an inappropriate delivery system, which can be very costly to recon® gure. Thus, it is of vital importance to design multiple channel strategies carefully and to think carefully about the most appropriate way of dispersing channel functions by delivery channel.

The strategy of pushing commodity products through a single sales and delivery channel to an undifferentiated mass-market is no longer an appropriate business model. Radically new strategies designed to con® gure electronic channels are needed. In addition, insight is required into how new electronic channel-oriented business models impact on channel-dependent organizations. As we move ahead into the future, electronic interaction in virtual channels is becoming increasingly commonplace. The careful design of such customer interaction and channel con® guration is crucial to most ® rms (Mohr et al., 1996). Unfortunately, few ® rms manage their channels in such a way as to take advantage adequately of electronic channel business models and capabilities.

## Channel functions from an exchange process perspective:

Accelerating technological change, increasingly aggressive competition and shifts in consumer demands are affecting distribution channels and are forcing companies to pay more attention to their channel con® guration, in line with their strategic corporate objectives (Anderson et al., 1997). We believe it is important to look at channel activities from a process perspective and to view any buying process as a set of partly sequential and related activities and exchanges between buyer and seller. Traditionally, channel members exist in channel arrangements to perform one or more of the following generic functions: demand generation, selling, physical distribution, after-sales service and credit extension or ® nancing for customers (Stern et al., 1996). These general functions are executed in most channels regardless of the product or industry, while the physical distribution is obviously different in service industries. From a seller’s perspective, there is a product or service that needs to be marketed to a potential buyer. A sales interaction process is initiated between a potential customer and a supplier of goods or services which might lead to a sales transaction, which is followed by payment. Subsequently, delivery of the product or service will take place, perhaps followed by some type of after-sales service. Throughout this exchange process, buyer and seller exchange information, goods or services and payments. Figure 1 depicts this situation.

Viewing a channel from a process perspective in which information, goods, services and payments ¯ ow between channel members provides us with the opportunity to reassess the effectiveness and added value of certain channel con® gurations critically. This implies new opportunities for channel redesign, as information, services and payments can be dealt with electronically.

## From traditional channels to electronic channels

Marketing channel theory is focused to a relative degree on channels with physical (tangible) product ¯ ows (Bagozzi, 1975; Etgar, 1979; Dwyer et al., 1987; Heide and John, 1988; Barabba and Zaltman, 1991). Demand generation and other marketing activities are often performed by the channel member (or organizational unit) responsible for the physical distribution of the product (for example, an independent dealer). These channel functions are often performed at more than one level of the channel, which often causes redundancies which are justi® ed through costs and risks for the channel as a whole (Stern et al., 1996). Channel members can shift functions forwards or backwards in the channel and these decisions are of a strategic nature. Managing these channel functions requires, for example, adequate synchronization of activities between channel members and/or functional areas within organizations. Traditional channel options evolved primarily around the choice of involving either a wholesaler or dealer, a distributor or an inhouse sales force to market and distribute products and services. IT makes it possible to move from traditional channel con® gurations to composite channel arrangements. Figure 2 depicts the traditional channel situation.

It is important to note that, in traditional channels, all channel functions (from demand generation to after-sales service) are usually performed by the chosen con® guration. In composite electronic channels, these functions can be distributed over several channels. This is displayed graphically in Figure 3. For example, the demand generation and physical distribution of goods can be separated, as certain computer companies such as Dell are doing. They delegate the delivery to express delivery services such as DHL, while demand generation and sales interactions are carried out through their web sites and call centres.

The speci® c characteristics of electronic channels have not been subjected to much research, even though they can have a signi® cant impact on the buying process in different channel con® gurations. Rayport and Sviokla (1994, 1995) introduced the term \`virtual value chain’ for the transactions which take place virtually and which are triggered electronically by customers. Within the virtual value chain, a company is able to utilize information more ef® ciently in order to create value for its customers (Rayport and Sviokla, 1995). The economics, distribution and skills needed to manage electronic channels are signi® cantly different from those needed to manage a physical channel or physical value chains (Rayport and Sviokla, 1995; Venkatraman and Christiaanse 1996). Effective management of electronic channels can create additional information-based value and change the nature of the relationship from a supplier-focused mentality to a problem-solving, buyer-focused mentality. Effectively managing electronic channels can further change the dynamics of the sales process so as to yield more sales, as an informed customer is more willing to buy interrelated services, making cross-selling possible. In addition, information-based services can be sold when the buying process is initiated electronically.

The electronic interface often becomes a channel in itself through which multiple products are marketed (any corporate web site), distributed (e.g. automated teller machines for banks) or sold (customized Levi jeans sold directly to a customer though the Levi’ s web site). These developments have led to the prediction that traditional intermediaries will disappear (Malone et al., 1987; Benjamin and Wigand, 1995; Sarkar 1997), based on the argument that traditional intermediaries will become obsolete as transaction and coordination costs in channels will be signi® cantly lowered. Although disintermediation is an interesting channel challenge and often the result of carefully designed electronic channel strategies, it is not the focus of this paper. Instead, we focus on the opportunities provided by IT of designing composite electronic channels in which an exchange process is facilitated, by making use of the decomposition of channel functions amongst various channel members. We focus on coordination and integration issues from a single-® rm perspective and discuss the coordination challenges involved in managing these processes in composite electronic channels. We will now examine our case example, the KLM Cargo Cyberpets project.

## Cyberpets case description

## Introduction and research methodology

The data for this project were gathered as part of a larger study on the role of electronic channels in the airline industry (Christiaanse, 1994, 1998; Venkatraman and Christiaanse, 1996).

The case material was compiled over a multiyear period of ® eldwork carried out within the KLM Cargo organization. Earlier casework was reported in Christiaanse and Huigen (1995) and Christiaanse et al. (1995, 1996). The ® eldwork for the Cyberpets project in particular was carried out in July± September 1997 and April 1998. We conducted more than 20 semi-structured interviews, in addition to extensive e-mail communications with key informants within the KLM Cargo organization and, in particular, within the Business Unit Special Cargo and the Business Unit Information Services. In addition, we had access to documentation (business plans, memos and other relevant information sources) to validate information gathered during interviews. Recent follow-up ® eldwork was carried out by contacting various KLM channels regarding questions touching on the transportation of pets in November 1998.

## Company background and the air cargo market

KLM Royal Dutch Airlines is an international airline which operates worldwide, with Schiphol Airport near

Amsterdam as its homebase. KLM and its strategic partners, one of which is Northwest Airlines, ¯ y to more than 350 destinations, in 80 countries on six continents. KLM has built up a solid market position in passenger transport as well as in cargo transport. In the ® scal year 1996± 1997, KLM carried 13 281 million passengers and 612 000 t of cargo and mail. KLM ranks seventh among the 200 IATA-member airlines in terms of international revenue t± km ¯ own. The organization is divided into multiple divisions, including the passage (passenger travel) and cargo divisions. The Cyberpets project, of® cially named \`Pets Travel Service’ is a project within the KLM Cargo division.

Air cargo is particularly used for goods which have a high value-to-weight ratio (e.g. gold products), goods which cannot survive a long ocean trip (e.g. live animals and fresh-cut ¯ owers) and goods which are needed on the spot very quickly in order to avoid company losses or even to save lives (e.g. emergency parts and medical equipment). Urgency thus plays an important role in air cargo transport. On the basis of weight, air cargo accounts for just 1% of total general cargo transport. However, on the basis of the market value of goods transported, the share amounts to approximately 25%. Of the \$200 billion total in world scheduled airline operating revenues, the air cargo industry represents a relatively small share at approximately \$30 billion (Air Transport World Airline Report, 1993).

It is the aim of KLM Cargo to rank amongst the top three customer-driven suppliers of high-quality transport, distribution and information services. To support this objective, KLM Cargo aims to be able to provide complete supply chain management solutions for its clients. The development of improved electronic communication and transaction possibilities is therefore essential for KLM Cargo, in order to enhance the service and quality of its core product; air transportation. KLM Cargo’s vision is to use electronic commerce to exploit the supply chain market-place. This strategy is necessary, as the pace of competition in the global aviation industry is ever accelerating and consumer expectations are increasingly high. In the cargo market, the competition which poses the greatest challenge comes from integrators with door-to-door freighting services.

KLM Cargo aims to pro® t by the utilization of various electronic commerce applications. For instance, substantial cost reduction and added ef® - ciency will be achieved while extending KLM Cargo’s services towards its customers, particularly in the area of supply chain management. More importantly, KLM Cargo will bene® t from wider access to its markets, introducing various electronic channels. Electronic transactions will allow direct integration with backof® ce functions, including sophisticated marketing and customer databases integrated with accounting databases. This creates new opportunities for the delivery of customized services.

## The Pets Travel Service initiative

At the start of 1997, an initiative was set up to explore the opportunities for and prove the viability of electronic commerce activities for KLM Cargo. This initiative began operations under the name Business Unit Information Services (BU-IS), although it was not a formal KLM Cargo business unit. The BU-IS came directly under the responsibility of the Business Unit Information Technology. The BU-IS was provided with a ® xed budget and a ® xed time period of 10 months to ful® l its assignment.

An enthusiastic team of experienced people, consisting of both KLM employees as well as several external consultants, undertook several projects. The BU-IS took a missionary approach towards internal and external customers and received positive feedback along the way as suf® cient opportunities arose. A primary role of the BU-IS was to re-engineer the interand intrabusiness processes through the application of electronic commerce. The BU-IS concentrated mainly on the interaction between business units, its customers and its parts suppliers. Innovative information tools and transaction processes were core products that could be offered to internal as well as to external customers. Consultancy and implementation services could be offered to support the core products.

One of the projects of the BU-IS was developed in conjunction with the Business Unit Special Cargo (BU-SC) of KLM Cargo. The BU-SC provides valueadded services for clients who ship items which require cooling (perishables) and also provides enhanced security (valuables), special regulations (dangerous goods) or special attention requirements (live animals) during transport. The Product Market Combination (PMC) Live Animals of the BU-SC focuses on the transport of all types of live animals and is the worldwide market leader in this ® eld. It all began in 1924, when a prize bull named Nico became the ® rst animal to ever travel in an aircraft. KLM Cargo transported this bull between Rotterdam and Paris. Since then, KLM Cargo has continued its tradition in transporting animals of all kinds, dogs and cats in particular.

Transporting animals is more than just offering space on an aircraft. Dogs and cats are live creatures, which demand the same type of comfort and attention that passengers require. An area is present on the premises of KLM Cargo at Schiphol Airport dedicated to the housing of animals before and after their ¯ ight. This area is known as the Animal Hotel, the facilities of which are unique in the world. Here, the animals are prepared for their ¯ ight or are given care after their ¯ ight by KLM employees or by their respective owners. The hotel meets all high European standards in quality and hygiene and ful® ls all ISO 9002 quali® cations.

One of the main activities of PMC Live Animals is the transportation of cats and dogs. These pets are transported because their respective owners are going on holiday or moving to another country and want to take their pet with them. In the ® scal year 1996± 1997, aproximately 2000 cats and dogs travelled on KLM ¯ ights. PMC Live Animals expects this number to increase over the next few years. PMC Live Animals does not actively promote their services, as too many resources are required in order to reach prospective clients.

## The \`before’ process description

Customers wishing to ship their dogs or cats by air usually contact their travel agency or contact the airline directly. These customers are then referred to KLM Cargo’s customer service department, which can only be reached by telephone or fax. When contacted, the customer service department provides the basic information needed and accepts orders, which are entered into their internal reservation system. For more speci® c questions or special needs, the customer service representative contacts the PMC Live Animals help desk. Payment for this service must be made in advance by means of a wire transfer or in cash upon delivery of the animal at the airport. A few hours prior to ¯ ight departure, the pet must be delivered to the Animal Hotel at Schiphol Airport near Amsterdam. After the pet has reached its destination in the foreign country, it will be picked up by or delivered to its owners or designated recipients. In short, in this older business model, revenue is earned through document handling, the care given to the pet prior to, during and after the ¯ ight and from the actual air transportation of the pet. A ® xed fee is paid by BU-SC to the customer service department for taking orders and handling information requests.

## The \`after’ process description

It is the aim of BU-SC to migrate from its physical role as a special cargo carrier towards a virtual role as an intermediary by applying and exploiting their knowledge in the transport of live animals. A facility was developed for PMC Live Animals to make ¯ ight reservations via the internet for travelling cats and dogs and named Pets Travel Service. Through this web site, which can be found at http://www. petstravel.klmcargo.nl, customers can access information related to the transport of cats and dogs and can complete the full reservation-to-payment cycle on-line with a click of their mouse. According to Ronald Hack, project manager of Pets Travel Service \`The most important reason for starting the Pets Travel Service initiative is to expand our market reach. KLM is, for non-Dutch consumers, not well known as a transport facilitator of pets. Through the Internet we will have an instant global reach for this service.’ After several pilot projects and testing periods, the Pets Travel Service was made publicly available in the last week of October 1998.

The ® rst release of Pets Travel Service aims to implement an Internet-based distribution channel, enabling customers to organize their pet’ s travel through KLM Cargo. It informs visitors about the options, veterinary information, kennel requirements and other speci® c regulations and air transport restrictions for their dog or cat and allows them to reserve a ¯ ight for the transport of their pet with KLM Cargo. In the ® rst phase, the Pets Travel Service initiative is limited to airportto-airport services in the KLM Cargo network. Currently, only credit card payments are accepted when making a reservation, but a close watch is kept on upcoming trends in on-line payment mechanisms. Figure 4 depicts the homepage of the Pets Travel Service web site.

In the second release, Pets Travel Service will be positioned as an on-line \`cats and dogs travel agency’ connecting the customer and various participating airlines. In that case, KLM Cargo will be the preferred airline. The scope of the services will expand to include airport-to-airport services with partner airlines, pickup and delivery services, quarantine facilities and vaccination services. These physical services will be open not only to users of Pets Travel Service, but also to clients making reservations through other channels. With these valuable services, Pets Travel Service provides a \`one-stop’ shipping concept for the air transport and handling of pet travel worldwide. Furthermore, extra functionality will be offered, such as tracking and tracing. In theory, the complete cycle from information gathering through to pet delivery, including all marketing and communication stages of the process with the customer, can be dealt with through this electronic channel.

Revenue from this service is earned not only from the regular handling and air transportation, but also from sales commissions received from related services performed by third parties. In addition, certain cost reductions are realized, as no fees need be paid to the customer service organization, Pets Travel Service being owned by BU-SC. The off-line promotional activities of Pets Travel Service will initially be aimed at embassies and consulates, veterinarians and expatriate agencies.

## Cyberpets case analysis

Pets Travel Service is a valuable addition to KLM Cargo’s electronic commerce portfolio, but will need to prove its practical viability in the near future. This section analyses the main changes to KLM Cargo’s business model as a result of the introduction of the new electronic channel.

## Customer interaction

The Pets Travel Service web site provides KLM Cargo with a dramatically different way to interact with its customers. Many channel functions can now be dealt with electronically in a more customer-oriented, selfservice mode. The ® rst phases of the buying process in particular (demand generation and information provision) and the payment of the sales transaction can be accomplished through the electronic channel by the customers themselves, at a time convenient for them. Customers can exchange information with other customers on the speci® cs of pet travel with KLM Cargo as part of the after-sales service, possibly leading to new sales interactions. For KLM Cargo, the advantages of the new electronic channel lie in the areas of cost reduction, attainment of instant global reach, generation of extra revenue from ancillary services and sales commissions from third parties, and, added to these, the advantage of the corporate image as being one of an innovative company.

The new business model provides a higher level of service customization for the speci® c user, which in turn provides added value for the customer. The fact that more information is available to the user is also valuable to the customer. Rather than earning revenue only from KLM Cargo, additional revenue can be earned through the electronic channel by providing extra and related information services to the client. Thus, revenue based on reservations and transactions fees is also earned when a third party performs the actual service.

## Channel coordination

An enterprise may choose to work with multiple channels, creating a differentiated approach to various customer segments. In the new situation, it becomes a company challenge to manage and integrate the multiple functions of the composite channels ef® - ciently. The customer service department, where customers can call in to make their reservations and obtain information, coexists with the Internet channel. The challenges for KLM Cargo involve the coordination problems stemming from the new channel con® guration. The success or failure of this multichannel initiative depends on the fact that the overall quality experience of Pets Travel Service rests largely on the ability of KLM Cargo to manage the composite channel, with its channel functions being performed by various parties (inside and outside the KLM Cargo organization). Due to a lack of integration with and cooperation between Pets Travel Service and the KLM Passage division, important promotional and crossselling opportunities are lost.

## The new business model: electronic intermediation

The market that Pets Travel Service aims to serve should be considered as an information-intensive, speciality market, involving different regulations and vaccination requirements per destination. Pets Travel Service has the opportunity to include other airlines in its system and to assume the role of an electronic intermediary, earning revenue through the provision of this specialized information and leaving the physical distribution to other carriers. In this way, the carrier would separate information provision as well as the other aforementioned channel functions and serve as a logistic service supplier. This might be a viable strategy, given the information intensiveness of the search process and KLM Cargo’s capabilities and expertise in animal transportation as the world’s foremost air carrier of live animals. Presumably, it could capitalize on the expertise it possesses in this specialized market, in its role as an electronic reservation agent.

## Learning by doing

An aspect of Pets Travel Service important to the KLM Cargo organization is the capacity for adaptive learning in the new virtual competition arena. According to Anderson et al. (1997), \`An effective ® rm launches many small experiments or trials, carefully analyses only a few options, and reacts quickly to feedback from the experiments. There is no time for exhaustive forecasting and analysis, and it is dif® cult to pin down means± ends relationships and forecast outcomes’ (p. 63).

The competencies acquired through experimentation with the redesign of existing products and services can lead BU-IS towards the development of a new generation of Internet-based services, which may or may not retain some relation to the nature of the original services. BU-IS could begin to offer the same Internet possibilities to other companies, becoming their market channel or their information, communication, distribution and/or transaction processing partner.

Given the unknown impact of some electronic channel initiatives, the implications could presumably be far-reaching. According to Harrington and Reed (1996); \`Electronic commerce will require broad changes in organizational approach and structure, as well as in skills, mind-set, human resources, and measures of economic success. Many will have to cannibalize existing businesses or channels and risk demotivating the traditional organization while building the new’ (p. 77). The potential rewards are substantial: a new platform and set of tools for competing in a new and dynamic market-place. ## Conclusions

This section concludes with a set of generic channel implications illustrated by the Pets Travel Service case which are applicable to other multichannel settings as well. We will discuss organizational and technical bottlenecks, implications for composite channels and conclude with suggestions for research and management.

## Organizational bottlenecks: channel silos

As organizations move towards the active exploration of electronic channels, an important issue arises, namely how to embed these channels within existing organizational structures. Functional silos having signi® cant coordination problems between functional areas are a characteristic of many organizations with a functional± organizational structure. The process of setting up new channels involves the avoidance of these types of problematic \`channel silos’. The KLM Cargo division market focus is on the business-to-business market rather than on the consumer market. With the Pets Travel Service project, a new \`consumer’-oriented channel was initiated by the KLM Cargo division.

Setting up new channels has many, often unforeseen, implications. Electronic channels cross the boundaries of existing organizational structures, business processes, brands and responsibilities. Many organizations realize that it is dif® cult to coordinate and integrate new channels and activities into traditional organizational structures. For example, who is to be held responsible and accountable for the performance of a particular product or service which is sold through several channels simultaneously? Is it the brand manager, the product manager, the call centre manager or the web site manager? How should IT departments deal with these new channels?

## Technical bottlenecks: front of® ce ± back of® ce integration

Setting up new front of® ce applications often has serious implications for back of® ce processes as well. A primary challenge is the integration of existing back of® ce processes with new front of® ce applications, such as interactive web sites and call centres. Seamless interfaces are a major bottleneck and dif® cult to realize with complicated legacy systems in particular. This leaves many organizations faced with the question of where to begin. Reorganizing the back of® ce often requires multiple years of intense effort and organizations do not often wish to postpone their \`web presence’ until such obstacles are resolved. In addition, the back of® ces of electronic channels need to be integrated with the back of® ces of other traditional channels, in that reservation processes need to be coordinated. This involves the integration of the electronic front of® ce processes with the existing back of® ce systems and procedures.

## Strategic channel design

It is clear that strategic channel design is a primary issue for top management. The management of multiple channels is a signi® cant strategic venture in today’ s competitive market-place, where electronic channels will become increasingly prevalent. Channel functions can be allocated to speci® c channels. The management of the customer interface across channels, in order to accommodate customer requests during the full interaction process from need orientation towards after-sales services, requires new business models and new strategies which interact with existing and new markets. The challenge of which channel to use to distribute the product or service is no longer important, but, rather, to discover which channel is best equipped to provide the service requested by a customer at any particular point in the buying process. This information could be provided on a web site during the orientation phase, face-to-face contact at the actual buying moment of decision, electronic payment as transaction and call centre support for after-sales service. A customer may prefer to interact with different channels, resulting in different functions at different moments in the buying process. It is a key challenge to manage the coordination of the digitizable functions and physical functions effectively, which may be executed by different parties. It is essential to take advantage of the advancing IT capabilities to improve system coordination. According to Anderson et al. (1997), \`Channel functions are the basic building blocks of the design process. While functions cannot be eliminated, they can be combined creatively to reduce costs and to improve responsiveness and can be dispersed among several different players’ (p. 68).

## Suggestions for further research and management

## Suggestions for research

Suggestions for further research are numerous, as little research has been conducted in this new and challenging area. Research is limited, as it is a relatively new development for organizations actively to enable (sales and payment) transactions through electronic channels and to implement several (electronic) channels simultaneously. Although the focus of the case study presented in this paper was on the airline industry, most of the issues raised in the analysis and conclusion sections are issues that also confront other companies in all types of industries. An interesting topic of investigation would be the differences between channels with tangible versus intangible products and services, in relation to multichannel coordination dif® culties. In our view, some of these coordination problems are most visible in information-intensive and service industries, in that the services and often the product itself can be \`digitized’ . This facilitates added information processing within the channel itself, as well as between channels (Moorman, 1995). Few organizations actively manage their channels and channel performance in a cohesive way. Additional research is needed into the link between business performance and channel management. However, as long as cost-per-sale information is unavailable to most organizations, this will inhibit these organizations in accurate channel management. In addition, the relationship between channel organizational structures (channel silos) and other business processes remains an unexplored area. Case studies on the characteristics of effective electronic channel management are needed to serve as a guide for testing studies in a more empirical fashion.

## Suggestions for management

There are many issues in electronic channel management which remain unexplored by managers, consultants and researchers. In our view, the following issues are of particular importance to managers.

(1) Managers should not be conservative in their estimates of electronic channel usage: eventually, the great majority of corporations and consumers will be on-line. Telephones were also once envisioned for the happy few; the Internet, however, will have a more signi® cant impact on the way people buy, sell and interact.

(2) The issue of integrated channel management arises at a corporate level more often than at a divisional level and therefore requires corporate coordination. Management should actively avoid building channel silos utilizing entirely new procedures and IT infrastructures for each and every newly channelled function. Electronic channels should not be considered in isolation, as a customer will want to reach the company through a variety of channels for different products, in different phases of the buying process, at their convenience.

(3) Customer data should be recorded for future use in an integrated way, enabling the identi® - cation of a customer regardless of which channel they are using.

(4) Electronic channel opportunities should be explored and experimented with in a \`learn by doing’ fashion, in order to learn new and improved ways of bringing potential customer groups into contact with new products and services. Competition in the electronic market space has only just begun.

It is our main premise that electronic delivery channels require a new way of looking at channel con® guration options and design and that this should be prevalent in most industries. Multichannel management implies an understanding of the importance of the relationships between various channel functions, channel con® gurations and designs in any buying process. ITs are radically changing the marketing and buying process and require the adoption of radically different electronic channel strategies. Electronic channels require an integrated multidisciplinary approach, involving marketing channel theories as well as insights gained from information systems literature. We hope that this paper raises interesting issues for further exploration and testing in research and that this paper stimulates further research in this new and challenging area.
