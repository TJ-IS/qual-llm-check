# Understanding slow Internet adoption: \`infomediation’ in ship-broking markets

NIKOLAOS PISANIAS and LESLIE WILLCOCKS

Oxford Institute of Information Management, Templeton College, University of Oxford, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

Debate on the impact of the Internet on socioeconomic and business environments has been dominated by technological determinist perspectives, narrowing the range of discourse around the implications of these new technologies. One important and neglected dimension is the impact on market relationships. Technological determinism suggests that the transactional ef® ciencies associated with the new medium should have a major impact on market structures. If that argument is correct, the case should be stronger in broking markets where information exchange, information mediation and communication ¯ ows are paramount. However, an examination of various broking markets reveals signi® cant differences in the implications of this technological innovation. Drawing upon participant observation research conducted in 1998± 99 in the shipbroking market, the paper explores the limitations of the technological determinist perspective and puts forward a model to explore the changing nature and dynamics of market relationships generated by information ¯ ows via the Internet. Application of the model helps to locate the reasons for slow adoption of the Internet in ship-broking markets.

## Introduction: exploring the implications of the Internet for markets

The ship-broking market was chosen for analysis because of the seemingly slow rate of adoption of an innovation ± the Internet ± that, from a technological and economic perspective, appears to have a high degree of relative advantage over existing technologies (Plant and Willcocks, 1999). The research contributes to a so far relatively neglected area in Internet studies, namely how user (i.e. market agent) relationships in¯ uence the diffusion processes within a market. The approach focuses on information and communication (I&C) ¯ ows in market relationships and explores their interactions within the technological infrastructure enabling physical implementation. An analytical framework is ® rst presented and then applied to inform and elucidate research into ship-broking markets, with speci® c reference to companies based in Piraeus, Greece and London, UK.

A market can be viewed as emerging from the various relationships between agents. Relationships establish I&C channels among the agents participating in the relationship. The relationships and the corresponding ¯ ows can be one to one, one to many (n), many to one and many to many, and can be unidirectional or bidirectional. A channel for exchanging physical goods (or distribution channel) is also established for some of these relationships. The set of distribution channels represents product ¯ ow. (Kotler (1991) de® ned the term product to cover all vehicles capable of delivering satisfaction of a want or need. Here the term offering is used instead. In this context product will signify the physical component of an offering, unless explicitly stated otherwise.) The set of all I&C channels creates a conceptual network, which represents I&C ¯ ows between agents. This conceptual network is implemented physically using a variety of communication and information exchanging mechanisms, ranging from face to face contact, to the use of analogue media such as telephone, fax, telex and mail, proprietary or public digital computer networks and broadcast and print mass media. Each of these mechanisms has certain unique characteristics which make it more suitable for speci® c types of information exchange and communication.

The Internet provides a \`universal’ channel for I&C ¯ ows as it can accommodate all kinds of I&C ¯ ows, except perhaps face-to-face contact, although advancements in the ® eld of virtual reality may reduce the requirement for physical proximity. Being a digital, multimedia network it can be synchronous or asynchronous, it can transfer audio, visual material or text, and it can establish private (one to one), broadcast/push (one to many), pull (many to one), or multicast (many to many) connections. As a public channel with non-proprietary standards it provides a common medium for all agents. Since information physically exists on the same medium that all agents are connected to, it is much easier, cheaper and faster to enable new I&C ¯ ows by establishing appropriate virtual connections. It is argued that the ¯ exibility of establishing virtual connections may affect the relationships between agents.

The mutual shaping of technology and society has been discussed since the early days of research on the social construction of technology (MacKenzie and Wajcman, 1985; Hughes, 1987). The perspectives of the authors vary, with Hughes’ (1987) ideas, for example, being expressed as a soft form of technological determinism, as clearly argued in a more recent work (Misa, 1995). The reciprocal process implies continuity of shaping, thus remedying the problem of stabilization of technology which, as Grint (1995) pointed $\operatorname { o u t } _ { \mathfrak { s } }$ is inherent in strong technological deterministic perspectives; however, a problem often underlying the arguments and ideas of mutual shaping is the implicit assumption that technology is an entity that can be clearly separated and distinguished from the various social environments in which it is engaged. While this might be an analytically useful device, it is hardly an assumption that should be accepted unquestioningly. Actor-network theory acknowledges the arti® ciality of this separation and proposes the symmetrical treatment of heterogeneous sociotechnical networks (Callon, 1987; Latour, 1987, 1996; Law and Callon, 1992). The importance of social networks and communication in the diffusion process has been highlighted in the relevant literature (Rogers, 1995). However, when the technology itself is a communication medium, the relationships which describe the social patterns of communication are affected during diffusion, thus de® ning an inherently recursive process.

In this paper it is suggested that the process of informing should be a focal point for exploring the implications of information systems (IS). Therefore, a way of understanding the mutual interactions is through the conceptualization of relationships between agents as I&C ¯ ows. The conceptual network of I&C ¯ ows is enabled and also restricted by its physical implementation. Therefore, relationships between agents are indirectly de® ned by the physical implementation of networks, i.e. technological evolutions. Relationships are also in¯ uenced by institutional and economic factors; hence, it should not be suggested that technological change alone will affect market structures. However, as new possibilities of physical implementation enable new conceptual schema for I&C ¯ ows, new structures, and relationships can materialize. The evolution of the Internet can be both a response to the demand for more ef® cient arrangements for I&C ¯ ows and a driver of the emergence of new arrangements between market agents. ## Research design

The objective of this research is to explore the interaction of new communication and information technologies with social institutions. As this interaction is not yet comprehensively understood, an exploratory approach is considered to be more appropriate.

Various approaches have been proposed in the literature for researching issues related to IS. A number of factors de® ne the appropriate research approach. Among these the philosophical standpoint of the researcher, the nature of the phenomenon under investigation and the current knowledge of the area seem to be the most in¯ uential. It has been argued though that the ® eld of IS can best be understood and analysed with the help of pluralistic models (Benbasat et al., 1987; Stamper, 1987; Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991; Gable, 1994). However, Lee et al. (1997) noted that \`while there has been great success in applying natural science and engineering models to research into computer technology, they have been inadequate and inappropriate in explaining the human, group, organisational, and societal matters which surround the use of IS’.

Participant observation research was chosen as more suitable at this stage of the research, because it allows rich study of phenomena in their organizational and market contexts. Two months of observations were compared with evidence collected through extensive interviewing. Engaging in the daily activities of the market, even mostly as an observer, and collecting ethnographic evidence was valuable in studying the relationships and the patterns communication and information exchange within this speci® c social group. In-depth interviewing can provide rich evidence but has certain limitations. The participants are engaged in a self-re¯ exive process and their interpretations are in¯ uenced by their constructs. Observation and participation allows the researcher to interpret through his/her perspective. It also allows richer descriptions and understanding of how the group operates from the inside (Bernard, 1994). The combination of the perspectives of the research and of the group provides triangulation which increases the validity of the ® ndings. Participation also allows the discourses of market agents to be placed in a speci® c context. This is important because, while the researcher and agents spoke the same language, certain concepts acquired different meanings within the speci® c social group.

While the aim of this research is to explore the impact of the Internet on a market, this phenomenon of change cannot be studied in isolation from the particular environment where the change takes place. In order to create the context of the ship-broking market this research set out to explore the communication processes, cultural aspects, socioeconomic and political factors speci® c to the market and historical technology. The analysis of their interconnections assumes a multilevel, longitudinal approach (Pettigrew, 1990).

Establishing good access was challenging, as is often the case with elite groups which have very closed boundaries. A number of contacts were established and eventually suf® cient access was granted for the purposes of this research. During the research, the ability to explain and justify the researcher’s presence was important in gaining the con® dence and cooperation of the group.

The main sources of evidence were as follows:

(1) Ethnographic material collected by spending an extended period within ship-broking of® ces (including observation of communication ¯ ows, informal discussions, participation in meetings and social events).

(2) Semi-structured interviews with ship brokers, ship owners and other stakeholders in the market.

(3) Archives recording past communication.

(4) Financial documents and balance sheets were used to calculate the relative costs of communication and explore issues.

(5) Shipping newsletters, magazines and newspapers.

Shipping is an international business. The ® eldwork for this research was primarily carried out in four companies situated in Piraeus, Greece and, to a lesser, extent in London. A possible bias is identi® ed here, as no part of a global organizational arrangement such as the shipping market can be studied in isolation. This is particularly true when looking at information exchange and communications which naturally bypass local borders. While the paper deals with the broking market in general, one should not ignore the particular local conditions, especially since not all countries are technologically advanced to the same degree. This is acknowledged as a limitation of the existing study, which only claims preliminary ® ndings of the global ship-broking market. The next stage of this research involves further exploration of ship-broking of® ces in London and New York.

## Information and communication ¯ ows in the market

Essentially we are an information network. This is what ship-broking is all about (interview with broker).

Brokers often refer to the ship-broking market as an information network, an obvious yet very intuitive metaphor. An information network is a network of people, as well as a technological network. Both can de® ne, accommodate and facilitate communication and information exchange. These networks could be conceptualized as a network of I&C ¯ ows; this represents and corresponds to both the human actors’ network and the technological network. Actor-network theory has questioned whether we are entitled to make an a priori distinction between those networks. As an analytical tool in the case of IS research it is useful to refer to the conceptual level of I&C ¯ ows, as at this level human actors and technological artefacts are treated in a symmetric way. This does not imply that there do not exist differences between them, but rather that those differences should not be taken for granted (Walsham, 1997).

In the framework proposed (see Figure 1), at the conceptual level of I&C ¯ ows both the social actors and the technology are treated in an undifferentiated way with primacy given to neither. However, at the level of market relationships, agents are treated as re¯ exive, knowledgeable actors (Giddens, 1984), while at the physical implementation level the focus is on technological characteristics. It is argued that the I&C ¯ ows created by actors are facilitated and maintained through the use of technology which both enables and restricts those ¯ ows and, therefore, the relationships. At the same time, an IS is not a stabilized entity that should be taken for granted by its users. While most of the time technology has been developed in a different sociological milieu (i.e. outside the direct in¯ uence of the social group that constitutes the market), the users’ perceptions and actions in¯ uence the way speci® c technologies are further developed within the market, while the perceptions and actions are being in¯ uenced by technology. Since these are represented by I&C ¯ ows, we can argue that the structure of the relationships in the market shape technology through its use and is being shaped by the use of technology. The interaction of social groups with technological artefacts could be described through this amphipoetic process.

As explained above, I&C ¯ ows can be used as a link between market relationships and technological networks. However, while appealing as an analytical tool, this approach is challenging in its application both in ® eldwork and write-up. Participant observation research has been chosen as the most appropriate method for the collection of evidence, as explained in the methodology section. The following sections are an attempt to write-up the evidence collected in the ® eld in a way consistent with the model. The shipbroking market is described in terms of relationships and information technologies, while both are analysed in terms of I&C ¯ ows. It is believed that this approach reveals the interconnections existing between both perspectives, while offering suf® ciently rich evidence from the ® eld to allow the reader to reach their own conclusions.

## Structural properties of the market

## Types of market

Shipping is a diverse industry which concerns all forms of transportation by sea, as well as related activities.

## Understanding slow Internet adoption

As a result of this diversity there are various types of broking markets, with different degrees of regulation and standardization of offerings in each of them. The following is a brief description of the main ship-broking markets. Cargoes are mainly divided into dry, wet and containers. In 1997 a total of 5.5 billion tons of cargo was carried in 700 million dwt (loading capacity of ships in tons) of shipping. Of this cargo, 48% was dry, 9% containers and 43% wet cargo of which approximately two-thirds was crude oil (from the Baltic Exchange Website). The tanker market is a fairly standardized one, in terms of cargo, vessels and charter parties (contract between carrier and charterer), while containers are increasingly becoming a liner market with scheduled routes. Sales and Purchase of vessels is a large parallel market.

This paper focuses on the dry cargo market. It is the least standardized shipping market, comprising a large variety of vessels and cargoes. Numerous agents (charterers and owners) of various sizes are involved. Charter parties vary considerably. This versatility provides opportunities for the various agents to make money in different ways. Currently (1999) the dry cargo market is undergoing a major crisis as tonnage oversupply coincided with the 1998± 1999 Asian crisis. There is no central regulatory body for the market, as explained (with wit) by a veteran broker in Piraeus: \`the market operates on an accepted, though not always respected, set of behaviours’. The Baltic Exchange, the self-regulated market in London, has established a code of practice and a set of unacceptable practices for its members, represented by its motto \`Our Word, Our Bond’. However, it should be noted that no similar institutions are established in other countries and even in the UK membership of the Baltic Exchange is not compulsory.

The ship-broking market is geographically widely distributed, although there are a few international maritime centres such as London, Tokyo, New York, Singapore, Rotterdam, Hong Kong and Piraeus. London has always been one of the leading international centres for the shipping and maritime services industry mainly because it can offer a comprehensive range of world class shipping services.

## Broking of® ces

The structure of ship-owning companies has not changed considerably in Greece during the last 20 years, except for the upgrade of ¯ eets. It has been argued that Greek shipping companies gain competitive advantage through their organizational structure and mode of operations. Being mostly family-owned small businesses they have much more ¯ exibility and the ability for quick decision making. The success of the Greek shipping industry is largely attributed to this entrepreneurial ¯ air. (According to Lloyds Register’s ® gures, in 1997 Greece owned the largest ¯ eet of 118.5 million dwt, with Japan coming second with 88.7 million dwt. This excludes Greek owners based in the UK, US and Canada.) However, new international safety regulations and the associated costs provide burdens for small ® rms and change in the pattern of ownership and control is expected in the future.

In the ship-broking market things are different. In Piraeus, Athens, there are a few large of® ces with eight to 15 brokers and approximately 150 small of® ces operating with one or two people. During the last few years there has been a tendency for broking houses to consolidate, forming larger ® rms. The company provides the necessary infrastructure and staff to support the broking activities, but the brokers mostly work independently with their own clients. Similar trends have been observed in London. Representing a large ¯ eet of vessels increases the ability of an of® ce to respond to the demands of charterers, which builds a record of successful deals and helps the of® ce become the preferred source. This creates a relative stability of work and, to a certain extent, reduces market uncertainties for the of® ce. On the cost side, larger of® ces have lower operating costs per broker. They can also afford higher investment in infrastructure, which is justi® ed by the increased I&C ¯ ows.

In terms of information, larger ® rms have increased I&C ¯ ows, as they correspond with more people. More importantly though seems to be the informal subnetwork of information ¯ ows that is formed within the company. Information, judgements and opinion can be exchanged face-to-face in an interactive way. Meta-information is also created through casual interaction during breaks. On the relationship side, while the of® ce builds a reputation as a company, the client can form very strong ties with the individual broker. Very large of® ces may risk losing the personal aspect of the relationship. Even the informal subnetwork within the company may be lost in larger organizational structures.

Large of® ces are not always associated with high quality of service. Some act as \`transmitters of large volumes of information ¯ ows, without providing a good service besides that’ (interview with broker) (while this was a consistent theme during ® eldwork, no access was established with such brokers!). The quantity of information ¯ ow does not ensure quality of service. It is at this point that it is relevant to understand the value added by the broker beyond the information.

## Agencies and agents

## The role of the broker

Intermediaries can be viewed as sets of interdependent organizations involved in the process of making an offering available for use or consumption (Kotler, 1991). Each middleman constitutes a channel level and the number of levels is the channel’s length; in the ship-broking market every middleman adds a commission, usually 1.25%, which has always been an incentive to keep the channel length as short as possible. The functions performed by intermediaries have three things in common: they use scarce resources, they can often be performed better through specialization and they are moveable between channel members (Kotler, 1991).

The primary role of the ship broker is to bring together the two parties of a contract, the charterer and the ship owner. The ship broker performs a complex task which could broadly be categorized in the following dimensions: (1) information acquisition and dissemination, (2) advisory role through knowledge of the market, (3) negotiations and representation and (4) informal arbitration/facilitation.

These functions are closely intertwined and their boundaries are blurring. Having the right information is a prerequisite in building image and reputation in the market and, thus, being able to provide an advisory role. Knowledge of the market is also important in negotiations, along with the necessary negotiation skills. The informal intervention in disputes can be carried out successfully only if the parties concerned trust the broker to represent their interests.

Brokers usually specialize in either acting as representatives of traders who want to carry cargoes or as representatives of ship owners. It is not uncommon though to have certain brokers represent both charterers and ship owners. The merchant employing a broker is known as a principal. This description is mainly based on the ship owners’ broker perspective, but it is very similar in a symmetrical sense with the charterers’ broker.

According to their arrangements with their principals, brokers may be exclusive, semi-exclusive or competitive. A principal may grant a broker exclusive representation of their interests, which means that any agreement concerning the principals’ cargo or vessel will go through that broker. Alternatively, principals may prefer to work with a small number, usually two or three, semi-exclusive brokers; these are also known as \`direct’ , as they are talking directly to the principal. Brokers may also compete with other brokers in the market to ® nd suitable matches between advertised vessels and cargoes trying to reach a ® xture. Those are known as competitive brokers.

An owner will choose a speci® c broker based on reputation, personal relationship and skills. A good broker is described as one who has the right information: they are well connected having a broad coverage of the market, understand the market and can offer advice on chartering, have the necessary experience to offer advice on related legal and insurance issues, have negotiating skills and consistently bring in favourable deals and can act as a facilitator in disputes that may occur during the execution of the charter party. In the following section the main functions of a broker will be examined in order to understand better their role and position in the market, connectivity with other agents and interaction with technology.

## Intermediation and the process of being informed

As a veteran member of the market put it: \`the foundation of ship broking is good information, which means having the right information at the right time’ . The process of informing and becoming informed is central to the broker’s life. The core information in the ship broker’s job concerns available vessels and cargoes. Peripheral to this, but certainly very important for successful performance, is information regarding recent ® xtures, trends in the market and knowledge of the competition. In order to obtain quality information on time, brokers need to establish and maintain a network of contacts. Contacts are made with owners, charterers, charterers’ brokers and other owners’ brokers. These networks operate on the basis of reciprocal information exchange. A well-networked broker will have direct contact with principals and the right contacts with serious partners. A \`serious’ partner is understood as one who is reliable, trustworthy, ® nancially sound, has good customers and a proven track record. By establishing such a network a broker can achieve a broad and swift coverage of the market.

The broking of® ce acts as a hub in an information network with in-¯ ows (information about the market) and out-¯ ows (coverage of the market). Once or twice a week, usually every Monday and Thursday, brokers advertise a position list of ships or cargoes to a list of correspondents. This information is distributed mainly to other brokers and secondary to charterers. The recipient list is updated over time, with new contacts added and others removed following the changing patterns of the relationships. The criteria used for updating the list are related to the likelihood of doing business with that agent.

Brokers talk with a lot of people in order to become informed. Market information is acquired through various sources. Many deals are published, but some remain private. These are communicated only orally, usually over the telephone, through the informal networks of personal relationships. The informal arrangements that constitute the process of informing in the market was found very similar to the process described by Preston (1986) in his research within a manufacturing organization.

While having the right information when needed is crucial, it is not suf® cient in order to perform well as a ship broker. Brokers interpret this information to create an understanding of the market. The ability to interpret the information is a combination of talent, knowledge and experience acquired through years in the profession. Brokers use heuristics and often devise their own information management schemes for creating the meta-information that can add value to the transaction in various ways. It increases the trust and preference the principal will show them, as well as their ability to negotiate. While some members argue that the value a broker adds is the information they have, a careful examination of their practices and closer interviewing with various stakeholders suggested that the meta-information level is more important in the performance of the broker’s role. Information and meta-information cannot of course always be distinguished directly.

As explained, the broker does not act simply as a transmitter of information ¯ ows, but also as an information processor and knowledge creator. This point exposes a danger from underplaying the role of agents within markets and examining markets as impersonal constructions. Viewing information, communication and interaction only as the outcome of mechanistic networks, as the use of IS network terminology above suggests, may be misleading. This kind of analysis would focus our attention on the structural properties of networks, thus giving primacy to the technological aspects that accommodate information, rather than its source. A balanced approach which explores the interplay of human actors within their structural and cultural contexts, with the technological networks that enable and restrict choices, actions and even structure and culture is argued throughout this paper.

Brokers play an important advisory role. Through knowledge of the market conditions they are able to estimate the risks associated with the various options of the owner. They present the available options along with their judgement and opinion. Experienced brokers can optimize market arrangements. They can sometimes create jobs that do not exist. They can think ahead and know which job will bring another one afterwards. They know other alternative charterers. They are also able to offer advice on legal issues, insurance and other aspects of the shipping process. While this is not the expert advice that a specialized lawyer could give, owners ® nd it useful.

A broker also represents the client’s interests, an aspect that highlights the relationship role of the broker and the owner (or charterer). Representation is based on a varying degree of trust between the owner and broker and the broker’s negotiating abilities as well as salesmanship. In defending and promoting their clients’ interests, they may sometimes try to hide certain aspects of the job or present them in a different way in order to obtain a better deal.

In the negotiations phase, detailed knowledge and understanding of the market conditions can affect the outcome. Knowing the position of the other party, their deadlines and other constraints, the availability of other ships that are in the area in alternative options and the availability of other potential cargoes for his ship ± all this helps a broker negotiate a better deal for the client. Knowing the demand and supply of the market well and particularly of the specialized submarkets, a broker can estimate the likelihood of ® nding another job and, thus, pretend to have better offers or other jobs, even without actually having them yet. Suf® cient knowledge of the market supports argumentation and effective response to the other parties’ arguments. The negotiation process is lengthy and, amongst other things, requires good salesmanship. A lot of this process is being ® ltered and the principal represented by the broker only obtains formed offers. Negotiations for dry cargo vessels are far more complex than those concerning other types of ships, as there are many parameters to the deal. Some aspects of the negotiations may be private and con® dential. In those cases communications are always verbal and occasionally face-to-face.

In the post-® xture phase of the deal, i.e. the period after the agreement where the actual transport takes place, problems and disputes may arise between the owner and charterer. The process of solving these disputes through formal channels is often a lengthy and expensive one, hindered by the international nature of the market; it is therefore bene® cial to resolve disputes through informal channels. Intermediaries can smooth things, being more cool and impartial. During interviewing some brokers recalled anecdotes which showed very vividly the owners’ limited ability to handle negotiations in a cool and successful way. While we do not need to accept that this is necessarily the case, it is indicative of the brokers’ pertaining perception of their own role. It was also pointed out that the involvement of brokers in informal handling of disputes disassociates the dispute from the particular owner or charterer, thus doing less damage to their reputation

The description above highlights the value added by the ship broker. However, the role of the broker cannot be suf® ciently understood if we do ignore the fact that by introducing ef® ciencies, they introduce cost savings.

Cost savings and value added are closely interrelated and could be understood as different perceptions of the same entity. There are arguments, however, to view them separately, at least at an analytical level. They are more tangible, easier to quantify, easier to manage and, thus, they more often constitute the basis of decision making. The primary economic role of an intermediary is to reduce the total cost of production and delivery through improving transactional ef® ciencies (Williamson, 1975). Hence, the ef® ciency of an intermediary depends on whether the total transaction or coordination cost from using an intermediary is more or less than not using it.

## Personal relationships: trust and information networks

The importance of personal relationships in this market was consistently underlined in the discussions and interviews. It must be mentioned though that observation of and participation in daily business and social activities revealed that agents’ perceptions of what constitutes a personal relationship differ considerably. The following refer to what could be described as the common ground of their perceptions.

Personal relationships are built through years of acquaintance and successful cooperation. Relationships are also established through personal references and the reputation that an agent has built. Trust is established through personal relationships. Brokers maintain a high code of professional conduct where the reputation of each broker becomes important. Conduct is based on trust as re¯ ected in the motto of the Baltic Exchange, \`Our Word Our Bond’. Trust has the advantage of reducing transaction costs. Repeating transactions with the same people who have proved trustworthy in past can reduce uncertainty and risk (Sabel, 1993; Powell and Smith-Doerr, 1994); through this process relationships are maintained and strengthened. This is particularly important when the offering has a low degree of standardization. It is not argued that personal values do not in¯ uence the way members conduct business, but that there are also business incentives for professional and ethical behaviour. Of course, the code of conduct is not observed by all members, but those breaking it seem to constitute a minority. An agent breaking the unwritten rules of the market will be stigmatized and treated with suspicion in the future.

However, good relationships alone are not the basis on which decisions are made. A certain preference or bias may exist, but the brokers made it clear that at the end of the day decisions are taken on a ® nancial basis. Given similar offers, they would go with the person they know better, even if they are a bit more expensive, but a relationship would not justify a big difference. It is inferred that personal relationships and other intangible attributes are implicitly valued within the overall deal. The degree to which such attributes are valued varies among agents and even for the same agent’s deals under different circumstances.

Subnetworks are created within each market that are relatively closed to external bids. This suggests that the market does not operate as a free market, but it creates links of preference, based on various attributes. Some of those are more intangible or relational, such as past history, reputation, personal relationships and references, while others are more tangible or structural, such as the size of the ® rm, number of vessels and exclusivity. No matter how much the other party is trusted, the agreement will eventually become written. This is necessary in case a dispute arises during or after the period of the contract. In addition, it may be that the recollection of each party may differ slightly as far as certain clauses are concerned. It has been mentioned, however, that certain aspects of the deal may never become written.

Apart from the negotiation and ® xing phase, personal relationships play an important role in the dissemination of information. In an information market, obtaining information a few minutes or a couple of hours earlier may provide a substantial competitive advantage. Personal relationships are crucial to information acquisition and representation in negotiations. The information aspect of the role of the broker is facilitated and impacted on by the new technology, but the advisory/representation aspect seems to be more solidly founded on personal relationships and, thus, less in¯ uenced by technological improvements.

The two aspects of the role are very closely interrelated and obviously a broker is expected to have good information and knowledge of the market, besides a close personal relationship with the client. However, to view the broker’s role only as an information provider may be misleading in our understanding of their interaction with technology. Technology certainly facilitates the ¯ ow of information as well as communication and this has direct or indirect implications for a job that is so highly dependent on I&C. However, it seems to be the demand for technologies that can facilitate the ¯ ow of information that de® nes the adoption of innovations.

## Bypassing brokers

Understanding the role of the broker gives us insight into examining the incentives and disincentives for bypassing brokers. This is particularly relevant at a time where new information technologies, such as the

Internet, may question the cost-ef® ciencies related to the use of brokers, as has been suggested by various authors Malone, et al., 1987; Benjamin and Wigand, 1995; Rayport and Sviokla, 1995; Evans and Wurster, 1997). During the research, the views of both brokers and ship owners were explored. It should be noted that their views coincided considerably. This could suggest a common viewpoint of different stakeholders, that is compatible with the researcher’s observations and the overall understanding of the market.

According to the evidence from the ® eld work, the main incentive to bypass brokers would be cost savings or the belief that talking directly to the other party would result in a better deal. However, brokers have a very strong position and there are various disincentives towards such a move. They mainly fall into two broad categories. As the relationship with a broker seems to be a continuous, repeated, long-term interaction, such a move could be perceived as showing lack of commitment and professionalism, which would undermine the relationship. Brokers clearly indicated that where they are frequently bypassed by a speci® c charterer or owner, they abandon this account. However, to view the disincentives as mainly stemming from the power of the broker to refuse future transactions limits our understanding of the relationships within the market. The broker does add value to the exchange in ways that have been explained above. A broker’s specialization and information of the market can compensate for their commission. A trusted broker with direct contacts can provide better deals or ® nd jobs in a bad market. In addition, bypassing the broker may reduce the ability of an owner to obtain information through the private channels that are established.

The ship owners that were interviewed felt that the value brokers add to the transaction justi® ed their 1.25% commission and, thus, suggested that it is usually unwise for owners to bypass brokers in order to save the commission. It should be noted, however, that under the professional code of conduct for shipping it is considered unethical though not strictly illegal to bypass the broker. The accepted way of how things are done is deeply mixed in with the respondents’ discourse and, therefore, it is more likely to refer to what they would do under normal conditions and not what they could do if pressed by special circumstances. Owners as well as brokers also pointed out that if often bypassed, brokers would be reluctant to operate with those owners and they have suf® cient power to refuse service or not give preference. Bad relationships and a bad reputation for the owner would damage their interests in the long term. Nonetheless, owners sometimes bypass brokers, establishing direct communication with charterers.

There appears to be a clear tendency to reduce the number of intermediaries involved in one deal. While brokers are being employed, high preference is given to those with direct relationships with principals. As a result, the channel length decreases and usually two brokers are now involved in a transaction, while there are cases where only one broker is being involved. Brokers whose function was mainly to forward information without having direct contacts are being bypassed as I&C technology enables wider availability of information. In addition, the increased preference of ship owners to employ house brokers is a way of bypassing independent brokers, as will explained later.

## House brokers

Large ship owners have a suf® cient volume of work to justify the employment of a full-time broker responsible for chartering. House brokers as they are called, are employees of the company rather than independent agents and, as such, they could be seen as a way of internalizing transaction costs, as well as information ¯ ows. House brokers may talk directly to charterers, negotiating the chartering on behalf of their employers, thus bypassing the brokers. However, they often use brokers as a source of information or as a point of access to speci® c charterers who represent the owner’s interests and make decisions about the chartering.

Small ship owners do not have suf® cient business to justify the employment of dedicated persons for the chartering of their vessels and a part-time person does not obtain the same information as a dedicated broker In addition, the information ¯ ows towards that of® ce will be fewer, as it would not attract other brokers or charterers for two reasons. The ® rst is closely related to cost. Sending information is expensive under the current technological arrangements; therefore the various parties will send information only when there is the potential to do business. Apart from the cost of disseminating the information, it is also questionable whether the list of correspondents (i.e. people who regularly receive the information from a broker/their network) will increase. There seems to be a limit to the number of contacts needed for a successful chartering of the vessels. Information beyond a point does not necessarily improve the likelihood of reaching a successful deal, while there is an overhead associated with handling this information. The overhead may be time or communication costs. Therefore, there is a stage when the quality of information becomes more important than the quantity and it is the agents’ aim to improve the quality of their information networks. This network is built based on its history. Successful deals strengthen ties between parties and encourage further cooperation, as a means of reducing uncertainty.

## Technology support for information exchange and communication ¯ ows

Communication and information exchange are central to the job of a ship broker. A variety of related technologies are being used in order to facilitate I&C processes. From a technological perspective, the main communication protocols that are being used as at 1999 are as followed:

(1) Voice (telephone networks including mobile and satellite telephones).

(2) Telex (sometimes used as telex emulation).

(3) Fax (mainly through telephone networks).

(4) e-mail (communication based on Internet protocol).

The telephone is widely used through all phases of a charter party, as its characteristics make it very suitable for the direct, real-time, interactive, personal communication that is needed to support negotiations. The telephone allows for more informal and con® - dential communication, therefore a lot of information about the market is acquired through it. It is also a more effective medium when unstructured information is communicated. Finally, as often mentioned with the telephone: \`you feel you know who you are talking to’.

The telex is one of the most frequently employed technologies. Introduced in the 1950s and widely adopted towards the end of the decade, today it is still reliable although technologically outdated compared to digital transmission protocols. However, it is a legacy system which is the de facto standard of the ship-broking market. It being the only transmission technology that is legally accepted as proof of communication in court, this is a primary driver for its universal usage. Another important reason is that it is a truly global network available even in technologically developing countries with very unreliable communications infrastructures. Computer-assisted versions of telex machines, combined with archiving and searching capabilities, have considerably improved the versatility and usability of the telex protocol.

The fax was introduced in the early 1980s and was widely adopted by the end of that decade. It is much cheaper and often more convenient than the telex, as it can transmit handwritten messages, diagrams and photographs quickly, albeit at an inferior quality to the original.

E-mail was introduced during the last few years and has been adopted by many of® ces. In their discourse often the e-mail and the Internet are treated as synonymous. The wide expansion of the World Wide Web and the repeated theme of the Internet has led some of® ces to establish an Internet presence. This has also been treated as synonymous with the Internet.

Each technology has its merits and downsides and as such is used in different phases of the chartering process. For advertising available vessels and cargoes, telex is the most usually employed method, with fax playing an important role too and e-mail a more peripheral one. As mentioned earlier, the main circulation is usually weekly and is addressed to the selected list of recipients. This is partially the result of restrictions in communication technologies and of choice. The process is costly for the sender and timeconsuming for both the sender and the receiver. The various agents deliberately want to limit the list of recipients to avoid the ¯ ow of low-quality information from agents they do not want to do business with. The objective is to maximize the likelihood of ® nding a good match for their vessel or cargo, i.e. have \`good coverage of the market’, while minimizing the amount of information they handle. A more frequent distribution would result in increased information ¯ ows; the value added by this information is minimized by the information handling overhead both in time and cost. To view the reduction in information ¯ ows as driven solely by the high cost of information exchange is misguiding. The actual information ¯ ows in this case also derive from the way agents structure their relationships. On the other hand, agents’ communication behaviour is guided by their bounded ability in information processing or bounded rationality (Simon, 1972; March, 1978). This is de® ned by two different though often interrelated parameters, namely the complexity of the information related to a speci® c phenomenon and time restrictions. Information technology can alter those parameters, thus affecting the agents’ boundaries; enabled by information technology, new patterns of relationships can emerge.

The telephone is used for con® dential or preferential information, for example giving a tip a few minutes before it becomes more widely available. During negotiations, offers and counter-offers are exchanged primarily by telex or fax, with the concurrent use of the telephone. This creates parallel I&C ¯ ows through different media, trying to take advantage of the speci® c characteristics of each.

As I&C costs are a considerable portion of the overall costs of a ship-broking of® ce, various companies have introduced alternative systems that attempt to reduce those costs. Some of these use a combination of private and public networks under a common interface to accommodate the various types of messages. Those communication systems have focused on reducing the cost of existing communication practices, rather than affecting the way business is conducted, although some of these provide a degree of computer integration to increase the ef® ciency of managing communication and information exchange.

Communication was found to be the third largest expense after brokers’ commissions and salaries. Salaries are a ® xed cost. As commissions are proportional to the income that a broker brings to the of® ce, increases in commissions paid to the brokers increase the pro® ts of the of® ce. Communication costs, although related to the amount of work the of® ce has, are not directly proportional to income or profits; in a bad market, due to intensi® ed competition, fewer of the negotiations end in successful deals and longer communication is required. For one of the largest ship-broking of® ces in Piraeus in 1997, communication expenses were 13.1% of total expenses and 19.7% of the net pro® t (these ® gures do not include document delivery services, i.e. post and couriers). For the same of® ce in 1998, communication expenses were 23.1% of the net pro® ts. The last couple of years have been particularly bad for the dry cargo market. Pro® ts have fallen signi® cantly. For 1998, the net pro® ts for one of the largest of® ces fell by 58% compared to 1995, while another large of® ce posted loss to the equivalent of 1 month’s operating expenses. Many ship owners, particularly small ones, have gone bankrupt.

From an IS perspective, the systems being used certainly do not incorporate state-of-the-art technology and the use of information technology is often inef® - cient. This does not necessarily coincide with the perception of the market agents, as ef® ciency is a relative term. Ef® ciency could be de® ned with respect to the capabilities of existing technologies or, as market agents seem to de® ne it, with respect to the improvements from the way they used to conduct business prior to the application of technology. The contradiction is resolved if it is understood that the adoption and use of new technological innovations is driven by market demand, rather than technological capabilities.

An example of this is the way e-mail received over the Internet was handled in one of the companies researched. The e-mail messages would be received once a day and the secretary would manually copy those that were considered important to the integrated messaging system to which the brokers had access. This ¯ ow of information would be handled in a different way from the other in-¯ ows. Usually brokers would have access to all messages and decide on the best way of handling them. It appears that, for this particular information ¯ ow, another employee in the of® ce would make a ® rst ® ltering of the information. The justi® cation for this was that the e-mails were usually coming from small players and regarded advertising of jobs and vessels that were not of interest to the company that operated in the larger ships’ market. In addition, it was maintained that the brokers that had jobs of interest would not have used e-mail. The manual process did not seem to annoy the employees, as e-mail constituted only 5% of the total communication in-¯ ows and 80% of it was discarded. From the company’s perspective that use was ef® cient as it matched their perceived needs, at a very small cost. The use to which a technology is put is de® ned both by what agents perceive as their needs and the capabilities of the technology.

## Interactions between markets and technology

The introduction of new I&C technologies invariably results in increased information ¯ ows. This has been the case with the fax, telex, computers and, recently, the Internet. Current technology has increased in-¯ ows three to eightfold. ISs integrate the ¯ ows from the various networks and provide a common interface for responding to and archiving all types of messages. The database built offers the capability of quickly searching past messages; integrated with client and vessel databases and journey estimation software, it can enable increased speed and capacity of handling messages.

Increased information ¯ ows in the of® ce, coupled with some appropriate tools for handling them, has increased the number of vessels brokers are able manage by three- to fourfold. The increased capacity of brokers to represent more ships combined with the growth in the average size of dry cargo ships and a reduced demand in transportation capacity due to the crisis of the last few years have reduced the number of deals. This has intensi® ed competition in the market and, as a result, the structural properties of the market are being reshaped as described above.

According to Evans and Wurster (1997) hierarchies of choice or power are created by channels; they enable communication of high-quality information, but constrain choice and forge asymmetries in information accessibility. Such subnetworks of choice and power were observed in the marketplace. It is argued though that the constraint of information accessibility is a deliberate act of market participants, rather than a restriction of technology alone. The position of intermediaries in the ship-broking market is founded to a certain extent on their ability to exploit information asymmetries. It has been argued that wider availability of information could reduce the asymmetry (Bakos, 1991). However, a detailed examination of the market showed that, while the Internet could result in increased information ¯ ows, this is not necessarily associated with the elimination of information asymmetry.

In an information market, quality information is valuable. Relationships are structured in such a way as to maximize the value of information by ensuring its quality. The availability of information is constrained both by technological limitations and the willingness of agents to divulge the information. While there is evidence to support the gradual elimination of intermediaries who exploited information asymmetries caused by technological limitations, there is no significant indication of similar developments to asymmetries created by other factors. Both trends could be explained in terms of market demand as the way this innovation is being used is de® ned by users’ needs. Users’ perceptions of their needs in relation to the capabilities of technology in¯ uence their attitude towards that technology (Grint and Willcocks, 1995; Williams, 1997).

Evans and Wurster (1997) argued that the pervasive trade-off between richness and reach of information has determined the way companies communicate, collaborate and conduct transactions. Hagel and Armstrong (1997) maintained that the advent of the Internet will minimize this trade-off, challenging established relations. Benjamin and Wigand (1995) and Malone et al. (1987) predicted a major shift in economic activity from single-source channels to electronic markets. Rayport and Sviokla, (1995) argued that the creation of hyperarchies will result in disintermediated electronic markets. While the observations and assumptions behind their conclusions are pertinent, the evidence so far that markets will necessarily be disintermediated is not convincing. This discrepancy shows the limitations of the technological deterministic perspective adopted by those authors. Through a detailed examination of the ship-broking market, this paper argues that I&C ¯ ows within that market are de® ned by the agents’ relationships, as well as the technology. Therefore, while the functions performed by intermediaries may change and some brokers may be bypassed by the establishment of direct communication channels, there is little evidence towards a disintermediation of the market.

The existing power arrangements within the market will resist such change, preferring the advantages of reduced uncertainty that the current establishment provides. It is the less powerful players that will be motivated to even the ® eld by changing the rules, but it is questioned whether they have the necessary power or critical mass to sustain such a change. There are indications that submarkets of wider information availability will be created within markets. As pointed out before, the market operates in subnetworks of I&C ¯ ows. It is not unlikely, therefore, that there will be demand from users within some of these subnetworks that will force the implementation of those ¯ ows using the new medium in a speci® c way. More powerful players could stick to their own subnetworks and adopt a different use of the medium or reject it all together. It has been observed that more technologically adept small players ® nd e-mail an inexpensive way to increase their circulation list. However, the larger players discarded those e-mails as they were not interested in doing business with those agents. In this case the technology enabled information ¯ ows that did not match the information ¯ ows deriving from the relationships and for that reason there was no demand for them. However, some of those ¯ ows may have reached agents that were not in touch before because of prohibiting costs; in this case there is a need being addressed by the technology and, as such, new relationships are built following the information ¯ ows enabled by the Internet.

During a period of low income, of® ces are eager to cut costs. Use of the Internet can reduce transaction costs signi® cantly (Kambil, 1997; Whinston et al., 1997). While there is a demand for reducing communication costs, the Internet has a relatively slow adoption compared to other markets; the main reasons for this were identi® ed as ® nancial constraints, legislation gaps, a general lack of interest in computerization and technological concerns regarding the medium.

According to agents, ® nancial constraints are a major reason for suppressing the demand for innovative technologies. The recession facing the ship-broking market has not justi® ed investment in IS that do not have signi® cant, measurable and tangible bene® ts. Another pragmatic reason that was offered as an explanation for the low demand of the Internet was that it is not accepted as legal proof of documentation. However, the same is true for the fax. Moreover, it is dif® cult to push for legislation when there is insuf® - cient demand. The third reason is a cultural issue. The structure of the market is such that communication about new technologies is slow and incomplete. Lack of information and understanding of new technology creates a certain degree of indifference, even technophobia. While the largest of® ces have reasonable yet not impressive computer capability and I&C integration, the situation with the majority of small of® ces is quite different, though we found new-generation employees seemingly more keen to use new technology.

Finally, the Internet is still considered as an innovation rather than as a mature technology and, as such, it is still perceived as having a range of limitations. Issues concerning the security and reliability of the medium were raised by the most technologically knowledgeable agents. While the need for con® dentiality is

understandable in this market, it should be questioned why this technology is accepted as secure enough for credit card transactions, fund transfers between bank accounts and trading of stocks and not for exchanging offers for chartering. In addition, observing the information ¯ ows it was noted that con® dential information was rarely passed by telex or fax. In any case, it is technically feasible to develop mechanisms for improving reliability and security, but it is the users’ perception of the medium’s capabilities that were found primarily to in¯ uence their decisions on the use of technology.

While more widely we see a consistent discourse that seems to praise technology, it refers mostly to established systems. Even so, the Internet has been a major focus of recent attention, the dominant message seeming to be that organizations need to get in early or they will \`miss the boat’ (for examples, see Hamel and Sampler, 1998; Kelly, 1998; Seybold, 1998). However, in ship broking there seems to be a lot of scepticism about the adoption of new technologies. This conservative though not close-minded approach seems consistent with the general behaviour of the agents with whom relationships were established throughout this research. Key stakeholders of the market argued that they would not expect the Internet to have any impact in the market soon, while the history of the broking market shows how innovations in communication technologies have in¯ uenced the structure of the market as well as the agents’ role. New technological capabilities created demand when they revealed market inef® ciencies in the existing arrangements. Older brokers remembered the times when brokers would often be implicated in transactions simply to facilitate information exchange hindered by communication technologies. The value added did not concern the representation in the negotiation process, but the facilitation of I&C ¯ ows. Improvements in communications infrastructure made the use of such brokers redundant and, thus, were soon eliminated. The position of brokers that function in a similar way today is also being threatened.

The sales and purchase market is a good example of how I&C ¯ ows stemming out of agents’ relationships determine the demand for adoption of new technologies. I&C ¯ ows in this market are of a different nature, as speed is less relevant and so is quantity of ¯ ow. The technological infrastructure that enables those ¯ ows is suf® cient, making relationships less dependent on it. Information ¯ ows are not restricted by technology, but by the channels within the market. Combined with the relatively low communication costs, this could account for the lack of demand for new communication technologies. The Internet is viewed as interesting, due to the hype the medium has acquired during the last few years, but not potentially important. Sales and purchase brokers seem to agree that informal channels and personal relationships play a crucial role. Through these channels, brokers can acquire unof® cial pre-information, which gives a competitive edge to the broker. Most important though is the acquired information regarding deals that are private and con® dential. Some information is not published or is published later or is spread only by word of mouth much later. Access to this information is critical, as it gives a competitive edge.

## Conclusions

A way of linking relationships in market and information technologies would be to focus on the process of becoming informed and informing and examining I&C ¯ ows. In that way, it is argued, we will be able to understand how the technological systems integrate within the social structures and agents’ relationships. This is useful when exploring the role of market demand and users’ perceptions and actions in the adoption of innovative technologies. This focus allows the examination at an analytical level of the interaction of organizational arrangements and information technology.

The application of such an analysis in a broking market suggests that user demand can de® ne the way technology is being put into use, which in turn alters user understanding of the technology and demand. The process is continuous and situated in time in such a way that it makes no sense to distinguish which comes ® rst. In fact, it is argued that primacy should be granted to neither. The adoption of technological innovations is driven by market demand. This demand is created by users’ perceptions of the capabilities of technology and their I&C needs. The perceived capabilities of technology are related, but do not necessarily coincide with the capabilities designed by the creators of the technology. Those perceptions are developed through the communication channels of agents, which to a large extent coincide with the communication channels of the market. The realization of technological capabilities created demand that matched market requirements.

While there is evidence to support these early ® ndings, additional research is needed to substantiate them. Further exploration of the ship-broking market and a comparative study with other information markets in stock and insurance broking, are currently in progress. The analytical framework proposed may well see further development in the light of these further research experiences.
