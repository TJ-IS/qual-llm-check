The concept of decision support systems that emerged in the seventies offered the promise of computer-based tools that would enable decision makers to develop better and more creative solutions to the problems they face. The realization of this promise depends on the validity of two assumptions: (1) individuals can be helped to be more creative and (2) software, appropriately designed, can provide this help. Evidence from creativity research supports the first assumption. This paper explores the second assumption. A set of guidelines for designing DSS is developed from the creativity literature and two hypotheses involving the use of a DSS developed in accordance with these guidelines are proposed. To test these hypotheses, an experiment involving threc groups of auditors from a big eight' accounting firm was conducted. Each group was asked to respond to two different decision situations. Two groups were given separate versions of a DSS specifically selected because it contained features that were thought to enhance creativity. Another group was given no software. The results of the experiment indicate significant differences in the creativity of the responses generated by the three groups. Possible explanations for these differences are explored.

In addition, individuals often develop a series of mental blocks (stereotyping, fear of failure, etc.) that inhibit their creativity (Adams 1979). Helping individuals be more creative is frequently cited as a benefit which results from the use of a decision support system. However, based on the published studies of DSS usage (Alter 1975, Keen 1981, Hogue and Watson 1984), the highly interactive creative problem solving environment envisioned for DSS remains to be realized. The current state of practice raises concerns within the academic community and motivates discussions as to why the broad charter of DSS to support the creative and intuitive aspects of decision making is not followed (Elam et al. 1985). Young (1983) states that most DSS applications address structured problems using features that are “left-brained." Young argues that a whole class of problems (e.g., formulating general policy, determining methods and processes to influence individual and group behavior, conceptualizing alternative new products) are essentially qualitative in nature and in order to support them DSS must incorporate “right-brained" attributes. El Sawy (1986) found that conventional DSS did not have the capabilities necessary to aid executives in understanding how information technologies might be used to improve competitive advantage, strategy development for information resources, or identification of marketing opportunities in information technologies and services.

This current state of practice is not surprising when one examines the research on design features and guidelines for development that have appeared in the DSS literature over the last 11 years. In a recent review of this literature, 28 out of 211 DSS articles from major information systems journals were identified as focusing on design features (Elam et al. 1986). Only one article in this review specifically focused on issues of creativity (Young 1983). In a review of the major information systems conference proceedings (Hurt et al. 1986), only 5 additional creativityrelated articles were found. The link between creativity and DSS, while acknowledged to be important, has received little attention in the DSS research community.

The goal of the research reported in this paper is to increase our understanding of the link between creativity and DSS. Toward this goal, we first propose a new perspective for viewing the design of DSS that supports the creative and intuitive aspects of decision making. This perspective is based on a well-established body of creativity research from both the social and the cognitive psychology domains. This perspective substantially augments previous DSS research in this area which has tended to draw exclusively on cognitive psychology as its reference discipline (Manheim 1985, Weber 1986, Young 1983)

We then explore whether a DSS designed according to this perspective can make a difference in the decision making processes employed and the creativity of users' responses to particular decision situations. Two hypotheses concerning the effect of a DSS on decision making and creativity are developed that are drawn from a theoretical model of creativity. To test these hypotheses, an existing DSS is selected that contains features representative of this perspective and used in a controlled laboratory experiment

## 2. Theoretical Background for Research

DSS design guidelines have been heavily influenced by Simon's (1966) intelligence-design-choice model of decision making. Scott Morton (1984), in a review of the DSS literature, states that all methodologies in the DSS arena use this basic view of decision making. Interestingly, models of the creative process look remarkably similar to models of decision making (Anıabile 1983, Campbell 1960, Hogarth 1980, Wallas 1926). 'They are stepwise models that include problem identification, information gathering, alternative generation. assessment and selection. For purposes of this research, we draw upon an integrated five-stage model, Figure 1, developed by Amabile. This stage model is described below.

The first stage begins with problem or task presentation. The task may be one found by the individual (internally stimulated) or one presented to the individual (externally stimulated). The second step is one of preparation. Here the individual builds or recalls information and solution approaches relevant to the task at hand. These approaches may be either algorithmic or heuristic depending upon the nature of the task and the individual's level cf knowledge about the task domain. Response generation is the third stage where alternative(s) are produced. This is followed by response validation where various alternative(s) are evaluated. The final step is an outcome stage where three possibilities exist: the process may be terminated with a successful outcome; the pocess may be terminated in failure with no acceptable solution produced; the individual may conclude that some (but not sufficient) progress has been made and loop back to some earlier step in the model.

Like traditional DSS research, this model can be used as the basis for designing a creativity-enhancing DSS and tools can be orovided to aid a user in addressing each stage. This "decision model" perspective, however, is not sufficient for designing a creativity-enhancing DSS since it has not been established that differences in the creativity of decision are actributed solely to differences in the decision process. Thus, an additional perspective is needed to drive the design of creativity-enhancing DSS, i.e., a DSS that supports the generation of creative solutions to various problem situations. This perspective is drawn from the creativity literature.

FiGuRE 2. Amabile's Componential Model of Creativity

A general theoretical framework of creativity includes both a focus on distinctive personality and individual characteristics usually associated with highly creative individuals as well as social and environmental conditions that hinder or foster creativity. Amabile (1983) proposes that creativity be conceptualized as a set of components that are necessary and sufficient for producing creative responses in any domain. This conceptualization of creativity, which involves both cognitive and social dimensions, integrates several notions proposed by previous theorists. These components do not represent cognitive processes, rather they represent a set of factors that control, determine, and enter into cognitive processes. The components of Amabile's model, shown in Figure 2, are reviewed below.

## Domain Relevant Skills

The domain specific knowledge base that an individual possesses is critical to creative performance. A higher level of domain relevant knowledge should facilitate higher levels of creativity. That is, creativity involves combining known but previously unrelated facts and ideas in such a way that new ones emerge. Clearly, one must have the components to combine.

Other domain-relevant skills influencing creativity can be thought of collectively as the set of trait-based attributes associated with highly creative individuals. Guilford (1967a) identified several traits associated with creativity including a general sensitivity to problems, originality, and an ability or tendency to redefine problems. Guilford (1956) suggests that the trait of curiosity leads people to build a larger memory base upon which they may draw for facts, ideas, approaches, and strategies in the future. Gough (1957) also identified curiosity as an important trait-based determinant of creativity. MacKinnon (1983) in his study of architects found creative individuals to have the following traits: inventiveness, independence, individuality, enthusiasm, determination, industry, self-acceptance, openness to new experiences, and tolerance of increasing tension when striving for solutions. Barron (1955) noted in his study of artists that creative people have a desire or preference for cognitive complexity as well as an ability to reject suppression, and be less controlled.

## Creativity-Relevant Skills

Creativity-relevant skills aid creative thinking and problem solving. These skills include facility with breaking out of a set way of viewing a problem (perceptual set breaking) and of solving a problem (cognitive set breaking), divergent thinking and delayed judgement. Breaking out of established patterns either perceptually or cognitively allows for the development of a broader range of alternative, perhaps creative, solutions (Boring 1950; Duncker 1945; Katona 1940; Newell et al. 1962; Wertheimer 1959).

Divergent thinking refers to the production of a variety of alternative solutions. Divergent thinking may require breaking the perceptual and cognitive sets described above. Availability of time, ease of producing solutions, and the decision maker's willingness to persevere also influence the number of alternatives considered. The production of a greater quantity of possible solutions generated from different perspectives is linked in the literature with enhanced creativity (Guilford 1967b, Osborn 1963). In addition, later, less obvious solutions are more likely to be creative ones so the prolonging of alternative generation is likely to turn up creative solutions.

Related to divergent thinking is the practice of delaying judgement. The idea here is to suspend critical evaluation of ideas until a great deal of “free"thinking has been done. This deferring of judgement has been suggested as a factor contributing to creativity (Kuhn 1963; Milgram et al. 1978; Osborn 1963). As mentioned above, creative solutions are likely to be later solutions. Thus, thinking of a larger quantity of solutions is important. Once the focus of thinking shifts to critical evaluation, convergent thinking displaces divergent thinking. While convergence to a final solution is necessary, premature selection of a solution may preclude the conception and consideration of more creative alternatives.

## Task Motivation

Task motivation may be the most important determinant of the gap between what an individual can do and will do. Amabile (1983) suggests that task motivation is a combination of an individual's baseline attitude toward a task (a trait) and their perception of the reasons for undertaking the task (a state). Thus, task motivation may range from completely intrinsic interest in a task the individual enjoys to completely extrinsic motivation to perform a disliked task. It has. been hypothesized that there is an inverse relationship between extrinsic constraints and intrinsic motivation, and that freedom from extrinsic constraints will enhance creativity (Amabile et al. 1976; Condry 1977; Deci 1971, 1972a, b; Kruglanski 1975; Lepper and Greene 1975).

Research suggests that intrinsic task motivation is promoted when individuals perceive themselves to be in control of their own task engagement (deCharms 1968; Deci 1971; Lepper et al. 1973; Rogers 1954). Retained control of task engagement means that individuals are free to undertake the task in whatever way they choose. This freedom nurtures the deep involvement and playfulness that many theorists believe is critical to creativity (Einstein 1949, Koestler 1964 Meichenbaum 1975).

## 3. The Link Between Creativity Research and DSS

Attending to the social and environmental factors in the componential model of creativity (i.e., task motivation and creativity-relevant skills) offers a new promising avenue for DSS researchers interested in developing creativity-enhancing DSS when a DSS is viewed as a special type of social environment. Social environments influencing creativity can be changed easily and can have immediately observable effects on performance. By contrast, there is not much that can be done about innate abilities and personality characteristics. Certainly DSS are not likely to instill or eradicate individual traits. Domain knowledge can only be supported by providing access to problem-specific knowledge bases which may be impractical for many problem domains.

A creativity-enhancing DSS has several unique attributes that distinguish it from traditional DSS. A creativity-enhancing DSS is process-oriented. A creativityenhancing DSS provides an environment that facilitates the use of a number of creativity-enhancing skills. A creativity-enhancing DSS is generic in the sense that it can be applied to any problem context. In contrast, a traditional DSS is structure-oriented. It provides a structure through its data base and model base to represent a particular problem.

As a result, creativity-enhancing DSS places more emphasis on problem conceptualization than traditional DSS. Current DSS design practices recommend that a model describing the decision making situation be identified and, either implicitly through a data-based DSS or explicitly through a model-based DSS, made available to the user as an aid to exploring alternative decisions. Creativity is supported in these DSS through the generation of alternative scenarios based on the underlying model. Such an approach, however, runs counter to much of the research concerning ways to facilitate creativity. The generation of alternatives is convergent in nature since the overall structure of the problem is pre-determined. Convergent thinking too early may inhibit creativity by bounding the set of solutions that will be considered. Creativity-enhancing DSS first tackle the issue of getting the problem formulated—the use of models and data follow after this.

Since creativity-enhancing DSS are different from traditional DSS, a new perspective is needed for designing them-a perspective that views the creativityenhancing DSS as a special type of environment that can raise the level of task motivation and support creativity-relevant skills. While a generic creativityenhancing DSS does not directly augment the domain-relevant knowledge of its user through databases or knowledge bases, the creativity-enhancing DSS through the process it provides may help a user reorganize and better utilize the existing domain knowledge that he or she has.

A set of guidelines for designing creativity-enhancing DSS has been developed from this perspective by the authors. A detailed description of these guidelines can be found in Elam and Mead (1987). For completeness, these guidelines are reviewed below. The first three relate to general capabilities of a DSS. The remaining two involve the delivery of these capabilities to the user. The guidelines provide a new perspective for building DSS and a framework for the development of specific tools to support creative decision making.

Design Guideline 1. The DSS will allow users to stop, store work sessions in process, then resume work later.

This guideline calls for DSS features that remove any system-induced necessity to complete all work on a task in a single session in order to avoid incurring large startup costs in subsequent sessions. Such features give the user control and choice over when, and to some extent how, to cngage in the decision making task. Further, this ability to easily stretch the time dimension in decision making facilitates delayed judgement.

Design Guideline 2. A DSS should provide depth and positive tenor in its feedback.

Depth of feedback requires that the DSS identify imperfections, search for causes to account for exceptions, find new directions or strategies for solving a problem, or present the problem to the user in a different way. In these ways DSS can assist users in breaking their cognitive and perceptual sets surrounding decision making tasks. Positive tenor requires that meaningful, helpful, and encouraging responses be provided to increase task motivation. All of these effects should encourage prolonged alternative generation and delayed judgment.

Design Guideline 3. The DSS will make available to the user a full range of qualitative as well as quantitative decision aids.

Qualitaive tools can expand the way a user views a task as well as provide new avenues for evaluation of alternatives. Most DSS rely heavily on quantitative tools that allow for the generation of alternative scenarios based on previously identified models. While this affords users choice in decision making, it falls short of supporting creativity. The generation of alternatives is convergent in nature since the overall structure of the problem is pre-determined, thus limiting the ability to freely conceptualize and formulate the decision making task.

Qualitative aids may include rehearsal tools, sketching tools, analogical tools, script writing tools, pattern matching tools, and decomposition tools (Weber 1986). El Sawy (1986) employed relevance trees, morphological analysis, and heuristics of lateral thinking in a system intended to support divergent thinking. Young (1983) suggested that idea processing capabilities support problem conceptualization by incorporating and manipulating verbal data in a combinatorial fashion. A range of qualitative aids such as these can support the user in employing creativity relevant skills.

A range of both qualitative and quantitativc tools should be provided to support each stage of the Amabile model. Too often tools are only provided for the response generation and validation stages.

Design Guideline 4. A DSS will be technically easy to use and conceptually challenging.

Ease of use is a theme that runs through much of the DSS literature and is viewed primarily as a means to engender positive user attitudes toward DSS use

Our emphasis on technical ease of use is intended to draw a distinction between systems that are functionally straightforward and those that are conceptually easy to use. Maintaining broad conceptualizing capabilities as suggested with the third design guideline may render a DSS conceptually more difficult to use. That is, these DSS require the user to do more abstract thinking than conventional DSS. This effort is necessary if creative solutions are to be found. However, the system need not introduce technical difficulties that distract the user. DSS should assist the user in focusing on the primary task of decision making rather than on the intricacies of operating the software.

Design Guideline 5. The DSS will provide an enjoyable or “fun" computing environment.

"Fun" has a connotation from gaming that includes enjoyment and becoming deeply engrossed in an activity. Thus, an enjoyable environment offers inducement for the user to spend more time in alternative generation for the decision making task. This offers the advantages of encouraging the user to examine a larger number of solutions and delay judgement. Additionally, an enjoyable environment will help to reduce stress as well as reduce the saliency of any external control the user may feel. Finally, “fun" is often associated with play rather than work. The experience of play or playfulness has been linked positively to creativity (Einstein 1949, Koestler 1964, Meichenbaum 1975).

## 4. Experimental Design

4.1. Hypotheses to be Tested. A laboratory experiment was designed to test two hypotheses related to the use of a creativity-enhancing DSS:

Hypothesis 1. A user of a creativity-enhancing DSS will adopt a multiple step decision process, whereas a user of no software will adopt a single step decision process.

Hypothesis 2. The use of a creativity-enhancing DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses than the use of no software.

Hypothesis 1 focuses on the relationship between the use of a creativity-enhancing DSS and the decision making process. In §2 we noted that the design of DSS has drawn heavily on models of the decision making process. This suggests that the designers view the use of a DSS to guide a user in explicitly addressing the stages within such a model as a desirable outcome. Whether a user of a DSS actually addresses the stages as planned is not known. In order to begin to understand how a creativity-enhancing DSS might impact creativity, it is first important for us to know if such a DSS can actually influence the user to adopt a systematic, stage by stage approach to addressing a task.

Hypothesis 2 focuses on the relationship between the use of a creativity-enhancing DSS and the creativity of responses generated. Implicit in our research is the belief that DSS can, indeed, be instrumental in providing an environment that encourages creative decision making. Amabile (1983) hypothesizes that creativity in any given task wıll be directly influenced by an individual's domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant skills, and task motivation. We propose that a DSS designed according to the guidelines outlined in §3 may be employed to support and extend an individual's creativity relevant skills. Further, this DSS may encourage and sustain intrinsic task motivation and, thus, further enhance creativity. Of course, to the extent that DSS fails in providing such a conducive environment, creativity will not be enhanced. Indeed, creativity may be inhibited.

## 4.2. Software Used in Experiment

Rather than develop a creativity enhancing DSS ourselves for this experiment, we chose to try to locate an existing software package that represented one possible implementation of the design guidelines. T'he developers of software to support problem solving and decision making processes were contacted and a set of potential candidates were identified. From these candidates, we selected ods/ CONSULTANT which was produced by Organization Development Software Inc., and which ran on a MacIntosh computer. This package had not yet been released for sale at the time of the experiment.

ods/CONSÚLTANT was selected for several reasons. Most importantly, it was designed from a creativity-enhancing perspective. The developers were experienced professionals who understood the creativity literature and who had previously taught courses on building creativity-enhancing skills. ods/CONSULTANT primarily supports the problem conceptualization phase of problem solving. ods/ CONSULTANT encourages the user to think about options, alternatives, comparisons, etc. The package does not gather dati, analyze information, make judgements or solve problems directly.

The features that were incorporated into ods/CONSULTANT matched closely the creativity-relevant skills contained in the Amabile's Componential Model of Creativity. Qualitative thinking aids designed to encourage idea generation were included as well as routines to facilitate brainstorming, provoke questions and produce combinations of idea fragments. (uantitative thinking aids included utilities for prioritizing or grading a set ot ideas according to a single user determined criterion. Additional aids allowed evaluation and rating of ideas based on two or more criteria. Other utilities assisted the user in identifying interdependencies among ideas or tasks and allowed for categorizing of ideas along user selected dimensions. None of the other packages we examined came close to providing this comprehensive set of features.

Also important for our experiment was the fact that ods/CONSULTANT was easy to learn and use. This minimized the time required of each subject in training and in completing the experimental tasks. The pretest indicated that subjects could learn enough about ods/CONSULTANT in the short time allocated for the warmup task to complete the experimental tasks. ods/CONSULTANT used the graphic capability of the MacIntosh computer to create an icon-and-mouse driven environment. Thus, there were no commands and syntax to remember. Online, context-sensitive help was available at all times.

ods/CONSULTANT offered one other important feature. Rather than supporting a single process, ods/CONSULTANT offered six different process models, each with its own procedures and cues. We expected subjects using ods/CON-SULTANT to generate more creative responses than users without software regardless of the process model used. However, by using two different process models in the experiment, we could get some initial insights into the validity of the design guidelines (i.e., do users of the version perceived to be more challenging to use generate more creative solutions than users of the other version?) ## 4.3. Experimental Design

There is one independent variable in this experiment—software treatment at three levels: no software, Version 1 of ods/'CONSULTANT, and Version 2 of ods/CONSULTANT. We chose a no software treatment rather than a traditional DSS since a traditional DSS and a creat.vity-enhancing DSS such as ods/ CONSULTANT do not share the same functionality (as noted in the previous section). Thus, a traditional DSS is not an appropriate tool to use for tasks designed to employ the unique features of a creativity-enhancing DSS.

There are two dependent variables in this experiment—decision making process employed and creativity of response.

An independent creativity measurement was also used to yield a baseline creativity score for each subject. This was done to ascertain that subsequent differences in measured creativity of responses was due to manipulation of the independent variable of the experiment rather than due to individual differences in creative aptitude. For the two software treatment groups, measures of the users’ perceptions of the quality of feedback, ease of use, and fun to use were also taken.

## 4.4. Subjects

We were fortunate to have the cooperation of practicing professionals in carrying out this experiment. All 14 junior members of the auditing group of a “big eight" accounting firm in Austin, Texas, agreed to participate. Two of these auditors participated in a pretest. The remaining 12 were used in the actual experiment. All subjects but one were in thei? early to mid-20's, had worked with the accounting firm for an average of 2 years, and had bachelor degrees in accounting. One subject also had a MBA.

The sample size for the experiment was cetermined by the size of this audit team. Each subject addressed two tasks yielding 24 observations. While a larger sample size could have been obtained using students at the University of Texas, we chose to work with the smaller available pool of active professionals for two major reasons. First, it was important for us to control the domain knowledge that the subjects brought to the experiment and their motivation for performing the task. This was accomplished by selecting a group of professionals with similar education and experience backgrounds and with the same work environment. Second, the findings of this experiment are intended to be used to support active professionals in their daily work. Using subjects other than working professionals reduces the external validity of our findings.

## 4.6. Measurement Procedures

Decision Making Processes. Transcriptions of individual think-aloud protocols recorded during the completion of each task as well as the saved on-line protocols for those individuals using software were used to capture the decision making process of each subject. These protocols were divided into segments which in turn were matched to the five steps of Amabile's model. Figure 4 shows the matching scheme.

This matching was straightforward for protocols of experimental sessions using software. For experimental sessions involving no software, two independent coders matched the subjects' verbalizations to the steps of Amabile's model. The coders agreed on 87% of the matches. Disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus.

Based on these results, the decision process emploved for each session was then characterized as either (1) multiple step or (2) single step. Multiple step sessions represent instances where the subject takes several steps to reflect about a task and its various aspects. Single step sessions represent instances where the subject made an immediate, “off the cuff"’ decision. A multiple step decision process included at least two steps of the Amabile model in any order. Our expectation was that subjects who used the software would be influenced to use multiple steps while the subjects who did not use software would demonstrate “cognitive laziness” and tend to adopt single step processes.

Creativity of Responses. The assessment of the creativity of responses produced with and without the DSS was a critical measurement required in this research. A consensual assessment technique developed by Amabile (1983) was used to assess this creativity.

Amabile's technique is based on the following definition of creativity:

A product or response will be judged as creative to the extent that it is both a novel and appropriate, useful, correct or valuable response to the task at hand.

The consensual assessment technique is a subjective technique that calls for a group of qualified judges to assess the creativity of a set of responses independently of one another. The judges are given instructions to use their own subjective definitions of creativity in making their assesstnents. The judges receive no further coaching concerning creativity

The assumption is that, whether or not they can articulate it, experts have consistent internal definitions of what const tutes creativity in their domain of expertise. Provided the judges’ inter-rater reliability is high, these subjective scores are used as a measure of the products' creativity. Amabile argues that such measures must be accepted as not only consistent, but valid as well. That is, if experts in the task domain agree that a product is creative, then we must accept the validity of their judgement.

For this experiment, there were two subsets of judges—-one for each experimental task. The judges for the business task included a corporate vice president, a corporate president, and two independent management consultants. The public policy task was judged by a professor, an inventor, and a director of a national scientific funding agency.

Judges each received a packet of all responses for their respective task. All solutions, regardless of whether they were generated using the DSS or not, were presented in an identical manner to the judge. A creativity rating was assigned to each solution by each judge. The ratings rcflected a comparison only among the responses presented. not against an absolute standard. After the evaluations of individual judges were collected, a single, overall ranking of the responses was determined from the mean of the judges' rar ks for each subject's response. The highest and lowest tanked responses to each task are given in the Appendix. Inter-rater reliabilitics among the judges were high (Cronbach's alpha = 0.8267 for the business task; Cronbach's alpha = 0.7245 for thc public policy task).

Each subject was asked to indicate which adjectives out of a total of 300 adjectives she would use to describe herself. ## 4.7. Experimental Procedures

Subjects were assigned randomly to software treatments. Each subject was asked to address both experimental tasks. Within each software treatment, half of the subjects received the business task first, and the other half received the public policy task first. Each session was a one-to-one meeting of researcher and subject. Upon arrival, the subject was told that the experiment had to do with the MacIntosh. Creativity was never mentioned at any time before, during, or after the experimental sessions. Further, subjects were told that the tasks they would address were open-ended situations that had no right or wrong answers and that they were free to deal with these tasks as they thought best. The subjects were then asked to complete a set of warm-up exercises.

The warm-up exercises used for all subjects served several important purposes. First they provided an opportunity for subjects to try out the instructions they were given and ask clarifying questions if needed. This gave the researchers a chance to remind subjects to speak up if needed and ascertain if the instructions were clearly understood. Second, they were intended to allow subjects to settle into the experimental environment before data gathering began. Finally, for subjects using software, the warm-up exercises provided an introduction to the software's basic menu, format, and help facilities.

Following the warm-up exercises, subjects using no software were immediately presented their first task. Subjects using software were asked to reconsider the final warm up task using the software provided. Subjects were informed of the help facilities available within the package and shown how to access this help. They were also asked to read a brief description of the various quantitative and qualitative aids available. Finally, they were asked to step through their assigned software procedure in order to familiarize themselves with all possible steps and cues provided by their treatment. After this po nt in time, subjects asking questions were directed to use the help facilities in the software. The researcher offered no additional guidance or assistance.

When the first experimental task was prcsented to a subject, regardless of software treatment, they were told that approximately 15 minutes had been planned for the completion of the task. They were also told that this was a flexible guideline and that they should work at a comfortable pace. This was done to suggest a uniform time frame for subjects while introducing as little external control as possible. Subjects using software were told that they were now free to use the software in whatever way suited them est. They were told that they could disregard any step or cue suggested by the sottware that they felt was not helpful or relevant to the task at hand. It is important to note that ods/CONSULTANT supports this flexible usage. That is, while the software suggests certain cues, it operates effectively when these steps are used in any order as well as when steps are omitted.

The second experimental task was presented as soon as the first was completed Upon completion of the second task, the subject was asked to respond to the questionnaire concerning their experience with the two tasks and the software. Subjects also completed The Adjective Checklist at this time.

## 5. Results

Hypothesis 1. A user of a creativity-enhancing DSS will adopt a different decision making process than one who does not use any software

This hypothesis was tested for two groups—those using either version of the creativity-enhancing DSS and those using no software. The decision processes for all subjects using software consisted of multiple steps. For subjects not using software, the decision processes of three subjects were classified as multiple step and five as single step. This hypothesis was confirmed using a Chi-square test (X2 = 9.1263, p = 0.0025, df == 1).

Using Fisher's Exact Test, it was possible to further test this hypothesis for each of the two different software treatments with no software. Significant differences in the decision processes of subjects using Version 1 and subjects using no software were found $( p = 0 . 0 1 2 8 )$ as well as significant differences in the decision processes of subjects using Version 2 and subjects using no software $( p = 0 . 0 1 2 8 )$

One of the potential advantages of using a DSS in general, and a creativityenhancing DSS in particular, is that the user is given an explicit process model for making decisions. In the creativity-enhancing DSS used in this experiment, this process model was an integral part of the software and there existed a range of both qualitative and quantitative tools to support each stage in this process model. The subjects using software for the most part followed step by step the process model presented. Our results strongly indicate that the DSS will influence a user to follow the underlying multi-step decision process in a systematic way. The decision making processes of the subjects not using software were more varied. However, we did not give these subjects a multi-stage decision process model to follow. Further research is needed to better understand whether it is the capabilities of the DSS to show and support a multi-stage decision model or just knowledge of such a model that would influence decision making processes.

Hypothesis 2. The use of a creativity-enhancing DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses than the use of no software.

Even though all users of the software employed a multi-step decision process, the outcomes of the two groups were markedly different. This emphasizes the fact that encouraging users to adhere to a standard decision process alone is not enough to ensure favorable outcomes.

## 6. Limitations of Study

Several factors need to be considered when evaluating the results of this experiment.

First, the subjects were never told that this was a study on creativity in order not to provide any external controls of how the subject addressed the tasks. This conforms with the experimental protocol used by Amabile. External control as mentioned earlier has been shown to inhibit cieativity. Since the subjects were not given the criteria by which their responses were to be judged, it is possible that the responses were not representative of the best creative thinking that the subjects were capable of. In addition, it is possible that the subjects would have invoked a different decision strategy if asked to come up with a creative innovative response. Thus, the results of this study must be interpreted within the framework of how the task was presented to the subjects.

Second, our sample size was fairly small ano represented a homogenous type of user. The results of this experiment should be replicated with a larger number of subjects of different backgrounds.

Third, we did not control for time. We followed the general experimental protocol used by Amabile which specifically excluded a time limit. However, the

Version 1 software treatment group, the one with the most creative solutions, took on the average more time to create their solutions than any other treatment group. This additional time may have contributed to the increased creativity of this group although there is no relationship between time taken and creativity scores when all three treatment groups are considered.

Fourth, one of the tasks used in the study was more familiar to the subjects than the other one. Our results found that the relative creativity rankings by individual task were the same as the relative creativity rankings with both tasks combined. However, it is possible that differences in creative performance may be more significant in tasks environments where the subjects have a pre-existing knowledge base than those where the subjects do not. Our results may have been different if we had only included a single task domain in the study that all the subjects were knowledgeable with.

## 7. Conclusions and Future Research Issues

The fundamental questions addressed by this research were whether or not a creativity-enhancing DSS could influence the decision making processes of its users and whether these systems could affect the creativity of the decisions produced by its users. Both questions were answered positively. The experimental findings indicate that the best results in terms of creativity enhancement will be attained when a clear picture of intended users’ perceptions of the DSS user-interface in terms of fun to use, ease of use, and helpfulness for task is known by the developer of decision support systems.

The specific questions posed by this research and the answers suggested by the experimental results are summarized below.

1. Can a DSS influence the decision process adopted by its users?

The experiment indicated that software that incorporates stepwise models of a decision making process leads users to follow those models more closely than they typically would without software. The results of this research make clear, however that merely encouraging the users to adopt an explicit decision making model does not in any way guarantee higher levels of creativity.

2. Can the use of software designed in accordance with the principles proposed in this paper lead to solutions that are more creative than solutions produced without the aid of the software?

The only statistically significant results were obtained for differences in the creativity of responses between the two software versions. However, the mean rankings of the three treatments showed the no software group firmly placed between the two software treatments. This is an intriguing result since both versions of the software incorporated many of the same general design principles and differed primarily in the underlying process model presented to users.

The ability of software to undermine creativity as well as enhance it underscores the importance of understanding the ways in which software influences creativity and the decision making process. The software version that was perceived to be more fun to use and more helpful, although conceptually more difficult to use, was associated with the most creative solution. This preliminary result suggests that it is critical to consider the perceptions of DSS users rather than designers in implementing even general design principles. In addition, features that are related to fun and helpfulness may gain even more importance as ways to aid and motivate the user to work through conceptual difficulties.

The research reported in this paper can strve as a basis for further research along several dimensions. First, extensive research surrounding the implementation of the creativity enhancing design principles presented in §3 is needed. Our research has demonstrated that broadly defintd general design principles are not enough to ensure that the software will have the intended effects. The two versions of ods/CONSULTANT used in this experinent appeared on the surface to adhere to the same design principles. The theory of creativity cannot be used to distinguish between the two versions.

The results of this study indicate that it is difficult to specify precisely what a creativity-enhancing DSS really is or to recognize one when it does exist. You cannot look at a DSS in isolation in understanding its effect; you must consider the user-DSS interaction. Further research is needed to test hypotheses such as the following:

1. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to have greater facilities for stopping, storing work sessions, and then resurning work later than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

2. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to have greater depth and positive tenor in its feedback than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

3. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to have a fuller range of qualitative and quantitative decision aids than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

4. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to be technically easier to use than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

5. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to be conceptually more challenging than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

6. A creativity-enhancing DSS that is perceived to be more enjoyable or fun than other DSS will result in higher levels of creative responses.

Second, further rescarch on influencing users' decision making processes through software can significantly impact DSS design practices. Our rescarch has shown that, through software, it is possible to intervene in a subject's decision making process, resulting in performance that closely fəllows a stepwise model of decision making. Further, we have shown that adherence to such a model cannot insure creative results. It should also be possible to provide alternative forms of intervention; for instance, software that leads users to employ divergent thinking without the imposition of a stepwise process or software that offers dynamic step ordering as the user proceeds through a decision making scenario. Determining processes that are most beneficial for users to adopt in a particular task environment is a worthwhile research task.

The theoretical work presented here provides a basis for more extensive research. The empirical results presented in this paper begin the task of understanding the link between creativity and software. We hope that the significant results and insights found here will encourage other researchers to join in this effort.\*
