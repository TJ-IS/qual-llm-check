# User Involvement as an Interaction Process: A Case Study

Michael Newman and Faith Noble

Department of Accounting and Finance

Universitv of Manchester

Manchester M13 9PL, England

User involvement is recommended to analysts as a technique of successful system development, but as a process it is little understood. This case study compares four process models of user involvement-learning, conflict, political and garbage-can-with each other and with an empirical example of system development. Different models are seen as appropriate to explaining the nature of user involvement in different stages of development and contexts. Structural conditions and issues of power are shown to be decisive in the development of conflict and conflict resolution. A two-stage model of user involvement based on Robey and Farrow's work (1982) is proposed which distinguishes conflict development from conflict resolution.

## 1. Introduction

User inol  infomton sst evelom D)  for sm im been seen as contributing to successful implementation, and recommended as a strategy to practitioners on that account. Yet the precise nature and consequences of user involvement have been insufficiently explored empirically. Studies have focused on demonstrating a correlation between user involvement and success. A recent example (Tait and Vessey 1988) illustrates the shortcomings of that approach. Although they tried to take account of the complexity of the relationship, they found only a weak association, and concluded by calling for longitudinal studies. In many cases, neither concept has been clearly defined, and the term user involvement has been used globally. both of which contribute to the lack of convincing evidence of such an association, despite its intuitive appeal (Ives and Olson 1984). In recent years, however, mcre attention has been paid to user involvement as a process, and to how and why it might contribute to successful development. In this paper, we define user involvement as a process of interaction between systems specialists and users or their representatives. We examine four process models and attempt to fit the facts of a case study to them in order to assess how well they apply. This is an exploratory study which, in Franz and Robey's taxonomy (1987), has the primary purpose of discovery over multiple time periods.

Markus and Robey (1988), comparing variance and process models of the role and use of information systems in organizations, pointed out that process models have greater empirical fidelity, although they are harder to construct and to test. One of their main advantages is their incorporation of the historical dimension. Outcomes are seen as resulting from a number of conditions, each of which is a necessary but none a sufficient cause. These conditions are interrelated and interdependent, and the sequence of their occurrence is significant. Process models also can allow for the role of chance or random elements in shaping outcomes. By taking a single case of system development and observing the interaction between designers and users over time, as we do here, it is possible to clarify many aspects of user involvement. One can see for example how the nature of user involvement differs at each stage of the process, and how what has occurred in the preceding stage affects the next one. The outcome can be more clearly defined and the relationships among the various factors which have combined to produce it can be observed more clearly than would be possible in a purely synchronic study (Yin 1984).

At the same time, the process of system development needs to be seen in its context (Kling and Scacchi 1982). Such aspects of the larger organizational setting as structure, culture, and history affect relations between users and systems specialists when they begin to design systems, and constrain their freedom of action throughout the process. Simultaneously, the development of a system can change the context. The case study method is particularly suitable for showing change in its context.

## 2. Process Models of User Involvement in System Development

We have abstracted from the literature four process models of user involvement; in so doing we are aware that we have over-simplified the views of the named authors. Two of the models are prescriptive, and concerned with how user involvement leads to successful implementation. The others are more descriptive, and agnostic as to outcome. The later models build to some extent on earlier ones, but add new elements. Thus they are not mutually exclusive. They differ in how they conceptualize user involvement, and the way in which they think it works (see Figure 1).

## 2.1. The Learning Model

The simplest variant of the learning model sees user involvement as an opportunity for designers to educate users about the system. As a result, users' expectations become more realistic, and they resist change less (Bjorn-Anderson and Hedberg 1977). "Success" is user satisfaction with the system, and use of it as the designers intended.

In this model, learning is largely one-sided. Designers have little to learn except details of user requirements. In contrast to this “traditional rationality," Boland (1978) developed a mutual learning model, in which users and specialists recognize each other's distinctive capabilities and views, and cooperate in order to produce a joint resolution to a common problem. Each side learns from the other in the course of development, which Boland calls “alternative rationality." Boland's experimental evidence showed that such an approach produced a more “usercentered" solution, whereas traditional rationality resulted in designs which focused on the organization as a whole. Either outcome could be said to be successful, depending on one's point of view.

These learning models differ in how much they see users as having to contribute to the design process. but both treat the problem of development as essentially cognitive, bounded, and technical. They are thus consistent with a rational perspective of system development (Franz and Robey 1984). While rationality is always the justification for developing systems, many design situations are not so straightforward. Exclusive reliance on a learning model neglects the possibility of conflicting goals and interests. Such models may fit some design situations, for example, where past experience with computer systems resulted in organizational learning or where there is an organizational climate of trust, but it is obviously inadequate for many others.

## 2.2. Conflict Models

Conflict models, on the other hand, have been developed to explain situations where there are complex problems, incompatible goals, and multiple criteria of success (Robey and Farrow 1982). Common or shared information systems which cut across departmental boundaries provide examples of such situations (Keen et al. 1982). Here, conflict in ISD may arise fror the differing interests, goals and world views of 1) systems designers and users, 2) different user departments, 3) individual departments and the organization as a whole (Markus 1981; Newman and Rosenberg 1985; Hirschheim, Klein and Newman 1987).

Robey and Farrow's (1982) conflict model, which was supported by their survey data on reported involvement, influence and conflict in eight organizations, said that user involvement led to increased conflict as development proceeded. A subsequent test of the model (Robey, Farrow and Franz 1989) showed that participation led to conflict only if it was accompanied by user influence. At the same time that it created conflict, participation with influence created opportunities for conflict resolution (the revised model is shown in Figure 2). Here, user involvement is conceptualized as a process through which users and designers discover and resolve their differences. Thus mutual cognitive learning does play an implicit role. However, besides learning about systems, users “learn how to exert influence towards conflict resolution as well as conflict generation as the development process proceeds"(Robey and Farrow 1982). In contrast to the previous model, the concept of learning is wider, it leads at first to more rather than less resistance, conflicts of interest are recognized, and the goal or outcome is the resolution of conflict. Success is defined as a solution which is acceptable to the entire group.

DeBrabander and Thiers (1984) expressed similar ideas, although they did not discuss conflict explicitly. In their view, success (i.e. users use the system as agreed) depends on effective communication, and it is this which explains how user involvement works. The semantic or knowledge gap between specialists and users already makes effective communication difficult; in this situation specialists are tempted to use sanctionary power (threats and side-payments), if they have it, in order to speed up the process. But if this occurs, communication will be distorted and implementation will be less successful. Because of their specialist knowledge, as well as their position in the organization, designers often have potential power over users. DeBrabander and Thiers argue that they should not use it, or should be discouraged from doing so by a third party.

The conflict model appears to be superior to the learning model in that it recognizes the potential which user involvement has for producing conflict, and it sees the amount of influence users have as instrumental in conflict resolution. Also, Robey and Farrow build on Edstrom's (1977) idea that user participation and influence may vary over the course of development, depending on the nature of the task at each stage. Thus, in their survey, reported conflict was low in the initial design stage perhaps because the implications were not yet clear to users. It became greater as development progressed; Robey and Farrow speculate that this occurred because the task becomes more structured, users learn, and can begin to see the implications for their work as development progresses.

Although this model seems to explain more of what happens during user involvement in system design, and how it could lead to successful solutions, it does not explain how conflict is structured in the first place, or over what issues it arises, nor does it adequately address the problem of power. DeBrabander and Thiers moved some way in this direction with their concepts of semantic gap and power symmetry. Designers were enjoined to allow users to influence the process, thus assuming that designers have power but car abjure its use. This prescriptive element of the model would seem to limit it to situations in which designers are not greatly constrained by time and budgets, and where quality rather than speed of implementation is the main organizational objective. Salaway (1987) in an action research study of user/analyst communication shows that more open communication leads to better etror detection during design. However she fails to show the conditions under which open communications might arise. We need models which are more empirically based and which take into account power and the way conflict is structured.

## 2.3. Political Models

Empirical work in recent years has paid more attention to the political aspects of ISD (Keen 1981; Markus 1983; Newman and Rosenberg 1985; Hirschheim, Klein and Newman 1987; Kling and Scacchi 1982; Kling 1987). The organizations into which information systems are introduced are seen as political orders, with established distributions of power which may be disrupted or confirmed by the design of the technology (Pettigrew 1973; Markus 1981; Markus and Pfeffer 1983). Moreover, writers such as Keen and Gerson (1977), Franz and Robey (1984), Kling (1987), and Markus and Bjorn-Andersen (1987) have pointed out the political nature of the development process itself. Kling (1987, p. 312) explicitly includes a political model as part of the assumptions for his “web" model of computing in organizations.

Political models take account of conflict but are concerned with the way in which conflict is structured by the existing organization, and the role of power in conflict resolution. Users and systems specialists have interests which they pursue through the development process. Political tactics such as bargaining and negotiations are relevant to conflict resolution. If these are not successful, ISD can become a power struggle with a win-lose outcome determined by which side has the most resources. “Success" is then a suspect concept, as it is telative to whether you are on the winning or the losing side.

Political models of ISD add to our understanding of user involvement, but in the past have tended to be more descriptive than prescriptive, and thus offer only isolated suggestions rather than a clear guide to practitioners. They also tend to neglect specifying the conditions under which political behavior is more or less likely to occur (see, for example, Pfeffer 1981, Chapter 3). The existence of conflicting interests is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for organizational politics. Individuals must also perceive those interests and pursue them. Salience of the issue to the individual (Madison et al. 1980) and individual political sensitivity (Vredenburgh and Maurer 1984), also information, awareness and political skills are intervening variables. Political science over the last century has demonstrated both the basic truth and the limitations of rational interest models of political behavior.

## 2.4. The Garbage-Can Model

A fourth model, which is well known in the organizational literature, has not been explicitly applied to system development. although it appears relevant to a number of the published cases. In this model, randomness and accident play a large part. Actors are seen as having limited rationality; they are not sure what their interests are and pursue them only intermittently. Involvement and participation are fluid. Key players leave the scene, the composition of the team changes constantly, as do the goals. In this model, to paraphrase Cohen et al. (1972), ISD is a “collection of choices looking for problems, issues and feelings looking for decision situations in which they might be aired, solutions looking for issues to which they might be an answer, and decision makers looking for work." The decision, or adopted design, is the result of these four streams: problems, solutions, participants, and choice opportunities.

The garbage can model was intended to apply to a particular type of organization, or decision situation, described as “organized anarchy," which was based on universities. Its applicability may therefore be restricted to organizations which are only loosely coupled (Weick 1976), and where both goals and technology are unclear. Also, its prescriptive use is limited, as outcomes are basically non-deterministic.

## 2.5. Approaches to the Integration of the Models

We have tried to contrast these models, but it should be apparent they are not entirely mutually exclusive. Cognitive learning may occur in conflict, political, and even garbage-can models. Politics incorporates the idea of conflict, and the garbage-can model can accommodate politics, if expectations of the actors are lowered. How then can we reconcile or choose among these models? We suggest two approaches:

(1) We can take a contingency approach and attempt to specify the empirical conditions under which one or the other is likely to be more useful. Organizational characteristics and intended system design features are two of the types of conditions which could be relevant. We have suggested for example that learning models may be appropriate for simple organizational contexts and design problems. Political and garbage-can models may apply in situations of greater organizational and information system complexity and uncertainty, and when we find a lack of fit between organizations and systems (Ginzberg 1980; Markus and Robey 1983). Differences among participants in the development process in terms of knowledge, interests and power (Figure 1) are partly shaped by context.

(2) A second approach would be to see whether different models of user involvement apply at different stages of the design process. Boland and Pondy (1983) showed that two different models explained the same empirical situation, but at a given point in time one was dominant and the other submerged, in a “figure and ground" relationship. The Robey and Farrow evidence about conflict varying over stages also points in this direction.

We suggest therefore that each model may contribute something to the understanding of system development, but that the relative importance of each in a given case may depend upon contingent conditions such as organizational context and system features, and upon stage of the development process. Taking account of both vertical (structural) and horizontal (processual) dimensions provides a fuller view of the whole phenomenon (Pettigrew 1988). Since our example involved a case where conflict was apparent, we have analyzed it in two phases, in order to take into account both the way in which conflict was structured by the context, and the processes through which conflict was resolved. We have applied the models to each phase.

## The Case Study

## 3. The Development of Conflict

## 3.1. Background

This case study is based on interviews with computer center staff, users, department heads and top managers (vice-presidents) in a state university. They were conducted between September 1985 and December 1986, with a follow-up in December 1987. Figure 3 illustrates the organizational relationships which existed at the university at the time of the interviews.

The university was in the process of implernenting a computer-based student information system, which was intended to pool and distribute data from a number of different departments, in order to provide better service to students. The plan was to proceed department by department, according to priority of need. The episodes discussed in this part of the paper concern the implementation of an on-line, screen-based admissions system called Thruway, which was to be part of the student information system. Financial Aid and Accounts Receivable in the Bursar's office had already been completed. ## 3.2. The Purchase Decision

The university had a computer center which handled a collection of independent batch processing systems, designed in the 1960's. Their adequacy varied from department to department. Some middle managers (heads of administrative departments) succeeded in getting vice-presidential approval in 1980 to install an integrated student information system. A selection committee was set up, and several vendors made presentations.

The members of the committee agreed that the pressing need of the university at that time was to computerize Financial Aid. That department was operating an inefficient manual system and finding it increasingly difficult to meet requirements. A new director of Financial Aid had been appointed from within the department; she had accepted the position only on condition that the work would be computerized. In choosing among vendors, therefore, the committee chose the one which had the best financial aid package. Very little thought was apparently given to how the choice of this vendor would affect other departments.

The vendor, Alpha, had no admissions package. If admissions had figured prominently in the committee's decision, it seems unlikely Alpha would have been chosen, as there were many other software vendors who had developed admissions systems at that time. The Director of Undergraduate Admissions, the largest single admissions user, was on the verge of retirement and did not take much interest in the new system. Thus, this department had little involvement in the initial purchase decision, and the events which followed are traceable in part to this fact.

Early and critical decisions about the information system seem to fit the garbage-can model better than any other. The introduction of an integrated on-line system was seen as an opportunity to do something about the problem of Financial Aid, which affected students and impinged on other departments. Competitive politics did not seem to be important, and heads of departments which would be directly affected did not appear to see the implications of the decision for themselves. Influence could be said to enter in only insofar as the Director of Financial Aid would not agree to accept the position unless she received a promise that the department would be computerized. The chance fact that the Director of Admissions was about to ietire meant that an important user department was not involved in the original decision, and this had serious consequences later on for the whole system.

## 3.3. The Admissions System-The Structuring of Conflict

Since the vendors, Alpha, did not have an admissions package, they agreed to cooperate with the university computer center in developing an on-line admissions system to replace the tape-based batch systems then in use. The new system, Thruway, was designed to combine several different university admissions functions; therefore it was not specifically tailored to meet the needs of Undergraduate Admissions, the largest single user. Also, Alpha hoped subsequently to be able to market the product to other U.S. universities, and the need for a marketable, generalized system imposed significant design constraints, as will be seen below.

A Technical Coordinator was appointed from Undergraduate Admissions as user liaison with the system designers. Although her systems knowledge was limited, she was enjoying the challenge of the new position and was keen to learn. She comments below on the users' resentment at the beginning of the design process, which arose from their lack of participation, and the inherent conflict between her department and an admissions system which was being designed to serve several different functions.

TC: We really didn't have the control that we wanted ot the overall decision. We thought we needed more control actually than we probably did at the time. I think we entered the thing quite defensively. It was designed for several different adnussions functions of (the) university, some totally different, so that they were trying to have a best fit situation, which really wasn't making any one person happy. And we felt, because we were by fır the largest volume user, that the tail was wagging the dog. We went through all kinds of agony .

Undergraduate Admissions had what they felt were satisfactory means of processing admissions, which they were reluctant to give up for the sake of a new system which might or might not work. This lack of perceived need influenced their attitude towards the new system and the time and effort required to develop it.

However, there were strategic organizational considerations external to the needs or desires of Undergraduate Admissions. Recruiting rather than just processing admissions was becoming increasingly important to the university as a whole. It was facing declines in student numbers after several years of healthy growth, and there was an increasing awareness of the competition from other educational institutions, especially for the more able students. Competitive pressures therefore led to a desire for a more piofessional, marketing approach to admissions, and the new system was to include a recruitment module. Also, when the Director of Undergraduate Admissions retired, the appointment was given to someone who was committed to recruiting, as well as experienced in computerized admissions.

We have thus seen how historical, organizational, and chance factors created the potential for conflict in the early phase of development. The university, Alpha, and the computer center all had an important stake in the development of the admissions system. Undergraduate Admissions, a key department, did not, or at least did not perceive its importance. Another aspect of the context which was to be important was structural: administrative departments appeared to be nominally equal in power (Noble and Newman 1988). This included the computer center, which did not enjoy a privileged position vis a vis user departments. All of these factors contributed to the way in which development proceeded and the form that user involvement took

## 3.4. User Involvement in the Early Design Stage

Users from Undergraduate Admissions were involved in the design of the system by means of representation on a user committee of four, which included a Technical Coordinator newly appointed from within the department. Their main task in the early design phase was to specify their needs in scope documents:

TC: We started to become oriented to it. I think that's the best way to describe it. They introduced us to the system and we created things like scope documents, what we wanted out of the system, what we felt we needed, what it should do for us, staffing issues.

The nature of “involvement," at this stage, fits the learning model. Users performed the traditional task of providing the systems people with information concerning user requirements, and began to become acquainted with systems. The Project Leader for Admissions (PL), who was appointed from the university computer center, was the systems counterpart of the Technical Coordinator. She had considerable technical experience but it had been gained on relatively small projects. This was in contrast to Thruway which involved dealing with several offices. Her notion of “user involvement" is indicated in the quotation below:

PL: With the user, trying to find out all their functions and detailing all their functions in the scope document. If we left out anything they were to call us, or write it in, or whatever, and identify those needs and problems and we would address them. So at that level they were involved before the design ...

User involvement in the early design phase thus fits Boland's model of “traditional rationality." The designers extracted the user requirements, then went away and designed a system for them. But when the systems people came back and demonstrated the system, the reaction of the users was unfavorable:

PI: . . a very negative reply came out when we showed them the whole system and we had three weeks of meetings with them. They came back with a very negative correspondence on the system as a whole ...

Although they were involved in the early design phase, users' resistance was not lessened. In fact, it had only begun.

3.4.1. Number of Data Entry Screens. As the conflict model would predict, user involvement in the design process brought to, the surface a conflict of interest between users and system designers. In this case, it concerned the number of data-entry screens the Undergraduate Admissions department would have to use in the new system. The number of screens w&s a problem for the users because they believed it would interfere with their work. They had 15,000 to 16,000 applications to process each year; to go through eight to ten screens to process each individual applicant would take half an hour. The design of the system was cumbersome, and because of that, the new Director of Admissions believed, it was error-prone. Furthermore, Admissions knew that at peak times they would have to use temporary, untrained labor, who needed to be able to enter data without too much difficulty. 'The Director of Admissions herself had originally tended to treat the complaints of her subordinates as “resistance," but after trying to use the system she was more sympathetic to their point of view:

DA: I guess I have begun to think, after my work with t this morning—-people have been telling me for a long time that tht system i poorly designed, and I have been writing that off as the normal resistance of a group ot people to change. This ısn't tıy first data system—I guess, after workıng with it this morning, 1 came to the conclusion that maybe we'll be able to make it work and maybe we'll be able to fit it to ur needs, but it is at least on frst approach to it fairly poorly designed. It's just cumbersome

3.4.2. Multiple Users. The need to design a shared admissions system required compromises among the needs of different offices, which meant that no one office was fully satisfied. These compromises were left to the systems designers to work out. Instead of negotiating directly among themselves and agreeing on the elements they needed, departments insisted on including in the system all the elements they were used to. 'The result was the large number of screens required by any one user. Also, because departments did not confront the issue among themselves, the systems designers became the targets of the user departments frustration with the system.

The difficulty of designing an integrated system which would at the same time satisfy the needs of individual offices was commented on by several of the other people interviewed. The Project Leader knew that the lack of customized screens was a common complaint, but felt she couldn't do anything about it within the design constraint of one screen to one file. This constraint was another point which made communication between specialists and users difficult.

The design situation was therefore complex and this led to conflict and politics. The project leader appeared to operate with a simple learning model and could not handle the problems which confronted her.

## 3.5. Semantic /Knowledge Gap

The issues which we have discussed so far show how the design context structured conflict. We now look at the semantic gap. The semantic gap between users and analysts has to be explored from both sides, starting with the users.

3.5.1. Users' Lack of Technical Knowledge. In the early design phase, the users' inexperience and lack of technical knowledge made it very difficult for them to define their needs or to have a realistic idea of what a system could and could not do.

TC: Really, we were operating blindly because we had very little to no experience with it.

Interviewer: Did you find that you knew what you wanted from a new system?

TC: No, no. We found what we thought we wanted ... There's always a trade-off and there are things we wanted without really realizing... “It would be nice if we did this, this, and this,"once you understood more about it, you realized that doesn't buy us enough of an end product in order to justify the work that would be put in . .

Interviewer: Were there things you'd missed out in the original?

TC: No, not really missed out. What I'm saving is that we didn't have an altogether ... realistic idea about an on-line system and what it could do for us and what it couldn't ... We were asked to make decisions with background we didn't have.

These comments make it clear that the users had indeed been through a learning process; they now knew that some of what they originally wanted they did not need or was not worth the cost, but they did not know that when they were being asked what their requirements were. What the users learned may have reduced their resistance to other issues, which were not discussed because they were resolved successfully. But it did not change their attitude to the issue of screen design, and no amount of education from specialists could do so.

3.5.2. Specialist Knowledge of Designers. The Project Leader found it difficult to explain the constraints and benefits of the new system to the users. From a systems point of view, a clean design was one which linked one file to one screen. This was the Alpha standard, and one which the Project Leader, with her technical knowledge, was in a position to appreciate, even though it made more difficult her job of selling the system to the users. They were naturally less interested in elegant design than in getting their work done efficiently, which to them meant seeing all the data they needed on one screen and preferably in a form they were accustomed to:

PL: ... in Thruway, we get multiple files and we try to keep data on one file and one screen most of the time. It just seems a cleaner way of doing business. There's a lot of things that, because of data processing and what we're trying to achieve in coordination with Alpha, that's very difficult to explain to a non-data processing user. And so it's not been any easy task in that way.

PL: I feel as though Alpha has come up with exceilent design considerations and, as a systems person, I really support much of the design that Alpha has put forward ... but unfortunately, ... someone else perhaps, in Undergraduate Admissions, who has a specialised form, even though this involved four files in thıs one form, it's very difficult fo: them to see why we can't put all that form on just one screen...

What appeared to be the optimal from a technical or systems point of view was unworkable from the point of view of the users, who did understand very well the requirements and pressures of their work,

The project leader could see the benefits to the university and to the department of the integration and rationalization the new system would offer, but found it hard to persuade the users that these outweighed the costs of implementation and giving up their old systems.

PL: You can go and give presentations and explain as much as you possibly can. First of all many of them are not data processing people-and even thost that are give you a lot of resistance—but most of them are not. So they really are very difficult, even when you explain the benefits. There's no way I believe you can really convince them at that point in time that the struggles that they're going through now will pay off later.

The limitations of the learning model are apparent here. The project leader had originally hoped that a positive presentation of the benefits of the new system to the university would be enough to persuade them. She had now abandoned this aim. Of course, the users had understood only too well the large cost required of them in exchange for very little tangible benefits. Instead of cooperation and mutual learning there would now be conflict.

3.5.3. Analysts' Lack of Understanding of the Work. The admissions process itself was not particularly complicated, but users fet that the systems designers could not grasp the problem presented by the sheer volume of applications:

Interviewer: Do the systems people truly understand the work of Admıssions?

TC: Yes and no. I don't think they truly understand or nave a concept of the volume . .. It's easy to say that you get 15,000 applications, but unless you have them piled on your desk and have to address each one of them in a certain way, I don't think you understand... it's really more or less the size of the task, in Undergraduate Admissions anyway Other than that, there's nothing really mysterious about the processing of admissions ... 1 think they still don't grasp the enormity, the volume of it.

The Project Leader however believed that she knew what the users needed; unfortunately, the users did not agree:

PL: So I guess they will . .. question your authority because they say "We know our business best. I've done transfer credit evaluations for 20 years and here you are working on new projects and you don't know what my office needs are."

Interviewer: That's what you're trying to find out.

PL: We said, "Well, we know what your office needs are. You might have to change the way you're goıng to do business." And that's the hard part.

The Project Leader interpreted the users’ attitude as “resistance," due merely to entrenched habit, or dislike of any form of change:

PL: Some people have been here a number of years and they have their own ways of doing business and they have their specific forms. One of the problems was that “This is the way our office does business. Take this form and put it up on the terminal. I want to do exactly the way I'm doing it, except instead of doing it on a piece of paper, I want you to develop for me a screen that does exactly what the piece of paper does. And I want exactly the same format. I don't want to change it because it took me 20 years to develop the format that I have today. And people in data processing should not be telling me where I should be doing my business."

The learning which had occurred had largely been one way. Users had learned about systems, but not enough to sacrifice making their work more difficult for a clean systems design. The project leader tried hard to educate them, but on this issue did not succeed because there was a real conflict of interest between the users' view of their needs and the computer center's definition of the requirements of a well-designed system which would serve the university as a whole. There is little evidence of mutual learning at this stage, because the computer center staff seemed resistant to the expressed needs of the user department and had shown little inclination to negotiate or compromise.

## Summary

So far we have seen how user involvement in the development of the admissions system was in part a learning process, but that learning did not lead to lessening of resistance, as suggested by the learning model, but to increased resistance and conflict, as predicted by the conflict model. We have seen how the conflict was structured by the initial decision, and lack of participation in it by an important department, and why there was so little involvement at that stage. The conflict which developed between the admissions users and the computer center staff was partly due to the semantic gap, which made it difficult for each side to understand the other's point of view, and partly to a conflict of interest, the users being concerned with getting their work done efficiently, and the specialists with adhering to the original design considerations. The conflict emerged only when the users could begin to see the implications of the design for their work. A conflict model which recognizes the importance of user learning best explains what has occurred so far.

## 4. Process of Conflict Resolution

The process through which the confict was resolved can be seen as a series of episodes of interaction between designers and users, in which each party tried to promote its own view and secure its favored solution. We have here a sort of dance", but it takes the form of a series of tactics and counter-tactics in what the parties perceive as a win-lose situation. The political model best explains user involvement in this stage. The tactics can roughly be grouped under persuasion, threats, and coercion. Over time, there was a definite progression or escalation from one to the other, until one side prevailed.

## 4.1. Persuasion

The project leader at first attempted to work out a compromise to the problem of the screens by developing a single screen which would contain most of the data Undergraduate Admissions needed. However. the users were still not satisfied because they had to use a number of other screens as well. The designer tried to convince the users that the number of screens was not a problem because it would be easy to flip from one to the other to input the necessary data.

PL: There will be the trackıng screen, which will have ail their documents that they track, all on one page, and their high school screen, which is all on one page, which they can flıp very easıly between within the system. But that does not seem to appease them.... They can easily go from the admissions module with just a little keystroke basically to the tracking module ... It's very quick and yet at the time we were presenting the modules it was very difficult to make that point and for them to accept it.

## The doubts of the users are expressed below:

TC: The reaction of the systems people was “Oh, it's goung, to go like this. It's just going to go really quıckly once you get used to it, but you are just nervous about it." Well, you don't know how much of that is true and how much isn't, because you've had no experience.

In the test period, the users' fears were confirmed. They were sharing the mainframe with other departments, the response time was slow, and it took a long time to get from one creen to another. This further undermined the credibility of the computer center staff in the eyes of the users:

TC: We would sit for over 60 seconds and wait for the screen to change, and we were hysterical. We were hysterical because all you had to do was .. multiply this by eight screens by 2,000 applications and we would be dead in the water. There would be no) way this would work.

The computer center responded by blaming the slow response time on the conditions of the test period. But that explanation did not survive, and the users lost even more confidence:

TC: Originally, I remember .. . when we had eight screens or nine or ten screens to access to enter in a coding form, and we would complain, they woul tell us. "Oh, but the screens are going to change like this" Well, when we got into the test .. and that was very slow, Then they said, "Well, test environments are lways slow because they get the last priority as far as the mainframe or whatever goes " So all right, we accepted that And we got into the production and had these 2 minute and 45 second waits ... . So there were all kinds of suspicions that they were leading us on

The volume of applications and the number of screens combined with the slow response time represented an intolerable burden to Admissions. A series of reassurances offered by the computer center was perceived as manipulation, and led to growing mistrust.

It appeared to the users that the designers were hasically unwilling to compromise any further on the matter of the screens, and that the designers were working in their own self-interest rather than the interests of the users:

TC: We were very defensive, because we felt they were trying to have us fit the system and we wanted to have the system fit us ... . But we really did start out in an adversarial kind of way. We were convinced they weren't after our best interests and they were convinced we weren't after theirs. So (they thought) that we were just against anything new. We had quite a time of it.

Interviewer: Sometimes a technical person can say things to you in such a way that you will do what they want to do, rather than what you want to do

TC: That's really truly what we believed. We felt that they didn't have our best interests ın mind, they had their best interests in mind. We literally didn't believe a word they said in the beginning

Although the designers had made one concession with regard to screens, the constraints of their situation (the contract with Alpha) were such that they did not feel able to go any further to satisfy the users' demands. Their main tactic thus far in dealing with the users had been persuasion, but it had not succeeded.

The users also tried to use persuasion with the designers. Their arguments and pleas having been to no avail, the users resorted to logging the length of time it took for the screens to change in order to convince the computer center of their problem. The designers were not persuaded of the significance of the issue, until one day by chance the Project Leader was able to observe for herself:

TC: I remember specifically one day going over to our Project Leader ... and I was showing her something I think on our securities system, and we literally sat there for, I think it was two minutes and forty five seconds before the screen changed. And that was not as a remote user. We were right there and they're tied in directly to the mainframe. So at that point there was no more convincing that needed to be done.

This demonstration may indeed have contributed to the Project Leader's acceptance of the users' complaints, but the conflict was not resolved by means of rational argument and evidence alone. The users began to try to exert influence.

## 4.2. Threats

When the issue appeared to be at stalemate, Admissions threatened to withdraw from participation in the system:

PL: And the fact is that in Undergraduate Admissions one of the people there said “We don't really want it. We've talked it over and we don't really want this new system." And here are we: we've been working two years on this, it's just about ready, and you don't want it. In other words, basically we (the users) have a system that works, even though it's an archaic batch system and it takes us two weeks to get updated information... “It doesn't matter. We resist the change. We don't want to spend the time working at this pace with you. We don't want to give."

This represented a significant escalation of the conflict. As we have seen, the participation of Undergraduate Admissions was essential for the development of the rest of the system.

Threat was met by counter-threat. The response of the computer center was to argue that the users had no alternative; the old system would be turned off:

PL: And basically the computer center says "Well, look, you have no choice". I guess this is not the computer center. I guess maybe the mandate comes from higher level. "The old system will be turned off and Thruway will be here to stay and you're not going to have that choice." But basically that almost has to come down on them, not from thi. level but from somebody above this level, maybe on the VP level, saying “We are going with the new system."

As events unfolded it became apparent that this threat was never executed. The computer center were themselves coerced into changing the design.

## 4.3. Coercion

Neither the project leader as an individual, nor the computer center as a department, had the power to compel the users to accept the multi-screen solution. Users had influence because of their structural position, not because of the restraint of systems staff. As the computer center was on the same level in the university hierarchy as the department of Undergraduate Admissions the conditions were of power symmetry, and neither side could compel the other. According to the conflict model, this should have led to a joint solution through compromise. Instead, they both looked to the structure of authority in the university to support their competing claims; first of all the computer center looked to the vice-presidential level to enforce the users' compliance. There were many indications, however, that this support was not forthcoming. Several of those interviewed commented on the vice presidents'lack of involvement in the aevelopment of the system and their reluctance to become embroiled in the political issues which it stirred up. Therefore, Admissions could not be forced to accept a particular solution. Neither could they be allowed to withdraw, because their participation was essential to the whole project. At this point, the computer centre staf' might have realized that their role had to change from designers and educators to negotiators. Yet they seemed unprepared to make concessions which would provide a benefit to Admissions to outweigh the very evident costs of continued participation. How then was this apparent impasse to be resolved?

The decisive factor in the resolution of conflict appears to have been the use of power by the new Director of Admissions, going over the heads of the computer center to her vice-president.

We have seen above that she was critical of the design of the admissions system. At the time of the first interview, she had also apparently decided that she would have to do something about it:

DA: So the system serves all masters and, in serving all masters, really it doesn't serve anybody. I think it's everybody's hope that we ultimately can persuade our computer center to design something which is nothing but an input screen, that dloesn't have any other use in the world but allowing us to take all the data elements that we’re going to input, put them on one screen and put them in that way. That' my agenda toward it

In any contest with the computer center, the new director had several important resources. First of ali, she had been hired with a mandate for change from the university authorities. Her experience with and commitment to computer-based admissions had weighed heavily in her selection, as had her commitment to recruiting. Secondly, her knowledge of computerized systems gave her an advantage with the computer center staff an inexperienced user manager would not have had. Thirdly, a Ph.D. in political science, she cvidently had political skills. At the time of the interview, she already had a clear strategy for dealing with the systems people. Whatever some of her staff might say, she realized that the department could not refuse to participate, and she was prepared to negotiate in order to get what the users wanted:

Interviewer: Is it poorly designed in such a way that it just can't be used?

DA: I've discussed this with the computer center and we have agreed that first you get the system up and then you try to fix it. So, given that there is really no choice in the matter, that is the approach that we are taking. We're going to go with it. We're going to learn the system and then we will be in a strong position to say “But we need this", or “We can't have it work that way".

The Technical Coordinator explained in an interview a year later how the influence of the new director had tipped the balance:

TC: We didn't really get anywhere with the powers that be until after (the former director) had retired and the new person ... came on and started on things. And I don't know whether they call that the honeymoon period of a new person, who pretty much gets what they want. They get their year, you know. But what has happened, which is new and different was that our systems people have finally heard our plea. I think basically it came down to an ultimatum between our Director and the systems people, that they create for us one screen, where we can enter data on one screen that will then link itself into the system and go to all the various places in the system where the data is required ... Then all of a sudden that was possible and they've done a wonderful job.

Thus the designers at last gave way on the issue of one screen. In addition, another partition of the main frame was assigned to the work of Admissions which appeared to be another concession. This relieved the problem of slow response time.

It is significant that once these concessions had been won from the computer center and the central issue of the conflict was resolved, the rest of the development of the Undergraduate Admissions system proceeded relatively harmoniously.

TC: I think once the computer center realized we weren't going to back down on certain things ... then all of a sudden it was not impossible ... once we realized that they were willing to do things like that, our attitudes changed also. So we kind of grew together. This whole thing evolved. So, right now, we have a very good working relationship with them and they've bent over backwards for us.

The middle development phase—test period—was a phase of heightened conflict in which users tried to influence the process by a number of different means. Conflict which arose in the first stage was resolved neither by rational argument nor an adaptive bargaining process, but by appeals to power.

## 5. Outcome of the Systems Development Process

In one sense, the result of user involvement in this case was success, in that users got what they wanted and were satisfied with the system. Presumably, they would also be likely to use it as intended. Yet from the point of view of the university as a whole, the development of the Admissions system was more problematic. Other units in the university, having seen the computer center concede to Admissions over design issues, were likely to make similar demands for customization. The “clean design", which was Alpha's standard and which was strongly supported by the project leader, was being eroded. This erosion was deplored by the project leader who despite the conflict was convinced that the original multi-screen design was both elegant and highly commendable. The integration of the student information system was made more difficult because of various departments going their own way. The software vendors, Alpha, ran into difficulties and withdrew from the project. The computer center then undertook the continued development of the whole project on its own. The consequent delays led to frustrated departments developing the r own systems, which would make future integration even more difficult. User involvement and the subsequent conflict and user-domination led to a design which was more user-centered, but certainly less satisfactory for the organization ,is a whole.

## 6. Discussion

Users were involved with the design process in this case, less as a matter of policy, than as a result of (1) the designers’ need to know their requirements, (2) the users' structural position in the organization, which allowed them to influence the process. This influence took on the character of a power struggle, which the users won. As a result, the design suited the uscrs, and from their point of view development would be considered a success.

The four models of user involvement discussed above each contribute to the analysis, but at different stages of development (Figure 5). The initial choice situation to develop a system with Alpha exeraplified the garbage-can model, but this model does not seem to fit the later stages, where a conscious and sustained effort was made by cach side to win over the other. Many more parties were involved in the initial purchase decision, and undoubtedly much greater uncertainty surrounded it. Only one strong vested interest appeared (student aid), and the others readily concurred. Although political models explain other choice situations (Pettigrew 1973), they do not explain this one. Parties to the decision did not seem aware of the implications for their areas. Yet this decision shaped the whole subsequent course of development.

The development process in Admissions was characterized by a shift from learning to conflict to politics. Although some form of learning no doubt continued throughout the whole design stage, the early stage most closely fits a simple learning model, in that users learned about systems and provided specialists with their requirements. Although users approached this project with some suspicion, there is no evidence of conflict or politics at this stage. However, as soon as the first design was shown to the users and the implications for their work became apparent to them, an open confict of interests emerged. In a manner consistent with the conflict model, each side at first attempted to explain its position to the other, and the systems people made one concession, the high school screen However, because of the contextual constraint, the conflict could not be resolved through discussion and negotiation. It therefore resulted in the use of political tactics by both sides, culminating in a successful appeal to high authority by the user manager. Attempts at persuasion continued in what we have called the political phase: even during a political struggle the participants will from time to time attempt to persuade or reach a negotiated settlement as part of the armory of political tactics. Yet the general impression is of each side trying to achieve its own aims. As for mutual problem-solving, it was not until after the conflict had been resolved that development proceeded by mutual learning and adaptation (see previous quotations from TC). Only after the important conflicts of interest had been removed was “alternative rationality" an appropriate model. In other cases, the struggle may continue over a number of years (e.g. Markus 1983).

## 7. Conclusions

Learning is an important element in user involvement, especially in the early stages. Some kind of mutual learning is essential if designers are to understand user requirements and if users are to be able to use the system. The problem of learning could be considered from both sides, and seen as a way of reducing the semantic gap. What is to be learned should include not just cognitive information regarding tasks and system, but an awareness on both sides of organizational and design context and how it shapes the choices to be made. Strategies for educating users, as well as training for analysts in social and organizational aspects of systems development, need to be incorporated into a learning model. Such learning can then be expected to reduce resistance which is simply due to fear and unfamiliarity.

Such fears are not the only cause of resistance. The learning model breaks down because conflicts of interest may be structured into the design situation, and education alone is not sufficient to overcome them. Ideally, they would be dealt with by negotiation and mutual agreement (Keen 1981)

This wili not be possible where any party has nonnegotiable demands and the power to insist on them. Both these conditions are determined by the organizational context. The conflict model has to be extended by considering the way in which conflict is structured by organizational and historical precedents. These would include an assessment of the system's organizational validity (Ginzberg 1980; Markus and Robey 1983; Schultz and Slevin 1975), the organization's climate of trust (Zand 1972), and the scale of the changes being considered. Models which take account of the political order effectively do this. At the same time, political models need to apply more attention to the process of conflict resolution, and the tactics and resources employed by different parties. Nonetheless, the concept of power may be crucial to the resolution of conflict. Once conflict has arisen it may not be possible to resolve it by a mutual and dispassionate exchange of ideas. In this case, as in Markus (1983), the impasse was resolved by an exercise of power after several attempts by the computer center to persuade, threaten and coerce the users had failed. We would suggest that this progression is not an isolated example (Hirschheim and Newman 1988). Thus, the learning model seems more relevant to the first stage of user involvement and to straightforward (e.g. one user department—one function) design situations. The conflict model, as intended, tells us more about later stages and about more complex situations. The political model could apply at any stage, but especially the initial choice situation, and the mid-development phase, when design implications have become clear. It is also applicable to complex systems which cross traditional organizational boundaries (Kling and Scacchi 1982; Markus 1983) Political tactics will be resorted to when the limits of learning and compromise have been reached

In this exploratory study, we have compared four process models of user involvement with empirical evidence from a case study. We have shown some of the strengths of the models, indicating the stage of design at which a model appears to predominate. Although a single case study has obvious empirical limitations, nonetheless one can use it to construct a theoretical model which has a wider application in describing the process of user involvement (cf. Yin 1984). Each of the process models used offers only a partial description of the user involvement process, which is not surprising, either because of their normative nature or the limitations of the empirical methodology employed.

This leads us to suggest a two-stage model of user involvement for complex design situations, where conflict is most likely to occur. The first stage would describe the structuring of conflict and its development (this usually occurs in the earlier stages of system development, for example, determining information needs (Edstrom 1977)) while the second stage would describe the process of conflict resolution (which takes place in the later stages of development). There are several contextual issues which are important in influencing whether the process of conflict development will lead to mutual learning, a negotiated solution or to an'escalation of conflict as in the case, resulting in a win/lose outcome. These we would call “conditions for conflict". For example, we would expect structural antecedents to impact upon the development of conflict. If the proposed system has low organizational validity (Ginzberg 1980; Markus and Robey 1983; Schultz and Slevin 1975) we would expect this to be associated with more conflict. On the other hand, a positive climate of trust (Zand 1972) would be associated with an attenuation of conflict and an increase in participation activities. Finally, we would expect large systems to be the source of more conflict during user involvement than smaller systems (Neumann, Davis, and McKeen 1980). There are no doubt other factors relevant to the processes, but we believe these to be the main effects. While this approach supports the use of Robey and Farrow's early model (1982) and the subsequently derived version (Robey, Farrow, and Franz 1988) for understanding the conflict development process, we have extended it in two important ways, both of which address the issues revealed by this case. Firstly, we recognize the pre-existing conditions for conflict which can strongly influence the likelihood of conflict developing. Secondly, we have separated the development of conflict from its resolution and extended the range of solutions.

Recognizing the likelihood of conflict at different stages of systems development should assist researchcrs and practitioners alike.
