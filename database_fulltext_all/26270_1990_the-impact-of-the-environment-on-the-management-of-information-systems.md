# The Impact of the Environment on the Management of Information Systems\*

School of Business Administration

Rochester, Michigan 48309

The dynamic environment of an organization makes management difficult. This is especially true in information systems (IS) departments. A new theoretical model, based on structured interviews with IS executives, proposes ways that some dimensions of the environment create problems for IS management. IS managers develop coping mechanisms to attenuate or prevent these problems. They use these coping mechanisms either to resolve the problems directly or to influence the environment to prevent the problems. The new theoretical model facilitates an organized study of the relationship between the IS department and its dynamic environment.

Information management—Information systems—Information technology

he effective management of information systems (IS) requires an understanding 1 of the diversity of environmental influences, the salience of these influences, and the management processes for coping with the problems they create. IS managers drawn from different organizational levels have reported problems resulting from changes in technology, government regulation, competitors, customers and IS end users (Lederer and Mendelow 1986). The IS department often must devote extensive resources to dealing with these problems. Practitioners and researchers alike need to understand how environmental influences can cause IS management problems and what mechanisms will alleviate these problems.

This paper clarifies the relationships between environmental influences, typical problems these influences create for IS management, and mechanisms that IS managers apply to attenuate the problems. Theories concerning the impact of the environment on organizations in general are used to understand the relationship between the IS department and its environment. The paper proposes a model to describe this relationship.

## Organizational Theories of Environmental Impact

Organizational theorists have long recognized the significance of the environment to the welfare of organizations (Barnard 1938, Dill 1958, Katz and Kahn 1966,

Thompson 1967). The organization's environment is defined as those physical and social factors that are outside the boundaries of the organization but are still relevant to its success (Duncan 1972). The environment can be viewed either as a deterministic force to which organizations respond or as an interdependent entity which organizations try to modify.

The deterministic perspective considers organizations reactive entities, at the mercy of the changing environment and the problems it causes. For instance, Emery and Trist (1965) demonstrated that the rate of change and the complexity of the environment might dictate organizational structure. In addition, the problems associated with uncertain environments prompt managers to maintain flexible organizational structures (Burns and Stalker 1961, Perrow 1970, Thompson 1967, Woodward 1965). The environment is thus viewed as a causal variable, and organizations adapt to its foibles through internal means. Successful adaptation results in high organizational performance (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967).

The interdependent perspective extends the deterministic perspective. It views the environment and the organization as entities that interact regularly. Research has shown that some organizations adopt proactive policies and try to modify their environments. Pfeffer (1982) suggested that organizations try to do this for their common good, both where relationships are commensal (i.e., involving similar organizations) and where relationships are symbiotic (involving unlike organizations).

Organizations seek not only to minimize the environmental impact before it is felt but also to provide opportunities for themselves. Kotter (1979) indicated that organizations can and should manage the environment by taking action before the environment forces them to react. For example, Porter (1980) demonstrated that organizations can dictate the structure of their industries through competitive moves. Pfeffer and Salancik (1978) suggested that managers should identify the specific environmental resources upon which the organization depends and take action to reduce this dependence. Indeed, they suggested that this dependence on environmental resources makes the “external constraint and control of organizational behavior both possible and almost inevitable" (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978, p. 43).

Organizations have the potential to modify their environments through the use of coping mechanisms. The choice of a coping mechanism depends on the nature of the environment. As Galbraith (1973) observed, contingency theory holds that there is no single best way to cope. Weick (1979) pointed out that the choice of a particular coping mechanism depends on management's perception and interpretation of the environment. Some authors have suggested that organizations succeed or fail due to managers' choices based on their own perceptions and interpretations of the environment (Child 1972, Hambrick and Mason 1984).

## The Environment of the IS Department

An IS department is surrounded by its host organization's environment as well as by everything within the organization that lies beyond the borders of the IS department. Observers have viewed the IS department as a business within a business (Cash, McFarlan, McKenney and Vitale 1988, Gerrity and Rockart 1986, Zmud, Boynton, and Jacobs 1986). The IS department depends on environmental resources (e.g., user requests) in the same manner that the entire organization depends on its environment (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). Thus, the conceptualization of the environment of the IS department is analogous to the conceptualization of the organization's environment. (However, in addition to customers, lenders, stockholders, suppliers, unions, technology, the government, and general economic conditions, the environment of the IS department includes users and potential users.) Inferring from the interaction between the organization and its environment, one would expect the response mechanisms of the IS department to be similar in their basics to the organization's response mechanisms (even if they differ in their detailed operation).'

Thus, like the total organization, the IS department faces environmental problems and develops mechanisms to cope with these problems. In order to better understand these problems and coping mechanisms, the authors have constructed a model for testing formal hypotheses. This type of model can be developed either from existing theory and research or from field data. This paper proposes a model derived from field data gathered during interviews with IS executives. The model is explained using existing organizational theory and research. It takes into account the components of the environment and the coping process. Thus, this paper takes an initial step toward developing theory that addresses the relationship between the environment and the management of IS.

## Methodology

The current study is the second phase of an ongoing research project. During the first phase, the authors determined that environmental impact creates difficulties for information systems managers. Three separate Nominal Group Technique sessions (Delbecg, Van de Ven, and Gustafson 1975) elicited the difficulties of top, middle, and operating management when developing IS plans (Lederer and Mendelow 1986). The 24 participating managers identified 13 difficulties. One of these—the impact of the changing IS environment—was common to all three levels of management. In particular, the participants identified new technology, government regulation, competitors, the market, and the user as the troublesome environmental factors.

The current phase of the research project consisted of a series of structured, indepth interviews with 20 senior IS executives from a wide variety of industries. The interviewees were the managers responsible for their organizations' IS departments They were employed by medium to large organizations. Interview appointments were set up by telephone. All managers contacted agreed to participate, indicating a great interest in the problem.

The interview with each IS executive lasted approximately three hours. Anonymity was guaranteed. A written agenda guided an exhaustive discussion of a wide variety of IS management problems, techniques, and philosophies. The following questions, specifically related to this paper, were asked:

(1) Is responding to the impact of changing environmental factors, such as new technology, a difficulty that you experience?

(2) If so, why? If not, why not?

(3) What do you do (or have you done) to overcome this difficulty?

Similar questions were asked with regard to government regulation, competitors, the market, and the user. The interviewees were also asked to identify any additional troublesome environmental factors, but none were mentioned.

Both researchers conducted all interviews and took extensive notes. They transcribed the notes as soon as possible (usually within two days). With the subjects

Priority Resetting. This is the major problem arising from environmental impact. Altered priorities frequently result in the reassignment of programmers and analysts to new tasks in different systems. Thus they must continually learn new systems and later relearn old systems when priorities indicate the need to start them again. Relearning is inefficient. Excessive delays take place and cause the total development effort to suffer.

Simultaneously, users are frustrated when they find that priority resetting will delay their own projects. The IS department's credibility suffers even when the department is not responsible for the reprioritization and obtaining user support for future projects becomes increasingly difficult.

Buy or Wait. Many IS managers face uncertainty about the availability and capability of new releases of hardware and software. The manager's dilemma is whether to proceed immediately using existing technology or to delay development until anticipated products with better cost-performance capabilities are available. Proceeding with existing technology may risk obsolescence even before the final implementation of a new system. Conversely, IS managers may wait for new technology that never appears.

Technology Mania. Frequently users and IS managers become blinded by the flash and glitter of technology. As a result, they search for technology rather than for solutions to problems. When technology is overemphasized, systems may be developed to address nonexistent problems. Colloquially, this is described as being “solution rich but problem poor."

Incompatibility. Acquisitions of new hardware and software may result in incompatibility with existing systems. Incompatibility includes the inability to transmit data between systems, to run programs on different computers, or to transfer skills to new systems.

Although some incompatibility problems result from IS management decisions, IS managers are particularly concerned when users purchase their own equipment and develop their own systems. During this process, line managers may usurp IS department authority and disregard IS department advice. As a result, line managers may suffer incompatibility problems later when they attempt to link their systems to the organization's mainframe. The IS department is then expected to solve the incompatibility problems, even though it did not create them.

Unrealistic Expectations. Users often do not fully understand the complexity of IS development. They do not comprehend data interdependencies and the critical characteristics of hardware and software necessary for IS development. Accordingly, users often make unrealistic demands for the acquisition of hardware or software. The IS department must then explain the unsuitability of the users' choices or must later fix a user-chosen system.

Sloppy Svstemns. IS management responsibility has increasingly been dispersed to line managers who now develop their own systems (Rockart 1988). Many of these end-user-developed systems have defied the rudimentary rules of systems analysis, design, and documentation. Elementary rules for data validation, file back-up, and security have been disregarded. Duplicate data created by such systems may result in inconsistent reports. The IS department may be blamed for this even if it was not involved in either the data collection or processing. Although the IS department has the responsibility for maintaining the integrity of the organization's data, it generally does not have the authority to do so for user-developed systems.

## Dimensions of the Environment

During the two research phases, managers reported that changes in five dimensions of the environment caused problems. Applying Ives, Hamilton, and Davis' (1980) concept of environmental entities as a classification scheme, the dimensions are divided into interorganizational (comprising technology, government, competitors, and customers) and intraorganizational (comprising users) groups (Figure 1). This division of the environment permits insight into the different processes by which the categories of problems arise and insight into how the coping mechanisms deal with these problems. For example, the IS department may have more knowledge about intraorganizational groups than interorganizational groups. The former are closer and possibly easier to understand. At the same time, the effects of the intraorganizational groups on the IS department are more direct since these groups reside within the organization. Moreover, these effects may also be more intense, since intraorganizational groups are subject to the interests of the dominant coalition (Danziger et al. 1982), and thus to the power and politics of the organization.

Changing Government Regulations Cause IS Management Problems. Interviewees pointed out that legislative changes force reprioritization in highly regulated industries, such as banking, and also in the administration of compensation, benefits employment and taxation in all organizations. The IS department, being partly accountable for the company's response to legislation, must juggle priorities to accommodate the changes.

Unpredictability in the timing and content of new legislation frequently impacts IS management. Although pending legislative changes may generally be known in advance, details are often uncertain. This can make the resources needed for compliance unclear. These uncertainties, together with short lead times, often force IS reprioritization.

Sometimes legislators fail to evaluate the direct costs and opportunity costs of compliance. In one instance, an interviewee reported that legislation was passed despite his industry's protests. Eighteen months later, his company complied by altering its IS at a huge cost, but its competitors did not comply. Once the government realized the demand on industry resources, it rescinded the legislation. In effect, the company in compliance was punished for compliance!

## Coping Mechanisms

In reaction to the problems caused by the environment, the IS department develops coping mechanisms to attenuate their impact, as shown by arrow B in Figure 1. This reaction is consistent with Pfeffer and Salancik's (1978) suggestion that organizations develop strategies to manage their dependence on environmental resources to reduce the extent of environmental control. The reaction also recalls Kraemer and King's (1981) observation that IS departments in city government develop policies to reduce computing problems arising from the environment. Interviewees discussed several coping mechanisms that they adopted to minimize environmental impact. These are summarized in Table 6.

In Figure 1, specific coping mechanisms internal to the IS department rely on systems professionals and attempt to deal directly with specific categories of problems (arrow C). Conversely, coping mechanisms external to the IS department rely heavily on parties outside the department in order to indirectly reduce the impact of the problems (also represented by arrow C). These external mechanisms may even attempt to proactively influence specific dimensions of the environment (arrow D) and thus attenuate the problems (arrow A). This recalls Kotter (1979), who points out that coping mechanisms may be directed at either internal structures or processes or at external entities. In addition, each coping mechanism can be used to respond to more than one problem. However, the application of a specific coping mechanism to a particular problem depends not only upon management taking a rational approach but also upon chance, timing, and the nature of the environment (Galbraith 1973). Hence the model proposed in this paper encompasses both a rational model of decision making (Kaplan and Schwartz 1977, Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret 1976) and March and Olsen's Garbage Can Model (Cohen, March, and Olsen 1972, March and Olsen 1979).

Failure to effectively apply coping mechanisms exacerbates problems and increases the pressures on IS management. In addition, attempts to use coping mechanisms to solve problems may produce new problems, resulting in a domino effect (although the interviewees did not mention this effect). This is because, as Leavitt (1965) points out, any change in one of the four organizational variables of task, structure, people or technology may result in changes to the remaining three.

The interview transcripts suggested a number of categories of internal and external coping mechanisms (Table 7). The following sections describe these categories in detail.

## Coping Mechanisms Internal to the IS Department

Systems Design. Systems design techniques can facilitate systems modifications. This enables the IS department to better cope with priority resetting. Many interviewees described their use of major systems analysis and design techniques such as functional decomposition, data flow diagrams, and data dictionaries (Gane and Sarson 1979, Orr 1977, Ross 1977) to facilitate systems modifications required by changes in the environment.

Administrative Policies and Processes. IS managers use administrative techniques to cope with the problems of buy or wait, technology mania, incompatibility, priority resetting, and sloppy systems. For example, clear goals help IS managers evaluate trade-offs when environmental changes force decisions about technology acquisition and reprioritization. Moreover, formal environmental assessment with frequent reviews of issues better prepares the IS department to anticipate environmental change.

Interviewees described a policy of staying close behind the “leading edge" of technology to help them cope with problems caused by technological change. They fear that the premature use of new technology may cause late and over budget projects. They indicated a preference for adopting a new technology only after others have proved it practical,

The interviewees also reported coping with technology mania and incompatibility by using a formal acquisition approval process, including vendor lists and veto authority. They use a cost justification process to prioritize proposed projects and thus reduce priority resetting. They apply standards, rules, and guidelines to minimize sloppy systems.

Vendor Watching. Vendor watching helps anticipate technological change in order to prevent the problems of buy or wait and incompatibility. Many IS managers assign subordinates to monitor specific vendors and technologies. Sources of information include seminars, trade journals, user associations, product announcements, and reports from consulting groups.

A novel method of vendor watching is to serve as a beta test site. By using prereleased hardware or software, the IS department can evaluate emerging technology first hand and influence the vendor's final product. (To the extent that the IS department attempts to modify its environment, this technique may also be viewed as an external coping mechanism.)

## Coping Mechanisms External to the IS Department

Public Relations. IS managers use this coping mechanism to inform users about issues, events, accomplishments and even failures in the IS department. The premise is that users who understand the problems facing the IS department will be less demanding and have fewer unrealistic expectations. Interviewees reported that training, education, and white papers help users separate glamorous technologies from those crucial to the organization's success. For example, a low-key information center can improve public relations by educating users about systems requirements in a “soft sell" manner, thus reducing sloppy systems. Public relations also helps reduce technology mania and creates an atmosphere of understanding and confidence in the IS department.

Political Action. Political action can influence the government (or even industry associations and vendors) before proposed policies necessitate priority resetting. For example, the banking industry is well known for its efforts to influence federal legislation that would result in exorbitant IS expenses.

Transfer the Problem. Another way of coping with environmental impact is to “pass the buck" by persuading groups beyond the IS department's borders to help it anticipate environmental change. For example, the IS department sometimes relies upon departments most affected by government regulation to foresee legislative changes and their impacts on systems. Similarly the IS department might ask the marketing department to anticipate shifts in customer preferences and competitive moves. If revised priorities become necessary, responsibility for the new priorities can then be more clearly assigned to the other department.

Divisional IS departments sometimes rely on their corporate IS department to track technological developments, since it may have greater resources. One interviewee discussed ways his larger corporate IS department operated as a beta test site and then distributed its findings to him. Thus, potential technology mania and buy or wait problems can be shared and attenuated.

IS departments use purchased software packages. Software vendors are responsible for modifying their packages as environmental change occurs. For example, payroll packages usually include a tax update service. This reduces the IS department's responsibility for coping with priority setting problems arising from such change.

Finally, the IS department may advocate manual processing. User requests for system changes might be refused and users told to handle changes manually. Manual processing transfers a problem to users and avoids priority resetting.

## One Additional Coping Mechanism

Muddle Through. Although IS managers realize that some of their difficulties are related to environmental impact, they may deliberately refrain from developing policies in response to it. Lindblom (1959) referred to this as “muddling through." In effect, the changing environment is looked upon as a fact of life, and dealing with it is seen as a cost of doing business. The IS department and users are prepared to face all categories of problems and to live with the resulting inefficiencies in systems development and operation.

When muddling through, the IS department might treat all environmental demands on an emergency basis. This causes the ad hoc reprioritization of projects and reallocation of resources. Users never know when their new projects or ongoing service may be delayed due to environmental exigencies.

## Contributions to Theory and Research

One can argue that information systems are a creation of their environment. Whereas many previous studies have considered the impact of IS on the environment, the model discussed here emphasizes the need to study the impact of the environment on the management of IS. In so doing, this paper makes several contributions to theory and research.

First, it describes the impact of the environment on IS management by identifying specific types of environmental influences, the difficulties which they can cause, and the means by which IS managers attempt to respond to them. This suggests that IS managers attempt to more than merely satisfy their users'needs as they occur. IS managers consider potential impacts of the organization's environment in order to anticipate users' changing demands for information. This proactive behavior is especially important. Traditional IS research has suggested that IS management reacts extensively to users' perceived needs for information; however, this study suggests that IS management is becoming more anticipatory.

Second, this paper contributes to the development of IS as a field of study. It builds IS theory by adapting the theory and research of another field, organizational studies. This will permit future researchers to use common definitions and assumptions to study the relationship between IS and the environment.

Third, this paper applies accepted techniques for generating grounded theory, in this case, related to the IS field. Glaser and Strauss (1967) suggest that theorists collect a vast number of qualitative facts to achieve generality. This was achieved in the current study through 20 structured interviews conducted in diverse organizations. Glaser and Strauss (1967) also suggest that theory must be faithful to everyday realities and must be induced from diverse data. This, too, was achieved by the interviews. Thus this paper demonstrates how such accepted techniques may be applied to the IS field.

Fourth, the paper contributes to IS theory and research by providing the groundwork for some testable hypotheses, the next step recommended by Glaser and Strauss (1967). Future research should test the model in Figure 1. Such an examination might begin by confirming the existence of the relationships represented by the arrows using a larger sample and more objective (i.e., survey-based) data. Sample hypotheses and their rationales might be:

H1. During periods of greater technological change, problems of buy-or-wait are more extensive (arrow A).

Rationale. During periods of many hardware or software releases, prospective purchasers will vacillate between acquiring a system based on existing technology and delaying until anticipated, improved products are available because they are unsure of the release dates and capabilities of the new products.

H2. Organizations that have experienced more extensive incompatibility problems have established more extensive administrative IS policies (arrow B).

Rationale. Organizations react to their inability to transmit data between systems, to run programs on different computers, or to transfer skills to new systems (incompatibility problems) by establishing extensive policies to standardize their hardware and software (administrative IS policies).

H3. Organizations with more extensive vendor watching have less extensive incompatibility problems (arrow C).

Rationale. Organizations increase their certainty about the release dates and capabilities of new products by closely monitoring the vendors.

H4. The political action coping mechanism is used more extensively in more heavily regulated industries (arrow D).

Rationale. The heavily-regulated banking industry is well known for its efforts to influence federal legislation that would cause exorbitant IS expenses.

While the current research established the present model based on structured interviews with 20 top IS executives, a survey with a broader sample might establish a more comprehensive and parsimonious model.

Thus future research should focus on understanding the relationships suggested in the model. Specifically, research should identify conditions under which various dimensions of the environment give rise to specific categories of problems (arrow A in Figure 1). For example, do highly competitive industries (where managers are under great pressure) experience more technology mania than less competitive industries? Research should also identify the conditions under which specific categories of coping mechanisms best resolve particular categories of problems (arrow C). For example, when do administrative policies, such as the reporting relationship of the top IS executive, ameliorate the priority setting problem? (It might be argued that a higher reporting relationship is associated with greater understanding of business objectives, and thus fewer priority setting problems.) Moreover, research should identify the categories of problems that give rise to categories of coping mechanisms (arrow B). Finally, it should determine the coping mechanisms that best influence the dimensions of the environment (arrow D).

Future research might also investigate the salience for IS managers of the effects of the problems and coping mechanisms. (The current study indicated only that these effects were salient enough to elicit discussion of problems and coping mechanisms from IS managers.) Future research might assess the actual financial impacts of the environment on IS management and determine the costs of the coping mechanisms. The complexity involved in successfully applying coping mechanisms might also be investigated.

Finally, future research might study the model from the point of view of general and functional managers. Thus researchers might determine the manner and extent to which these managers believe that their departments shield the IS department from the impact of the environment. Shielding of core technologies from the impact of the environment was proposed by Thompson (1967). Research might test the applicability of this theory to the IS function. Nolan and Wetherbe (1980) applied Thompson's buffering concept to IS and thus ignored the inter-organizational environment in their IS model. Since the current study found IS managers quite concerned about the environment, future researchers might question general and functional managers in order to better understand the presence or lack of this buffering in the IS context.

## Summary and Conclusion

Organization theory has evolved from viewing the environment as a dictator of organizational response to a belief that managers actively attempt to influence their environment in order to gain control over it.

The IS department is surrounded by a dynamic environment—an environment often seen as inflicting change in an unpredictable and dictatorial manner. The IS department may not possess the rewards and sanctions necessary to bring the environment under its control. Therefore, it has developed coping mechanisms to deal with environmental problems. This paper has laid the groundwork for organized study that could aid IS managers as they attempt to react to and influence their environment in order to achieve trouble-free use of the information resource. Apparently, the task of IS management is much more complicated and diverse than previous research has suggested. This article has explicitly identified interesting targets for future research to aid in the understanding of this complexity and diversity.\*
