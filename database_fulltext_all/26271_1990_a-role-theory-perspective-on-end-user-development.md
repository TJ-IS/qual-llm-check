# A Role Theory Perspective on End-User Development

Dennis F. Galletta

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business

University of Pittsburgh

Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260

R. L. Heckman, Jr.

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business

University of Pittsburgh

Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260

Recent advances in computer technology have been accompanied by significant changes in the roles of both users and developers. One well-known example is end-user development, which here provides a context for the application of role theory to IS issues. A framework is proposed for classifying IS roles in a way that can be used for role behavior analysis. The framework takes the form of a matrix, with activities relating to the construction and use of information systems on one axis, and activities relating to generic organizational levels on the other. Role theory permits analysis of organizational phenomena from either a structural or a process-oriented perspective. Propositions from both perspectives are presented which can be used to direct empirical studies.

Developers—End-User computing—End-User development—Role theory—Users

## 1. Introduction

ne of the most dramatic impacts of information technology has been how it has enabled the alteration of previously-existing tasks and contribution of entirely new tasks to be performed by office workers. Previously-existing tasks have been changed, or in some cases eliminated by automation of repetitive operations. More importantly, however, entirely new tasks have been added in many diverse areas. Examples of such tasks include data retrieval and analysis for decision-making, development and maintenance of decision models, and coordination and planning for these uses of technology.

The structure of jobs (or positions) in an organization is determined by the interaction of a number of complex forces. One such factor is the task environment, and from this perspective a job (or position) can be viewed as an array of tasks. It is therefore not surprising when the introduction of new and changed tasks is accompanied by the redistribution of organizational roles, positions, power, and rewards. It has often been reported in the MIS literature that the dramatic alteration of day-today tasks by changing information systems can trigger or exacerbate organizational problems (for example, see Markus 1984, Zuboff 1982, Dickson and Simmons 1970). New strategies for task assignment and retraining are needed to minimize the individual and organizational difficulties. In order to formulate such strategies, tools are necessary for describing, analyzing, and understanding the impacts of new or changed tasks on the affected workers. It is unfortunately very difficult to develop such tools, because there is a complex network of relationships among information systems, their developers, and users.

This article describes role theory as one possible tool for MIS research and practice. using end-user development (EUD) to illustrate its use. EUD is an especially appropriate application area for role theory because, as this article describes, many current EUD problems can be addressed by role theory. Brief reviews of the literature in EUD and role theory appear in §§2 and 3, respectively. Role theory is then applied to the study of EUD in §§4 and 5, by first defining roles and actors and then focusing on propositions for further investigating EUD. Finally, §6 presents limitations of the role theory perspective and conclusions from this preliminary work.

## 2. End-User Development

The relationships between users, developers, and systems have changed dramatically in recent years. Users now develop their own systems in many cases to avoid MIS department backlogs. This end-user computing (EUC) phenomenon has received much recent attention in the MIS literature, especially in the area of EUD. Head (1985) defined EUC (and, by extension, EUD) as “the direct assumption of system development and data processing tasks by the user of the service for his own direct benefit" (p. 24). Other authors offer similar definitions (e.g., Amoroso 1988. Wetherbe and Leitheiser 1985, Rivard and Huff 1985).

One of the significant characteristics of EUD has been the rapidity of its advancement; over a short period of time, EUD has become a significant effort in many organizations (Brandel 1988, AMA 1988, Rockart and Flannery 1983, Benjamin 1982). It is reasonable to expect such swift change to be accompanied by significant problems. Indeed, much of the EUD literature focuses on significant problems arising from the changing user-developer relationships. One persistent theme in the literature is the confusing environment faced by users (Gerrity and Rockart 1986, Henderson and Treacy 1986, Lederer and Sethi 1986, Brancheau et al. 1985, Davis 1984, Rockart and Flannery 1983, Hammond 1982) and IS personnel (Benjamin et al. 1985). Brancheau et al. found that there was sufficient confusion in five firms to allow novice users to provide support to other users, one symptom of an alarming "trend of relatively inexperienced users taking on major projects" (p. 13). More recent EUD studies point out the need for well-defined policies to promote proper completion of development tasks by appropriate personnel (e.g., Galletta and Hufnagel 1990, Painter 1988, Swider 1988, Alavi et al 1987–88, Harrison and Dick 1987, Munro et al. 1987–88). Of particular concern is the element of system development tasks performed by users inexperienced or untrained in such tasks.

Rivard and Huff (1985) identify explicitly the development tasks of requirements definition, design, programming, and implementation, and note that users are often completely inexperienced with such tasks. Users are more and more frequently developing applications with little assistance from MIS departments or information centers. They are becoming system developers who are virtually self-sufficient. One study (AMA 1988) reports that many user departments have sole responsibility and authority in acquiring mainframe, minicomputer, and microcomputer hardware and software, and in developing applications on those machines. The majority of end-user developers surveyed in another recent study (Sumner and Klepper 1987) report that they are wholly responsible for all phases of systems development including design, construction, and on-going maintenance.

A number of studies (Sumner and Klepper 1987, Rivard and Huff 1985, Benson 1983, Rockart and Flannery 1983, McLean 1979) offer prescriptions for overcoming problems associated with EUD (lack of documentation, data integrity, audit trails, back up, data security, etc.). Other studies suggest ways to integrate the development activities of end-users into the information requirement specification process conducted by IS departments (Ball 1987, Kozar and Mahlum 1987, Pliskin and Shoval 1987. Kraushaar and Shirland 1985, Batiste and Jung 1984).

Most of these studies at least comment on the obvious fact that the EUD phenomenon involves significant changes to the information system roles played by individuals. There has, however, been little attempt to assess the ways in which these role changes affect the individuals or organizations in which they occur. As “users" become "programmers," “analysts," and "designers," what will be the effect on the relationships between users and IS departments? How will relationships between individuals in these departments change? What will happen to the formal organizational structures and to informal coalitions? Will jobs be designed differently, and how will individuals' self-perceptions and perceived self-efficacy change? Investigating these and other questions in light of the changing IS environment must begin with identification of generic information system actors and roles, with special emphasis on EUD.

The sociology literature has addressed complex relationships, as well as accompanying ambiguity and conflict, using role theory in both personal and professional settings. Some MiS researchers have borrowed role theory from the reference discipline of sociology for MIS research in the past (Green 1989, Baroudi and Ginzberg 1986, Baroudi 1985, Similä and Nuutinen 1983). However, such use has concentrated on the traditional development environment. In this article, we will extend the application of role theory to the EUD environment, which in most firms provides ample opportunity for studying complexity, ambiguity, and conflict. The next section provides a brief review of the role theory literature.

## 3. Role Theory

In this section, role theory will be defined, and its foundations in the literature will be discussed. Examples of role theory research will be described, and common role constructs will be explored.

## Definition of Role Theory

Štryker and Statham (1985) describe role theory as the study of “the degree to which individual behavior, social interaction, and the social person are constrainied by social structure," (p. 311) with a focus on each person's role. A role is defined by Biesanz and Biesanz (1978) as a "bundle of norms that defines the rights, obligations, and privileges of a person who occupies a particular status" (p. 145). Roles emerge from the “necessary division of labor" in an organization (Stryker and Statham, p. 332) and from the “expectations of others" (p. 324). A major metaphor for discussing role processes is the theatre. Social interaction is visualized as actors playing assigned parts in a script written by a culture in the course of adaptation to environmental circumstance (Stryker and Statham 1985). The nature of organizations (and other social structures) is such that they can be understood in terms of the interactions and functional interdependencies between individuals and groups.

A central notion in organizational role theory is that people tend to enter interactions with pre-existing expectations about how others categorized in certain ways are likely to behave. Over time these expectations become widely shared, stabilize into predictable patterns, and evolve into a collective structure of behavior. People learn through socialization to hold such expectations of themselves and others as a result of the social positions they hold. These stable, shared patterns of expected behavior which are associated with positions become the basic fabric of organizational roles. The effects on individuals in turn have an impact on the organization as a whole

Pfeffer (1982) asserts that behaviors in role are reinforced and behaviors out of role are punished or ignored. This leads to a particular perspective unique to role theory. Pfeffer (1982) has categorized various theoretical perspectives in organization theory in terms of the level of analysis at which activity occurs and the theory's perspective on action. As Table 1 shows, Pfeffer suggests that role theory analysis occurs at the individual, coalition, or subunit level as opposed to the total organizational level. Table 1 also indicates that, according to role theory, the actions of individuals are significantly constrained or determined by the external environment.

Some studies have given dramatic support for role theory's assertion that the configuration of roles and positions in an organization is an important determinant of behavior, attitudes and performance. In Lieberman's (1956) classic study, plant workers shifted to other positions and back adopted attitudes remarkably consistent with the particular role occupied at the time. In Zimbardo's (Haney et al. 1973) well-known study, carefully screened (normal) subjects randomly assigned as “prisoners" and “guards" provided powerful evidence of the influence of roles on subjects' increasingly undesirable attitudes and behaviors (leading to sudden termination of the project). Milgram's (1965) famous study illustrates how two-thirds of normal adult subjects, told by experimenters to administer electric shocks to pleading victims, willingly obeyed.

While Pfeffer's categorization is useful and fundamentally accurate, it fails to capture fully one important aspect of role theory. Stryker and Statham (1985) have pointed out that the concept of role is especially useful because it is an articulation of that critical point where the larger social structure and the individual interact. It is true that most role theory research has been concerned with individual perceptions and responses, but this may well be an artifact of the research methods used rather than of the theory itself. Role theory differs from other perspectives and is a promising reference area for MIS precisely because it has the potential, if appropriate methodologies can be devised, to transcend the level of analysis problem and shed light on both macro- and micro-level phenomena.

The role theory literature has taken, in some instances, a structural view, and in other instances, a process-oriented view. The former perspective emphasizes the bundle of norms and expectations associated with a position in a certain structure, while the latter emphasizes the processes of communicating these norms and expectations. Although it is probably unlikely that either view can be considered fully without some reference to the other, a primary focus on one or the other is probably useful.

## The Structural Perspective

Structural foundations of role theory can be found in the work of Linton (1936, 1939), Merton (1957), Bates (1956), and Parsons (1951). Bates described a role as “normative and structural in character and not behavioral" (p. 314), observable “only indirectly by observing behavior" (p. 315). Common structural terms are position, status, and office. A position denotes a location in the organizational space of a social structure which is associated with designated rights and obligations. The terms status and office are commonly used as synonyms for position. In contrast, a role is a part of a position consisting of essential recurring actions. Role refers to what social actors are expected to do as opposed to what they are or how they are labeled.

Role relationships can be described in both structural and process terms. From a structural perspective, there exists for each role a paired, reciprocal role in a different position. À role set (Merton 1957) denotes the complement of role relationships in which an individual is involved by virtue of occupying a particular organizational position. This concept recognizes the fact that positions are composed of arrays of roles, each linked to its reciprocal role found in a different position.

Bates (1956) elaborated on this idea when he pointed out that groups are composed of parts of persons. That is, any individual is likely to occupy a number of positions in a number of groups. Bates postulated that “each culture contains a limited fund of roles" that build positions (p. 315). Some of these roles are found more than once in the positions of a social structure. For example, a generic role such as disciplinarian may be found in the positions of father, military leader, and foreman in a factory. Within the context of EUD, it is being demonstrated that traditional IS roles such as programmer, analyst, designer, etc., are being redistributed into a variety of positions, much like Bates' generic roles.

## The Process Perspective

Many theorists have pursued role theory from the process point of view, concentrating on analysis or prediction of role effects. The framework in Figure 1 depicts Katz and Kahn's (1978) process model. The model addresses factors involved in organizational role taking

The model shows that certain actors called role senders communicate role expectations to a focal person, or role receiver. The focal person practices role behaviors which, to à greater or lesser degree, conform to the sent roles. Other major assumptions of this model are that attributes of the person and interpersonal factors affect the communication process. Those attributes and factors are affected by the behaviors of the focal person, and also affect the role sender. Finally, both organizational factors and the behaviors of the focal person affect the role sender's expectations. A more detailed discussion of the model, and the meaning and evidence for these relationships is beyond the scope of this paper, and may be found elsewhere (Katz and Kahn 1978). Of interest here are two outcomes of the role sending-role receiving process which are commonly studied by role theorists: role conflict and role ambiguity.

Role Conflict occurs when a position requires an individual to play a role which conflicts with his or her value system or to play two roles that conflict with each other (Van Sell et al. 1981). Several authors have described the ways in which conflict is generated between roles and positions in a social structure (Katz and Kahn 1978, Kahn et al. 1964, Goode 1960). Kahn et al. have identified several forms of role conflict, including intersender conflict (where demands of one member of the person's role set contradict demands of another), intrasender conflict (where demands of one member of the person's role set are self-contradictory), interrole conflict (where demands of one role, such as employee, contradict demands of another role, such as parent), person-role conflict (where there is moral or ethical conflict), and role overload (where the demands of a single role might be overly burdensome).

Since the role set comprises a number of people holding different positions, there is always a potential for differing and conflicting expectations among those in the role set. Since an individual typically takes part in many different role relationships, he or she is thus likely to face a wide, distracting, and sometimes conflicting array of role obligations. Therefore, these difficulties are very common.

Role ambiguity has not been as thoroughly conceptualized as role conflict in the literature. Generally, role ambiguity exists when clear information is lacking regarding (1) the expectations associated with the role, (2) methods for fulfilling known role expectations, and/or (3) the consequences of role performance (Van Sell et al. 1981).

Kahn et al. (1964) note that both role conflict and role ambiguity can be thought of in terms of inadequate role sending. In the case of role conflict, lack of agreement or coordination among role senders produces a pattern of sent expectations which are incompatible or overwhelming to the focal person. In the case of role ambiguity, the pattern of sent expectations does not contain sufficient information. Thus, as Van Sell et al. (1981) point out, even though role conflict and role ambiguity are conceptually distinguishable, one should not expect their empirical indices to be necessarily unrelated.

Role relationships can also be described in process terms. Role-bargain refers to the transactional nature of role relationships. Interactions between paired roles are thought of in quasi-economic terms, with role occupants attempting to maximize their rights and minimize their obligations, or establish the most favorable role “price" possible (Goode 1960). The norms and values associated with a group's role structure are diversified, interconnected in complex ways, and largely internalized through the process of socialization, limiting the ability of individuals to alter their role bargain significantly (Goode 1960).

In summary, these forms of conflict and ambiguity arise in a differentiated social structure as individuals occupying positions represent their interests by trying to improve their role bargains. They attempt to find ways to reduce the expectations of those in their role set, while at the same time increasing the demands on their role partners.

Role conflict and ambiguity have been shown to be related to a number of other variables. For example, Miles (1977) and Linton (1939) report that role conflict is increased (1) when relative authority of individuals in the focal person's role set is greater than that of the focal person; (2) when organizational distance (number of formal boundaries) is great between the individuals in the focal person's role set and the focal person, (3) when functional dependence of the focal individual upon those in his or her role set is high, (4) when the focal person's performance is highly visible to the individuals in the role set, and (5) when the existence of conflicting demands is not obvious or visible to those in the role set, making conflict resolution the responsibility of the focal person.

A number of studies have documented the relationships between role conflict, role ambiguity and dependent variables such as job dissatisfaction, job-related tension, anxiety, productivity, and turnover (see Van Sell et al. 1981, for a review of this literature). Other studies have demonstrated empirically the effects of role configuration on conflict (Miles 1977, Rizzo et al. 1970) and supervisory behavior (Pfeffer and Salancik 1975).

## Summary of Role Theory Principles

This brief sketch of the fundamental principles of role theory suggests that these ideas may help us better understand the organizational effects of changing information systems roles. The situations that arise when users assume the roles of programmers and system developers can be described and analyzed using structural and process role principles. Applicable structural principles are that roles can be combined in different ways to form different positions, and can be variously located in organizational space. Process principles would attribute difficulties to the arrangements found, and would provide a framework for avoiding such difficulties.

In the next section, end-user actors and roles are discussed. A framework for categorizing information system roles is presented in order to provide a foundation for analysis of factors affecting role difficulties in the context of information systems.

## 4. EUD Actors and Roles

If, as Bates asserts, there are a limited number of generic roles which can be combined in different ways to form positions, how might EUD roles be defined? While role theory may be applied to any area of information systems, the EUC/EUD movement gives us a unique opportunity to study IS roles. The application of role theory is particularly relevant in this area because the EUC/EUD movement calls into question the traditional roles of information systems “specialist" and “user."

Several authors have provided categorization schemes for end-user developers. Two appear to be based at least in part on education or experience of the actors. McLean (1979) divided end-user developers into DP professionals, DP amateurs, and non-DP-trained users. Rockart and Flannery's (1983) oft-cited categories include nonprogramming end users, command level programmers, end-user programmers, functional support personnel, EUC support personnel, and DP programmers. In addition to the background dimension, Rockart and Flannery's categories also include the target of system development (for self or others).

Two schemes appear to take a behavioral point of view to some extent. Rivard and Huff (1985) proposed viewing EUD actors as micro-DP department users, staff analysts, and opportunity seekers. Defining roles using such a scheme is difficult, because the categories are not mutually exclusive or exhaustive. Davis (1985a) proposed viewing users in three categories: direct, autonomous, and indirect users. This scheme presents a useful basis on which to build a role-oriented framework, but the dimension of development target is missing; only users are represented in the list.

The following framework is proposed as a means of classifying information system roles in a way that can be used for role behavior analysis. It combines the behavioral dimensions found in the schemes above, and also takes into consideration the functional purpose of an actor's organizational behavior. Table 2 is a matrix that classifies IS roles in terms of two activity-oriented dimensions. The column headings describe patterns of activities which relate to the construction and use of the information system itself. The column headings are arranged along a using/providing dimension. The centermost columns represent more intense contact with the information system. The row headings describe types of activities associated with generic organizational levels.

Each cell in the matrix can be thought of as a cluster of expectations or norms which define a generic role relative to both the organization and its information systems. The use of the organizational level dimension recognizes the fact that norms often cluster “horizontally" in an organization, creating subcultures that are bounded more by organizational level than by departmental or occupational affiliation (Davis 1985b, Louis 1985). Previous work has also pointed out implications of organizational level on information needs (Anthony 1965, Gorry and Scott-Morton 1971) and task structure (Simon 1960).

Synthesis of functional and organizational level dimensions has appeared in the organizational literature (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967) as well as in the MIS literature (Davis and Olson 1985). Such literature depicts the effects of both dimensions on roles of people or systems. This implies that the concept of “user" is an oversimplification without further specification of organizational level or function. The matrix represents a cross-classification scheme that extends the synthesis of the two generic dimensions to the EUD domain.

Throughout this paper, the terms "position," "role," "sub-role," and "task" are used as follows. A position indicates a formal organizational appointment. A role indicates a cell of the EUD role matrix. Sub-roles indicate the more specific bundles of norms (expectations). Finally, tasks indicate processes that are actually performed.

The five categories on the Information System activity dimension are defined as follows:

Indirect User: Uses output from the information system, but does not personally interact with it. When playing this role, an actor uses IS outputs which have been filtered by others who are likely to be direct users.

Direct User: Has hands-on direct interaction with information system inputs and/ or outputs. This category covers a broad range of activities which could include batch data entry, use of standard output reports, or interactive modeling for decision support.

Autonomous Developer: Similar to Davis' Autonomous User category. Develops applications for his/her own use, typically making use of fourth generation languages or spreadsheets, but may use more traditional tools such as COBOL, APL, etc. Autonomous developers typically perform all system construction tasks such as planning, analysis, design, programming, testing, etc.

Traditional Developer: Performs the same system construction tasks as the Autonomous Developer, but the application is for the use of others. Most traditional IS activities would fall within this classification, as would McLean's DP professional, Rivard and Huff's Micro-DP department users, and many activities performed by Rockart and Flannery's Functional Support Personnel, EUC Support Personnel and DP Programmers.

Facilitator: Performs tasks specifically intended to communicate information about the IS to those playing other roles. These activities may include conveying or eliciting information requirements, system training, or documenting. Many of the tasks performed by information center personnel, "chauffeurs," and functional support staff (as defined by Rockart and Flannery) would fall into this category.

The three categories on the Organizational Activity dimension are defined as follows:

Clerical: When playing this role, an actor is involved in the manipulation of data as an end in itself. The organizational purpose of the manipulation is outside the domain of the clerical role.

Professional: This actor uses information in the performance of functional (nonmanagerial) activities, such as analysis, planning, or research.

Managerial: A manager directs the work of others and/or is responsible for allocating organizational resources.

We should note once again that it is possible, and indeed likely, that a number of these roles may be combined into one organizational position. For example, it is not unusual for a middle manager to sometimes work in what has been here labeled the professional role and at other times in the managerial role. Role theory asserts that, however roles are combined into positions, one role is at any given moment active, the others temporarily remaining latent (Bates 1956).

As user-managers and user-professionals begin to perform development tasks, situations may arise in which some important sub-roles are neglected. For example, it is possible that no person in any position will formally accept a role which includes responsibility for data integrity or security. Only by explicitly defining the needed sub-roles will such omissions be identified. The EUD role matrix might serve as a tool for such definition.

## Summary

The past decade has led to development and usage distinctions that are no longer clear or formal. Besides developer-only and indirect and direct user-only roles, new roles of autonomous developer and facilitator have emerged. These roles might differ for managerial, professional, and clerical levels. The resultant matrix might provide a better taxonomy for scrutinizing the new EUD-related roles of today's organizations.

An important contribution of role theory is to define the current "division of labor" (Stryker and Statham 1985, p. 332) in an organizational setting to discover “expectations of others" (p. 324) resulting from this division. The matrix of EUD roles might be used as a basis for such definition in an EUD context. The following section presents a discussion of how role theory can facilitate understanding of the changing environment brought about by EUD.

## 5. EUD Understood Through Role Theory

Role theory can be applied to studies of EUD using both structural and processoriented approaches. The propositions presented in this section can be used to suggest hypotheses or sets of hypotheses for empirical studies. The distinction between structure and process approaches is similar to the distinction made by Markus and Robey (1988) between variance theory and process theory. Such theory may be used to develop and test specific hypotheses based on the propositions.

## The Structural Perspective

Bates (1956) shows how family roles such as husband can be legitimately subdivided into finer, discrete sub-roles such as friend, confidant, sexual partner, etc. Likewise, the EUD actors' roles can be subdivided into sub-roles. Such a decomposition is shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2 depicts one possible dyad in the more traditional IS setting: an indirect user-manager and a traditional developer-professional. An indirect user-manager's sub-roles might include that of planner, superordinate, resource allocator, negotiator, and information user. The sub-roles of a traditional developer-professional, on the other hand, might include that of documenter, tester, information analyst, auditor, coder, and designer. Further, the user's sub-role of information user could be linked to the developer's reciprocal sub-role of information analyst.

A role theorist would claim that, to understand more fully the behavior of these actors, their roles and sub-roles must be known (e.g., see Stryker and Statham 1985, Pfeffer 1982, Katz and Kahn 1978, Bates 1956). Such an approach has already begun in the MIS literature (Green 1989, Baroudi and Ginzberg 1986, Baroudi 1985, Similä and Nuutinen 1983).

The roles have changed dramatically with the advent of EUD. For example, Figure 3 illustrates one possible set of sub-roles of an autonomous developer-manager. This role-set could result from an indirect or direct user's adoption of many of the sub-roles of the traditional developer-professional. The literature describes several instances where users have adopted sub-roles for which they have little or no experience or skill. This has been shown to trigger many problems discussed earlier, such as inadequacies in developer training (Brancheau et al. 1985) and in system controls (Davis 1984). Therefore, user roles appear to be expanding to some extent, and researchers as well as practitioners might gain a great deal of understanding from spelling out these roles. In like fashion, modified roles of the other actors in the matrix can be explicated.

PRoPOsITION 2. The roles and sub-roles in the EUD setting are being combined in such a way as to form positions that contain more sub-roles than those in the traditional setting

Differences in attitudes and expectations between EUD actors in the five categories might be largely attributed to their offices, as has been shown in non-MIS studies discussed earlier (Haney et al. 1973, Milgram 1965, Lieberman 1956) as well as in MIS studies (Green 1989, Robey and Markus 1984). For example, an indirect user who is indignant about the length of time required for system development might begin to develop his or her own systems. As an autonomous developer, specific experiences could change this user's expectations regarding the system development process. If the user's environment includes only third generation programming languages, then he or she might develop more appreciation for the developer's task. On the other hand, if the user's environment includes facilities for modeling (e.g., using a spreadsheet), then he or she might develop even higher expectations of developers. Therefore, the elements of an actor's position must be examined in some detail to predict attitudes, expectations, and/or behaviors.

PROPOsITiON 3. An actor's position in the EUD role matrix can determine to a large extent attitudes and/or behaviors of that actor.

For example, a user who moves to the user/developer position or to one of the developer-only positions in the matrix can undergo changes in attitudes, expectations, and behavior. Using such a proposition, one might make the following sample predictions:

PRoposrTioN 3a. The user's attitudes, expectations, and behaviors will more closely resemble that of developers if their roles are truly similar, resulting in expectations for behavior similar to that of developers.

ProposITiON 3b. The user's attitudes, expectations, and behaviors will diverge from that of developers if their roles are not truly similar, but the user perceives that they are. Dissimilar roles would imply different sets of expectations for behavior on the part of others.

An important contribution of role theory to EUD research would be to enable description, analysis, and prediction and control of system impacts. Since these are fundamentally process issues, propositions derived from a process perspective of role theory are relevant. Some considerations are based primarily on changes in roles of a single EUC actor and can be described with little reference to other actors (except as the source for the changed role expectations). Therefore, this first group might be called nonassociative in nature.

## The Nonassociative, Process Perspective

Several nonassociative propositions might be explored. For example, when an autonomous developer serves as his or her own analyst as well as information user, sheer overload of all sub-roles assumed might result in role conflict. In the traditional MIS environment, the development task has been shown to lead to significant role conflict (e.g., Baroudi 1985, Goldstein and Rockart 1984, Bostrom 1980). In the EUD setting, Davis (1984) provides a description of the number and difficulty of EUD tasks, and warns of problems this might cause.

PRoposrTioN 4. The magnitude and difficulty of EUD tasks will result in role conflict in the form of role overload.

In addition, role ambiguity might also be expected, since evidence of dysfunctional role ambiguity has already been reported in the general MIS literature (e.g., Baroudi 1985. Goldstein and Rockart 1984. Bostrom 1980). Since there is not a long history of autonomous development, it is difficult to predict which development tasks will be taken up by the autonomous developer. Studies by Rivard and Huff (1985) and Sumner and Klepper (1987) provide some preliminary indication of such ambiguity. Rivard and Huff found some end-user developers with titles of “programmer"or “analyst," while Sumner and Klepper found no evidence of end-user developers with such titles. At the same time, the end-user developers in both studies reported that they did not think of themselves as doing "data processing" jobs, even though in some cases they spent as much as 80% of their time programming. Such confusion promises at least some degree of role ambiguity associated with EUD.

ProposıτioN 5. Lack of formality in assigning EUD tasks will result in role ambiguity, due to the absence of official organizational sanction (such as titles, job descriptions, hiring, or training) for performing these duties.

## Associative, Process Considerations

Some process considerations focus primarily on relationships among actors, and are therefore labeled associative. Using Figure 2 as an example, relationships between decision makers (indirect user-managers) and analysts (traditional developer-professionals) can be described more explicitly. That is, expectations on the part of the indirect user-manager might include more than the traditional developer-professional can offer: The user might expect computer literacy training, for example, and the analyst might be unwilling or incapable of providing it. The importance of comparing sent and received roles was reviewed in detail by Katz and Kahn (1978). Indeed, Green's (1989) research underscores the presence of and difficulties inherent in mismatches in these points of view.

PRopOsITIoN 6. Mismatches between sent and received roles of EUD actors can be described specifically. Higher levels of mismatches lead to higher levels of role conflict and ambiguity

This greater detail in description will enable analysis for greater understanding and explanation of behavior. Continuing with our example, the difficulties created by the user's need for basic training might manifest themselves through interpersonal friction as well as through technical errors. Knowledge of the role expectations and needs on the part of the user would assist in revising the roles of the analyst, or in creating positions that contained needed roles (in accordance with Griffin et al. 1985 and Pfeffer 1982). Therefore, one might sketch the restructured roles and reciprocal role expectations of each position and make sure that all needs are satisfied.

PRoPosiTiON 7. EUD roles can be restrucured into redefined positions. This reallocation of responsibilities among the positions will ascertain that important roles are formally recognized and assigned.

Reciprocal role holders should be named explicitly to avoid role relationship diffculties. Implementing the role revisions might result in fewer actual role-sending and receiving mismatches, and fewer symptoms of role conflict and ambiguity. This is consistent with Pfeffer's (1982) assessment that, according to role theory, restructuring of jobs and duties can alleviate role stress and role pressures.

PRoPOsiTiON 8. Restructuring EUD roles into redefined positions will affect the level of mismatches between sent and received roles. Reduction in the level of mismatches will result in reduced levels of role conflict and ambiguity.

Another area of concern involves power and control between actors (DaBrabander and Thiers 1984, Markus 1983, Robey and Farrow 1982, Saunders 1981). Robey and Markus (1984) have asserted that “MIS scholars have drawn sparingly from these developments" (p. 5). An extreme view is that of Dearden (1987), who predicts that users will soon attempt to gain control over system development activities and will derive net benefits from doing so. In the upcoming power struggle, the relationships between role-bargaining positions and control could become important and interesting research issues.

Therefore, other associative process considerations would address the role-bargaining propositions of Miles (1977) and Linton (1939) addressed above, in the areas of relative power and influence, functional dependencies, performance visibility, and the role-set's awareness of conflicting demands. Some of these factors strengthen the user's role-bargaining position:

PRoPOsITiON 9. The user's role-bargaining position will strengthen as EUD activity increases.

This proposition can be decomposed into several more specific propositions for developing and testing hypotheses. For example, relative power and influence and functional dependencies are two significant constructs that are associated with strengthened role-bargaining positions. As EUD activity increases, users will no longer depend completely on IS department actors for system development and maintenance. Other kinds of IS support will still be needed, but the most visible activities will diminish. More specific propositions follow:

PRoposITiON 9a. Increases in EUD activity will reduce the relative power and influence of the IS department actors.

PROPOsITION 9b. Increases in EUD activity will reduce the functional dependence of users on IS department developers.

Similarly, from the point of view of the IS department, EUD weakens the IS department's role-bargaining position:

PRoposiTioN 10. The IS department's role-bargaining position will weaken as EUD activity increases.

This proposition might appear at first glance to be the same as Proposition 9; however, the IS point of view is different in many important ways from that of the users. To clarify this, the constructs of performance visibility and awareness of conflicting demands (Miles 1977, Linton 1939) are useful.

PRoposITIoN 10a. Increases in EUD activity will increase the users' ability to observe, understand, and evaluate the IS department's role performance.

PRoposITioN 10b. Increases in EUD activity will reduce the IS department's ability to take advantage of observable, conflicting user demands.

DaBrabander and Thiers'(1984) study presents an example of how propositions such as 9 and 10 might be subjected to empirical examination. In their laboratory study, constructs such as power asymmetry and implementation behavior are operationalized and studied. Extension of their work can be instrumental in addressing Propositions 9 and 10.

## Discussion and Summary of Propositions

Role theory might assist in providing greater power for researchers and practitioners to describe, analyze, and understand the impacts of technology in a complex and ambiguous environment. The EUD setting provides such an environment to illustrate some potential contributions of role theory; tentative propositions outline research questions and hypotheses that might assist in solving some of the problems encountered by EUD actors. The propositions might be summarized as follows.

Some propositions provide greater understanding of structural issues. Proposition 1 asserts that it is possible to outline roles of each actor to provide greater understanding of behavior. Proposition 2 contends that once this is done, it will be discovered that EU D has enlarged the role-sets of many actors. Extending this analysis, Proposition 3 argues that behavior of the actors can be to some extent explained by the definition and contents of actors' roles. At this level, mismatched expectations reported in the literature (Green 1989, Robey and Markus 1984) can be explained more fully.

Further propositions focus on nonassociative process considerations that can be investigated following the definitions afforded in the first three propositions. It has been reported that users suffer from role overload (Davis 1984) and role ambiguity (Sumner and Klepper 1987, Rivard and Huff 1985), and Propositions 4 and 5 address these concerns.

The balance of the propositions concern associative process considerations that can be addressed by understanding structural and nonassociative process issues. Proposition 6 asserts that mismatches between sent and received roles can be described in terms of role conflict. Proposition 7 states that analysis will enable restructuring of roles that are in conflict, while Proposition 8 provides a prediction that such restructuring will, in fact, reduce the mismatches found in the previous step. Propositions 9 and 10 contend that the relationships between users and developers are shifting, in that users' positions are strengthening while developers' positions are weakening.

## 6. Limitations and Conclusions

## Limitations of Taking a Role Theory Perspective

Notions such as role, abstractions created by researchers and theorists, only have value according to their analytical usefulness. At this early stage of theory building in IS, the broad, generic role categories presented here are believed to be sufficient for hypothesis generation and testing. However, some questions will not be addressed by such study. The most serious limitations relate to Pfeffer's (1982) perspectives shown in Table 1: the extent of external constraint and the level of analysis.

The deterministic nature of role theory could raise objections by some researchers. However, any assumptions of external control should not be taken to mean that there is absence of individual choice and freedom. That is, role theory can provide some measure of explanation of behavior, but does not claim to explain all behavior. In the words of Thomas and Biddle (1966), the role theory perspective is a “"limited, social determinism that ascribes much, but rarely all, of the variance of real-life behavior to the operation of immediate or past external influences" (p. 17). Katz and Kahn state that “we become what we do" (1978, p. 197), and further state that “the person has an occupational self-identity and is motivated to behave in ways that affirm and enhance its valued attributes." This often extends to acts that people “do not understand and that may violate many of their own values," a phenomenon that is “frequently underestimated" (p. 194).

The literature therefore shows that actors do tend to think and behave in ways predicted by their received roles. Although this might be seen as reducing the “dignity" of the actors, the effects have been nevertheless observed to be quite dramatic in many cases. Such effects have also been seen in the MIS literature (Green 1989, Baroudi 1985).

The second issue concerns the level of analysis addressed, which must be chosen with care (Markus and Robey 1988). The individual or subunit level of analysis is appropriate for studying some IS or EUD problems, but it might be seen by some researchers as too narrow, missing important factors at higher levels of abstraction (like planning, budgeting, etc.). While all of the propositions presented in this paper address the individual or subunit level of analysis, we have argued earlier that role theory has the potential to transcend the level of analysis problem and shed light on both macro- and micro-level phenomena.

It must be acknowledged that it is not a simple matter to integrate theories which operate at different levels of analysis. Different levels of analysis or different perspectives on action may result in incompatibilities which make integration impossible. Stryker and Statham (1985), however, have argued compellingly that the construct of "role" is powerful enough to be the basis of an integrated framework which "facilitates movement from the level of social structure to the level of the person, and vice versa, as well as explanatory principles articulating the two levels that reflect the inherent complexity of both" (p. 311).

In sum, it is important to consider that role theory might be promising for making predictions of individual or subgroup behavior. More knowledge about behavioral effects should better inform our management decisions and thereby bring about improvements in organizational performance. Results of role theory-based research might provide a richer understanding of the impacts of end-user development.

## Summary

The MÍS literature has for some time foreshadowed the role issues raised by EUD. Dearden (1972) claimed that it would be easier to teach technology to functional personnel than to teach functional specialties to information technologists, while Whisler (1970) suggested the creation of a “think tank" of technologists to serve organizations. In the 1970s, information technology was so little understood in the functional areas of organizations that there seemed no alternative to this structural arrangement, but recent EUD efforts are calling into question the centralized IS structures of the 1970s and 1980s. This has been reaffirmed by more recent work by Dearden (1987).

The recent sudden and dramatic advances in computer technology have altered significantly the roles of both users and developers. A framework for categorizing information systems roles is built from a matrix of information system and organizational activities. The information system activity dimension is composed of indirect user, direct user, autonomous developer, traditional developer, and facilitator categories. The organizational activity dimension contains clerical, professional, and managerial categories.

The resulting matrix can facilitate research in EUD. Descriptive research can identify how roles are being combined and identify roles that do not presently exist. Propositions based on the matrix can be refined by identifying important variables and hypothesizing their relationships. Such hypotheses might be tested to extend our knowledge in this area.

Organizational role theory offers a focus and theoretical grounding so often called for in self-examinations of the IS discipline. It is relevant to the investigation of a number of organizational, behavioral and social issues important to the IS researcher, and can be the basis of a continuing research program which contributes to the building of a cumulative tradition. The IS practitioner will find that role theory can offer guidelines for more effective management of the human resources involved in construction and use of information systems. Practitioners and researchers alike may find that application of this theory offers insight into troublesome questions about the appropriate structure and role of IS departments within organizations. he resulting improved understanding of current conflicts and difficulties can be valuable for both researchers and practitioners in the field.\*
