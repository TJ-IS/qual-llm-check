Information security has not been a high priority for most managers. Many permit their installations to be either lightly protected or wholly unprotected, apparently willing to risk major losses from computer abuse. This study, based on the criminological theory of general deterrence, investigates whether a management decision to invest in IS security results in more effective control of computer abuse. Data gathered through a survey of 1,211 randomly selected organizations indicates that secur ty countermeasures that include deterrent administrative procedures and preventive security software will result in significantly lower computer abuse. Knowledge about these relationships is useful for making key decisions about the security function.

## 1. Introduction

ver the last several decades, managers have become aware that information and information systems are critical organizational resources. It is reasonable to expect, therefore, that they would also consider the security of information to be a crucial activity. Curiously, this is not the case. Managers, in fact, seem to have a low regard for security. 'Through 1986, for instance only 60 percent of all organizations had initiated information security as a functional area (Hoffer and Straub 1989), and nearly half of those dedicated less than nine hours a week to the function (Straub and Hoffer 1987). While the security function is well positioned in the information systems (IS) department in most organizations (Straub 1988), security officers are typically assigned to loweı-level managers rather than to senior IS managers. Moreover, opinion surveys since the early 1980's indicae that managers do not even rank security among their top 10 critical issues (Ball and Harris 1982, Dickson et al. 1984, Brancheau and Wetherbe 1987, Niederman et al. 1990).' Clearly, security is not seen as ımportant by many managers, and this attitude may be leading to low levels of protection,

What explains this low regard for security and correspondingly low level of investment? One explanation is that management has made a conscious decision not to invest in security. Goodhue and Straub (1988) theorize that managerial concern about security is a function of several factors, such as risk of abuse inherent in the industry. That is, when risk is low, concern should be low, and when risk is high, concern should be high, ceteris paribus. If a manager correctly perceives risk to be low, he or she would therefore be well advised not to overinvest in security.

Another explanation is that managers simply have differing opinions about the real value of security (Goodhue and Straub 1988)—opinions which in some cases may be misinformed. Managers have various perceptions, for example, about whether an investment in IS security actually deters and prevents losses from computer abuse. A mistaken perception in this case, however, could have dire consequences. Less than adequate security could expose the organization to large-scale losses.

Thus, the consequences of underfunding or not funding security when a reasonable investment would have prevented a major loss are serious. In short, it is important for managers to know whether security activities actually do result in significantly lower computer abuse. Additionally, if security proves to be a value-added investment, they need to know how it should be managed. This study attempts to address these questions by examining the link between losses from intentional system misuse and security countermeasures frequently used to deter and prevent such losses. Research constructs are drawn from the criminological theory of general deterrence.

Data gathered through a survey of 1,211 randomly selected organizations indicate that investment in IS security can significantly reduce computer abuse through countermeasures that include both deterrents and preventive software. They also show that data security activities (i.e., electronic as opposed to physical security measures) are integral for decreasing abuse. These study findings suggest that those organizations currently without an IS security function (up to 40 percent of IS Departments (Hoffer and Straub 1989)) may want to reconsider their security posture and alter priorities to allocate resources to this area. Finally, the study shows where security resources may best be placed for maximum effectiveness.

## 2. Prior Research

Prior studies include surveys of the victims of computer abuse (Ernst and Whinney 1988, ABA 1984, AICPA 1984, Kusserow 1983, Wong 1985), archival data gathered from media and police reports of abuse (Parker 1976, 1981), and surveys of prosecutors' offices (BloomBecker 1986). None of these studies, though, has investigated causal relationships between the activities of security administrators and computer abuse. They present valuable descriptive data, such as frequency distributions of computer abuse by dollar loss, offender motivation, and victim industry, but they do not test relationships among key variables.

A number of conceptual studies, however, have reflected on the ability of security countermeasures to reduce the risk of computer abuse (Madnick 1978, Martin 1973). Parker (1981, 1983), for example, advocates the value of deterrents, such as guidelines and policy statements, in lowering abuse by white-collar amateurs. Deterrents clarify what constitutes legitimate use of the information system and discourage weakly motivated potential offenders (Dunn 1982). The absence of deterrents, however, leads to a misunderstanding of acceptable system use (Klete 1975) and hence to system abuse.

Among the alternauve or rival explanations for low levels of computer abuse are countermeasures known as preventives. Classes of preventives include physical security of facilities as well as security software (Hsaio et al. 1979). A well-known form of security software, for example, is password protection.

Additionally, rival explanations include certain motivational factors that encourage potential abusers to commit computer abuse. One of these factors is occupational role or degree of system privilege. The relative mpunity of highly privileged system users, such as system programmers, EDP auditors, security officers. and top executives, may encourage them to engage in large-scale computer abuse (Parker 1981). The literature also suggests that strongly motivated abusers will commit more damage than weakly motivated abusers (Chambliss 1967). and that employees, because of their knowledge of system weaknesses, will cause more damage than nonemployees (L.ce et al. 1986). Another factor that is believed to increase damage from computer abuse is perpetrator collusion. Parker (1976, 181) proposes that larger losses occur when offenders reinforce each other's actions

The final set of rival explanations are envıronnental factors such as the tightness of the security environment and visibility of security administrators, which may correlate negatively with computer abuse (Parker 1931). Tightly controlled environments with highly visible security staff are thought to result in less frequent and less severe computer abuse.

## 3. Study Background

To address issues raised in the literature, two primary research questions were posed for this study:

Q(1). Have IS security deterrents been effective in lowering computer abuse?

Q(2). Can rival explanations, including use of preventive security software, explain lower incidence of computer abuse?

Because of the varying ways the term “computer abuse" has been used and the need for researchers to limit the scope of their investigations (Taber 1980), the term was restricted to abuse perpetrated by individuals (or groups of individuals) against organizations (Kling 1980). ## 4. Theoretical Base: General Deterrence T'heory

The foundation for this study is the criminological theory of general deterrence. The long tradition of research in this area has well-established research constructs, well-specified causal relationships, and high con'sensus among experts on the explanatory power of the theory (Blumsteın et al. 1978, Cook 1982). A corollary economic theory supporting general deterrence theory was proposed by Ehrlich (1973) and verified by Vandaele (1978). Recent work in the area has further supported this theory.²

Deterrence theory focuses on “disincentives" or sanctions against committing a deviant act and the effect of these sanctions on deterring others from committing criminal acts (Blumstein et al. 1978). In the literature, disincentives are represented by two subconstructs: (1) certainty of sanction and (2) severity of sanction (Blumstein et al. 1978). When the risk of punishment is high (deterrent certainty) and penalties for violation are severe (deterrent severity), the theory predicts that potential offenders will be inhibited from committing antisocial acts.³

The theory assumes that potential violators are made aware of efforts to control anti-social behaviors. In short, it is assumed that people respond to “policing" and the punishment that is associated with effective policing. In IS, the comparable “policing" activity occurs when, for instance, security officers use deterrents to monitor and enforce policy and distribute information about organizational guidelines for acceptable system usage. Enforcing severe penalties for serious security violations is thought to dissuade potential offenders, especially less motivated potential offenders, from illicit behaviors (Straub and Widom 1984).4

## 4.1 Modelling Deterrents and Rival Explanations

The Security Impact Model (Figure 1) presents the theoretical relationship between deterrents and computer abuse. The model shows deterrents as a “summative unit" of behavioral “properties" (Dubin 1978, pp. 66–67) such as IS security efforts. As in the traditional deterrence research, such behavioral properties of deterrents are surrogates for the way security deters potential abusers. In short, deterrent security activities represent how potential abusers perceive risk (Blumstein et al. 1978). Likewise, the computer abuse construct is a representation for the summed observed and measurable losses from security breaches.

The relationship between rival explanations and computer abuse is also modelled. Use of security software, for example, is predicted to be negatively related to losses from computer abuse. ## 5. Instrument Validation

The victimization questionnaire was selectd to examine the linkages between deterrents, rival explanations, and computer abuse (see Straub 1989, for validated instrument).⁵ (ross-sectional surveys have long served to evaluate causal relationships in criminologv (Greenberg and Kessler 1982); therefore, a victimization questionnaire modified foi the computer security environment was felt to be appropriate for this study.

Questionnaire measures were based on general deterrence theory, victimization surveys in criminology, and previous computer abuse surveys. Measures were devised and tested in three phases over two years. The questionnaire was tested for content validity, construct validity, and reliability (Straub 1989)

To make certain that the questionnaire content was representative (Cronbach 1971), the first phase entailed pretesting a draft instrument by means of personal interviews. Participants included academic experts in research methodology, criminologists, IS practitioners and auditors, and staie and local law enforcement officers. The 37 participants, drawn from public and private sector organizations that include banking, insurance, manufacturing, trade, utilities, transportation, education, and medical services, made an expert assessment of the content of the draft instrument (Cronbach 1971). Participants held diverse organizational positions, and included

CEO's, IS managers, operations supervisors, information security officers, database administrators, and internal auditors.º

To assess construct validity and reliability, the second phase employed both interviews and questionnaires. Interviews were conducted in 44 organizations with security officers, IS managers, and internal auditors. In each organization, a parallel questionnaire seeking identical information was administered to a different, but equally knowledgeable informant (primarily IS managers and internal auditors). Responses from these maximally dissimilar data gathering methods were correlated using Campbell and Fiske's (1959) multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) analysis. Analysis of the MTMM matrix shows generally acceptable construct validity, i.e., low method variance and high convergent/discriminant validity. The instrument also included equivalent, maximally similar measures (Campbell 1960) to gauge the extent of the random error (reliability). The instrument demonstrated high internal consistency with Cronbach alphas of 0.80 or more (Nunnally 1967).

In the third phase, additional tests of validity and reliability were carried out through a pilot survey of 1,000 randomly selected DPMA (Data Processing Management Association) members. One hundred and seventy questionnaires were returned. To assess construct validity, principal components factor analysis was used (Allen and Yen 1979). Key measures of the deterrents construct (number of full-time security staff, part-time staff, total staff hours per week, and staff salaries) loaded heavily on a single factor. After four extractions with eigenvalues of at least 1.0 (Nunnally 1967), the factor structure accounted for 97 percent of the variance in the data set. Calculation of Cronbach alphas for the deterrent measures revealed values of 0.94, 0.93, 0.98, and 0.94, which were more than acceptable by the 0.80 standard (Nunnally 1967).

The three phases of instrument validation demonstrate that the research instrument had reasonably good content validity, construct validity, and reliable measures.

## 6.1 Computer Abuse Construct

Computer abuse was measured by three quantitative items: total number of incidents (item 25); theft, replacement, recovery, and legal costs (item 39); and opportunity losses or revenues lost due to the unavailability of system resources (item 38). A fourth subjective measure was a seriousness index of the breach (item 37). This measure is especially useful whenever actual and opportunity losses are trivial but the victim believes intangible losses, such as the cr dibility of system security, are significant (ABA 1984, Straub 1986).

## 6.2–Deterrents Constructs

Deterrent certainty was measured by seven items, including number of full-time security staff (item 10), part-time staff (item 11), total security staff hours per week (item 12), data security hours per week (item 14b), and salaries of security staff (item 15). Deterrent certainty also uses a measure of the success of the security effort, derived from the elapsed months between the inception of security efforts and the occurrence of the abuse (item 13 less item 35 or item 13 less item 28 less item 36). In essence, this derived value measures the age of the security operation at the time the abuse first occurs. The more mature the security function is, the more effective it is presumed to be. Contrasting with hours of IS security devoted to physical security (item 14a) is data security hours per week (item 14b). Data security is likely to be crucial because most significant violations are electronic rather than physical in nature. A subjective estimate of the effectiveness of the local security group to deter abuse (item 22) was included as an alternative method of measuring the construct (Cook and Campbell 1979).

Deterrent severity was measured by three items, including the severity of the disciplinary actions taken against computer abusers (item 19) and number of informational sources about penalties and acceptable system use (item 18). Both measures are traditionally used for deterrent severity in criminological research (Cook 1982). As with deterrent certainty, a subjective measure of the deterrent effect was used to enhance the operationalization (item 22).

## 6.3 Preventives Construct

Preventives were measured by two items: number of operating system (OS) and database (DBMS) security programs in use (item 16) and number of specialized security programs in use (item 17). When security software is used, fewer abuses are expected because it both prevents abuse outright and deters other potential abuse by raising the level of difficulty.

## 6.4 Motivational and Environmental Factors Constructs

These factors were assessed by five items measuring motivational factors and two items measuring environmental factors. The first four motivational factors were offender system privilege (item 30), offender motivation (item 32), amount of collusion (item 29), and offender employment status (item 31). It is widely believed that system risk is a function of the number of computer users assigned high system privileges to read and alter data and programs (Parker 1981). On the questionnaire, offender position (item 30) gauges the privileged status of those who have abused systems. “Offender motivation" discriminates between presumed strength of motivation, ranging from weak incentives, such as ignorance of proper professional conduct, to strong incentives, such as maliciousness or revenge. Highly motivated abusers like saboteurs are believed to be unresponsive to deterrents in situations in which firsttime embezzlers would be deterred (Straub and Widom 1984). As with item 29, “amount of collusion," the proclivity to abuse should be stronger among colluders than among individuals working alone (Parker 1976, 1981). The most highly motivated perpetrators of abuse have been found to be those with the greatest knowledge of the victim's vulnerabilities. Item 31 gives a measure of this motivation in asking the employee/non-employee status of the offender.

The final motivational factor is duration of abuse (item 36 or item 28 less item 35). Duration of abuse is important because strength of motivation may be related to the length of time an offender or offenders have been successfully engaged in computer abuse (Parker 1981). Therefore, the longer an abuse remains undiscovered, the more offenders become psychologically committed to the outcome and the less likely that deterrents will have an effect.

## 7.2 Tests for Nonresponse Bias

Tests for nonresponse bias were performed to ensure that respondents did not differ systematically from nonrespondents. Two quantitative tests were performed. The first tested for differences between the group to whom surveys were mailed (the mailed group) and the group who returned the surveys (the respondent group). The second compared time-dated waves of respondents.

The mailed group and the respondent group were compared on the basis of the geographic characteristics of city and region, which are particularly appropriate comparisons in the case of computer abuse. Parker (1976) has argued that geographic distribution of the data must be carefully examined to guard against sampling error which results in disproportionately stressing the experience of heavily populated regions. For this analysis, the Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test was employed (Siegel and Castellan 1988). The results of the Chi-Square tests reveal that no significant differences between the mailed group and the respondent group exist at the 0.05 level.7

## 8. Results

Statistical techniques chosen for the study included LInear Structural RELations modelling (LISREL) and multivariate and univariate correlational tests. LISREL was used to investigate the simultaneous effects of deterrents and rival explanations on computer abuse. Additional corroborative tests chosen to strengthen the analysis were nonstructural tests of covariance equality (canonical correlation), distributionfree tests (Kruskal-Wallis), and zero-order or cirect-effect tests (Chi-Square Contingency Tables).

## 8.1 LISREL Modelling

The Security Impact Model (Figure 1) was tested using the LISREL statistical package. Observed values for measures were entered, as were parameters (constraints) specifying causal relationships between constructs. The LISREL package uses confirmatory factor analysis to generate loadings that best describe the specified relationships between measures and constructs. Through structural equations, LISREL then generates causal coefficients that best model the fit between constructs.º

LISREL was selected because it offers several advantages over other multivariate technigues. First, LISREL can be used to test relationships between constructs with multiple measures (Bagozzi 1980). Regression, on the other hand, cannot accommodate multiple measures of the same construct within the same linear model (Bielby and Hauser 1977). I ISREL is also superior to multivariate techniques because it permits complicated causal relationships to be expressed through hierarchical or nonhierarchical and recursive or nonrecursive structural equations (Blalock 1969). Because intricate causal networks often characterize real-world processes, this capability for mathematical modelling is useful both for development of theory (Blalock 1969) and application to practice (Dubin 1978). Finally, unlike other multivariate techniques, ordinal and interval data can be handled with equal facility by the

LISREL program. For ordinal data, polychloric distributions are generated from the data and from a range specified by the researcher (Joreskog and Sorbom 1983).º

LISREL statistical assumptions appear to be reasonable for this data set. LISREL assumes that (1) error terms between constructs and measures are uncorrelated; (2) error terms of observed values are uncorrelated across structural equations; and (3) the observed values are multinormal in their distributions. LISREL's ability to separate common from unique variance means that the first assumption is tenable. Given the independence of traits from methods that was revealed in the MTMM instrument validation analysis, the second assumption also appears to be reasonable. Since departures from multinormality affect only the standard errors of the causal coefficients and because parameter estimates in LISREL are consistent (Joreskog and Sorbom 1983), the large sample size achieved in this study means that the third assumption is also reasonable.

Finally, because LISREL models need to be just-identified or over-identified to ensure that estimates are meaningful in LISREL (Long 1983), tests for identifiability were run. These tests, including a test of the determinant of the information matrix generated by LISREL (Joreskog and Sorbom 1983) and a test for necessary or rank condition (Long 1983), confirmed that the Security Impact Model shown in Figure 1 was identifiable.

## 8.2 LISREL Model Fit

LISREL analysis indicates that the Security Impact Model fits the actual sample data. Figure 5a shows causal coefficients for the linkages between deterrents and computer abuse. These betas of —0.786 and –1.572 were significant at the 0.05 level. The model fits the covariance in the data moderately well, as demonstrated by a goodness of fit index of 0.680 with an adjusted goodness of fit of 0.597, where 1.00 is a perfect fit. Perhaps more revealing is that the model has a squared multiple correlation of 0.366, which may be interpreted to mean that the model accounts for 36.6 percent of the variance in the data set. It is clear, moreover, that deterrent severity has greater explanatory power than deterrent certainty, as their respective standardized coefficients, -0.587 and -0.107, indicate.

In a second LISREL model testing the rival explanations (Figure 5b), causal coefficients (beta, = -0.856; beta, = −1.56; gamma = -0.444) were all significant at the 0.05 level." The model goodness-of-fit index of 0.692 (adjusted goodness-of-fit = 0.610) and the squared multiple correlation of 0.374 (or 37.4 percent explained variance) also support the explanatory power of the model. This model fit was superior to the “deterrents only" model as indicated by an improvement of 20.34 in chi-square from the first to the second model, larger than the one degree of freedom lost by estimating the additional parameter.

The improvement in the adjusted goodness-of-fit from the first to the second model is more meaningful than change in the other indices because the goodness-offit and the squared multiple correlation always increase with relaxation of fixed parameters (Joreskog and Sorbom 1983). This outeome is roughly equivalent to the inevitable rise in R-square in regression analysis when variables are added to the linear model. By chance alone, more variance will be accounted for by adding variables or, in this case. by relaxing constraints. Thus, LISREL modelling indicates that general deterrence theory may be a good explanation for incidence of computer abuse.

## 8.3 LISREL Tests for Recıprocal Causal Effects

Among those who had experienced incidents of computer abuse within the last three years, nine percent of the reported incidents occurred before security was initiated. The possibility of a reverse causal linkage was therefore considered. One measure of the strength of deterrent certainty is the elapsed time between the inception of the security unit and the abusive incident. When the abusive incident occurs before the inception of security, the derived value of this measure will be negative. In the LISREL estimation procedure. the factor loading will be dampened if the effect is pervasive, and its sign will be negative. Since the loading on this factor was not negative, we may infer that the effect of computer abuses on the creation of new IS security units did not counterbalance the deterrent effect posited in the Security Impact Model.

As a further test, LISREL analysis was performed on an alternative model positing a reverse causal linkage. However, the beta was not significant at the 0.05 level. This result is not surprising given the small number of organizations in this sample that initiated security during the three years following an incident.

## 8.4 Canonical Correlation Tests

The relationship between deterrents and computer abuse was also tested using canonical correlation. This technique tests the independence of the covariance structures of two sets of measures by maximizing the correlation between linear components of sets of measures (Hair et al. 1979). For the linkage between deterrent and abuse measures, loadings were significant at the 0.05 level. The square of the canonical correlation for this set (0.51) yielded a shared variance of the two sets of 25.6 percent (Figure 6a). Because the test was bidirectional, the covariance in the deterrents set accounts for 25.6 percent of the covariance in the computer abuse set of measures or vice versa. To estimate the simple and unidirectional effect of deterrents on computer abuse, a redundancy index was calculated (Hair et al. 1979), yielding a value of 0.242. Deterrents, therefore, accounted for 24.2 percent of the variance in computer abuse.12

Another causal test was the canonical correlation between rival explanations and computer abuse. As shown in Figure 6b, this test indicated that rival explanations were not canonically correlated with computer abuse (chi-square = 32.45, p-value = 0.79). Thus, rival explanations did not account for a significant portion of the variance in computer abuse.

## 9. Discussion and Implications for Practice

As predicted by general deterrence theory, these tests were nearly unanimous in suggesting that IS security deterrents result in reduced incidence of computer abuse. Both the LISREL and canonical correlation analyses confirmed this relationship. Additional support for general deterrence theory was found using nonparametric analyses. Tests of a reverse causal linkage, however, were not significant, indicating that deterrents do result in lower abuse. Rival explanations of computer abuse, moreover, were generally insignificant. In rank order (cf. Table 4), deterrents and preventives that proved effective for IS security were (1) weekly hours dedicated to data security; (2) overall weekly hours dedicated to security; (3) use of multiple methods to disseminate information about penalties and acceptable system usage: (4) statements of penalties for violations; and (5) use of security software.

In practical terms, several actions should be taken on the basis of the research results. First, managers who determine that their security effort needs to be improved should establish policies regarding proper and improper use of the information system. A clearly defined set of policies is the precondition to implementing all the effective deterrents. The more detailed these policies are, the greater the deterrent impact on unacceptable system use. A policy of having employees sign a “data contract," for example, assigns responsibility and accountability for certain data.

After policies are in place, IS security officers should inform and educate users about acceptable system use. This information can be communicated in employee orientation sessions or in other settings, and IS security officers should stress that computer abusers will be punished according to the severity of the violation. For example, file trespassing may merely invoke a reprimand whereas purposeful destruction or modification of critical company data may be grounds for dismissal and legal action.

Next, with clearcut guidelines in place, IS security officers can strengthen ongoing data security efforts such as assigning and monitoring of passwords, classification of data and programs by security level, and surveillance of suspicious activities on the system. Follow-up of all identified violations will deter potential abusers and encourage compliance with security directives.

Finally, because resuÍts suggest that computer abuse can be prevented by use of security software, IS security officers should consider implementation of software preventives. Among the security software options are operating system security, DBMS security, and specialized security packages such as ACF-II, RACF, and Top Secret.

In summary, as general deterrence theory suggests, deterrent countermeasures can reduce the risk of large losses by computer abusers. When security surveillance is in place and is known to be in place with clear guicelines for system use, abuse drops off significantly.

## 10. Study Limitations and Directions for Further Research

In spite of the methodological precautions taken in the study, these findings could be artifactual. For example. there may be incentives for certain categories of respondents to not respond or the instrument may be flawed even though it has been validated. Other explanations may exist for patterns of abuse in organizations. The employment of deterrents and preventives by IS security explains lower incidence of computer abuse to some extent, but industry type and size of local IS departments also appear to have explanatory power (Straub and Hoffer 1987). Strong motivations, such as malice or greed coupled with opportunitv and incentive, may also explain abusive behavior.

Multiple statistical techniques were used to oset the biases of individual tests, but it is still possible that these findings are inaccurate. While multivariate techniques such as LISRFI and canonical correlation we·gh multiple measures of constructs, they also assume that the underlying model is well specified. If the model is not well specified, the techniques will still converge on a best ht that may, in fact, have significant causal coefficients. In this regard, these techniques are similar to regression and other multivariate techniques.

The use of I ISREI and canonical correlaticn, moreover, represents a model-fitting approach to research rather than traditional hypothesis testing. Model-fitting seeks to identify combinations of weighted measures leading to the best possible correlation between the measures being examined, which is a different approach to the use of statistics than is commonly found in 1he literature. Findings based on such model-fitting approaches may be capitalizing on chance factors.

Alternative research methodologies can help determine if the results reported in this paper accurately portray the impact of IS security. By using different approaches to study a phenomenon, we can test whether indings hold across methods, times, and settings (Cook and Campbell 1979). Among the possible new directions for research are studies that employ stronger checks for internal validity, such as field and laboratory experıments.'4 Because of a possble linkage between potential offender attitudes and abuse, laboratory studies rnight also be undertaken. An experimental deterrent treatment that simulates a computer security awareness training session could test for lower post-treatment abuse. In addition, qualitative research techniques, field interviews, and case studies would enhance our perspective.

## 11. Conclusions

The major implication of this study for the administration of computer security is straightforward: IS security has been effective. An active security staff and a commitment to data security are effective controls, as are activities in which security staff inform users about unacceptable system use and penalties for noncompliance. Organizations that articulate their policies on computer abuse and actively enforce these policies should benefit from doing so. Security measures such as computer security awareness training sessions also reduce losses from abuse.

Security software likewise helps to curb computer abuse, a finding now corroborated by another study in the field (Nance and Straub 1988). When system users become aware that IS security is actively monitoring their system activity and that security software can alert security personnel to violations, the deterrent impact on future abuse should be significant.
