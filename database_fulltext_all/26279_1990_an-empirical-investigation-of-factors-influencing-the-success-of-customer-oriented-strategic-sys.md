A case research strategy was utilized to study first-mover strategic systems which companies had built and offered to their customers in support of a primary product or service. The study investigated eleven systems to discover the factors which enabled or inhibited the following outcomes: developing a first-mover customer-oriented strategic system (COSS); achieving a high level of adoption of the (OSS by customers; obtaining competitive advantage from the COSS. In general, the findings supported previous research in the IS implementation and strategic systems literature. Factors that are related to the successful implementation of information systems and the competitive environment of the firm were associated with systems that were developed and introduced to the market first. Factors that are related to the adoption of innovations and information systems and to successful product marketing were associated with high adoption. There were several findings which had not been previously reported in the literature. Early adoption of the system was inhibited by poor support for the sales force and poor quality pilot tests. Long-term penetration was inhibited in cases, where the champion lost direct control over the COSS. Competitive advantage was not achieved by any system which had spent less than three years in the market or by those which did not achieve high long-term adoption.

## 1. Introduction

questions:

(2) What factors influence the adoption rate of a customer-oriented strategic information system by its customers?

(3) What factors affect the type and sustainability of the competitive advantage achieved by a company using such systems?

The study was designed to be of value both to business and information systems (IS) managers who are interested in achieving competitive advantage with information technology (IT) and to researchers investigating strategic systems. Our attempt to understand how several systems moved from the idea generation stage through to eventual outcomes provides another way of obtaining knowledge about the success factors for strategic systems. To date, such a comprehensive examination of the stages that eventually lead to the achievement of strategic advantage has not been reported in the literature.

For this study, the following definition of a strategic information system (SIS) was adopted: “. . . information systems used to support or shape the competitive strategy of an organization" Wiseman (1985, p. 7). We investigated one specific category of SIS—those which link a company to its customers. To distinguish these from other types of strategic systems, they were called Customer-Oriented Strategic Systems (COSS).

COSS may be also be viewed as a subset of interorganizational systems (IOS) defined by Cash and Konsynski (1985) as “an automated information system shared by two or more companies." Not all IOS are built for strategic business purposes, nor do all IOS necessarily link a company with its customers (Johnston and Vitale 1988).

## 2. Background

Since the early 1980's, examples of IT which shaped strategic business plans have emerged in the IS literature. These works were influenced by Porter's (1980) models of competitive strategies and by IS academics who, based on these models, began to describe the strategic potential of IS (Parsons 1983, McFarlan 1984). Subsequently, several frameworks were developed to help a company identify opportunities for such systems (Ives and Learmonth 1984, Wiseman 1985, Porter and Millar 1985, and Synott 1987)

To date, much of the focus has been on describing how, rather than systematically investigating why, IS lead to competitive advantage. While these efforts have been valuable in identifying the types of SIS and detailing their possible effects, academics have called for “a research agenda to develop and test explanatory models of IT and competitive advantage" (Treacy 1985). The empirical articles investigating SIS are summarized below. Many of their findings have been incorporated into the research models described in §3.

In a mail survey of 51 IS executives, King et al. (1989) investigated the use of SIS. The most important facilitators of a company's effort to create strategic systems were: technical support within the firm, existing IT leadership position, and pressure from competition. Similarly, the factors reported to be the strongest inhibitors were: the importance of other priorities, the difficulty of assessing tangible contribution, lack of appropriate planning, and lack of top management support. Johnston and Carrico (1988) interviewed in 11 companies to investigate the organizational and industry factors which enable a company to use IT strategically. They reported the importance of the interaction of industry conditions (e.g., high information content in the company's relationship with customers/suppliers, competitive pressure, and limited product life) and internal capabilities (e.g., senior management IT leadership integration of IT and business strategy, and direct contact between IT and line management). Tavakolian (1989) reported that companies in different strategic positions (using Miles and Snow's typology, 1978) had significantly different values on IT centralization variables. The more aggressive a company was reported to be (i.e., prospector vs. defender), the lower the reported centralization score.

In one of the first attempts to systematically analyze SIS, Runge (1985) used a multiple case study approach to study 35 systems in Britain to determine what structures or processes enabled them to exploit strategic opportunities. Runge used two criteria to determine the key enabling factors: their incidence in the sample and their correlation with adoption rate. Five factors were identified as key enablers: the presence of a product champion, a high level of customer involvement in the development process, an existing system upon which to base the COSS, a high degree of attention to marketing the COSS, and the circur vention of existing IS planning and prioritization procedures.

Copeland and McKenney (1988) conducted an intensive study of airline reservation systems and reported three broad features facilitating the strategic use of IT: economies of scale arising from large installed processing capacity, technical competence, and intelligent persistence on the part of company management. Other studies investigated the difficulties inherent in convincing top management of the strategic value of IT (Lederer and Mendelow 1986), the problems in implementing strategic IT plans (Lederer and Sethi 1988). and the factors in fluencing the success of IT planning (Pyburn 1983).

In summary, while the previous literature gave us some insight about the organizational factors surrounding SIS in general, few had ¿nvestigated these factors as they related to a specific system and only Runge (1985) had looked at the full range of events which occur over the lifetime of a SIS

## 3. Research Models

Since Runge's study focused on the strategic system as the unit of analysis, it provided a foundation upon which to base this esearch. We extended his work by making several changes to clarify and enhance the research framework

## A. T'he Dependent Variable

Runge (1985) focused on one dependent variable as a measure of COSS success: the rate of adoption of the system by its customers in the first year after it was released. It appeared to us that a wider perspective should be taken to examine COSS. In order for a COSS to influence the profitability of a company, it must first be created by the company, then it must be widely adopted by its customers, and finally, it must change customers' behavior to such an extent that competitive advantage is achieved. Each phase in this continuum is necessary but not sufficient for the accomplishment of the subsequent step.

An examination of Runge's findings provided additional support for taking a wider perspective. Two of his enabling factors, a produt t champion and the avoidance of IS planntng gutdelınes, were not correlated with adcption rate although they were present in a large proportion of his sample. Another factor, customer involvement, was correlated very highly with adoption rate. These results indicated the presence of two separate outcomes: development of the COSS (which is influenced by champions and IS planning guidelines) and adoption of the COSS (which is influenced by user involvement). Further, since high adoption does not guarantee competitive advantage (e.g. automated teller machines), the competitive advantage which accrues from a COSS was investigated.

We, therefore, defined COSS “success" as a multi-stage concept, having three, time-related, dimensions:

(1) A timely development of the COSS (i.e. by being a “first-mover" in creating it)

(2) High adoption by the customers (Runge's dependent variable), and

(3) An improvement in the competitive position of the company.

## B. The Independent Variables

This study was essentially a qualitative investigation guided by three preliminary models which identified major areas or variables for inquiry. Because a similar multistage treatment of strategic systems had not been attempted before, variables chosen for inclusion were from several sources: (i) prior theory in organizational and SIS literature, (ii) findings of empirical studies, and (iii) descriptive/prescriptive models. We proceeded knowing that our models (shown as Figures 1, 2 and 3 and described later in the paper) were potentially incomplete and therefore designed the data collection process so that the discovery of new factors would not be precluded.

The study was structured to gather information about the five major entities in the COSS environment: the sponsoring Company (including corporate level and IS function characteristics), its Customers, the Industry, the underlying Product supported by the COSS and the COSS itself (including its development, functions and support). These were used to categorize the variables for investigation and to report the findings.

(1) Factors Influencing COSS Development. Figure 1 depicts our preliminary list of factors which have the potential to influence the speed with which a company develops a COSS. In this model, the dependent variable is the time, or order, of COSS introduction into the market. It distinguishes between first-movers and followers.

The factors, with the sources from which they were identified, are as follows:

(a) Three of Runge's (1985) findings: a champion, avoidance of IS planning and an existing system

(b) Copeland and McKenney's (1988) suggestion that technical competence was important was interpreted as “technology awareness" of corporate level managers and “competence" of the IS function.

(c) Pyburn's (1983) finding that IS status and business awareness were important to tie business strategy to SIS development

(d) IS-related factors which King et al. (1989) found to be facilitators: IT leadership (proactive/reactive stance), existing technological support, and planning policies.

(e) Johnston and Carrico's (1988) finding that competitive pressures will lead to the development of SIS was investigated using elements from Porter's (1985) five forces model.

(f) Because a COSS has customers as its target, it can be thought of as a product moving through the new product development phases of opportunity identification, design, test, introduction and life-cycle management (Urban et al. 1987). Variables describing aspects of the COSS development project were added to reflect this view.

## 4. Research Methodology

## A. Overall design

We considered two approaches to investigate the research questions—a case study approach which emphasised qualitative data and a sample survey which emphasised statistical inference. Bonoma (1985, p. 207) states that case research methods are “useful when a phenomenon is broad and complex, where the existing body of knowledge is insufficient to permit the posing of causal questions and when a phenomenon cannot be studied outside the context in which i occurs." The first and last qualifications are true for the COSS phenomenon; the second one depends on the current inventory of empirical literature. Although several empirical SIS studies had been conducted, only Runge's study (1985) had focused on the system as the unit of analysis. It had conceptualized success in a narrower way than we proposed. Additionally, measures had not been developed for most of the variables in the models making it difficult to use survey techniques with any assurance that the results would have adequate reliability or validity. Thus it was, decided that the case research strategy would be the best vehicle to investigate our models.

## B. Sample

The study was conducted in Canada. As the population of COSS in Canada is unknown and discovering it was beyond the resources of the study, it was decided to use a convenience sample generated from industry contacts. The first author contacted colleagues in industry to solicit names of companies and information systems which were leaders in their field. The criteria for including a COSS in the study were:

—The company had to be profit oriented

—The COSS had to be operated at the customer's place of business, either through a remote terminal or a stand-alone computer with a telecommunications link.

—The customer had to be able to refuse the COSS while still purchasing the product.

—The COSS had to have been available in the marketplace for at least a year (in order that adoption rates could be measured).

The COSS had to have been the first such system offered to customers in the target market. For example, if the company's target market (as defined by the company) was Canadian hardware retailers, the system had to have been the first of its kind offered to hardware retailers in Canada.

We chose to investigate examples of a subset of the COSS population (the first movers) for three main reasons: (i) first-mover systems were easier to identify as a group than followers. The concept of “follower" was considered difficult to operationalize. For example, are all COSS after the first mover considered to be followers, regardless of the additions or modifications to their capabilities and structure? (ii) Selecting first-movers allowed us to investigate them as innovations from a customer's perspective. (iii) We felt that the usefulness and interest of our study to practitioners would be heightened if we had a sample of first-movers.

Once a company was identified as being a candidate, both the line executive who had responsibility for the COSS and the IS manager were contacted for participation in the project. In eight out of 1 1 systems investigated, we were granted interviews with both line and IS management. The sample consisted of 11 systems located in nine Canadian companies (see Table 1 for the descriptions).

## C. Measurement Issues

Most of the factors which were hypothesised to influence the success of a COSS had not been rigorously operationalized in previous studies. Since it was beyond the scope of this project to attempt to develop measures, these factors (e.g., customer involvement, COSS priority, customer bargaining power) were investigated by asking one or more questions during the interviews (§F contains an example)

The dependent variables were explored in more detail as described in Appendix A. The notable outcome of this effort was to create two dependent measures of adoption —the rate of adoption after one year (as Runge used) and the penetration of the COSS after five years (measured as a percentage of customers who adopted it relative to the target set).

## D. Interview Guide

An interview guide' was structured to lead the respondent through the chronological history of the COSS, from idea generation to results. It contained more than 100 questions, many of which were based on Runge's question set. Additions and alterations to Runge's interview guide were made to fully investigate the dependent variables. For example, questions were added to probe for quantitative measures of adoption rate such as the actual number of customers over time, number of target customers, and usage rates by customers. Questions regarding competitive advantage were added to investigate the impact of the COSS on: (1) the company's profitability (2) other measures of competitive strength (such as image, entry barriers, customer loyalty), and (3) internal changes in the company (such as strategy, skills required, structure).

There were several open-ended questions throughout the interview, such as “How influential was [factor X] in achieving your results on [dependent variable Y]? These questions resulted in the identification of new explanatory factors by the respondents. These factors were then probed for in the remainder of the cases and data from previous cases was analyzed to specify, where possible, values for these new factors. Several of them (e.g.. champion continuity, support for the sales force) had good support, others (e.g., focus on technology  s. focus on information in the COSS) did not.

## E. Reliability

Data was largely collected from long (1.5 to  hours) interviews, conducted by the first author. with participants directly involved in the promotion, development and management of a COSS. This data collection method is likely to suffer from problems such as the recall of the interviewees, shading the truth, and the influence of outcomes on responses. These problems were alleviated and the reliability of the data was safeguarded in several ways, as discussed in Appendix B.

## F. Data Analysıs

There were four major stages in the analysis of the raw interview data.² The first two constitute a within-case analysis, the last two a cross-case analysis (Eisenhardt 1989).

The first step was assimilation. A value was assigned to each of the factors for the COSS. For example, "customer involvement" had been investigated by asking two questions (Were customers involved in development at any stage?; What was the nature of their invoivement?). The tape recorded data was reviewed by the researchers and a value of Yes or No was recorded for the customer involvement factor. In this way, all information about a single COSS was transcribed onto comprehensive worksheets. The process of interpretation began with summarizing data onto high level models. Using C ompany, Customer, Industry, Product, and COSS as headings, the information whıch the interviewees had porticularly noted concerning the four dependent variables was summarized from the worksheets. This resulted in a concise overview of each system which identified the most important facilitators and inhibitors of each outcome. The systems were then ranked based on their dependent variable values—Adoption Rate for the first year, Penetration measured four or five years later and Competitive Advantage. This resulted in the systems' being categorized into two or three groups for each dependent variable (i.e., slow/moderate/fast early adoption rate, high/low penetration percentage, and winners/hopefuls/losers on competitive advantage measures). Finally, a process of comparison was used to determine which independent variables showed support for the models shown in Figures 1, 2 and 3. In making this determination, we decided not to utilize statistical analysıs techniques because of our case research approach and the limited size of the sample available to us. Instead, we relied upon a simple inspection of data to determine how various factors influenced the dependent variables.

## 6. Findings

## A Factors Associated with "First-movers"

Being a first-mover in building and implementing a COSS was a criterion for inclusion in the study sample. Ten of the eleven systems met this criterion (during the interview process, it was discovered that the eleventh system was not a first mover, and it was not included in the analysis of this dependent variable). Of these ten, many have similar characterıstics. Findings which sunımarize the commonly observed (defined as existing in over 50% of the companies} factors are presented in Table 2.

Because of the nature of the sample (i.e., all but one were first-movers), the factors identified in this section should be considerec as part of a hypothesis generation process, to be tested in future studies. We can report the commonalities among first-movers, but cannot speculate which of them would also be found among followers. Several of the findings merit discussion

—Most of the companies mentioned a strong, drive to be number one, either in all parts of their business or in the specific product line affected by the COSS.

—Many firms reported the presence of an individual who championed the idea of the COSS, lobbied for support, and oversaw its development and deployment. In other firms, the CEO recognized the value of the COSS project and, for the first time, personally monitored the development of an IS

—In most organizations the IS function was very proactive, continually seeking innovative ways to utilize IT. IS people had developed high levels of competence that enabled them to design and deliver the COSS.

—Building systems to link with customers is a normal way of doing business for many of the companies in the sample. Most reported previous COSS experience.

—Rivalry, both product-oriented and COSS-inspired, among existing competitors was very high. Perceptions that rivals were currently developing COSS, a strong threat from new entrants, and customer bargaining power were key motivators for building a COSS.

—Most of the systems took on a very high profile within the company while they were being developed. They were accorded high priority, and an abundance of resources.

—The high-pressure environment surrounding a COSS did not, as one might suspect, result in short-changing the testing phase. Companies reported comprehensive pilot tests. Possibly the companies viewed the pilot test as a marketing tool, the beginning of the adoption process, rather than as the end of the development life cycle.

—One of Runge's strongest findings was the avoidance of IS planning guidelines, i.e., not following the rules (payback period or ROI) which determine which systems are to be developed. In this sample, the same trend was observed, albeit at a lower level. Six of the companies reported avoiding IS planning guidelines and another two reported that the planning guidelines were structured to support strategic systems.

## B. Factors Relating to Adoption and Penetration

The second question investigated was “given the existence of a COSS which has been developed in a timely manner, what factors influence customers' adoption rate and the eventual penetration of the COSS market?"

(1) Factors Discriminating Between Fast and Slow Adoption Rate Measured after One Year. Of the eleven systems, six were reported to have had fast adoption, four slow adoption and one a moderate rate of adoption by customers during the first year of their introduction. The variables which clearly separated the systems enjoying fast adoption from the ones being adopted more slowly are shown in Table 3.

Only three COSS had been built using customer input during the design phase. All three had reported rapid adoption in the first year.

All the companies which had experienced slow or moderate adoption reported disappointment with the competence of their sales force in selling the COSS. The interviewer was aware that attribution effects could have caused these replies and asked the respondents to describe the support provided to their sales team. Many companies had expected product sales people to install, demonstrate and sell the COSS with little training. Therefore, the true cause may have been poor support for the sales staff rather than low sales force competence. This finding was predicted by Johnston and Vitale (1988) who stated “training personnel whose jobs will be affected by an IOS is an obvious but often neglected step towards assuring the system's success."

## C. Findings Relating to Competitive Advantage

As mentioned in Appendix A, it was not possible to measure the competitive advantage quantitatively. Although all respondents considered their COSS to be a success, their description of the specific benefits the COSS had generated did enable us to separate them into three groups: the “winners" (2 COSS), the “hopefuls" (7) and the “losers"(2).

The winners had reaped significant, positive profitability and competitive strength changes based on high penetration. Sales were up, costs were down and they had attained strategic goals for their products. Their systems had been available for over five years. The hopefuls had, for the most part, introduced their COSS into the market within the last four years. They had created the potential for significant gains by raising switching costs or erecting entry barriers. These results, however, had not yet been realized on their income statement. The losers had failed to differentiate their products or to lower their costs substantially. 'They had a small number of COSS customers and were now trying to strengthen the COSS. Their systems had been available for more than five years.

There were three factors which differentiated between winners and losers. One, “COSS adoption penetration," had been included in the model (Figure 3). Two others emerged during the data analysis and should be considered as hypotheses for future studies.

(1) Adopton penetration: Both winners achieved high penetration; both losers had attained less than 50%, of their targets. This finding implies that high adoption penetration might be a necessary factor, but we do not believe it is sufficient for competitive advantage since other SIS (e.g., ATMs) have shown that high penetration alone will not achieve competitive advantage.

(2) Time in the mrhetplace No COSS which was less than four years old had achieved any significant quantitative gains. Many of the “hopefuls" still expected gains to be forthcoming but, for a variety of reasons, customers had been slower than predicted in adopting the system or, after adootion. in changing their behavior to favor the COSS supplier. Examination of the "hopefuls" group leads us to believe that results from a COSS will be only discernable after a long period in the market.

(3) Persistence: The winners reported that their original champion was still in charge of the direction of the COSS; the losers' champion was not. As Copeland and McKenney (1988) predicted, “intelligent persistence" had paid dividends.

## The Winners vs. the Losers

This section briefly describes the history of two pairs of systems, each consisting of a winner and a loser. One pair supported information intensive products, the other commodity products. None of the systems to be described has enjoyed success in all stages of its lifetime. As a group, they experienced technological setbacks, unexpected events in the marketplace, lack of enthusiasm from customers, and internal organizational problems. Some survived and prospered others did not. From this collection of cases, we can identify their differences and speculate about their importance, but we cannot infer causality.

Both the winner and the loser supporting commodity products had enjoyed high early adoption rates and had the backing of organizational champions. They differed, however, in the following ways:

The winner used the early gains to deliver more product-related service with their salespeople; the loser was satisfied with the early adoption rate and put the system into maintenance mode. The winner always tried to understand the customer's environment and provide informatior-based service; the loser focused on technology-driven solutions. The champion is still overseeing the winning COSS; the loser's champion is now overseeing other areas of the business. The winner had a long term strategy which the COSS supported; the loser was using the COSS to defend against imminent loss of market share.

Both systems which supported information-intensive products had top management support, and both companies were very technologically sophisticated. Both suffered from slow early adoption. They differed however, in that

—The winner built the customer interface and subjected it to a complete rewrite after the pilot test. The loser took an existing internal system and offered it to the customer with little modification or testing. Although the product champion was transferred away in both cases, the winner's champion regained control and re-instituted the original vision for the system. The winner's system had many enhancements to bring it to full-feature status; the loser's system had fewer enhancements and has only partial features now.

## A Cautionary Note Regarding Interpretation of the Findings

The fact that our sample contained only first-movers influences the generalizability of the findings in several ways. With respect to the development of COSS, we cannot claim to know what distinguishes first-movers from followers, only factors common among first-movers. It is possible that some of these factors may also be found among followers and, therefore, factors which distinguish the two groups are, as yet, undetermined.

Our findings with respect to early adoption are also partly derived from the first mover situations since the customers were evaluating systems which had not been available before. If we had chosen to study followers, there may have been additional influential factors such as those relating to the presence of other COSS.⁴ Therefore, taking a conservative interpretation, the factors relating to the development and early adoption of the COSS should be considered as relevant only to first-mover situations. It should be noted, however, that many of our findings (as shown in Appendix C) support previous theory and empirical research.

By the time the COSS had been in the market for four years, others had been made available; thus our findings relating to adoption penetration and competitive advantage can be considered as representative of a more mature COSS environment and not specific to first-movers. The finding that early adoption rate did not appear to influence later adoption supports this interpretation.

## 7. Concluding Comments

When we began this project, our research models were shaped by findings from previous studies and the following beliefs: (i) developing a COSS as a first-mover is an act of innovation, very similar to that of developing a new product; (ii) adoption of a first-mover's COSS by customers would be similar in nature to adoption of other technological or administrative innovations; and (iii) a COSS would create competitive advantage based on industry and economic conditions and changes in the behavior of customers who adopt the COSS.

We gathered extensive data from the sponsors and developers of 11 COSS and matched them against our models. In general, the results concerning first-movers and adoption were similar to those predicted by previous researchers. A comparison to the previous literature is provided in Appendix C. What is more interesting, however, is a discussion of the findings that were not predicted by or which disconfirmed earlier results. These are discussed below. Following this discussion, we offer a tentative model to guide future research of COSS.

## A. Unexpected Findings

(1) Pilot Testing. The high incidence of long and thorough pilot tests was surprising since we had expected that even experienced IS professionals would be influenced by the very competitive environment surrounding the COSS and would shortchange the testing process.

(2) Marketing Programs and Sales Forces Both Runge (1985) and this study found a high incidence of superior marketing programs. He interpreted this variable as an enabling factor, whereas our respondents stated unequivocally that marketing decisions surroundıng the COSS were “business as usual" and were not influential in the outcome. Many executives remarked that the real problems lay in the ineffective performance of the sales force. Through questioning, they revealed that product sales people had been required to sell the COSS without undergoing significant training.

(3) Avoidıng IS Planning Guidelines. We found a lower incidence of avoiding IS planning guidelines as compared to Runge (1985). The explanation seems to be that, over time, companies are creating IS planning guudelines that are flexible enough to permit the evaluation and prioritization of SIß which may not look favorable when subjected to rigid cost/benefit analyses. We would expect this trend to continue as companies mature in their experience with SIS and in their planning and evaluation methodologies.

(4) Ear/r . 1doptton. Early adoption rate did not predict success in long term penetration or competitive advantage. Companies reversed early adoption failure in a number of ways, most notably by enhancing and modifying the COSS as customer preferences were revealed over time. Companies which turned their attentions elsewhere and did not enhance the COSS. lost any inherent first mover advantages.

(5) Continuity ofthe Champion. While the need for a champion in the creation of a new product such as a COSS is well reported, little has been said about the need for champion continuitv after marketing the product. Several companies emphatically stated that their lack of success was attributable to the absence of strong, consistent leadership which kept energies focused on the strategic goals. Both winners still had their original champions in positions where they oversaw the COSS. This implies that champions retain the “organizational memory which is vital to a long-running strategic project.

## B. A Proposal for a COSS Research Moael

This study attempted to extend Runge's (1985) work by investigating COSS success based on a proposed four-stage model: development, early adoption, later penetration and competitive advantage. Our findings partially support the view that these dimensions of COSS success are distinct from each other. It appears that success at a given stage is not necessarily an indicator of success at a subsequent one,⁵ and the factors influencing success at one stage are different from the factors affecting the success of other stages. Thus, there is evidenct to suggest that COSS success is made up of several components.

In Figure 4, we propose a generalized model of COSS success which contains five stages (idea generation was added since it precedes the development stage and there is much prescriptive literature on it (Synott 1987, Ives and Learmonth 1984). Each stage is shown as having several possible outcomes; one which signifies abandonment of the COSS and others signifying varying levels of success. For example, a development project for a COSS can end in failure, or the COSS can enter the market as a first-mover or a follower. Within the first year after entry, the COSS can be withdrawn because of low adoption or can continue to be supported while enjoying fast or slow early adoption rates. COSS which stay in the market for the next several years can achieve varyinglevels of adoption penetration and competitive advantage. Alternatively, the COSS can be superseded by other innovations or by changes in customer needs and be withdrawn.

One reason to propose a multi-stage model is our belief that each stage is supported by different bodies of IS and organizational literature. Because a COSS is an IS, a competitive weapon, and an innovation, both to its developers and to its adopters, the models which describe its behavior and effects derive from several sources. The right-hand column in Figure 4 tentatively identifies the supporting literature for each stage.

We do not suggest that the various outcomes or the stages are exhaustive, merely that some support for their existence has been shown in this studv and other literature. Our research design and the sample allowed us to study only the development and adoption stages and observe only a subset of the potential outcomes: firstmovers, fast and slow early adoption, high penetration with high competitive advantage and low penetration with low competitive advantage. We have identified and reported factors associated with these outcomes but have not made strong claims of causality.

The outcomes that we were not able to investigate have either been identified in the literature or are highly plausible. There are many examples of successful follower COSS such as the American Airlines Sabre system. High penetration without competitive advantage is evident (e.g., automated teller machines) and low penetration with competitive advantage is possible, given that changes might be precipitated with even a small percentage of influential customers adooting a given COSS. Finally, although there has been little attention to failures in the literature, we believe that a COSS may fail at any stage of its progression from idea generation to eventual competitive success. Whether it can be withdrawn is a testable question since withdrawing a service, however unprofitable it may be, may result in competitive disadvantage. Both of the “failures" in our sample continue to be offered to customers.

This model can be used to guide future research. It suggests that a sample containing nonentrants, first-movers, and followers is needed to identify the factors which separate these outcomes. A larger sample of COSS which achieved high penetration might illuminate those situations in which high penetration does not lead to high competitive advantage. A sample of failing, m ature COSS could be used to identify the barriers to exit and test the model's impiied hypothesis that a COSS may be withdrawn. The model also provides a justification to study any stage of COSS success without necessarily examining the prior ones, e.g., a set of COSS in a mature industry could be examined for factors leadinç. to competitive advantage.

In summary, we followed a case strategy to assess and refine the three preliminary models comprising variables which had the potential to influence COSS success. Given the lack of strong theory to guide this work, it was likely that not all of these variables would be significantly associated wit COSS success or failure. The study did, however, find results which are consistent with previous empirical findings in IS implementation and SIS. It also identified new factors (e.g., champion continuity, support for the sales force, pilot tests) which should be investigated in future studies In addition to examining individual variables the study attempted to take a more global view of the processes leading to eventual COSS success by proposing a multistage model. We believe that more holistic research designs may aid us to uncover the true nature of these SIS, and this empirical study reflects a beginning effort in this direction.

## APPENDIX A—Measuring the Dependent Variables

1. First Mover

First-mover was defined as being first to introduce a (OSS in the relevant market area. Therefore, if a company specified that its market was Ouebec, it had to be the first industry competitor to offer the particular COSS to companies in Quebec. Whether or not the COSS had been introduced in the U.S. or in other parts of Canada was judged to be irrelevant in measuring this construct.

Being “first" ıs a relative term, and several interpretations are possible. However, for many of the systems in our sample, these distinctions were unimportant since there was a long period in which the COSS was the only one available in a given market area. In the financial industry, however, every competitor would have a version of a mainline COSS within the space of two years, making a distinction between first-mover and follower more difficult. In the case of the banks, a period of two to four months was allowed within which the participants were considered to be “first." For example, both the Bank of Montreal and the Royal Bank were considered to be first-movers since their respective cash management systems were delivered within two months of each other, and this space of time was judged by the respondents to be too short for the targeted customers (large institutions) to change banks based on the availability of a COSS

In order to ensure that each system in the sample was indeed a “first mover," the interviewer asked industry informants to determine if other COSS were available. After this verification, the company was contacted and asked to participate in the study. Furthermore, during the interview, several questions were asked about the prior existence of competitive COSS and the timing of competitors’ responses

## 2. Adoption Rate

It can be argued that a company's choice of a target market or a targeted number of customers for adoption might have been either overly ambitious or too conservative. To solicit alternative measures from external sources would not, however, guarantee a more valid measure for the adoption target. We believe that the company's own targets are the relevant ones to measure success against since those figures were the ones used to cost justify and market the COSS.

This study tried to measure the adoption construct by using questions about the original target number of customers for the COSS, the number who signed up during the first and subsequent years, the rate (slow, moderate or fast) of adoption, the current number of COSS customers, and the current target number of customers.

The result of this line of questioning was a set of data which was difficult to force into a single dependent adoption rate construct. As Downs and Mohr (1976) have suggested, two dependent variables are appropriate in measuring adoption. We operationalized the time-based variable as “adoption rate in the first year" and based it on the rate of adoption as perceived by the interviewees. This variable was used to compare our findings with those in Runge's study, The usage-based variable was called “penetration at the end of 4–5 years" and was measured by calculating the percentage of the company's target population that had accepted the COSS. We believe that eventual penetration was a more meaningful measure of adoption success since strategic systems are usually built to create sustainable competitive advantage, not just to influence the first year's results.

## 3. Competitive Advantage

Because the contribution made by an information system to the profitability and competitive strength of a company is difficult to identify by examining the company's financial statements, it was felt that informed respondents would be the best available source for acquiring this information, Accordingly, the interviewer asked a comprehensive set of questions about the benefits received from the COSS in an effort to discover objective ways to measure competitive advantage. The respondent was first asked an open question about the benefits that the COSS had brought to the company. This was followed by a set of closed questions designed to quantify any stated benefit and to identify benefits in areas not mentioned. The interview was concluded with a “yes/no" question about the success of the COSS and an open-ended question asking the respondent to comment on the reasons for this level of success

From an analysis of the responses, we developed a measure of competitive advantage which had two major dımensions. One dimension consisted of quantifiable benefits such as new customers, increases in sales, reductions in costs, and increases in product price. The other consisted of qualitative benefits such as increases in image within the industry, increases in customer satisfaction, higher entry barriers, higher switching costs, lower customer bargaining power, and broader customer contact.

The quantifiable benefits had already affected the income statements. The qualitative benefits affected manv of Porter's (1985) “competitive forces"and had the potential to affect the income statement in the future. Because the items in both lists were not independen: and therefore not strictly additive, procedures which involved weighting and rating the items were not appropriate. Also, it was not clear that current quantifiable benefits would be sustainable. However, it was Jecided to give more weight to responses which included quantifiable benefits since they had already affected profitabılity. A ranking of the COSS on quantifiable benefits resulted in an acceptable ranking on qualitative benefits. In other words, COSS which reported high quantitative benefits also reported high qualtative benefits.

We classified as "winners" COSS which reported both hugh quantitative and qualitative benefits “I osers" had reported no sıgnıficant benefts of either kind COSS which had reported very small quantifiable gauns were placed in the “hopefuls" category if thes reported oualtative benefits and a plausible rationale of how these "sott" benefits would affect profitablity in the future For example, one COSS had not delivered quantifiable benefits because of certain market conditions These conditions were expected to change within the next vear, and the respondent felt that the (OSS systems which had been adopted would influence their market share position at that time
