As organizational environments become more turbulent and as managers spend more time in meetings in an effort to deal with that turbulence, using information technology to support meetings has become more important. This paper reports on an experiment that compared meetings supported by information technology to meetings with conventional manual support only. The experiment differs from most previous group decision support system (GDSS) experiments in that solutions to the task it used could be objectively scored, it introduced assigned leadership as an independent variable, and it is the first GDSS experiment to compare use of a subset of the University of Arizona GroupSystems GDSS tools to manual group methods. In addition to a communication condition (GDSS or manual) and assigned leadership, the experiment also investigated the effects of anonymity on group process and outcomes. The experiment found that GDSS groups were less likely to reach consensus, took more time to reach a decision, and had more equal levels of member participation than manual groups. No main effects were found for assigned leadership or anonymity.

## 1. Introduction

The world in which managers find themselves is becoming more complex and more turbulent (Toffler 1980, Huber 1984b, Senn 1987), and this increasing turbulence and complexity require that they spend an increasing percentage of their time in group meetings, for purposes of planning, coordination and integration (Huber 1984a, Hymowitz 1988). But meetings have their problems: for instance, as more people attend a particular meeting, less seems to be accomplished. Techniques such as Nominal Group Technique (Van de Ven and Delbecq 1974) and the Delphi method (Dalkey and Halmer 1963, Dalkey 1969) have been developed for addressing these problems, but recent attention has turned to computer support for improving group meetings. This computer support has taken many forms, including computer conferencing systems (Hiltz and Turoff 1981 and group decision support systems (GDSS) (Huber 1982, 1984b; Gray 1983; DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987; Kraemer and King 1988). (omputer conferencing systems are designed to allow geographically dispersed groups to hold “electronic conferences," even asynchronously. GDSS, on the other hand, are designed to provide communication and collaborative work support for groups—even face-to-face ones. This paper explores the impact of GDSS on the quality of face-to-face group meetings.

Groups have the potential for outperforming individuals on a given task because of the synergy that can result from having individuals work together. In the past, researchers have found, however, that groups often do not exceed or even equal the performance of the best individual in the group (Shaw 1978). This inabılity of the group to live up to its potential reflects the problems associated with group work. These problems are called process losses, because the process of working together as a group sometimes gets in the way of and dirninishes group performance (Steiner 1966). Too large a group may reduce the motivation of each member and render coordination more difficult. GDSS are designed to improve the group meeting process through reducing or eliminating process losses.

Several experiments have been conducted into the effects of GDSS (Steeb and Johnston 1981; Lewis 1982; Turoff and Hilt: 1982; Ruble 1984; Beauclair 1987; Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson 1988; Watson, DeSanctis and Poole 1988; Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988; Jarvenpaa, Rao ar d Huber 1988; A. Easton 1988). The study described in this paper was conducted using GroupSystems, developed at the University of Arizona. The study introduces assigned leadership as an independent variable and compares group performance using GDSS to manual groups on an objectıvely scored group task.

## 2. Conceptual Basis of the Work

McGrath's (1984) taxonomy of the variables and relationships that have been important to the study of small groups has been suggested as a theoretical basis for the study of the effects of GDSS (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987; Zigurs, Poole and De-Sanctis 1988). Those specific aspects of the McGrath framework important in a comparison of GDSS and manual groups are represented in a model developed independently by Reder and Conklin (1987) As shown in Figure 1, their model illustrates the relationships between the classes of variables important to the study of any communication medium.

The model demonstrates how influential group and task characteristics are on decısion and process outcomes, and it also demonstrates how the communication medium affects these outcomes. The impacts of the group and the task are filtered through the communication medium, directly affecting the outcomes and the message features, which in turn affect the outcomes. Although the direct influences of group and task characteristics are strong, the intervening influence of the communication medium may overpower them. For example, the more equal rates of participaton allowed (or even dictated) by a GDSS may generate solutions that may not have arisen otherwise, and these alternative solutions may prove more attractive to the group as a whole than the solutions preferred by the more dominant group members. GDSS use may also reduce social inhibition on the part of group members, allowing some group members to generate and exchange comments which are not considered socially acceptable, particularly if GDSS allow communication among group members to be anonymous. ## 3. Literature Review

## 3.1. Independent Variables

Reder and Conklin's model (1987) clearly highlights the importance of communication media: “The channel utilized affects the nature of their communicative and decision making activity, and the effects these relationships have on the outcomes of the decision making process" (Reder and Conklin 1987, pp. 3–4). Over many years of research, strong channel effects have been found. and researchers have theorized that the effects are intrinsic to the channels themselves.

The dıstinction between GDSS and manual groups clearly involves communication medium differences. A GDSS is any computer-based system which combines “communications, computer, and decision technologies to support problem formulation and solution in group meetings" (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987, p. 589). A GDSS may be configured in many different ways, ranging from having group members meet in one room at the same tirne. to having members interact from dispersed sites asynchronously. Meeting in the same room, group members have available the traditional verbal and non-verba. channels, in addition to the electronic channel the computers and network provide. Manual groups, as the term is used here, meet in the same room with no computer, communication, or automated decision aids for group support.

Another variable relevant to group decision processes and GDSS use is leadership. Assigned leadership refers to someone assigned to help the group reach a decision. Assigned leadership has been studied in a few computer-mediated communication systems experiments (T'uroff and Hiltz 1982. Barefoot and Strickland 1982). In most studies of communication media that employ assigned leadership as a variable, those who are assigned the leadership role are expected to demonstrate what Hemphill (1961) calls “attempted leadership" behavior. Such behavior consists of “recommending a rule of organization, or a procedure, or a technique, or a consistent pattern of behavior for the group to follow" (Hemphi] 1961, p. 202). This is process leadership, not content leadership. Assigned leaders are not expected to push for their own preferred solutions to the problems under consideration by the group. Instead, leadershıp assignment adds an additional layer of stiucture to the situation. Those who are assigned the leadership role are told to help the group reach consensus. They are not told to lead the group to adopt the solutions they themselves think best. In that sense, the assigned role might better be called “conductor" than leader.

Process leadership has important implications for groups using GDSS. There is always the possibilit of leadership emergence in an ad hoc group (Bass 1954, Hemphill 1961, Bass 1981). Strickland, et al. (1978), nowever, found that behavioral hierarchies were more likely to emerge and maintain themselves in face-to-face groups than in the computer-mediated communication groups. They suggested that the reduction of social cues in computer-mediated communication has a dampening effect on leader emergence. It was not clear from their findings whether electronic mediation simply prevents leaders from emerging or whether it also suppresses the dominance of formal leaders as well. A follow-up study in which they appointed group leaders (Barefoot and Strickland 1982) found no effect on the mean level of leader dominance across conditions. Turoff and Hiltz (1932) introduced an assigned leader as a means to impose a second type of group process structure on their computer mediated groups (the first type of structure being imposed by the computer). They found a strong interaction effect: the leader alone or the computer structure helped groups achieve consensus, but groups that had to deal with both together did as poorly as groups that had neither. Two other studies (I ewis 1982; Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988) have found that computer-mediated communication tended to prevent any one individual from dominating the group process, yet neither study assigned leaders. The study described in this paper further expləred GDSS effects on assigned leaders and their attempts to influence the group process.

A final variable, anonymitv, ıs defined as the inability of group members to correctly identify the source of messages they receive, and the destination of any messages they send. Anonymity is possible in both GDSS and manual group meetings, but anonymity in manual situations tends to be highly artificial.

Anonymity has some interesting implications for GDSS use. According to theories of deindividuation, individuals behave in ways that are outside of the realms of socially prescribed behavior when social cues are removed (Zimbardo 1969). The computer-mediated communication literature has found evidence of deindividuation in some forms of computer-mediated communication (cf. Kiesler, Siegel and McGuire 1984; Siegel et al. 1986). The most extreme form of such behavior takes the form of “flaming," which is hypothesized to be due in part to the removal of social cues. Interacting anonymously via a computer may remove some social cues, resulting in behavior that would not occur under other circumstances. This relaxation of social cues in anonymous GDSS groups was reported by Nunamaker, Applegate and Konsynski (1987) and in four experimental studies of anonymity and GDSS conducted at the University of Arizona. Anonymous GDSS groups consistently were found to generate more critical comments than identified GDSS groups (Jessup, Connolly and Galegher 1989; Connolly, Jessup and Valacich 1990; Valacich, Dennis and Nunamaker 1989). Jessup and Tansik (in press) found that anonymous, face-toface groups generated the highest number of critical comments. In one study (Connolly, Jessup and Valacich 1990), the increased criticalness of anonymous groups was associated with increased performance (more unique ideas generated), but there were no differences in performance in the other studies.

The removal of social cues also allows a group to divorce statements from their authors, allowing them to consider the merits of an idea rather than the merits of its author, hopefully leading to better outcomes. Anonymous GDSS interaction may also insulate group members from the conformity pressures that accompany group interaction (Asch 1951, Milgram 1977). Group members may be able to reach their own conclusions on an issue without being pressured to conform.

## 3.2. Dependent Variables

Previous experiments that have compared GDSS use to manual group meetings have tended to look at the same dependent variables. Reder and Conklin's model (1987) suggests three classes of outcomes resulting from GDSS effects on communication medium: decision outcomes, process outcomes, and message features. The relevant studies and their findings are discussed in this section and listed in Table 1 for reference.'

Decision outcomes are really measures of performance. Performance is one of the most important variables in cross-media comparisons for theoretical and practical reasons. Theoretically, it fits within the communication paradigm for cross-media comparisons outlined by Reder and Conklin (1987). Groups are involved in performing certain tasks using various media, and it is possible to predict the outcomes on a given task, which depend on the medium used. Practically, performance is very important. Designing, building, operating and maintaining a GDSS is extremely expensive. Given the expense, it is unlikely anyone would use such a system if it did not lead to increased group performance. Three measures of decision outcome performance are deciston quality, number of unıque alternatives generated, and consensus Some tasks have correct answers, so one measure of group performance is how the group's final decision compares to the best possible decisions. These tasks are called “intellective" by McGrath (1984). Other tasks, such as those called “creativity tasks" by McGrath, have no correct answer. The successful completion of creativity tasks may be measured by the quantity of unique or creative ideas generated, so another measure of how well groups do on a task is the number of unique alternatives they generate.

Across a large number of studies, the use of a GDSS appears to lead to better quality decisions (Steeb and Johnston 1981; Lewis 1982; Turoff and Hiltz 1982; Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson 1988; Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988; Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988). Three studies, however, have found GDSS groups to reach decisions that were no better than groups without computer support (Ruble 1984, Beauclair 1987, A. Easton 1988). All nine of these studies used tasks that could be classified as intellective, except for Ruble's.² In Ruble's experiment, the task was a multi-period business game, where the best performing group was determined through a series of financial ratios at the end of the game. In addition, group members in Ruble's experiment had no direct contact with the DSS that they were using for support; instead, they used the DSS through an intermediary. Having to work as a group through an intermediary may have produced enough process losses to erase any process gains from using an automated support tool

Beauclair had four treatments in her experiment. In one, subjects used only automated tools. In a second, they used only manual tools. In the third and fourth treatments, subjects used one automated and one manual tool. Beauclair's experiment can be viewed as a study of the presence or absence of two automated tools in a GDSS setting, as well as a comparison of GDSS and manual groups. That no effects were found seems more of a comment on the two tools than on GDSS vs. no computer support. A. Easton found that structure, whether automated or not, led to better quality decisions, but the structure supplied by GDSS was no better than that supplied by the equivalent manual process. The task in the tenth study (Watson, De-Sanctis and Poole 1988) was preference allocation, which would fall under McGrath's (1984) category of mixed motive tasks.

In some situations, especially in creativity or idea generation tasks, the number of unique alternatives generated can also be used as a measure of performance. One of the key rules of brainstorming as described by Osborn is the more unique ideas, the better (Osborn 1957). Diehl and Stroebe (1987) cite 18 studies of brainstorming performance which use the quantity of nonredundant ideas as a measure of group productivity. In addition, they use the same measure in the four experiments of their own that they report. Among the papers reviewed here, three have measured either the number of unique alternatives generated or the number of unique alternatives used. All three studies found GDSS groups to generate or use more unique alternatives than manual groups (Steeb and Johnston 1981; Lewis 1982; Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson 1988).

Finally, agreement of the group on their outcome—consensus—is also a measure of their performance. Consensus has been more of a concern in the related literature of computer-mediated communication (cf. Fowler and Wackerbarth 1980, Johansen 1977, Rice 1984). One GDSS study that looked at consensus found no relationship between the decision aid used by GDSS, free interacting and manual groups, and post-meeting consensus (Watson, DeSanctis and Poole 1988). The same study found, however, that pre-meeting consensus was positively related to post-meeting consensus for GDSS and manual groups, indicating that the structure provided to these groups allowed them to find their pre-meeting consensus levels and build upon them for post-meeting consensus. Turoff and Hiltz (1982), however, found GDSS groups less likely to reach consensus. In both studies, group members were asked to agree to a ranking of items, even though each individu.al group member may not have been completely satisfied with the overall rankings. This concept of consensus, in which acceptance rather than unanimity is a goal, is based on the work of Hall and Watson (1970).

Three process outcome variables—time to decision, participation, and satisfaction with the proces s—have been studied in the computer-mediated communication literature since its inception (e.g.. see Steeb and Johaston 1981). All three involve specific medium features, and all three are of both theoretical and practical importance. Computer-mediated communication may take more time, because typing takes longer than talking (Hiltz, Johnson and Turoff 1986). Practically, in what is perceived as today's crisıs-driven corporate environment, groups may not be willing to use a GDSS if it takes too long, even if it migh: lead to a better decision. In general, GDSS groups take longer to reach a decision than free interacting or manual groups (Steeb and Johnston 1981; Gallupe, DeSanctıs and Dickson 1988), although one study found no differences (Beauclair 1987). A. Easton (1988) found structuresupported groups to take longer to reach a decison than groups not supported by structure.

Computer-mediated communication encourages more equal participation among group members, because some social cues are removed that might prevent some members from participating. Further, all group members can type at the same time, and thus participate, rather than having only one person talk (and thus participate) at a time (Hiltz, Johnson and Turoff 1986; Nunamaker, Applegate and Konsynski 1987; DeSanctıs and Gallupe 1987). This could aid groups tremendously, as they could get valuable input they might not otherwise get. On the other hand, increased participation could also lead to increased noise.

Typically, GDSS groups have been found to oarticipate more equally in the problem solving process than their free interacting or manual counterparts (Lewis 1982; Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988). On the other hand, four studies have found no differences in particıpation rates between GDSS and free interacting groups (Beauclair 1987; Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson 1988; Watson, DeSanctis and Poole 1988; Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988). A. Easton found structure-supported groups participated more equally than those without structure, but there were no differences between groups with GDSS structure and those with manual structure. These differences in findings could be attributable to the particular GDSS used in each experiment. Some GDSS are designed to emphasize the electronic channel of communication, allowing all group members to communic ate simultaneously in the electronic mode. Others emphasıze the verbal channel of communication, so that factors that prevent equal participation in free interacting or manual group meetings, such as hierarchical authority and individual traits such as shyness, continue to work against equal rates of participation. On the other hand. these differences may not be due so much to individual GDSS as they are to other pertinent factors, such as task, group characteristics, or measures used.

There is currently no theory concerning satisfaction and computer-mediated communication, and in fact, the empirical findings have been equivocal. Two types of satisfaction have been studied in GDSS experiments: satisfaction with the process, and satisfaction with outcomes. The evidence on satisfaction with the process is contradictory. One study found GDSS groups to be more satisfied (Steeb and Johnston 1981), two found GDSS groups to be less satisfied (Watson, DeSanctis and Poole 1988; Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson 1988), and two studies found no differences (Lewis 1982; Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988). A. Easton (1988) found that groups using structured approaches were more satisfied with process than groups without structure.

Although the findings on satisfaction with outcomes are also inconsistent, they appear to lean more toward increased satisfaction for GDSS groups (Steeb and Johnston 1981, A. Easton 1988). Only Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson (1988) found those using GDSS to be less satisfied with outcomes than non-GDSS groups. Beauclair (1987) found no differences, Practically, if people don't like something, they are less likely to use it. Thus, even if a GDSS might produce better decisions, if no one uses it because they don't like it, it won't be useful

The last class of outcomes-message features—-includes uninhibited behavior. Kiesler and colleagues (Kiesler, Siegel and McGuire 1984; Siegel et al. 1986) have found considerable evidence of “flaming" in computer-mediated communication, again probably as a function of the removal of inhibiting social cues. Inhibition has not been a concern in experimental GDSS studies, but like consensus, it has been a concern in the computer-mediated communication literature (see especially Siegel, Dubrovsky, Kiesler and McGuire 1986). The underlying empirical question is whether the use of a GDSS, especially one in which communication may be carried on anonymously, can relax social inhibitions, and what effects that might have on process and decision outcomes. In broader terms, very little is known about the comparative characteristics of messages in manual and GDSS meetings. Only one published study (Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988) has rigorously studied message content in GDSS.

## 3.3. Summary of GDSS Experimental Results

Based on the 10 studies in Table 1, all of which were controlled experiments, several general conclusions can be made regarding the results of GDSS use across several different tasks, GDSS, and groups. Compared to manual or free interacting group meetings, it appears that GDSS use leads to better quality decisions, the generation or use of more alternatives, longer times to decision, and a more even rate of participation among group members. It may be that GDSS use is less likely to promote consensus and more likely to facilitate social inhibition than manual or free interacting group meetings, but the empirical evidence on each of these variables is scant at this time. The empirical evidence on group satisfaction with the process and with the outcomes of GDSS meetings varies widely, with about as many studies finding that GDSS users were more satisfied as those finding GDSS users were less satisfied.

The findings on decision quality, the number of alternatives generated, time to decision, and participation can be explained in terms of the Reder and Conklin (1987) model (Figure 1). Despite varying group and task characteristics considered in the studies reviewed, the use of the GDSS medium led to fairly consistent results for these four variables. This seems to indicate that GDSS use is a powerful intervening variable between group and task characteristics, and decision and process outcomes. In other words, at least for the tasks and group characteristics investigated in the studies reviewed, GISS use tends to result in better quality decisions, more alternatives to consider, longer time to decision, and riore equal participation. The inconsistent findings on satisfaction can also be explained in terms of the model. The variance in satisfaction can be explained as being a direct function of the task and/or group characteristics The intervention of GDSS use was not enough to overcome the strong effects of the group and the task on s¿tisfaction. As for the role of message features, unfortunately, there is not enough reported evidence on message content in GDSS studies to determine if differences in outcome variables can be attributed to the intervention of message features.

## 5. Research Methodology

## 5.1. Design

The ideal design for the experiment was a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design, with three independent variables: communication medium, leadership, anonymity. Normally there would be eight cells in such a design, but because of the perceived artificiality of anonymous communication in non-GDSS groups, it was not included. This eliminated two cells from the design, leaving six experimental treatments.

Group size was six for all groups. This is a larger group size than has typically been used in other GDSS experiments, which have generally used groups of size three or four. There is evidence to suggest that GDSS technology may be more effective for larger groups (George, Nunamaker and Vogel 1988). DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) suggest that, “Because large groups experience more dramatic communication difficulties, group decision support systems may have a more positive impact in large groups" (p. 598). One other published laboratory study (Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988) has used larger groups.

Because group members were randomly assigned to treatments, groups had no past history of working together as groups, even though some of the group members knew each other. This situation resembles initial task force and committee meetings in organizations. All groups had to solve the same task. The structure they used to work on the task was identical across all groups.

## 5.2. Task

The task was the “Parkway Drug Company" case (Greenhalgh). In it, a sales territory has just come open due to retirement, and two salesmen are competing for it. One believes he should get it because he has the most seniority of any salesman in the company. The other believes he should get it because he had the best sales of any salesman last year. As with many business cases, this case is full of both useful and irrelevant information. Subjects were asked to act as a committee of consultants that had been called in to solve the problem for the company.

Subjects were first asked to generate as many possible solutions to the case as they could. They were then asked to select the best single solution. The task, then, has two parts. Using McGrath's task circumplex, the first part of the task can be classified as a creativity task, and the second part can be classified as intellective. The case makes it clear that students are to find the solution that results in the highest level of sales for the company and the highest level of commissions for the salesmen.

## 5.3. Variables and Measures

The three independent variables were introduced into the experiment as either present or absent. Groups either used a GDSS or they did not. For manual groups, all interaction was through traditional verbal and non-verbal channels. For GDSS groups, all discussion and voting was done through the electronic channel. Some verbal communication was allowed during the preparation of the ballot used for voting, but only because of software limitations At no other time were participants in the GDSS treatments allowed to talk. This may seem an odd manipulation, but past research has noted that groups working through networked workstations produce fewer verbal remarks than those in conventional meetings (Nunamaker, Applegate and Konsynski 1987. Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988). Our own experiences with GroupSystems have confirmed this behavior. For assigned leadership, there was either an assigned Leader or there was not. Following from the methodology used by Barefoot and Strickland (1982), one member of each group was randomly selected to be the leader. I.eaders were instructed as follows (quoting from the experimenter's script): “Your responsibilities include helping he group generate possible solutions to the case problem, focusing the discussion, and promoting consensus." No specific leadership training was provided, and leadership behavior was not enforced by the researchers. Leaders in GDSS groups, like other group members, were not allowed to talk. Their attempts at exercising influence had to be made through the computer. For anonymity, the GDSS was either Anonvmo,s or it was not. To ensure anonymity in the GDSS facility, group members sat with two chairs between them and the next person on either side. In this way, no participant could see the computer monitor of any other person in the room.

There were seven dependent variables. Decision quality for each group's decision was measured as follows. Solutions reached by groups could be assigned a dollar value reflecting the joint benefit offered by the solution to the disputants. If a group reached complete consensus, then their solution was assigned a score representing its rank among possible solutions. If the group did not reach complete consensus, then each final solution generated by group member, was assigned a score. Each score was then multiplied by the number of group members voting for that solution, and the product was added to products calculated for cach of the other solutions. The final sum was then divided by six, the number of people in the group. This number represented the average solution reached by the group.

The number of untque alternatives generated was determined by counting the number of alternatives on the ballot for the first vote. All non-unique alternatives the group had generated up to this point were eliminated during the ballot preparation process. Consensus was measured as either the presence or absence of complete (100%) agreement. We measured consensus this way because we wanted groups to work toward unanimous agreement with a single solution thev thought best for the intellective part of the task. We wanted them to reach agreement on a single solution; we did not want them to rank order the set of solutons that had been generated. Our operationalization of consensus diverged, therefore, from that developed by Hall and Watson (1970).

T'tme to deczsion was measured as the amount of time it took the group to reach complete consensus or the total time allotted (30 minutes) if time ran out before complete consensus was reached

Sattsfaction was measured using a 16-item scale included in a post-test questionnaire. Cronbach's alpha for the scale was 0.82, indicating acceptable reliability of measurement.

Before participation could be measured, the transcripts from the sessions had to be analyzed so that the number of remarks made by each participant could be determined. The coding scheme used was based on that developed by Siegel, Dubrovsky, Kiesler and McGuire (1986). According to this scheme, each remark must have a subject, verb and object. The scheme is reasonably objective, so that only one of the authors coded the transcripts. A second author coded a sample of the transcripts, which compared favorably to the codings of the first author. Both authors had previously coded the transcripts generated during the pilot for this study, so both were very familiar with the coding scheme.

Each participant's rate of participation was considered to be the number of remarks s/he generated during the discussion part of the experimental process. For GDSS groups, all of those remarks were electronic; for manual groups, all remarks were verbal. Participation for the group was measured as the average relative standard deviation of group members' participation rates. This is a summary inequality measure directly proportional to the sample standard deviation of the proportions, where the measure varied between one (where only one group member participated) and zero (where all group members participated equally) (Siegel, Dubrovsky, Kiesler and McGuire 1986; Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988).

Extent ofuninhıbited behavior was measured as the total number of group remarks which included swear words, name-calling or insults (Siegel, Dubrovsky, Kiesler and McGuire 1986).

## 5.4. Research Setting

The experiment was conducted in what DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) refer to as a “decision room," and in an adjoining traditional conference room. The decision room held the GDSS, and all GDSS sessions were conducted there. The conference room was the site of all manual group sessions.

The decision room is equipped with 16 microcomputers, linked together with a local area network (LAN). The microcomputers are situated on a U-shaped table, with a chair for each microcomputer, and at the open end of the U is a public screen. A large screen projection system linked to the network permits the display of information intended for the entire group on the public screen. The software used was GroupSystems, developed at the University of Arizona (Nunamaker, Applegate and Konsynski 1987).³ Specific software tools used were Electronic Brainstorming (EBS), Issue Analyzer (IA) and Voting/Ranking. EBS is an electronic version of brainstorming (Osborn 1957). Each participant enters comments into a file via a microcomputer and transmits them onto the computer network in exchange for a file of ideas from another participant, to which the participant can add more comments. The EBS tool elicits one comment at a time from each participant.4 IA is a tool used for the identification and consolidation of issues generated during EBS sessions. IA was used to prepare ballots for voting. The Voting tool allows group members to privately vote on the list of issues generated by IA. For this experiment, subjects were allowed to vote for only one possible solution, although the Voting tool can be configured so that participants can rank order their choices. The software was controlled by a facilitator, who sat to the right of the public screen. The facilitator also ran the experimental sessions.

At the time the study was conducted. during the winter of 1987–1988, other software tools were in development, but the tools used-—EBS, IA and Voting—were the only ones readily available that fit the particular needs of the experimental task. EBS was chosen because it was specifically designed to support idea generation, which was the point of the creativity aspect of the experimental task. Voting was chosen because it supported the choice aspect of the intellective task and because it allowed a group to see how distant thev were from consensus. lA was used because it provided the necessary bridge from idea generation in EBS to idea choice in Voting.

The conference room is a traditional meeting room with a large table in the center surrounded by chairs. Participants sat around three sides of the table, to approximate seating in the decision room. They had a flip chart in place of the public screen.

The GDSS decision room and the traditional conference room are similarly decorated to simulate executive meeting rooms in actual organizations.

## 5.5. Subjects

The six cell research design calied for 30 groups of six subjects each, or 180 total subjects. The subjects were upper division undergraduates in the MIS department, so all had a working familiarity with microcomputers and GDSS. The mean age was 23.0)9 years (41% women, 59% men). Students participated in the experiment in fulfillment of a course assignment.

## 5.6. Procedures

Subjects signed up for sessions at times that were convenient for them. This was done across several classes involved in providing subjects, and particular time slots were rarely filled in a single class. In addition, the treatments were randomly assigned to scheduled sessions. These procedures promoted random constitution of groups across all sessions.

The processes for GDSS and manual groups were structured to be as identical as possible. Upon arrival at the experimental site, group members were read instructions by the person conducting the session, and they were then given the case materials and 15 minutes to read them. The group then discussed the problem for 20 minutes. At the end of that time, group members were asked to supply up to three possible solutions each. These were publicly displayed. The group was asked to vote on the solutions, each member having only one vote. If the group reached consensus on the first vote, then the experiment was stopped If not, the votes not receiving any support were discarded and the group was asked to discuss the problem for 10 additional minutes and vote again. This procedure was followed until the group reached complete consensus or until 30 minutes had elapsed. At this time, subjects were given a questionnaire to complete and were debriefed. The pilot studies that preceded the experiment demonstrated that 30 minutes of additional discussion and voting was enough for all types of groups to complete the ·ask.

In the GDSS sessions, EBS was used for discussion, IA for listing and consolidating possible solutions, and Voting for each vote. All electronic interaction was recorded by the system in the process of using it. In the manual sessions, all discussion was verbal, and the listing of solutions and voting were done with the aid of the flip chart. ## 6.2. Interaction Effects

There were two unexpected interaction effects: for Leadership and Anonymity on satisfaction, and for Leadership and Communication Medium condition on participation. Anonymous GDSS groups with assigned leaders, as well as those that were non-anonymous and had no assigned leaders, were significantly more satisfied with the decision process. The treatment means for satisfaction (GDSS only), on a scale of 16 (very dissatisfied) to 80 (very satisfied), were as follows: anonymous with leader, 63.8; non-anonymous with no leader, 62.3; anonymous with no leader, 56.63; nonanonymous with leader, 59.57. Also, manual groups with an assigned leader, and GDSS groups with no assigned leader, had more equal rates of participation than manual groups with no assigned leader and GDSS groups with assigned leaders, respectively. The treatment means for participation (non-anonymous only), on a scale of 0 to 1, were as follows: manual with leader, 0.070; manual with no leader, 0.117; GDSS with leader, 0.033; GDSS with no leader, 0.027.

## 7. Discussion

Table 4 summarizes the statistically significant findings. The findings are partially consistent with findings across many previous GDSS experiments. Use of the GDSS in this experiment led to more equal rates of participation among group members, and it took longer for GDSS groups to reach a decision. As discussed in §3.3, these findings seem to hold across several GDSS studies, even though different studies have used different GDSS, different size groups, and different tasks. In terms of the Reder and Conklin model (Figures 1 and 2), the intervention of GDSS use, across several GDSSs, tends to overcome differences in tasks and in group characteristics to facilitate more equal rates of participation and to require a longer time to reach a decision.

Participation seems to be clearly linked to GDSS design. Using a GDSS, all group members can participate at once, with the contributions of all members given legitimate consideration. This can be thought of as human parallel processing (Gray 1988). In the manual groups, on the other hand, one or two members contribute disproportionately, primarily because no aspects of the manual group process impede their ability to control the flow of a group process. Other group members are much less involved in the process, making the participation rates uneven.

Time to decision is closely related to the abılity of a group to reach consensus. It was predicted that GDSS groups would be less likely to reach consensus, and the prediction was supported. In the experimental manual group sessions, a natural leader would often emerge (Bass 1954, Hemphill 1961). This individual began to take charge of the process early on in the proceedings. The emergent leader pushed for his/her preferred solution to the problem. While forceful leadership did not always lead to group consensus on the emergent leader's position, many times it did. On the other hand, any attempts at emergent leadership in the GDSS groups were apparently muted by the use of the GDSS. The GDSS made it more difficult for an individual to control the flow of the group process, and to push for agreement on a preferred solution in a short amount of time. These findings are consistent with those of Strickland et al. (1978).

The other consistent findings across several GDSS experiments, that GDSS use tends to lead to better quality decisions and to the generation of more alternatives, were also predicted here, but found no support The task may not have been difficult enough for the process gains associated with GDSS to have taken effect. Gallupe and colleagues (1988) showed that GDSS were better for more difficult problems but were actually an impediment for problems that were not difficult.

Main effects were predicted for both of the other independent variables, assigned leadership and anonvmity, but none were found. Having an assigned leader, which provided another layer of structure to the group process, had no effect on any of the dependent variables, neither those for which specific relationships were predicted (decision quality, consensus, time to decision), nor any other. Since leadership behavior was not enforced, the lack of findings could be due to assigned leaders not doing as they were asked, but a review of the transcripts from the experimental sessions reveals that assigned leaders did indeed attempt to leao the group process, as instructed. The lack of a finding regarding decision quality can be seen as reassuring in the face of findings by Turoff and Hiltz (1982), in which combining an assigned leader with a GDSS led to low quality decisions. Here at least, having an assigned leader was not associated with lower quality decisions

Since there was a facilitator present in each treatment, those treatments with an assigned leader actually had two process leader. This will be a problem in any GDSS run by a facilitator where the GDSS is being used by a group with a formal leader where the facilitator has one mission (to get the groups successfully through the process) and the formal leader has another (to get the group to consensus). In some situations, the two leaders may have overlapping missions, and the results of such situations are not clear. Additional research is alled for to better understand the role of leadership in GDSS.

That anonymity had no effect on the dependent variables in GDSS situations might indicate how lıtle is actually known about the role of anonymity in GDSS. If anonymity could play a role anywhere in the GDSS process, it is reasonable to assume it would be in promoting the breakdown of social inhibitions, which we predicted, but which apparently did not occur. Anonymous GDSS groups generated just as few uninhibited comments as non-anon ymous GDSS groups. The anonymity may not have been enough, however, to successfully mute existing social cues. The fact that there were always other people in the room (other group members and the experimenter/facilitator) may have induced individuals instead to focus on social cues.

Carver and Scheier (1985) have theorized about two aspects of self-awareness, the private self and the public self. The private self is covert, hidden and inaccessible to others. The public self, on the other hand, is social, overtly displayed to others, and is intimately tied to relationships with others. A person's actions are partially determined by that aspect of the self on which he or she is focused. Past research, cited by Carver and Scheier, has demonstrated that attention to the private or public self can be induced: mirrors focus attention on the private self, an audience focuses attention on the public self. In the experiments conducted by Kiesler and colleagues, subjects were alone. This may have induced them to focus on the private self, making them more willing to type what they felt, regardless of what others might have thought. In our experiment, there was always an audience, which may have induced subjects to focus on the public self and hence on their relationships with the group. Subjects would be attempting to make good impressions in their interactions with others, minimizing flaming. This suggests that geographically-dispersed and same-room GDSS groups might experience very different levels of anonymity, with differing effects. These different levels of anonymity might exist even where senders and receivers of messages are identified. The work by Kiesler and colleagues indicates that some degree of anonymity exists in such situations, when participants are dispersed and isolated. There may be some degree of overlap, then, between our communication medium and anonymity manipulations.

Two interaction effects were found in this study. One was due to the interaction between leadership and anonymity on satisfaction. Anonymous groups with leaders, and non-anonymous groups without leaders, were the most satisfied with the group process. Anonymous groups without leaders and non-anonymous groups with leaders were less satisfied. Anonymous groups with leaders may have been happier with the process because the leader provided them with some direction. In the opposite situation, where the group knew who made each comment, an assigned leader and the additional structure s/he provided may have gotten in the way of the process. Identified groups without a leader were happier about the process.

The other interaction effect was due to the interaction between leadership and communication medium on participation. When a manual group had an assigned leader, and when a GDSS group had no leader, their participation rates were more equal. Manual groups without a leader and GDSS groups with a leader were more likely to have unequal participation rates. This finding makes intuitive sense. Assigned leaders in manual group situations attempted to manage the process. In such situations, each group member was given more chance to participate, as assigned leaders tended to ask others in the group for their ideas and comments. No one was there to do such things when there was no assigned leader. In the GDSS situation, the assigned leader's attempts to manage the process may have led to a disproportionate number of comments on his/her part, making participation in those groups more unequal. It should be noted, however, that participation rates in GDSS groups with leaders were more equal than those for manual groups with assigned leaders.

In evaluating the results of this study, it may be important to realize that there can be no “pure" test of GDSS effects. Any research involving GDSS necessarily tests not only GDSS in general, but also GDSS as operationalized in a particular system's software and hardware. For instance, differences in software design may account for why some GDSS are good at fostering idea generation, but not good at helping groups reach consensus (as may have occurred with this software and this task). Fit between software and task also may explain why past research findings are inconclusive with regards to participant satisfaction. More work is needed to identify task types and their relationship to software design considerations.

It is important to continue the study of GDSS in the laboratory, but it must also be pursued in the field. GDSS are beginning to be implemented in organizations now, and the results of their use in “real world" situations may differ remarkably from the results of their use in the laboratory, as has been demonstrated in our studies ofGDSS use inside IBM (Nunamaker et al. 1989). These studies have found the use of GDSS to reduce the time necessary for completing sev eral different projects, by an average factor of 55%. In addition, the levels of user satisfaction were found to be uniformly high. As differences such as these are found, thev need to be explained and reconciled. Such a process can help us improve the design of GDSS and better understand their effects on group processes and outcomes.\*
