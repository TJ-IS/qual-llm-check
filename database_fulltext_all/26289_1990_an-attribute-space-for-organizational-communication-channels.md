Robert W. Zmud

College of Business

Mary R. Lind

Forrest W. Young

The primary objective of this study was to identify the perceptual dimensions used by 158 managers and their professional staff at a single large manufacturing firm in differentiating fourteen distinct communication channels available in the firm. Six candidate criteria for differentiating these channels were examined (channel accessibility, information quality, immediate feedback, cue variety, personalization, and receiver accessibility) using multidimensional scaling. A secondary objective involved assessing whether communication direction influenced perceptions. Responses were obtained for two intraorganizational communication directions: lateral and downward. Results indicated that these individuals applied a perceptual framework involving three dimensions: information feedback, accessibility, and quality. Further, a perspective shift from the “message sender" to the “message receiver" was observed in moving from lateral to downward communication. The observations of directional differences demonstrate the inappropriateness of either ignoring communication direction in research designs and of directly transferring research models and instruments that pertain to one communication direction to another direction. Taken together, these results may prove helpful in developing a richer theoretical basis for exploring task/media relationships, which in turn may lead to future research findings providing recommendations for improving individual and organizational performance.

he act of organizing occurs through communication whereby information is exchanged and meaning fabricated (Katz and Kahn 1966). As such, organizations can be viewed as communication systems, in which networks of nodes (individuals, groups, departments, etc.) are linked with one another in a variety of ways and linked with elements in their external environments (Tichy et al. 1979). Such a perspective is consistently supported through ernpirical studies which show that managers spend much of their tıme in inter- and intra-organizational communication (Kurke and Aldrich 1983, Rice and Bair 1984 Jones and McLeod 1986).

An organization's communication nodes are interconnected through a variety of channels: face-to-face interactions, group mectings, telephone, memos, letters, reports, etc. The nature of the communication channel used in transmitting information can affect a receiver's interpretation of' the information (Randolph 1978, O'Reilly and Pondy 1979, Daft et al. 1987). While organizational communication exchanges have traditionally involved face-to-face interactions (O'Reilly and Pondy 1979), such channels can be expensive or impossible, for geographic or temporal reasons, undesirable- -the variety of socioemotional cues that arise in face-to-face communication can have dysfunctional effects (Rice 1987)—or ill-advised (regulations may require that certain matters be in writing). And, while individuals may prefer to use particular channels, and while specific channels may have inherent advantages in certain situations, it is often contextual (O'Reilly and Pondy 1979. McKenney et al. 1986, Fulk et al. 1987, Daft et al. 1987, Markus 1987, Rice et al. 1990) or economic (Rice 1987) factors that most directly influence channel use.

An assumption behind much of the research on channel use is that both organization behaviors and organizational designs will be rnore effective when individuals use channels congruent with task demands (Rice 1980, Daft and Lengel 1984, 1986, Rice 1984, Svenning and Ruchinskas 1984, Steinfield 1986, Culnan and Markus 1987, Fulk et al. 1987). Tolay, with many organizations investing, or considering investing. substantial resources in computer-mediated communication systems (e.g., electronic mail, voice messaging, teleconferencing, etc.), more insight is needed regarding their appropriateness vis-a-vis more traditional communication media (Culnan and Markus 1987). One avenue of communication research lies in uncovering the criteria that individuals perceive as differentiating available communication channels. Not only does such knowledge help in developing a theoretical base for the research exploring communication task/media relationshıps, but Daft and Lengel (1984, 1986) suggest that individuals who are aware of their criteria for selecting channels are more likely to match channel usage with task requirements and, hence, perform better.

A number of theoretical perspectives have been advanced to explain communication media choice decisions. Three such perspectives are those emphasizing rational choice (Short et al. 1976, Daft and Lengel 198-4, 986), critical mass (Markus 1987, Rice et al. 1990), and social choice (Fulk et a . 1987). While recognizing that rich explanatory models of media use are likely to contain elements of all three perspectives, attention here focuses on rational choice cognitions. According to this perspective, individuals select a medium based on it: suitability for a given communication task.

Such cognitions are also relevant to the critical mass and social choice perspectives. A main idea behind the critical mass perspective is that potential users of a communications medium will not use the medium until a sufficient number of their key communication partners also use it. When a new communication medium is introduced into an organization, the behaviors of ealy adopters in championing or sponsoring the medium are critical; and such behaviors are likely to be influenced by these individuals' perceptions of the relative advantages of a new medium. Likewise, a main idea behind the social choice perspective is that an individual's communication behaviors tend to imitate the behaviors of valued others. But, why do these “valued others" use a particular medium? Very likely because of their perceptions of a medium's advantages relative to other available media.

Prior studies examining individuals' perceptions of communication media invariably present respondents with researcher-specified channel attributes. Most often, the choice of which attributes to include is not grounded in strong theory, but rather, reflects those attributes used in prior studies as well as aspects of the particular communication phenomena being examined. One possible explanation for this lack of a strong theoretical base is the inadequate understanding that currently exists regarding how individuals cognitively differentiate among alternative communication channels. The primary objective of this study is to explore this cognitive structure and, hence, to add to our understanding of communication media.

A secondary objective is to examine the influence of communication direction on managerial attributions of communication channels, an issue previously addressed by Randolph (1978) and Sproull and Kiesler (1986). Related research has found that power and status effects do produce differences in vertical communication behaviors (Jones et al. 1963, Slobin et al. 1968). Upward, downward, horizontal and diagonal communication all occur within organizations, and message type and purpose differ to some extent across these directions (O'Reilly and Pondy 1979). Such findings and related arguments (Culnan and Bair 1983, Culnan and Markus 1987) prompt us to conjecture that individuals are, in fact, likely to apply differentiated schema when comparing communication channels across different communication directions.

This study thus examines attributions of fourteen traditional and computer-mediated communication channels by managers and their professional staff. First, a review of the relevant literatures identifies channel attributes likely to serve as important criteria influencing individuals' rational choice-processes regarding channel selection. Then, the methodology and findings of the study are described. Finally, the paper discusses the implications of these findings and recommends directions for future research.

## Identifying Candidate Channel Attributes

While prior examinations of communication channel use have focused on specific attributes of these channels, no overarching framework exists that provides insight into those attributes used by individuals in differentiating among available communication channels. In developing such a framework, it is important to recognize that all channels possess both communication strengths and weaknesses (Picot et al. 1982, Daft and Lengel 1986). But, what are the channel attributes most representative of these strengths and weaknesses? More importantly, can a relatively small yet robust set of such attributes be identified? Three related streams of research suggest candidate attributes: those examining information acquisition, information richness or social presence, and new computer-mediated channels

## Information Acquisition

It is generally accepted that uncertainty threatens rational action and that organizational members seek to reduce or cope with uncertainty by acquiring information. Individuals in their use of information, however, do not act under perfect rationality but are subject to the constraints imposed by organizational norms, the availability of information, and individual cognitive processing limitations (Ungson et al. 1981,

O'Reilly 1983). Also, individuals show tendencies toward selective perception, whereby the information sought is largely a function of the individuals' existing mental maps (Kiesler and Sproull 1982, Daft and Weick 1984). Consequently, research examining organızational information processing has begun to examine the processes by which organizational members acquire information (Allen 1977, O'Reilly 1983), as well as how they use inform.ation once it has been acquired.

Under norms of ratonality, it would be expected that individuals would acquire information on the basis of its quality, where quality refers to its relevance, accuracy, reliability, and timeliness (Zmud 1978). However, it has been observed that, rather than using information perceived to be of the highest quality, individuals tend to use information characterized by high accessibility (Allen 1977, Hardy 1982, O'Reilly 1982, Culnan 1983, Rice and Shook 1986). Subsequent research by Culnan (1984. 1985) suggests that accessibility refers to difficulties in both physically accessing a source of information and acquiring information from the source once physical access has been gained. Swanson (1987) has refined these ideas in his channel disposition model by distinguishing the quality of the information provided through a particular information channel and the individual's capability to access that channel. Applying such a view, two candidate attributes for differentiating communication channels thus are channel accessibility and infò rmation quality.

## Information Richness/Social Presence

As dıscussed above, a basic proposition in organizational theory holds that organizations and their members process information n order to reduce uncertainty. Building on the work of Weick (1979) and Daft and Weick (1984). Daft and Lengel (1984, 1986) expanded this view of organizational information processing by suggesting a second objective: that of reducing equivocality. Uncertainty presumes that an individual possesses a schema for interpreting environnental signals but lacks sufficient data to predict forthcoming outcomes or events. Equivocality, on the other hand, presumes either the lack of a schema or the existence of multiple, possibly conflicting, schema.

Individuals reduce equivocality by defining/refining the schema applied in interpreting a situation. Daft and Lengel (1984) argue that high equivocality is best reduced by allowing for rapid information cycles among individuals with few imposed rules, such that debate. schema clarification, and schema enrichment occur. Communication that overcomes different frames of reference or that clarifies ambiguous issues is thus termed “rich." Similar arguments were used by Short et al. (1976) in characterizing the extent to which communicaton media possess “social presence"; i.e., allow for cues which incorporate a sense of the social context within which communication acts occur

Communication channels are believed to vary in their capacity to promote rich communication. What characteristics are associated with rich communication? Using many of the notions initially introduced in the social presence theory of media which posits a complex interaction of technical capabilities, personal perceptions, and contextual characteristics (Short et al. 197t), Daft and Lengel (1984) suggested that the three main factors associated with rich communication media involved cue variety, immediate feedback, and message personalization. A channel with high cue variety enables an individual to both make use of multiple cues and to use cues which possess a wide range ot meaning. A channel with high immediate feedback enables an individual to ask questions and instantly receive answers, thus enabling message meaning and appropriateness to be more quickly assessed and modified as necessary. A channel with high message personalization would enable an individual to finely tailor a message being transmitted to best fit the recipient, the situation, or both. Three additional candidate attributes thus arise: cue variety, feedback, and message personalization.

## New Computer-Mediated Channels

Technological developments and improvements over the last decade have introduced a number of computer-mediated channels, such as electronic mail, voice messaging, and teleconferencing. Descriptions of these computer-mediated channels as well as their relative strengths and weaknesses can be found in Rice (1984) and Rogers (1986).

Most examinations of computer-mediated communication channels address the channel attributes discussed above. However, at least one additional candidate attribute can be identified (Rice and Shook 1986, Rice et al. 1990, Sproull and Kiesler 1986, Markus 1987, Culnan and Markus 1987): that of “potential access." Once a critical mass of an organization's members have physical access to and are prone to use a computer-mediated channel such as electronic mail and voice messaging, the potential exists to transmit a message to a large, yet selective, audience independent of the message receivers' relationship to the sender in terms of familiarity, geography or time zone separations. Thus, a final candidate attribute is receiver access.

## The Study

To investigate the perceptual dimensions used by individuals in differentiating among available communication channels, managers and professional staff working at the corporate headquarters of a Fortune 500 manufacturing firm responded to a questionnaire aimed at eliciting their perceptions of the following 14 communication channels:

face-to-face one-on-one consultations (formal) one-on-one chats (informal)

group group meetings (formal) group gatherings (informal)

written handwritten notes typed or printed memos or letters printed documents or reports charts and graphs computer reports

traditional phone communication voice conferencing technologies facsimile

computer-mediated electronic mail communication voice messaging technologies

These channels were jointly selected (and named) by the researchers and the study's sponsors within the manufacturing firm. Respondents were randomly selected from the non-clerical employees in each department (computer information systems, chemicals, engineering, personnel, purchasing research and development, sales and marketing, safety and securitv, technical services and transportation) and all were located at a single corporate campus.

It is clear that the respondents not only had access to these channels but that they used them. It can also be noticed from the t-tests comparing actual versus desired usage of each channel that quite favorable attitudes were held toward electronic media. This might be expected given the technical nature of these headquarters departments. Taken together, this information suggests that the “communications context" of this organization is appropriate for a research project concerned with respondents' perceptual distinctions among alternative communication channels.

Two additional aspects of the firm's communications context are noteworthy, as they suggest the possibility of particular biases in respondents' media perceptions (these observations arose from both interviews with senior executives and openended remarks made by respondents when asked to comment on their communications context). First, senior managers preferred voice messaging over electronic mail. Second, problems had been experienced with the recent installation of a new telephone system.

The study's secondary objective involved assessing the impact of communication direction on the respondent's channel attributions. However, sample size limitations enabled only two directions to be examined. Consultations with the study's sponsors resulted in the decision to examine lateral, e.g., peer-to-peer, and downward, e.g., superior-to-subordinate, communication. Of the 158 randomly selected respondents, 63 responded only for lateral communication, 64 only for downward communication, and 31 for both communication directions.

Given the study's primary objective of uncovering the schema applied by respondents in differentiating the fourteen communication channels, a multidimensional scaling (MDS) analysis was performed. With MDS, the investigator does not a priori determine the schema used by subjects in assessing stimuli. Instead, each subject independently indicates those aspects of the stimuli which frames their assessment. The MDS algorithm then arrives at a collective schema from the respondents, which must then be interpreted by the researcher.

Requiring respondents both to assess the similarity of 91 pairs of communication channels and to characterize each channel using 21 adjective-pairs was viewed by both the researchers and the study's sponsors as an exhausting task. In order to overcome this problem, the respondents' efforts were substantially reduced by randomly partitioning the 91 channel-pair combinations (into groups of 30, 30, and 31 pairs for the similarity/dissimilarity rating task) and the fourteen channels (into groups of 5, 5. and 4 channels for the attribute rating task). Such a partitioning strategy resulted in at least 30 randomly selected respondents in each adjective-pair/ channel subgroup. These respective data sets were then merged to form complete response matrices. Given that only 17 of the 1'5 individuals asked to complete the study's questionnaire failed to respond (a 90%, response rate), this procedure was apparently successful.

Multidimensional scaling (Kruskal and Wish 1978, Young and Hamer 1987) was performed to derive an MDS space for the fourteen communication channels. Specifically. the SAS ALSCAL procedure (Young and Lewyckyj 1979) was used to analyze the paired-comparison data for each conımunication direction using PROC ALSCAL's INDSCAI option (Carroll and Chang 1970). A skree plot analysis of goodness-of-fit measures for the lateral and downward communication directions suggested that 3-dimensional solutions were appropriate for both. Since these three dimensions for both MDS spaces were interpretable, these 3-dimensional structures are used for the remainder of the study (Schiffman et al. 1981). With the lateral direction, the $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values for a 4-dimensional structure are 0.402, 0.419, 0.439 and 0.448; with the downward direction, the $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values are 0.389, 0.399, 0.426 and 0.429. Note the dominating role served by the first dimension of both MDS spaces in discriminating perceptions of communication channels.

Nonmetric regression analyses (Carroll 1972) were run using the SAS PROC TRANSREG (Young and Kuhfeld 1987) procedure in order to fit the 21 adjectivepairs (e.g., stimuli attributes) within each of the MDS spaces (lateral and downward). When using nonmetric regression to fit attributes into an MDS space, Carroll (1982) and Hoffman and Perrault (1987) recommend that both vector and ideal point approaches be used. The vector approach, which is a special case of the more general ideal point approach, finds a direction through the scaling space which best corresponds to increasing amounts of an attribute. The vector, then, is a line through the space on which projections of the stimuli correspond as closely as possible with the attribute ratings of stimuli. The ideal point approach (so called because it dictates that some amount of the attribute in question is ideal) is represented by a point in the MDS space. The distance from the position of each stimulus to an attribute's point estimates the amount of the attribute in the stimulus: the shorter the distance, the greater the amount

With both the vector and point models, an attribute's multiple correlation coefficient indicates the accuracy of the estimated amount of the attribute in a stimulus. For technical reasons, the multiple correlation coefficient for the point model must always be the same or larger than the multiple correlation coefficient for the vector model. Thus, the point model can never less accurately describe the stimuli space than the vector model. There is a significance test which can be used to determine if the point model fits significantly better than the vector model. However, the significance levels of this test are overly optimistic, so the test must be used very conservatively. In our work, if the ideal point model fit better than the vector model at the 0.0001 level, we concluded that the attribute's ideal point model more accurately described the amount of attribute in each stimulus than the attribute's vector model. Otherwise, we concluded the vector model was superior. Tables 4 and 5 summarize this analysis and also provide projections of the attributes on each the three dimensions reflected in the MDS solutions.

## Dimension 1: Feedback

With lateral communication, the feedback attribute appears as a singular characterization of this first dimension. With downward communication, the feedback attribute again has the largest loading but is closely followed by other attributes which more fully characterize the nature (complex, objective, restricted, personal) of this feedback.

Interpretation: With downward communication, feedback is more intimately linked with the nature of the communication that occurs with a communicating partner than is the case with lateral communication.

## Dimension 2: Accessibility

With lateral communication, the dominant attributes are those typically associated with channel accessibility (dependabie, available, confidential, easy-touse). With downward communication, the dominant attributes seem to reflect an accessibility/receiver/situation interaction (personal, sensitive, public, visual, urgent convenient, hard, wide ranging)

Interpretation: Accessibility in lateral communication is viewed solely in terms of the channels themselves, while accessibility in downward communication is viewed in terms of gaining situational access to a particular receiver or to set of receivers.

## Dimension 3: Quality

With lateral communication, information quality seems to be characterized in terms of its accessibility (dependable, ambiguous, available, feedback). With downward communication, information quality seems to be characterized by more typical “quality" attributes (unreliable, inaccurate, wide ranging) and by a concern with the receiver (public, personal).

Interpretation: Getting access to the information seems to be a primary “information quality" concern with lateral communication. With downward communication, attention shifts to insuring that information is meaningfully transmitted to a receiver.

The MDS space for lateral communication constitutes a rather simple evaluative structure, whose emphasis seems to be on immediate feedback, channel accessibility and information accessibility. A more complex structure arises in downward communication, with issues such as “targeted" feedback, receiver accessibility and “tailored" information characterizing the evaluation framework. ## Discussion

This study's major objective was to explore the criteria used by managers and professional staff to differentiate fourteen conmunication channels. The prior research literature has tended to emphasize both channel accessibilitv and information quality, and both seem to be reflected in the evaluation frameworks constructed from these respondents' comparisons of the fourtecn communication channels. A third dimension was also identified: a communication channel's capacity to provide immediate feedback. The addition of this dimensio 1 confirms Rice's (1987) proposition regarding the salience of the feedback attribute and is important for at least two reasons. First, it merges the information richness construct with the larger body of literature on information acquisition and use. Second, it directly incorporates a twoactor orientation (feedback to or from another individual) to the information acquisition and use research stream. Both of these directions are likely to be essential elements of more comprehensive models explaining organizational information processing behaviors (Huber 1982, O'Reilly 1983, Swanson 1987).

The study's other main finding was that differentiated frames of reference do seem to have been used by respondents across lateral ind downward communication directions. These differences reflect that as attent on shifts from lateral to downward communication, the respondents' concerns shft as follows:

• from providing or obtaining feedback to targeting feedback

• from channel accessibility to receiver accessibility

• from information accessibılity to [a concern with] tailoring information It should be recognized that these distinctions are, at best, tentative, given the exploratory nature of this analysis. Still, they do seem intuitive and are consistent with related findings (Sproull and Kiesler 1986).

Finally, these directionality differences are useful in understanding the differences reflected in these respondents' appraisals of voice messaging and electronic mail across the two communication directions. The major changes observed with voice messaging occurred with the second and third dimensions. While access to voice messaging is readily available, not all potential receivers are adept users of this technology. Thus, channel accessibility would be expected to be greater than receiver accessibility. Further, voice mail messages (like many verbal messages) are often not as clearly thought-out and appraised with regard to how they might be interpreted by a receiver as are written messages. Without direct feedback (e.g., the asynchronous nature of voice mail), the possibility of receiver misunderstanding or misinterpretation does increase. Given that there is less concern for providing clear, unambiguous directives in dealing with peers (lateral communication) than with subordinates (downward communication), it thus becomes clearer why voice messaging might be seen as an alternative for the telephone in lateral communication but as an alternative for handwritten (informal) messages in downward communication.

Differences can be observed with electronic mail on the first and second dimensions across the two communication directions. While one is assured that a message transmitted via electronic mail will reach another individual who uses electronic mail, it is not possible to determine when the receiver will actually read the message, how he/she will interpret the message, and, if needed, when he/she will transmit a response. (A common complaint resulting from this study's open-ended questions was that many respondents complained that their potential communication partners did not use electronic mail or did not check their mail boxes frequently enough). ## Conclusion

These results both confirm and extend prıor findings regarding the nature of the evaluation frames used by individuals in differ ntiating among available communication channels. While this study's results validate the use of the channel and information attributes used in prior research, the desirability of incorporating a concern for “feedback" within research models was demonstrated. Additionally, most prior research on communication media has ignored communication direction. Our findings indicate that comprehensive treatments of organizational communication behaviors should explicitly account for the direction of communication. Further, this study's findings indicate that it may not be appropriate to transfer research models and instruments developed for and used with one particular communication direction to research designs addressing other communication directions.

At least four limitations of this research must be recognized in interpreting these findings and in pursuing further inquiry into these and related issues. First, even though MDS procedures were applied, the interpretation of the MDS dimensions was facilitated through researcher-specified channel attributes applying measurement scales with unknown psychometric pioperties. It is certainly possible that an improved, more comprehensive set of attribute scales might have eliminated unintended biases reflected by the current attribute set and might have promoted different interpretations of the two MDS spaces. Second, all respondents were from a single, large organization which has a particular history regarding the implementation and availability of the examined communication channels. It is impossible, as well as inappropriate, to disassemble these individuals' responses from this history. Readers should be careful in extending these results beyond this or similar organizational contexts. Third, the unidirectional aspect of downward communication most surely influenced the respondents' perceptions of these communication channels. Another explanation for these schema differences might thus be that respondents applied a bidirectional frame for lateral communication but a unidirectional frame for downward communication. This and related questions would benefit substantially from future research. Finally, this was not a study of either communication channel selection or communication use. The findings should not be directly extended to research models explaining such behaviors.

Much opportunity thus exists to build upon these findings. Specifically, future research is needed to improve this study's instrumentation, to validate these findings in other organizational contexts, to explore other communication directions, and to incorporate these results into richer models investigating communication channel selection and use.
