# Electronic Trading and Work Transformation in the London Insurance Market

Michael Barrett • Geoff Walsham

Department of AMIS, Faculty of Business, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Alberta, Canada T6G 2R6 Michael.Barrett@ualberta.ca

Judge Institute of Management Studies, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, CB2 1AG, United Kingdom gw10@eng.cam.ac.uk

he integration of information and communications technologies (IT) is playing a key role in transforming the nature of work. The link between IT and transformation is poorly understood, and further theoretical developments are needed to advance our current knowledge of this relationship. In this paper, we develop a conceptual scheme by drawing on and extending Giddens’ social theory of transformation that relates changes in modern institutions to shifts in self-identity. We illustrate the value of these ideas in making sense of the introduction of an electronic trading system, LIMNET EPS, across the London Insurance Market. Furthermore, our case analyses suggest some practical implications on electronic trading and work transformation.

(Electronic Trading; Giddens’ Social Theory; Modernity and Self-Identity; Work Transformation; London Insurance Market; Globalization)

## 1. Current Issues and Trends Concerning IT and Transformation

The nature of the link between IT and work transformation has been and continues to be the subject of dispute amongst scholars and practitioners. Since the early 1970s, when Braverman (1974) and Bell (1973) first articulated these debates, conflicting images have persisted concerning the future of work (Lowe 1997). These debates are now well-established across a number of academic disciplines, including general management literature, organizational theory and the information systems field, as well as populist literature. Advocates of technological utopianism suggest an era that fosters the development of a “postindustrial Nirvana of knowledge” (Clement and Myles 1994) constituted by elite knowledge workers (Drucker 1993) who are able to enjoy a higher level of skill development with the potential for improved job satisfaction (Piore and Sabel 1984; Zuboff 1988). This contrasts with the techno-critics who envision significant deskilled and dehumanized work in an era where high levels of unemployment will be the norm (Rifkin 1995; Nobel 1995).

The enduring nature of these debates concerning the nature and meaning of the relationship between information technology and changes in organizational work is a testament to their continued importance. A current theme highlights the importance of examining the relationship of information technology and the social transformation of white-collar workers (Kling and Dunlop 1993), managers and professional groups in particular (DuGay et al. 1996). Another emerging focus concerns the changing professional identity of such groups as new information and communication technologies increasingly support new ways of coordinating work beyond organizational boundaries to interorganizational relationships.

This paper describes a study of the development and attempted implementation of electronic trading applications in the London Insurance Market and therefore provides an example of these issues concerning social transformation. A number of other recent studies (e.g., Clemons and Weber 1990; Knights and Murray 1994; Fincham 1994) highlight the increasing importance of such marketwide transformations associated with IT in the U.K. financial services sector. These transformations are a testament to the popular view (e.g., Handy 1989; Bradley et al. 1993; Applegate 1994) that the competitive business environment faced by organizations in the latter part of the 20th century involving the rapid globalization of markets and the deregulation of industries is becoming increasingly dynamic and complex. Such organizational transformations, enabled by advances in information and communication technologies, have led to the emergence of what is popularly referred to as a new global information economy (Zuboff 1996). In this new economy, firms across a variety of industries have sought significant cost savings, efficiency, and improved customer service by developing a number of new organizational forms through networking and other electronic communications (Fulk and DeSanctis 1995; Yates and Van Maanen 1996).

New organizational forms often involve a vision of “virtuality” as suggested in recent terminology such as virtual teams (Hammer and Champy 1993), virtual organizations (Davidow and Malone 1992), and virtual communities (Rheingold 1994). Such a vision suggests social configurations whose structures and functions are highly dependent on IT, and are relatively unconstrained by spatial and temporal boundaries (Mowshowitz 1994). Recent technological innovations, such as the use of the World Wide Web for example, have provided new possibilities for electronic commerce between organizations and the development of intranet systems between offices within the same organization (Robey 1997). These and other technologies have fueled visions of “virtuality” but, as Robey points out, the logic underlying these arguments about information technology and organizational transformation is somewhat deterministic in nature (cf. Applegate et al. 1988; Hammer and Champy 1993; Scott Morton 1991; Rheingold 1994). IT, described by such writers as a “driver” or a “force,” is placed in the role of the causal agent capable of transforming organizations directly. However, other studies (Barley 1986; Orlikowski 1993; Robey and Sahay 1996) that examine comparable organizations employing identical technologies show contrasting consequences and do not support the deterministic account of how transformation is accomplished. Contradictory outcomes are reported, such as different effects on social roles (Barley 1986), or different social consequences resulting from IS implementation (Robey and Sahay 1996), where uniform effects might have been expected with a more deterministic and causal perspective of IT.

In addition to these contradictory outcomes of transformations associated with the introduction of new IT, other work (e.g., Zuboff 1988; Dunlop and Kling 1991) has highlighted the importance of examining how new information technologies are implicated in profound changes in the nature of organizational work. Furthermore, recent research has suggested the need to examine IT and transformation at a number of interrelated levels (Orlikowski et al. 1996): changes in the nature of work and personal security at the individual level; changes in form and structure at the organizational level; and increasing interconnection and globalization at the national and international levels. In particular, Orlikowski et al. (1996) argue that increased levels of globalization facilitated by information technology are interconnected with profound changes in personal identity.

A key goal of this paper is to develop a conceptual scheme which builds on these recent ideas to further our understanding of IT and transformation. In developing our scheme, we draw on and extend Giddens’ theory on the consequences of transformations in contemporary society and the relationship to changes in self-identity (Giddens 1990, 1991). The applications of these theoretical constructs are subsequently utilized to illuminate our analysis of an ongoing longitudinal case study of the introduction of electronic trading across the London Insurance Market. One of the research questions this study sought to address is: what are the social and organizational transformations associated with the introduction of electronic trading across the London Insurance Market? The Market experienced significant financial difficulties as a result of high operating costs and huge losses in the late 1980s and early 1990s. These pressures, along with intense global competition, had threatened its survival. Following this crisis, one of the key initiatives adopted by the London Market involved the introduction of new information and communications technologies, in particular interorganizational systems (IOS) enabled by electronic data interchange (EDI) for business transformation in the Market.

A key EDI service on the London Insurance Market Network (LIMNET), often viewed to be at the heart of electronic trading, is the electronic placing support system (EPS). This service was designed as a batch EDI concept using value added data services on an IBM network to coordinate the passing of risk packages between brokers and selected underwriters. With electronic trading, brokers input an electronic record of the risk at the start of the placement process after which these records are available to all participants involved in the risk at different stages of its life cycle. Therefore, the successful adoption of EPS is considered critical by market leaders for improved market efficiency, and the development of a new electronically supported trading environment is expected to have significant implications for the nature of work in this highly traditional marketplace.

The next section describes the key tenets of Giddens’ ideas on the consequences of social transformation in modern times and discusses its relevance in the development of our conceptual scheme. In the third section, we provide details of our methodology for the empirical study in the London Insurance Market. This is followed by our interpretation of some of the key actions and events concerning the introduction of electronic trading across the London Insurance Market. The fifth section illustrates the usefulness of our conceptual scheme to better understand the linkage between electronic trading and work transformation in the Market. The paper concludes by highlighting some broader implications for the London Insurance Market and suggests the relevance of the conceptual scheme in understanding IT and work transformation across a range of sectors and national economies.

## 2. Giddens Theory on Social Transformation and Conceptual Scheme Development

Whilst the influence of Giddens’ ideas on IS research has been established in recent years (Orlikowski 1992; Walsham and Han 1993; DeSanctis and Poole 1994), it has often been focused on his metatheoretical developments of structuration theory, both in the IS and the broader management literature (Whittington 1992). His later writings build on, and extend, the core tenets of structuration theory, and emphasize the importance of time and space in the structuring of social relations. Giddens (1990, 1991, and 1994) argues for a rethinking which “cannot be limited to the interpretation of time and space alone; it has to be directly related to the conceptualizing of modernity.” He therefore seeks to analyze the role of time and space in understanding the modern era and its transformations. Information and communications technologies are playing a key role in these transformations, though Giddens does not dwell significantly on these linkages in his writings. In developing our conceptual scheme, we not only synthesize and organize some of Giddens’ key concepts along with their interrelationships, but extend Giddens’ ideas by including an explicit IT dimension in our analysis. Table 1 summarizes our conceptual scheme, the main elements of which are now elaborated using a few examples of IT-related transformation in global insurance markets.

Globalizing Tendencies. In understanding the modern era and its transformations, Giddens suggests that we have to account for the extreme dynamism and globalizing scope of modern institutions and explain the nature of their discontinuities from traditional cultures. (Giddens 1990, p. 16). Giddens suggests that globalizing tendencies in modern times derive from three key interrelated sources: the separation of time and space; the development of disembedding mechanisms; and institutional reflexivity. As shown in Table 1, we argue that IT can facilitate the separation of time and space, redefining notions of time-space and social relations, can act as a type of disembedding mechanism requiring new trust systems, and may be an enabler of institutional reflexivity.

Giddens emphasizes the central role of time and space in the structuring of social practices. He suggests that, in order to understand the globalizing dynamic, one must appreciate how modern practices provide the basis for the separation of time and space and its recombination in different ways. Furthermore, he argues that the separation of time and space is the condition for the “stretching” of social relations allowing the articulation of social relations across wide spans of timespace, up to and including global systems. It is therefore necessary to consider both social relations between people present in time and space, and between those who are absent in time and space. Giddens (1991, p. 17) suggests that modern social organization presumes the precise coordination of the actions of many human beings physically absent from one another; the “when” of these actions is directly connected to the “where,” but not, as in earlier times, via the mediation of place. For example, in insurance markets, IT in the form of global EDI messages may facilitate the placement of risks between brokers and underwriters who are no longer in face-to-face contact, whereas traditional insurance practices have always assumed a personal meeting.

The process of separation of time and space is crucial for and strongly linked to the disembedding of social institutions that is prevalent in organizations today. In prior eras, the vast majority of institutions and practices were embedded in a physical location or place. However, social relations have become increasingly disembedded, being lifted out of local contexts of interaction and rearticulated across indefinite spans of time-space. Giddens uses the term “disembedding mechanisms” to describe the “stretching” of social relations which allow for the separation of interaction from the particularities of locales. One type of disembedding mechanism identified by Giddens is an expert system. Giddens’ terminology of expert systems as a disembedding mechanism should not be confused as involving a technical computer system. Rather, he describes them as “systems of technical accomplishment or professional expertise that organize large areas of the material and social environments in which we live today.” With these systems, the expertise of individuals becomes lifted out of specific contexts and subsequently incorporated within institutional frameworks.

We can extend the above example regarding insurance markets to illustrate the development of underwriting practices as an expert system and the role of information technologies as different forms of disembedding mechanisms. Underwriting is no longer an art which relies on the sole judgment and negotiation between brokers and underwriters (insurers) as it was in its original days. The development and widespread use of standardized actuarial methods for risk assessment has become a type of expert system drawn upon by underwriters in their work. Furthermore, IT, as a type of disembedding mechanism, has facilitated the development of information systems involving the codification of underwriting expertise based on actuarial methods that can then be reliably applied to an indeterminate number of cases. These systems, along with the development of global communication networks and EDI standards, are permitting the “stretching” of underwriting practices from situations of physical co-presence across borders and time zones. Whilst IT as a medium for embodying new social rules and expertise into technical systems enables disembedding, it should not be equated with Giddens’ definition of expert systems.

Disembedding mechanisms facilitated by IT and expert systems remove social relations from the immediate context and depend upon trust for their operation. In developing the notion of trust relations and how they may be sustained, Giddens distinguishes between trust relations that are “sustained by or expressed in social connections established in circumstances of copresence (same time and place),” and the development of trust in conditions of absence as a consequence of expert systems. For example, global insurance markets have developed as important expert systems of modernity which depend on trust by all participants, who may not fully understand them, for their successful and continued operation. At the same time, it is difficult for anyone to disengage from these markets as a result of their global interdependence.

The separation of time and space, coupled with disembedding mechanisms, provides the context of institutional reflexivity, the third key globalizing influence on the dynamism of modern institutions, according to Giddens. By this, he argues that knowledge in the contemporary age is provisional and mutable and, consequently, institutional reflexivity refers to the susceptibility of most areas of social activity to chronic ordering and reordering of social relations in the light of new knowledge. In other words, Giddens is suggesting that there is ongoing questioning and revising of existing knowledge and practices that may also lead to the reordering of existing social relations.

Changing risk environments have contributed significantly to institutional reflexivity in modern institutions, and insurance markets are no exception. Insurance as an institution has traditionally sought to “colonize the future” (Giddens 1991) by adopting an actuarial form of risk calculation that views risk in terms of parameters of probability that can be precisely assessed or calculated. However, the nature of risk environments has been inherently unstable of late, involving new parameters of risk which cannot be easily assessed. As an example, we consider the risks associated with nuclear disasters such as those experienced at Chernobyl, and the difficulty in assessing and evaluating such risks. Such evidence of a changing risk profile provides the context by which trust may be suspended in traditional insurance practices. This suspension of trust may lead to institutional reflexivity as to appropriate methods of risk assessment and evaluation. It may also lead to the questioning of existing social and work relations between participants in the value chain of insurance markets, as we shall discuss later.

Self-Identity. Giddens argues that there is an increasing interconnection between the dynamic globalizing tendencies at the macroinstitutional level, as discussed above, and the changing nature of selfidentity at the individual level. For example, Giddens considers that with new forms of mediated experience, particularly electronic communications, distant happenings are increasingly influencing both self-identity and the basic organization of social relations. Our conceptual scheme, as shown in Table 1, highlights three key concepts and links them to IT adoption: concern for deskilling, for existential anxiety, and the opportunity for reskilling and empowerment.

Giddens argues that the development and use of expert systems have penetrated all aspects of day-to-day life and have the potential to deskill by undermining local practices and knowledge. Furthermore, he suggests that the chronic monitoring and utilization of new knowledge, which is characteristic of institutional reflexivity, creates profound implications for personal activities that may lead to significant existential anxiety for the individual. Such anxiety is related to who they are and what their role is in the chronic reordering of social relations often brought about in the light of new knowledge.

Whilst examining possibilities of deskilling with the expansion of expert systems, Giddens cautions against adopting this viewpoint as the only alternative. New skills are continually created in the workplace and, in some part, by those individuals whose activities are being deskilled. However, he suggests that these individuals adopt different approaches to restore a coherent sense of self. Giddens’ concept of self is not as a distinctive set of traits possessed by an individual, as suggested by some psychologists, but is rather a “reflexive project” being continuously explored, constructed, and revised as part of a reflexive process. This active construction and revision of the self provides a basis for security and self-identity. As such, Giddens suggests two other possibilities other than reskilling: reappropriation and empowerment. Firstly, reappropriation of knowledge and control by lay agents is a basic aspect of what Giddens terms the “dialectic of control.” That is, while people may lose skills and forms of knowledge, they remain skillful and knowledgeable in the contexts of action in which their activities take place. Therefore everyday skill and knowledgeability stands in dialectical connection to the expropriating effects of expert systems, continually influencing and reshaping the very impact of such systems. This suggests that “technical expertise is continuously reappropriated by lay agents as part of their routine dealings with abstract (which include expert) systems” (Giddens 1991).

In addition to reappropriation, there are often periods of empowerment. The expansion of expert systems does not just involve disembedding but gives agents the power to alter the material world and transform the conditions of their own actions. Such empowerment is often made possible since the most cherished beliefs underlying expert systems are always open to revision and are altered quite regularly. Ultimately, there is always a selection to be made by agents between local ways of doing things and procedures offered by expert systems. This provides the opportunity for partial or more complete reskilling of agents who can choose from a wide variety of courses of action.

## 3. Research Methodology for the Empirical Study

The approach taken in research did not follow a topdown method where a conceptual scheme was first developed and the fieldwork then conducted to confirm its value. On the contrary, the usefulness of the theoretical concepts described above developed as part of an emergent process during periods of reflection between different stages of this intensive longitudinal research. Furthermore, an iterative process ensued whereby our case analysis was used to help develop and focus Giddens’ broad theoretical ideas into a conceptual scheme. In the following paragraphs, we discuss our ontological and epistemological stances in carrying out the research, and then discuss our methods of data collection and analysis.

In contrast to more realist approaches whose ontological beliefs are of an objective, external reality, we adopt an interpretivist stance believing the nature of reality to be a social construction by human actors (Berger and Luckman 1989). In taking an interpretivist approach, our underlying epistemological beliefs are that knowledge can be best achieved by getting inside the world of those generating it (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). A key advantage of this approach is that equivocal outcomes of IT adoption are potentially accounted for by understanding how the social meanings attached to the technology originate and evolve. An interpretive case study approach (Zuboff 1988; Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991; Walsham 1995) was adopted which examined the actions and perceptions of the human stakeholders concerned with the development and use of the electronic placing support system (EPS), and the changing contexts within which the attempted introduction of electronic trading was taking place.

The longitudinal and intensive nature of the study sought to observe and collect data on changing circumstances, perceptions, and actions over time, and as they occurred. This design was supplemented by detailed historical reconstruction of earlier periods prior to the start of the research study in October 1993. The main period of historical reconstruction started at the birth of the London Insurance Market Network (LIMNET) in 1987 until October 1993. However, this was further supplemented by a historical study of the London Insurance Market over its 300-year history, with a particular focus on the last few decades.

The principal method of data collection was through in-depth semistructured interviews of participants in Market associations for insurance brokers and underwriters, the Lloyd’s Corporation, and early adopter firms of the LIMNET EPS system. As Table 2 shows, we carried out 94 interviews with individuals at different hierarchical levels in four separate phases of the research. Interviews typically lasted about one to two hours. Extensive notes of interviews were taken during the first two phases and this was followed up by the use of tape recording in the latter two phases, when sufficient trust and positive rapport had been developed.

The phases of the research were conducted approximately six months apart and attempts were made to avoid extremely busy work periods such as the season where existing insurance risks are renewed yearly. The research design and site selection strategy was to choose three of the seven global brokers (constituting 85% of the market volume of trades) who were involved with the EPS introduction. The two brokers with the highest levels of Marketwide usage of the LIMNET EPS system were chosen along with a third who had the lowest levels. The choice of the four underwriting firms selected was also influenced by usage levels, but the key decision that was made involved using Lloyd’s underwriters as opposed to corporate insurers (e.g., Commercial Union). Staff from other key market entities were also interviewed. These included Lloyd’s, Market association directors, IT suppliers, and Limnet Ltd. which was responsible for coordinating LIMNET activities including the LIMNET EPS implementation. Within each of these firms, a number of individuals were interviewed including brokers and underwriters, IT directors and staff, and senior management.

The LIMNET EPS champions who were responsible for the implementation of EPS in their respective organizations guided the initial selection of interviewees in each organization. Within each organization, the strategy was to interview both individuals known to have high levels of EPS adoption and those who did not. Other important interviewees were often highlighted from market trade publications. In addition, at each interview we consciously asked each person who else in the Market we should interview (Kling 1987). Repeat interviews were conducted at each phase with LIMNET directors and implementation managers as well as the designated EPS champions.

In addition to recollections of key participants on these past events, a number of primary and secondary sources were used. Primary sources included strategy plans, mission statements, notes and memoranda from marketwide meetings, newsletters, and annual reports. Secondary reports included sectoral studies, trade journals, Lloyd’s publications and newsletters, local newspapers, and historical reports on the Market. A significant amount of time was also spent carefully observing the work practices of insurance risk placement in the Market around the city of London, brokers and underwriters offices, as well as the trading room at Lloyd’s. Finally, system demonstrations of old and new versions of the EPS software and computer based training materials were observed so as to better understand the proposed changes in methods of risk placement across the Market.

The theoretical developments that were drawn on to make sense of key issues and themes grew with continued data collection and analysis. Our ideas of appropriate theory also shifted through continuous study of the IS literature and other literature including social theory and organizational theory. During the early phases of the research, structuration theory was used as an initial guide to data collection. The theory was valuable as a sensitizing device during the fieldwork in examining human and organizational issues. In particular, we sought answers to questions such as: How did the actions of key participants reproduce social structure in the Market? How did agents exercise power and draw on norms of the Market to sanction their actions? How did actors reflexively monitor the consequences of their own actions, including any unintended consequences? How did this monitoring produce change in social structure?

It was only after phase 1 of the research, following preliminary case analysis and after subsequent reading, did it become clear to us that Giddens’ later ideas (1990, 1991) would be valuable for understanding IT and work transformation in the London Market. This evolution of theory and methodology in an iterative fashion should be expected in an intensive longitudinal study such as this one, which has already spanned over three years (Walsham 1995).

The field data were analyzed throughout this period by careful reading and reflection on the field notes and by frequent discussion between the two researchers to identify and develop key concepts, themes, and issues. This process of analysis of the data involved data preparation which identified and later combined statements with similar meanings so as to categorize the data from all the interviews and observations. For each of the Market participant groups, interview transcripts were examined to identify statements or actions that reflected assumptions or expectations surrounding the use of electronic trading for risk placement (cf. Orlikowski and Gash 1994), as well as its implications for the operation of the Market. Furthermore, we were sensitized to understanding the risk placement activity in the structured context of the London Market involving operating norms and relations of power. This approach involved reading the data and sorting it into categories suggested by the data. Once this process was completed for each group of market participants, it was followed by an analysis between these groups to determine whether the categories generated by each group’s data reflected common themes (Eisenhardt 1989; Orlikowski and Gash 1994; Barrett 1996).

Upon identification of these themes, checks were made throughout the field data to examine to what extent the categories and themes provided as full a coverage of the data as was possible (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). This iterative examination yielded a set of themes that constituted the key domains of the main subcultures assumptions. These domains were further examined for similarities and differences at all levels across the Market. Finally, the evolving themes and domains were shared with key Market participants at all levels of the Market for their feedback and discussion, to both validate factual data, and to obtain their opinion on our interpretation of the EPS introduction in the Market.

Our approach was similar to a “Straussian” adaptation of grounded theory (Strauss and Corbin 1990; Orlikowski 1993). As such, the earlier stages of the research involved more open-ended interviewing, whereas the later stages focused more narrowly on the emerging concepts and themes. From the beginning of the study, we explicitly acknowledged key concepts of the IT and transformation literature, and remained open to the operation of broader structural phenomena such as power relations (Layder 1993), and other related theoretical ideas. For example, the themes generated by the initial analysis during the first two phases of research spurred further reading of Giddens’ more recent theoretical developments on social transformation. In an iterative fashion between the reading of his theory and the ongoing analysis and reflection of the field data, his key concepts and ideas were synthesized and extended to explicitly include an IT dimension. The field data were then more formally reanalyzed to further illuminate the broad themes through the lens of our new concepts and their interrelationships.

## 4. Case Description: The Introduction of Electronic Trading in the London Insurance Market

The London Insurance Market is an important part of the U.K. general insurance industry built up around Lloyd’s of London, and attracts over £4bn in the form of invisible export earnings annually from its primary business activity in globally traded insurance and reinsurance. The Market comprises over 500 independently owned and managed brokerage and underwriting businesses (Carter and Falush 1994). These businesses form a complex network of linked but diverse players, including underwriting groups, brokerage firms, and Market leaders. While there is no central authority governing the market, each participant is affiliated to a trading association. Starting in the late 1980s and continuing in the early 1990s, the Market experienced major financial difficulties for several key reasons. Firstly, huge insurance losses could be attributed to heavy claims from an unprecedented number of catastrophes in quick succession as well as significant damages awarded by the courts for suspect underwriting practices. Secondly, there was intense global competition in the large commercial/wholesale insurance industry, which had been gradually deregulated over the previous two decades. This led to increased competition, with the flow of risk capital to new underwriting markets globally. Thirdly, clients started setting up their own self-insurance pools in these new underwriting markets often located in tax free countries. Finally, the Market had increasingly high operating costs, as manual business practices have persisted between underwriters and brokers over the 300-years of the Market’s existence.

Since the late 1980s and culminating in its Business Plan of 1993, the Market has sought to develop and use IT, EDI standards and messages in particular, to lower costs by streamlining business processes so as to improve the quality of service and interorganizational efficiency. With respect to the development of EDI standards and messages, there has been unprecedented cooperation in the form of a joint venture between the major network associations worldwide, including the London Insurance Market Network (LIMNET), the European reinsurance network (RINET) set up by large European re-insurers, and the U.S. network initiatives led by BRMA/RAA associations. As a result, UN/EDIFACT EDI standards and messages have been developed with the objective of ultimately facilitating international electronic communications for insurers, reinsurers, and intermediaries across different global insurance Markets.

As shown in Table 3, the Market has made significant progress in using IT to streamline a number of work areas including the use of four claims systems to support claims settlement and other initiatives such as accounting and outward reinsurance systems to support the automation of these activities across the Market. However, the development and use of electronic systems to support the placing of business between underwriters and brokers, often considered by many to be the heart of the work of this insurance market, has not been very successful. Whilst earlier placing systems such as C-DEX and E-Slip have been replaced by a joint market initiative LIMNET EPS, very low levels of adoption of the EPS system were still being experienced in this marketplace five years after its initial launch. As the introduction of the LIMNET EPS system is the focus of this paper, we briefly describe the traditional risk placement practices in this Market.

Traditional Risk Placement in the London Insurance Market. Clients worldwide requiring insurance or reinsurance coverage contact an insurance broker who investigates the details of the risk and collects any relevant supporting information. The broker negotiates with a “lead” underwriter who ultimately sets the appropriate financial terms such as premium rates, and adds any special conditions under which the risk will be insured. The risks placed in this “subscription” market are often large and complex. It is therefore often necessary for the broker to also ask a number of “following” underwriters in the London Market to subscribe to the risk under the same terms and conditions set by the “lead” underwriter.

This process is continued across the Market and, if necessary, other global insurance markets are accessed until 100% of the risk has been insured. The deal is eventually finalized between client, broker, and underwriter. Each participating underwriter accepts a proportion of the risk, the client pays the premiums, and the brokerage firm receives its commission. Indeed, unlike the stock market, the negotiation and placement of the risk is often just the beginning of the transaction, and future insurance activities on this risk could include renewals, endorsements or changes to the insurance policy, and claims.

The traditional placement of risk, which continued throughout the research period 1993–1996, involved participants manually transacting their insurance activities such as placing and claims on paper and through personal contact. For example, “placing” brokers carried a paper “slip” containing the details of the risk, and upon which subsequent negotiations between underwriters and brokers related to this risk were recorded. This manual placement process, involving face-to-face interaction in a physical marketplace, was often a lengthy one, with brokers queuing to visit a number of lead and following underwriters who may be willing to participate in a given risk.

Following the huge losses of the late 1980s and early 1990s, LIMNET EPS was designed as a batch EDI concept using value added data services on an IBM network to coordinate the passing of risk packages between brokers and selected underwriters. In this new electronically supported trading environment, brokers are now responsible for inputting an electronic record of the risk, called the common core record, at the start of the placement process. Therefore, the risk details need only be input once, after which they are available to all participants involved in the risk at different stages of its life cycle. The vision was that placements brokers could then electronically submit the risk to a “lead” underwriter, and then transmit it across the network to a number of “following” underwriters who may be interested in accepting a percentage of the risk. ## 5. Analysis and Discussion on Electronic Trading and Work Transformation

In this section, we draw on our previously developed conceptual scheme to further our understanding of electronic trading and work transformation in the London Market. In so doing, it should be noted that we are not merely illustrating the value of Giddens’ ideas, but are also explicitly linking them to the IT dimension which is not part of his original work. The strategy adopted in our analysis is to make Giddens’ unique terms and abstractions more accessible to our readers by interweaving our discussion of these terms with examples from the case study.

## 5.1. Globalizing Tendencies

Globalizing tendencies include how IT facilitates the separation of time and space, serves as a disembedding mechanism requiring the development of new trust systems, and may be an enabler of institutional reflexivity in a changing risk environment. As summarized in Table 4, each of these points concerning globalizing tendencies was illustrated by the London Market as they attempted to introduce new information technologies for electronic trading.

Time-Space Separation. The traditional marketplace at Lloyd’s and the London Market is similar to a pre-modern or oral culture where the primary of place, “market intelligence,” and the overriding importance of localized trust through personal relations are perceived to be important. Though somewhat critical in tone, a former senior EPS implementation director supported this view of the work culture in the Market:

They (junior and middle level brokers and underwriters) like its lack of structure, conviviality. The lack of surveillance . . . they chat amongst themselves as they call it ‘gathering market intelligence’ . . . They have an aura of mulling around the City doing big deals.

The introduction of LIMNET EPS to support electronic trading between brokers and underwriters would potentially involve the separation of time and space currently linked through place. That is to say, whilst the time and space of an underwriter/broker interaction has been traditionally linked through the particular place of the underwriter’s box or office, the introduction of LIMNET EPS would enable business interactions to take place across a number of different time-space configurations. Brokers and underwriters suggested that the introduction of the EPS system might erode Lloyd’s existing source of competitive advantage in the global market place. A key advantage of Lloyd’s unique market structure was that innovative insurance products could be developed in a short period of time through face-to-face interaction between brokers and underwriters in a discrete geographical area.

Disembedding Mechanisms. The EPS system can be viewed as a type of disembedding mechanism which allows the lifting out of placing activities from the localized contexts of the Lloyds’ marketplace, with the possibility of their restructuring across vast reaches of time and space. The primacy of place has the potential to be redefined by such processes of disembedding and time-space separation. While there is still close attachment to Lloyd’s as a physical marketplace, placing practices are no longer solely based locally. Disembedding occurs not just across companies in the London Market, but potentially via joint venture EDI standards and messages to other global insurance markets. The local and global have thus become even more inextricably linked.

With the EPS system, a broker can potentially place a risk with several underwriters in the London Market simultaneously, which could be expanded in the future to insurance markets globally. Despite these new possibilities, the persistent proclivity to face-toface interaction between underwriters and brokers, and the value they placed on social relations as a primary feature of their work, was totally underestimated by Market leaders. They had hoped for a similar situation to that experienced with the “Big Bang” at the London Stock Exchange. That market enjoyed a very quick adoption to screen trading, despite initial concerns that old cultural attitudes requiring face-to-face interaction would prevail in the short term. Following the initial launch of EPS, there was significant optimism amongst market leaders as to the bigger impact EPS could play. A market leader captured this mood with the following statement:

I think we are going to see an element of pure screen trading sooner rather than later.

However, as time went by, it became clear that history was not going to repeat itself in the London Insurance Market. The potential savings for the Market, even in times of crisis, were not sufficiently attractive to encourage adoption. Brokers and underwriters feared that disembedding would result in future relations becoming impersonal and at a distance. This was viewed as disadvantageous for at least two reasons.

Firstly, the importance of face-to-face negotiations was deemed critical to brokers and underwriters in the facilitation of effective negotiations in the best interests of their clients. A key broker explained:

The business is based largely on relationships and trust. This is why it is so vital to carry out business in a face-to-face manner . . . You are negotiating the business. It is important how well you put across the case . . . You use a lot of different skills in negotiating. You emphasise and deemphasise certain aspects, handle objections . . . It is a sales situation.

In addition, they emphasized the importance of continued business relationships in the insurance market at later stages of the life cycle of the risk after the initial placing. A senior broker contrasted the nature of trading stocks on the London Stock Exchange with an insurance transaction in the London Insurance Market:

In the stock market, the transaction is a simple buy/sell, and is completed once a price has been decided on . . . with insurance, the negotiation involves terms and conditions and once a price is negotiated, the transaction has just begun and could involve renewals, endorsements, and claims in the future.

A second reason why underwriters considered traditional face-to-face negotiations crucial was the context of scarce underwriting capacity and huge underwriting losses experienced over the previous few years. They believed that this form of interaction allowed them, at any given moment, to assess the full range of cues such as body language and tone of voice to help determine whether the risk should be accepted and, if so, at what price. Without face-to-face negotiations, brokers and underwriters feared that their ability to assess risks would be eroded. They believed that the delicate negotiation of a complex risk could not be adequately conducted except through physical proximity. The Market story which reflected this was recounted by an IT director in the Market with some doubt as to the extent of its validity:

There is an unfounded fear of having no face-to-face communication as they (the brokers) feel they have to see the whites of their eyes and to see if their hands are trembling when they (the underwriters) sign the slip (risk).

Such a view highlights the perceived importance of face-to-face interaction by underwriters and brokers in the continued development and maintenance of business relationships. These developments require trustworthiness to be established between individuals who are well-known to one another, and who, on the basis of long term acquaintance, have substantiated the credentials which render each reliable in the eyes of the other.

It is perhaps somewhat unsurprising that these traders experienced significant difficulty in developing new forms of trust when introducing disembedding mechanisms in the Market. Brokers and underwriters resisted the adoption of the EPS system as they perceived that the development of new forms of trust associated with the use of EPS in the placement process would take place at the expense of traditional trust between persons in face-to-face contact. Two other features of the EPS system use for broker-underwriter interaction increased this difficulty of achieving system trust.

Firstly, the EPS system was perceived to potentially undermine the underlying principle of trust in the Market, the Lloyd’s tradition/rule of Utmost Good Faith. This rule essentially states that a broker must display all known relevant information about the client and the insurance risk to underwriters upon presentation of the risk. In the EPS environment, where faceto-face interaction may no longer be mandatory, it was deemed necessary for the EPS system to be able to represent all details about the risk and the client accurately. A key issue concerned the difficulty of the broker inputting all relevant information about a risk into the EPS system, particularly when the system was being used for remote screen-based trading. This situation was especially problematic with earlier versions of the EPS system. There was inadequate development of the common core record (CCR) that was designed to be able to capture the risk details electronically. A director of Marine and Cargo in one of the broking firms involved with EPS from its pilot stage explained:

Just recently, there was an EPS meeting to discuss how it could be ensured that all the relevant information was revealed in EPS. There was concern that the new front end to the EPS system would store only the mandatory fields of the CCR. Brokers are concerned that other optional or conditional information about the risk could not be provided in the available fields of the EPS which would violate the Utmost Good Faith clause.

Secondly, the EDI (UN/EDIFACT) messages upon which the EPS system was developed were unable to transmit supporting documentation needed for risk evaluation, such as images and spreadsheets, between brokers and underwriters. These technical limitations further supported the argument made by the majority of brokers and underwriters that the EPS system was inappropriate for carrying out risk placement in the London Market. The introduction of new technology, therefore, did not aid the development of new trust systems, but rather the limitations in the technology created a distrust of the system.

Institutional Reflexivity. A final element of our conceptual scheme that relates to Giddens’ globalizing tendencies is the role of IT as an enabler of institutional reflexivity, referring to the chronic reflexive ordering and reordering of social relations in the light of new knowledge. Organizations across global insurance markets are being forced to re-examine their work practices; this includes using information technology to transform organizational work. For example, there has been a focus by leaders across these markets to reduce costs by cooperating in the development of globally shared EDI standards and messages. In the London Market, there has been a rethinking of the appropriateness of traditional insurance practices that rely heavily on local knowledge, market experience, and personal relationships. These concerns regarding current practices are intimately related to risk and uncertainty in modern society. These views have been recently echoed by a number of market leaders, including the chairman of Lloyds (Rowland 1995) who made reference to a recent paper written by the chairman of the Prudential Corporation, Sir Brian Corby:

Lloyd’s and other underwriters in the London Market are thus seeking to re-establish that, as knowledge workers, they add value in this high-risk insurance market. These pressures on underwriters come at a time when an inherently unstable new risk climate has developed which can be characterized by regular shifts in knowledge-claims and is often unsettling for both lay people and experts alike. As a result, this riskiness of risk defies the actuarial calculations traditionally used in insurance companies, and has significantly undermined the efficacy of underwriting knowledge and expertise in insurance markets globally. For reasons including these, there has been a big drive in the London Insurance Market to improve the expert judgement and monitoring of risks by using the LIMNET EPS to provide timely and accurate management information on risk exposures. To do so, the Market is now requiring underwriters to enhance their existing skills and expertise, based on tradition and experience, with professional underwriting qualifications and additional training requirements, particularly in the use of computer information systems. The ability to access and use third party Market information, such as claims records from Lloyd’s publications and Reuters information, may be particularly valuable in assisting underwriters in their somewhat subjective art of risk rating. It is also hoped that electronic feeds, from future versions of EPS to underwriter’s back end systems, will provide a more accurate and timely capture of risk details. These measures are expected to facilitate better management information on risk exposure levels, which will also provide useful new controls to prevent unfortuitous aggregations of risk. An EPS implementation manager of a Market Association touted this as a key benefit of EPS to underwriters. She remarked:

The LPC (London Processing Centre) has been telling underwriters that they should download the CCR to their systems as the use of the CCR is a key benefit to using EPS . . . Not only does it provide more timely information on risk exposure levels, but the CCR is expected to feed the electronic closing and accounting (ECA) system providing huge benefits in cost savings in the future.

Whilst we would agree that improved information on risks would undoubtedly be useful in monitoring risk exposures, it can also be legitimately argued that the shifting nature of risk environments makes risk assessment often incalculable, particularly in the technical sense of relying on the use of actuarial methods.

The above discussion highlights the different ways in which professional groups were aware and reflective about the changing institutional nature of insurance markets and the role of technology in enabling these changes. We now examine how these transformations at the macro-institutional level are related to the changing nature of self-identity at the individual level.

## 5.2. Self-Identity

Whilst a number of levels of identity such as individual, organizational, and industrial identity were certainly relevant in our case, we focus on identity at the individual level in this paper. Self-identity is interrelated to the macroinstitutional transformations of global insurance markets discussed above. Our conceptual scheme, shown in Table 1, identified concerns for deskilling and existential anxiety, and opportunities for reskilling and empowerment. As summarized in Table 5, each of these points can be illustrated by individual participants in the London Market as they attempted to adopt electronic trading.

Concerns for Deskilling with IT Adoption. Deskilling was perceived by the majority of brokers and underwriters to be inevitable in the context of electronic trading via the LIMNET EPS system. There was, and still is, a common feeling by brokers and underwriters that the future of their work will follow the same path as that of traders at the London Stock Exchange, with remote trade dominating as the mode of business exchange over the medium term. A senior broker and EPS champion within one of the large brokerage firms summarized broker attitudes as:

The attitudes of individual brokers is that there is a loss of humanity to electronic wizardry . . . they can’t see the balance, it is purely computer or old style. They have to see it as a tool to be used in our business.

They anticipate that, in an electronic trading support environment, the social nature of their work will become less interesting and involve longer work hours at reduced commission rates. A senior underwriter put it this way:

“there is a great deal of inertia with respect to the use of EPS by underwriters . . . they don’t want to be typing into keyboards all day.”

Furthermore, for underwriters and brokers alike, the use of the EPS system, involving asynchronous communication, was perceived to largely eliminate the spontaneity and excitement of the negotiation process that face-to-face communication allows. As remarked by a senior underwriter using the EPS system, there was concern by many as to the potential automation of their work:

“the actual bargaining is the reason most of us like to do underwriting. . . . the EPS system is seen to drive out the mystique often associated with financial service jobs in the city of London.”

There were also concerns about possible deskilling associated with potential loss of control by brokers and underwriters. These concerns at the individual level were related to issues of potential transfers of power between brokers and underwriters. No longer was sequential negotiation a necessity, as the EPS system could facilitate simultaneous risk order transmission. In other words, the sequential queuing of brokers to see underwriters one-on-one in the placing of a risk would dramatically change, particularly in the case of remote trading. With the EPS system, a broker can potentially place a risk with several underwriters in the London Market simultaneously, and this could be expanded in the future to insurance markets globally. Underwriters expressed concern for loss of control, perceiving that brokers may use the system to “flood” the market by indiscriminately sending the risk to a large number of underwriters. Linked to this sense of loss of control, there has been some apprehension among a number of underwriters of the new standards of professionalism, including computer literacy and training on marketwide applications such as LIMNET EPS. The acquisition of these new skills seems somewhat daunting for those underwriters and brokers without a basic foundation or experience in IT, and who are as a result somewhat computer-phobic.

In addition, there were fears of the loss of autonomy, which was enjoyed by brokers and underwriters in the manual placement of risk in this physical marketplace. It would now be possible for management to more closely monitor their work through the EPS system. This is viewed as especially desirable to managers who are seeking to improve their efficiency and productivity in a highly competitive market.

Participants were also concerned about two other issues related to deskilling. These are the potential for future personal meaninglessness in their work in an electronic trading environment, and fears as to the security of future employment in this Market. Giddens suggests that the self-disciplining of individuals to follow the dictates of impersonal efficiency associated with trust in expert systems lacks full moral meaning, and sows the seeds of personal meaninglessness. This was a perceived concern of brokers and underwriters in the future disembedded and distanced world of the London Market that was expected to involve more impersonal relations. They believed that the moral basis of the Market founded on honesty and trust of personal relations between brokers and underwriters as suggested by the Lloyd’s rule of Utmost Good Faith, may come under severe attack with the advent of electronic trading.

Security of employment has also been considered a threat, particularly for “following” underwriters, junior brokers, and underwriters. The increased efficiency facilitated through the use of LIMNET services will require limited, if any, face-to-face interaction between brokers and “following” underwriters. A number of the job functions carried out by junior underwriters and brokers, such as manual handling and distribution of risk information, can now be automated through LIMNET. As a result, it is expected that fewer brokers and underwriters, particularly following underwriters, will be required in the Market.

Existential Anxiety. These different concerns for deskilling are related to a deeper identity crisis for brokers and underwriters. The introduction and use of the LIMNET EPS system is bound up with significant troubling aspects or existential anxiety as to how they will function effectively in this transition from a traditional market to an electronically supported market. The point here is that the existential anxiety felt by individual brokers and underwriters cannot be put down solely to job insecurity, although it played a key role, but is also related to anxieties such as “what is a broker or underwriter?” This question is by no means new, and has been important to workers in a number of sectors throughout history. However, until recently, the professional identity of brokers and underwriters in the London market has enjoyed relative stability in role certainty. It is now being undermined by a number of recent transformations including the IT-related changes. A senior underwriter summarized the anxiety faced by many underwriters in the following way:

Risks are changing, technology is changing the way risks are placed . . . we are trying to embrace change in our business while also changing the way business is done . . . The nature of our business and the tools of the trade are history and experience. We use history and experience to project what might happen in the future. . We take considerable risk by doing that . . extraordinary risk. . We are now asked to take history and experience and turn it on its head.

Similarly, brokers are quick to point out the subtleties of redefinition of work associated with the introduction of LIMNET EPS, and its implications for the future role of placing brokers operation in the Market. Whilst LIMNET EPS may now allow junior brokers to remotely process relatively straightforward insurance activities such as renewals and endorsements (i.e., simple changes to a policy) in an efficient manner, these simple functions have traditionally been an important way for junior brokers to develop and establish relationships with underwriters, a hallmark of the traditional Lloyd’s market.

Our case analysis suggests that a related concept to existential anxiety is dilemmas of expertise which in some ways parallels institutional reflexivity at the institutional level. In the face of turbulence and uncertainty in the London Insurance Market, dilemmas of expertise have developed as brokers and their clients have increasingly lost faith in traditional underwriting practices. The widespread lay knowledge of the riskiness of modern risk environments has been made visible by the huge losses in insurance markets globally. This has led to the awareness of the limits of expertise, as the fallibility of risk assessment techniques has become increasingly evident. It is thus somewhat difficult for trust to be sustained in traditional underwriting practices and expertise and has led to a questioning of the

Market’s cultural and relational norms. Associated with these dilemmas of expertise in the London Market, the sacred traditional role of the underwriter as the expert has come under attack by a number of participants in the insurance value chain. For example, empowered client organizations have reskilled, leading to the inevitable development of alternative forms of underwriting. Specifically, as discussed earlier, there has been a growth of self insurance and risk management departments in client firms.

In addition, some of the large global brokers, faced with the pressures of disintermediation across insurance markets, recently have made the strategic choice to move into underwriting. A chief operating officer of a large brokerage firm explained:

The accepted view in the broking industry is that in the long run there will not be sufficient profit in the chain for the broker and underwriter. This has set up competition in the chain between underwriters and brokers. Fewer brokers and underwriters will likely be able to survive.

These financial pressures, along with increasing skepticism regarding the underwriter’s expertise, has led top managers in brokerage firms to be reflexive about their role and identity as a broker in the value chain. Accordingly, these managers have increasingly sought to create new skills for brokers in the Market.

Reskilling and Empowerment. Whilst these anxieties and concerns over shifting identities were widespread in the Market, a few underwriters and brokers saw the possibility of at least partial reskilling and empowerment in the new trading environment facilitated by the LIMNET EPS system. These individuals viewed the EPS adoption as a way of developing technical skills and to use them as an opportunity to advance one’s own individual status in the firm. They could see and articulate a brighter side to the EPS adoption process, as one senior broker remarked:

EPS widens horizons, some people see it as making their job a dead end one, but it will allow me to work faster if everybody plays.

For this person and some other placing brokers, the time spent queuing, especially for following underwriters, could be more profitably used in developing and placing new insurance business with underwriters and clients alike. In this way, they saw opportunities for increased productivity. The EPS champion broker at one of the participating brokerage firms on the EPS pilot commented positively on the potential of the system:

I can work when I am not usually working . . . I can conduct normal broking in the morning outside the core underwriting hours . . . which is between 11:15–1 pm and 2:30–4:15 at Lloyd’s. With more time available, I can serve more clients and customers and be more productive.

In addition to facilitating increased productivity, this broker viewed the introduction of EPS, despite its technical limitations, as a means to improve his IT knowledge. He remarked positively on his EPS experience to date:

It was a new project to get working, it (EPS) has done me a power of good, I have learnt a lot about IT . . . I would sit there and they (the IT personnel) were forced to explain everything to me (as they were responsible for my delays) . . . I read everything the IT blokes gave me. It was good for me and I am very pleased with it (the EPS involvement).

These brokers have also debated the required frequency of face-to-face interaction required in the development and maintenance of relationships across the Market, and have suggested that close relations could be sustained at a distance through virtual communities with occasional need for face-to-face meetings. For some brokers, then, the use of EPS was perceived to positively enhance access to global insurance markets for risk placement. ## 6. Implications and Conclusions

In this section, we start out by emphasizing some key practical implications as suggested by our analysis of the introduction of the electronic trading system, LIMNET EPS, in the London Insurance Market. We then conclude by offering some implications for theory on IT and transformation and suggests ways in which the conceptual scheme presented in this paper might be extended in the future.

## 6.1. Implications for Electronic Trading in the London Market

Our research suggests that work transformation in the London Market associated with the adoption and diffusion of electronic trading is related to broader cultural changes in the late 20th century which include an acceleration of globalizing tendencies and their profound links to personal identity. In the following paragraphs some key implications, which have emerged from our case analysis, are discussed. These are summarized below in Table 6.

At the level of the Market, globalizing tendencies, which include the separation of time and space, disembedding mechanisms, and institutional reflexivity, are having important implications as to the strategic approaches taken by Lloyd’s. A key implication from our study is that Lloyd’s needs to mediate the seeming paradox of external adaptation to global pressures through technological leadership, whilst maintaining its unique culture and identity, which has been responsible for unrivalled innovation in insurance markets over the centuries. Such a challenge for Lloyd’s may be perhaps best described as a tight rope balancing act whose successful performance may be crucial for its long term success.

A second implication is that the dilemmas of expertise in the context of an increasingly competitive value chain may provide market participants with the opportunity to utilize IT to change their business roles and scope in the chain. Specifically, in the London Market exist opportunities for a power shift in the supply chain, related to possible changes as to who acts as knowledge worker in providing expertise, such as the pricing of insurance risks. This potential for shifting roles and linkages in the Market could have serious implications for the changing role of brokers and underwriters as trading partners. An important facilitator of these potential interorganizational transformations is the introduction of new information and communication technologies that allow for the development of informated organizations (Zuboff 1988) across the market. IT has the ability to translate and make visible what resides within the minds of individual underwriters. As such, underwriting expertise such as the pricing of different layers of a risk, will now be available and accessible to brokers through interorganizational placing systems such as LIMNET EPS. A senior brokerage director commented on the strategic use of information and communication technologies by the largest brokerage firm in the market at that time:

Their strategy seems to be to learn about underwriting using technology to facilitate this process. . . . That is learning from quotes provided by underwriters as to how they price layers of reinsurance. They believe the clients are (or belong to) the brokers and not the underwriters, and are trying to maintain their power and client base. Their long run strategy is to turn themselves into an insurance company.

The current development of a World Insurance Network (WIN) by global brokers, initiated at the end of 1995, is expected to further facilitate such capabilities, as brokers will then be in a more strategic position to tie underwriters to their own electronic risk placement system. They will be able to take advantage of the visibility of information afforded by the network, and thus develop underwriting knowledge and expertise over time. This would provide their brokers with an opportunity to develop as knowledge workers using electronic information to develop their underwriting expertise.

With these new capabilities, at least two implications concerning potential future strategies can be envisioned. First, brokers will be in a better position to negotiate underwriting rates on behalf of their clients, and to demonstrate to them that they are adding value in the insurance risk transaction. Second, a more extreme scenario would envision not just a change in power relations, but a radical shift to the broker as the knowledge worker in the supply chain. In such a scenario, the broker may price the risk themselves and determine which underwriter, in their new role as merely suppliers of risk capital, would accept the risk at the predetermined rate. In this way, the broker’s job may be somewhat enriched involving further intellectual skills in the risk placement process. The broker would, in effect, act as an insurance company accessing risk capital from a variety of underwriting markets worldwide.

These implications can be considered congruent with the current focus on knowledge management in the management literature, the perspective here being one of knowledge as learning. However, an implication from our analysis is that social and organizational issues need to be very carefully considered to effectively operationalize this knowledge management strategy. The above implications presuppose a smooth transition from the present physical Marketplace to the future electronically supported Market. In the former, knowledge in the Market is largely held by underwriters and brokers and power resides in their own private business relationships; in the latter, knowledge is publicly available across the firm through past information stored in the system. However, an implication from our study on the EPS system suggests that significant incentives may be required for such a transition to be a reality as there are likely to be perceived concerns by individual traders for autonomy, prestige, and maintaining control and power over their business relationships.

Finally, the introduction of electronic trading, in particular electronic placing support, is associated with changes in the Market participants’ work and worklife. Furthermore, the nature of expertise in this traditional market which has been largely built on face-toface relationships is being re-examined. Electronic trading support enables remote trading to develop with, perhaps, only occasional need for face-to-face interactions to establish or re-establish business relationships. Such changes have the potential to radically change the structuring of relationships, how they become established and how they are continued and/or enhanced. The implication is that there may be a need for substantial retraining of brokers and underwriters. Such retraining needs to be much broader than the traditional view of technical training. There is a need to raise the level of debate in the Market as to how brokers and underwriters will be able to develop, work, and function effectively in this new electronically supported environment. This may involve a reconceptualization of work processes in light of this mixed media environment (i.e., EPS and face-to-face) which is likely to involve the establishment of substantially new forms of trust relations.

## 6.2. Conclusions

This paper has sought to further our understanding of IT and work transformation by developing a conceptual scheme that allows for its analysis at the macroinstitutional level as well as the individual level. Our scheme focuses on addressing emerging themes in the literature, which link the macrolevel changes associated with the introduction of new technologies and the social transformation of professional groups. Our theoretical developments can be seen also to contribute to the literature on IT and social change which is currently characterized by mixed and contradictory findings (Barley 1986, Markus and Robey 1988, Orlikowski and Gash 1994), highlighting the need for novel approaches. We argue that the scheme provides a rich set of analytical concepts to understand IT and social transformation which build on earlier approaches such as cultural analysis (Robey and Azevedo 1994) and contextualism (Pettigrew 1990). Earlier approaches have emphasized the importance of the social context of implementation and the subjective meanings ascribed to IT (Robey and Sahay 1996). Our conceptual scheme seeks to fruitfully analyze electronic trading and work transformation at the institutional and individual levels. Specifically, our scheme emphasizes the importance of “rethinking” time and space in the structuring of social relations at the macrolevel, and focusing on the potential for both existential anxiety and reskilling at the individual level.

In the development of our scheme, we have pointed out that macrotheories such as those developed by Giddens that examine societal and work transformation in contemporary times can be extended to explicitly consider the role of information technology, in particular its development and use across modern institutions. We have aimed to demonstrate that such an incorporation of IT is indeed possible, although only a modest start has been attempted here. Further developments of this scheme could also extend the levels of analysis beyond Giddens’ original focus on the macroinstitutional and individual levels to account for the organizational, interorganizational, and group levels, depending on the nature of the case study.

Finally, specific modifications could be made to tailor our conceptual scheme to examine a wide range of IS research issues such as the social redesign of technology or IT implementation across different sectors and/or national economies. Whilst we chose to illustrate the scheme using our case study in the London Market, we have carried out detailed empirical work in other sectors such as banking, government, telecommunications, and manufacturing, and in other economies including North America, Malaysia, India, and Jamaica. In all of these sectors and national economies, we found similar links between the influence of broad global trends at the societal level, and the changing nature of work and work-identity at the individual level.
