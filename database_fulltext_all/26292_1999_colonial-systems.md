# Colonial Systems

Jaana Porra

Department of Management Science and Information Systems, CBA 5.202, College and Graduate School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712-1175 Jaana.Porra@bus.utexas.edu

olonial systems are presented as an alternative to the mechanistic and organic systems paradigms. They are based upon the inclusion of an evolutionary social history (Ernst Mayr), species-level evolution (Eldredge and Gould), and local context (Martin Heidegger) as central premises in the definition of human systems. The colonial viewpoint of systems is differentiated from the mechanistic and organic models by ten different axioms that define how systems operate during both homeostasis (stability) and radical change. From the axiomatic descriptions of colonial systems, the method of punctuated prototyping is derived to clarify how local colonies survive and prosper by creating and adopting isolated prototypes. This change mechanism (1) requires the self-awareness to recognize the necessity for change, and (2) describes the empowerment necessary for the colonists to build and adopt successful prototypes. The colonial systems model can be applied to explanations about how information systems, organizations, and social institutions change. Further, colonies can be used as a metaphor to design new human systems that capture a greater degree of humanness than do the models of machines and organisms.

## 1. Introduction

We must redouble our efforts to take a leadership role in research on information and systems, broadly considered. Leadership requires adherence to high standards of quality, but as denominated by contribution and not only by convenient metrics inherited from established doctrine. We must adjust to the features of the frontiers we are exploring, and in turn, bring new insights from that frontier back to the established disciplinary communities. We must in other words, accept the risks and rewards of pioneers. (King 1993, p. 98)

With the new millennium, we are more cognizant than ever of the need to break from the traditions of the past. Our last thousand-year history has seen innumerable advances in scientific understanding replete with machines, medicines, and intellectual machinations all designed to enhance the human condition. Yet our modern human institutions and the effects they have on individuals are often little better than those of the medieval world. Though we have conquered famine, disease, space and time, at the close of the millennium the human condition remains full of trepidation and degradation. This is perhaps not a shortcoming in the extant institutions and organizations themselves, but due to the fundamental ways that we think about the systems—human and otherwise—that we design and deploy. The objective of this discussion of colonial systems is to provide an alternative to these design and analysis fundamentals, so that, in the future, we may build on human empowerment and autonomy rather than continue to rely on traditions that have devalued humanness.

Many of our explanations about societies, organizations, and groups have been based upon the theory of formal or mechanistic systems. Such explanations allow us to view our social endeavors as collections of predefined rules. Cultural traditions, rituals, and bureaucracies have all been cast as formal structures with fixed purposes and procedures. More recently, however, mechanistic system theory has been supplanted by the organic systems viewpoint. Although the organic paradigm adds “feedback and control” to the “formal rule-based” structures of the mechanistic paradigm, both fail to address the dynamic nature of change that occurs within social, organizational, and group environments and the information systems that support them. The rigid assumptions of mechanistic systems can hardly do more than suggest bureaucracies or computer systems composed of rule-based hierarchies. The more recent organic model does provide a mechanism for homeostasis (stability) through its inclusion of feedback. However, the organic model also includes an implicit assumption of progressive evolution, which is incorrect. The gradual accumulation of features that have survival value is not the evolutionary mechanism observed in nature or in our human systems. The assumed gradualism of the organic model fails to capture the nature of turbulent change that we observe both in the biological and human organizational record. While most theorists do not explicitly acknowledge their reliance on either the mechanistic or the organic model, the two are pervasive and, in the case of the organic model, often misapplied.

As an alternative to these two models, we propose a colonial system explanation which is based on more recent knowledge of how biological evolution proceeds through periods of stability (homeostasis) and radical change (punctuation). In this punctuated equilibrium model described by Niles Eldredge and Stephen J. Gould (1972), evolution does not only occur at the level of individual genes, as implied by the organic model, but also manifests itself at the higher level of the species (speciation). Using these species-level phenomena, we define a colony as a voluntary collection of individuals with the shared characteristics of (1) a common evolutionary social history (phylogeny); (2) common methods for realizing both stability and radical change (species level evolution); and (3) a common local context (being-in-the-world). These characteristics portray members of the colony as volunteers who share a common historical background and a unique local context. They also cooperate to maintain their collective conditions or change them when local conditions dictate that the historical methods no longer apply. Such colonial behavior is very different from the mechanistic or organic models. A mechanistic system can only operate within its hierarchy of rules no matter what the conditions and will ultimately fail when new environmental conditions appear. An organic system can be envisioned that might survive longer than a mechanistic system by making local corrections to its behaviors during dynamic times. But this organic model will also ultimately fail as the inadequacy of local refinement is overwhelmed by the magnitude of environmental change. The colonial viewpoint, however, attempts to explain how history, local refinement, and radical change are all required to operate in periods of both calm and turbulence.

In the biological sense, the ability of colonies to survive is predicated on the occurrence of unique genetic material that gives the colony survival value in times of radical change. This is not the case with the organic model that suggests that change is gradual and cumulatively progressive. Both the fossil record of biological systems and the history of human systems demonstrate that change is rare and, for the most part, stability is the norm. When change does occur it is violent, sudden, and complete. Though the cellular organic model relies on the fortunes of their genes to provide the capacity to change, colonies possess the ability to change through their independent and autonomous actions—beyond the limitations of genetic chance and the continuation of mechanistic rules. Viewing human systems as colonies rather than machines (mechanistic) or single-cell animals (organic) can provide a rich explanatory method to describe the dynamics of human endeavors.

To establish colonial systems as an alternative system foundation, we will first present two reviews. In the first view, we attempt to establish that our existing viewpoints about humanness rely on one of two underlying systems theories—either the mechanistic, or the organic. We will compare these two competing system theories in terms of their basic assumptions. This demonstration will show that both of the models are: (1) woefully inadequate to describe the complexity of human systems; and (2) limited to formal rule-based structures or simple feedback mechanisms that do not allow for significant change. In the second review we criticize the pervasive use of the two system paradigms for all types of human systems, using examples from information system research, organizational, and social theory. The microlevel nature of both the mechanistic and organic models will be shown as inappropriately applied to human systems at many different levels of scale and with many different units of analysis. While many authors claim to have transcended the mechanistic and organic, their models continue to apply the same fundamental assumptions.

After these reviews and criticisms, colonial systems are presented in the fourth section employing the same attributes used to compare the mechanistic and organic system models. Colonies are shown to be differentiated by their phylogeny or historical genesis rather than by their ontogeny or life cycle as assumed in the organic paradigm. They also possess a unique local context and exhibit cooperative behaviors appropriate for both maintaining stability and initiating change. The next section presents the concept of “punctuating prototyping” as a way to capture the colonial nature of human systems. This central component of the colonial model suggests that human systems must possess the ability to generate, build, and test alternative structures (prototypes). Additionally, the colony must possess the ability to recognize the need for adopting the characteristics of the successful prototypes. This autonomy from historical rules and the empowerment to alter its own internal structures gives the colony the required abilities to operate in both stasis and turbulence. Following the definition of colonial systems and the application of the punctuated prototyping, the paper concludes with suggestions about how to use the colonial model to more realistically describe and design humans systems.

Though we are aware that the viewpoint of colonies lies outside the traditional approaches to both systems and their application to human endeavors, it is critical to provide this comprehensive presentation to show how previous approaches differ from colonies, and how colonies might be used as an alternative explanatory mechanism. If colonies successfully extend the theory of systems, then perhaps we can also transcend the reliance on mechanical and organic evolutionism to explain humanness. Colonial systems have the potential to supplant our current system models that reduce humanness to rule-based hierarchies and replace them with a viewpoint where humanness is predicated on autonomy, cooperation, and empowerment.

## 2. Humanness as a System

The essential explanatory process in most literature on social and group phenomena is based on the use of the concept of a “system” to encapsulate and relate the processes and components of human behavior (Klir 1985). Systems are also used both to describe social interaction and to design organizations and information systems to direct behavior according to socially constructed contracts. As such, all human enterprises are based on some underlying idea of how people work together to produce the purposeful results (Schwartz and Kelly 1996). Presenting phenomena, be they social, economic, or technical, as systems has been shown to be a powerful way of directing the human kind (von Bertalanffy 1968, Emery 1970, Churchman 1971, 1979a, 1979b, Klir 1972, Laszlo 1972, Gray and Rizzo 1973a, 1973b, Weizenbaum 1976, Rapoport 1986, Wiener 1989). This reliance on a systems viewpoint extends far beyond our original use of systems theory to explain machines and other formal processes.

Like the scientific literature, sociology and other behavioral sciences use system descriptions to show how humans relate to each other and how the relationships operate through time. This engineering metaphor has been lifted directly from the hard science community and applied to human-based activity often without considering the consequences of such a metaphorical shift. Even though social science authors often attempt to transcend the confines of a systems model, their explanations about human endeavors are still essentially based on systems models that originated in science and engineering. This is because social scientists have not developed system theoretical premises potentially more suitable for their subject matter—humanness.

The history of system theories as explanations of humanness is a multifaceted phenomenon. Typical of a modern viewpoint, Geertz (1973), for example, approaches the study of social science by examining the tasks that groups undertake. Humanness for Geertz is the study of culture, and the games, social events, and artifacts with a shared purpose that is either written down or acted out. Concepts such as “life is a game,” “life is a stage,” and “life is a text” all describe the formal rules of behavior that characterize purposeful human interaction. This is not far from the essential systems theory assumption that systems are “purposeful” (Ackoff 1970, Emery 1970). Though Geertz attempts to view culture as being independent from the individuals who create it and as an abiological phenomenon, he states that the study of culture can be viewed as no more than “writing out of the systematic rules, an ethnographic algorithm, which, if followed, would make it possible so to operate, to pass (physical appearance aside) for a native” (p. 11). Though Geertz limits his discussions to human endeavors, his basic way of explaining humanness is to express it in terms of a system of rules.

The use of system descriptions as the common metaphor for explaining virtually all types of human behavior is perhaps more critical to the foundations of humanness than the specific details of any of the alternative approaches. System descriptions are applied in cases where humanness is expressed as a biological phenomenon, as in the case of evolutionism. They are also employed by those theorists who conclude that the essence of humanness lies beyond a biological description. One would expect a keen debate about system premises at all levels of human systems. Since all human designs are based on some theory (acknowledged or not), the available theories, principles, and axioms determine the possible outcomes. In more practical terms, this means that the societies, businesses, groups, and computer networks we design will contain the long-term mechanism for their own success or failure from the outset because of the kind of system principles they have been designed to rely on. When studied inside of disciplines, the number of such alternative basic theories may appear high (cf., Giddens and Turner 1987, Burns 1994). But from the perspective of their underlying foundations (i.e., beliefs, axioms, or theoretical assumptions), it can be shown that there are only two alternative system models available:—the mechanistic and the organic. Before attempting to demonstrate how these two system models are applied to discussions about humanness, the history and fundamentals of the two traditional approaches will be presented.

## 3. The Two Theories of Systems

From the strictest system theory perspective, only two archetypes are available for human systems—the mechanistic and the organic (Ashby 1972). All other human designs are derived from their basic axioms. Today, mechanistic and organic premises cause the world to appear as either a machine or a simple organic cell, respectively. At the level of the basic assumptions, no other equally coherent and adequately consistent ways of explaining systems exist. In order to compare the two, it is necessary to examine the specific assumptions made by each approach to systems.

## 3.1. Properties of Mechanistic and Organic Systems

Applications of mechanistic and organic systems have been discussed in contexts such as organizations (cf., Burns and Stalker 1961; Morgan 1986) and information systems (cf., Mason and Mitroff 1973). Additionally, authors such as Miller (1995<sup>1</sup>) have done considerable multidisciplinary work in identifying parallels between organic system properties and social systems at various levels of analysis, including cells, organs, organisms, groups, organizations, and societies. However, if one wants to return to the specifications of mechanistic and organic systems as formulated by the original general systems theorists, the literature is seldom comprehensive in describing the axioms. Most authors are not concerned with the lack of clarity in their underlying premises. They use mechanistic and organic systems as metaphors with no particular concern for a detailed logical analysis or accuracy of the involved system model (cf., Burns and Stalker in Morgan 1986). For example, anything fluid, flexible, rapidly or radically changing is called “organic,” although the original organic system model only supports gradual change within a specific range. It is also customary to discuss “organic systems” and “living systems” as if they were synonyms, when, in fact, the former is an abstract model on which systems have been explained, while the latter refers to observations about actual living organisms.

Ernst Mayr has acknowledged this problem in the context of biology. Dissatisfied with the mechanistic and organic models and unable to find any source which enumerates the central properties of inanimate and organic life outside of general systems theory, Mayr wrote his own (Mayr 1982). From his biological viewpoint, Mayr suggests that the ten critical assumptions about living systems are: phylogeny (evolution), ontogeny (lifetime), boundaries, complexity, structure, growth, goals, power, change, and control. These ten axiomatic characteristics can be used to compare the existing paradigms of mechanistic and organic systems and to characterize the proposed colonial system. The purpose of adopting Mayr’s list of living systems is to analyze whether colonies in nature and, thus, comparable human social formations can adequately be described by the current system premises. The table also presents the alternative colonial properties that will be discussed in detail in the remainder of the paper beginning in § 4—Colonial Systems.

## 3.2. Mechanistic Systems

The older of the two existing system paradigms has a history reaching back at least two thousand years. Even texts as old as Plato’s discuss forms that contributed to the kind of thinking that is known today as a mechanistic system (Ashby 1972, Mayr 1982). Generally, a mechanistic system is an assembly or set of related elements (van Gigch 1978). Beyond this definition, all of our varied concepts of mechanistic systems and their enhancements can be characterized by the attributes shown in column 1 of Table 1 (see § 3.1).

Specifically, a mechanistic system does not have temporality. As soon as the purpose (goal) for the design is determined by the initiator, owner, or builder of the machine, the system stops changing. This means it cannot evolve (i.e., it has no biological phylogeny) or have a lifetime of birth, growth, maturity, and death (i.e., it has no biological ontogeny), as living systems do. It merely degenerates (like an aging automobile) due to laws of physics. Changing parts will prolong the life of the system. However, over time, the overall effect of replacing old parts with new ones becomes ever reduced until the system cannot be saved (changing parts in an automobile will keep the vehicle in traffic longer but not forever). Entropy (disorganization) eventually takes over (automobile turns into junk). Because a mechanistic system does not recognize time, its boundaries are unchanging. A mechanistic system, therefore, is an unchanging object organized into parts and subparts of specialized pieces, which do not have meaning independent of the system. A mechanistic system is an object or a thing. Like a thing, a mechanistic organization or an information system design can only be altered by addition or division that does not affect the existing structure. Mechanistic change can only be accomplished by reduction and fractionalization. Because of these characteristics, mechanistic systems tend to become increasingly complex and grow in size over time without changing the overall purpose of the system.

When applied to organizations and their information systems, mechanistic principles lead to centralized power and control (cf., Morgan 1986; Perrow 1986). Metaphorically speaking, mechanistic human designs can be compared with empires surrounded by walls. Such empires tend to be long lasting through intentional isolation. They represent each participant and information system as impersonal and unchanging tasks (sets of rules) in an environment that hinders change. Mechanistic environments are designed to support the longevity of the design, not the participating people. Weber (1947) once believed that these mechanistic bureaucracies were eternal. Today, the mechanistic system principles are used in modern organizational design far beyond their role in defining bureaucracies. In spite of the numerous attempts to provide alternatives, most modern organizational designs are still mechanistic at their foundation level (Kast and Rosenweig 1972, Melcher 1975, Pondy and Mitroff 1979, Morgan 1986, Perrow 1986).

Mechanistic principles also continue to form the core of information systems development. Information system design approaches, such as the life cycle model, structured approach, and prototyping, are largely mechanistic by nature (Porra 1996). As such, most computer systems resulting from these design assumptions only automate operation-level routine tasks in relatively stable organizational circumstances. The same mechanistic principles have been used in attempts to automate the human mind in the field of artificial intelligence with predictably poor results (Graubard 1989). Today the results of mechanistic attempts, be they related to corporate structure, its information system, or the human mind, are widely criticized and their limitations well documented (Kast and Rosenweig 1970, 1972, Dreyfus 1979, Dreyfus and Dreyfus 1986, 1989, Pagels 1989; Fitzgerald 1996). As a result, many disciplines have abandoned mechanistic axioms for human designs and begun a search for fresh alternative approaches.

## 3.3. Organic Systems

Since Aristotle, the organic notion has been used liberally to refer to almost any analogy with nature, including theories about the human body; neural networks; organizations; economics; and most recently, the Internet. Early this century, however, the modern organic principles for human designs were redefined following a realization that the mechanistic methods fail to capture life (Rosen 1972). Today, the organic model is presented in almost every context where change or growth is discussed. This is often done without specifically stating the assumptions that underlie the organic paradigm.

During the past hundred years, the modern meaning of an organic system has been redefined mainly by men such as Charles Darwin, Herbert Spencer, and Ludwig von Bertalanffy (Gray and Rizzo 1973a, 1973b, Yates 1987, Mayr 1982). At the core of the organic model are Charles Darwin’s four theories of evolution (Darwin 1979<sup>2</sup>):

(1) the theory of common descent;

(2) the theory of speciation;

(3) the theory of gradual evolution; and

(4) the theory of natural selection

which form the modern foundation for understanding natural change in general (Darwin 1979, Gould 1987, Gould and Lewontin 1979, Gould et al. 1987). Together these four theories explain evolution as a gradual, continuous unfolding of events by incremental adaptation to the immediate environment. Lineages that most effectively mutate (i.e., present random changes beneficial in their immediate surroundings) survive while others become extinct. Adaptation is the only way of changing and is the overarching goal of the system (Mayr 1982). As mentioned earlier, Darwinian evolution has so widely been accepted in natural and social sciences as the mode of all change related to natural, technical, economical, and social phenomena, that it qualifies as a universal philosophy of change (Gould 1986).

Herbert Spencer (Spencer 1972, 1986, 1987a, 1987b) significantly influenced the adoption of this philosophy of change. Spencer established the idea that natural selection actually means “survival of the fittest” (Mayr 1982). Yet according to Darwin and the modern theory of evolution, there are no superior or inferior species, individuals, or genes (Gould 1992). Darwin actually never used the phrase “survival of the fittest” (Mayr 1982, p. 386). This tragic twisting of words was Spencer’s misinterpretation of Darwin’s theory of evolution. As a matter of fact, Darwin reminded himself never to use terms “higher” or “lower” (Gould 1992, p. 36). Yet, according to this Spencerian belief, now commonly called Social Darwinism (Hofstadter 1994), change is progressive. Evolution is a competition in which the superior will prevail. Therefore, change will always be for the better, since the inferior are continuously eliminated. According to Mayr (1982), Spencer’s influence on understanding change has been all negative. Although biologists no longer accommodate ideas of progress, Spencerian thinking continues to influence social sciences and economics (Gould 1992). That is, in spite of the high proportion of information systems development failures [Lyytinen 1987, Hirschheim and Lyytinen 1987], information systems research is still primarily based on this Spencerian idea of progress. Today, Darwin’s theories, combined with the Spencerian misconception, form the underlying assumptions for most modern explanations of evolution and change involving humans.

In the 1930s and after World War II, a theoretical mathematician, Ludwig von Bertalanffy, father of the field of the general systems theory, enhanced the near hundred-year-old organic change principles. His work stemmed from the basic question about what kinds of systems could engage in Darwinian/Spencerian evolution. Von Bertalanffy’s question led to the development of the theory of open systems (von Bertalanffy 1950, 1952, 1968). This theory, also called the general theory of systems, provides the theoretical assumptions for most modern organic human designs.

The model of an open system was patterned after an organic cell which evolves as a part of an organism in continuous adaptive change as an organized hierarchical structure of wholes (cells as an organic system) within wholes (organisms as an organic system) within wholes (ecology). Von Bertalanffy hypothesized that in order to change, this system had to interact with its environment. This means that all cells interact with other cells and the organism interacts with its environment. Von Bertalanffy defined reactive (adaptive) interaction in terms of the exchange of materia through input, processing, output, and feedback (control) between the system and its environment. Unlike mechanistic design principles, the organic design paradigm is filled with analogies from life. The open system grows from birth, through adolescence, to maturity and ultimately to death. The organic system is believed to: have a lifetime ontogeny; be simple when young; increasing in complexity while growing old; and dying of overwhelming complexity and rigidity at the end of its life cycle.

Following von Bertalanffy, the notion of materia being processed through the organic system is provided by correctly identifying the boundaries of the system and its environment. Though easy to see at the cellular level, the notion of input-process-output-feedback is more difficult (if not impossible) to identify in systems like groups, organizations, and societies. Even more difficult for the organic model is the central requirement of a genetic program (i.e., DNA). An organic system can only change in a way predefined in its genes (which are perceived as executing like a computer program). For all other so-called organic systems like groups and organizations, this change mechanism is not present. To date, researchers and designers have ignored this omission which dictates that, in the strictest sense, the organic paradigm does not support living designs. The roots of the organic assumptions are in models of cells. These cell models are, in turn, patterned after algorithmic processes like computer programs—not after life, humanness, or its manifestations in teams, groups, or organizations.

According to von Bertalanffy, it is not a coincidence that his open-systems model and, consequently, the organic paradigm resembles the workings of the general purpose machine or the computer (von Bertalanffy 1968). The open-systems model paralleled the development of the computer and was adapted from early computing principles. Additionally, the organic model appears familiar to those working with computers for other reasons. It has been used to describe a computerbased information system that receives input from the environment (users); processes it according to predefined rules; delivers output to the environment (users); and provides a feedback loop for corrective actions by the users reprocessing data or manipulating the processing rules for a better fit (maintenance). It has also been applied to “open-systems” technologies, “opencomputer networks,” and to explain the Internet as a social phenomenon.

A second shift in the organic theory has been referred to as the system/environment shift. During the past few decades, self-referencing or autopoiesis has come to play a central role in this transition (cf., Maturana and Varela 1980, Yates 1987). With insight into the recursive closure of systems that use their own output as input, cybernetics was forced to abandon the classical input/output model of the organic paradigm, together with its emphasis on mastery and control (Knodt 1995). In 1960, Heinz von Foerster introduced the “order from noise” principle as the defining characteristic of self-organizing systems (von Foerster

1960). This concept was a result of interdisciplinary research between physicists, cyberneticians, logicians, and biologists. Since then, the modern incarnation of the organic model—self-referencing—has been used to explain the behavior of subatomic particles, cells, weather patterns, insect colonies, and the stock market (Yates 1987). Thus the central distinction of these selfreferencing models has been to reconsider the control and power aspects of organic systems. This allows the power and control that were supplied by predefined rules in both the mechanistic and organic models to be supplanted with the endogenous ability to recognize the need for change and create new strategies for survival. Although a sense of self is clearly required to break from the limitations of fixed programs of action, there is little offered by self-organization, selfreferencing, or autopoiesis to explain the mechanism by which the self is expressed. Determining how to change behavior in other than predefined ways has been the goal of many cognitive and system models of thinking. But, to date, none have captured the ability to use self-awareness as a means to recognize the necessity for change and implement the modified behavior. Though we can be certain that humanness encapsulates these abilities, simple extensions of the mechanistic and organic paradigms fail to explain how self-awareness exhibits the ability to generate new goals. We are left with the assumption that both power and control as expressed in the organic viewpoint are, in general, feedback and control processes that have a very limited operating environment. They do not result in the ability to break out of the structures and hierarchies that define the organic system itself, which is operating homeostatically (adapting to the local environment to maintain stability).

## 3.4. Recent Organic Models

The pervasiveness of the organic system paradigm can be seen in many recent application areas. It has been used vigorously in information systems, organizational studies, and the social sciences. While many of these applications are traditional organic treatises, others offer novel organic interpretations. We can now review some of these interpretations and highlight where the organic axioms play a central role in explaining some of the misinterpretations of evolution and systems in general.

Evolution (phylogeny) has been recognized as a factor in the information systems research field since the early days of the field (Ackoff 1970). The consensus is that information systems evolve. The nature of this evolution, however, is not agreed upon (Ein-Dor and Segev 1993, Basalla 1988). Most often, the term evolution is used to refer to change of any kind. Chambers (1983) maintains that this state of affairs is “semantically unsound and . . . hazardous to the health of our public image.” Evolution, he points out, is a technical term with a specific meaning ignored by the information systems field.

However, the relaxed attitude toward principles of evolution in the information systems field is not universal. For example, Lucas (1978) introduced the method of “evolutionary design” in which evolution referred to stages of user suggestion, initial groping, mutual progress, conversion, and maturity. As the system matures, change becomes less frequent. Lucas evolution is not based on natural principles. Instead, they refer to engineering a product according to the principles of the organic ontogeny (lifetime). Similar ways of using the term evolution can be found by other authors such as Naumann and Jenkins (1982) or Blum (1982). In information systems research, the notion of a prototype is often used nebulously. Usually, the term refers to a progressive model of a future product: the first of the type (Budde et al. 1984). In this meaning, a prototype is a thing comparable with the mechanistic/ organic system explanation level principles. However, in the information systems literature, there are references to open-ended evolutionary prototypes, in which the final scope and role of the system is not defined in advance, but evolves according to the changing needs of the participating users (Rzevski and Road 1984). We use the term prototype in the latter sense and suggest that open-ended, evolutionary prototypes qualitatively differ from the more common productprototype metaphors at the axiomatic level.

More recently, Ein-Dor and Segev (1993) discussed the notion of evolution in the information systems field in detail. They point to Nolan’s stage hypotheses (Nolan 1973) as dominant examples. While Nolan’s stage hypotheses are often referred to as an evolutionary model, they fail to portray necessary characteristics for change (King and Kramer 1984). According to King and Kramer, at best, Nolan’s model is evolutionist by nature. In the information systems field, the problem is that evolutionist and evolutionary are used interchangeably (Ein-Dor and Seger 1993). The evolutionist premises usually refer to deterministic developments with predefined goals. Evolutionary most often means “open-ended.” Axioms for a system that could support this kind of change are not specified.

The reliance on the organic paradigm has been influential in the sociotechnical school, pioneered by scholars such as Enid Mumford (1997, 1990a, 1990b, 1992, Mumford and Weir 1979). According to this school of thought, the organic premises are more human than the mechanistic systems assumptions. When applied to organizations, the organic systems model of wholes-within-wholes-within-wholes is seen to support organizations of empowered teams (power). In organic organizations, these teams would participate in making decisions about their working circumstances, their future career, and the way computers are used to create more human information systems. Since the emergence of the sociotechnical school of thought, the information systems field has developed directions based on political theories, action learning, marxist economic theory, institutional economics, and employed qualitative research methods such as ethnography, stories, and in-depth cases to complement its relatively mechanistic past. The status of the field as a “fragmented adhocracy” is largely seen as enrichinging (Hirschheim et al. 1996). The premises of the information systems research are mostly borrowed from other disciplines such as cultural anthropology and social theory. The diverse research directions do not provide new interpretations of the mechanistic or organic model.

Outside the computer field, the organic axioms have been discussed extensively, in the organizational design literature. In Burns and Stalker’s (1961) classic empirical studies combining science and industry, the first people to recognize the “ideal types” of mechanistic and organic management systems were industrial scientists, company directors, skilled workers, production engineers, and foremen to name a few. After the initial excitement about the possibilities of complementing mechanistic organizing models with organic models, however, organic theory retreated back to mechanistic principles (Burns 1994). After decades of attempts by researchers such as Lawrence and Lorch (1967) and Katz and Kahn (1978), the field of organizational theory declared its failure to replace the mechanistic principles with the organic ones (Melcher 1975). During the 1970s, the organic foundations were discarded because they were too difficult to implement in human designs. In the late 1970s this manifested itself as a retreat from the wholesale rejection of the bureaucratic model (Burns 1994).

The publication of the full text of Weber’s Economy and Society in 1968 did something to rekindle the interest in his account of bureaucracy. A good deal of work was put into the search for some overall principle which would either incorporate or supersede the work of the previous two decades. (Burns 1994, p. xix)

During the 1980s and 1990s organizational studies have been enriched by many new perspectives by researchers in social theory, social psychology, and economics. The problem of this rapid diversification of organizational study to new and varied directions is well summarized by Burns.

What had been a variegated but nevertheless interrelated and fairly coherent set of ideas about the nature of organization— which amounted to what might be called a recognized ‘state of the art’ body of knowledge—seemed to be drowned in a welter of disparate approaches and findings. (Burns 1994, p. xx)

Burns asserts that this circumstance of diversity in organizational studies may have made way to hard-line managerialism (mechanistic paradigm revisited), which has manifested itself in the United States and Europe over the past several years (Burns 1994). Barley and Kunda (1992) go even further in their conclusions about the managerial discourse over the past century. According to them, what has appeared as a progressive development from industrial betterment (1870– 1900) through scientific management (1900–1923), human relations (1925–1955), systems rationalism (1955– 1980), to organizational culture and quality (1980– present) has, in reality, been no more than oscillation between mechanistic and organic forms of social organization. What in some circles has been viewed as a welcome richness of theory and method in social sciences has been taken to be a weakness in others. Not being clear about one’s system theoretical foundations may equal having no theoretical leg to stand on. More importantly, however, changing one’s point of view from classical management to culture, race, gender, action, stories, information, language, texts, social gatherings, etc. does not necessarily change the system theoretical axioms of the explanation.

In addition to the use of the organic paradigm in information systems research and organizational theory literature, it has also been used to describe social institutions in general. Today, social systems are seen to be separate from the human beings that participate in them (e.g., Luhmann) or they are taken to occupy an assigned abiological space—the mind (e.g., Giddens). The reliance on an occult entity such as “individual consciousness,” “symbolic system,” or “rite” as humanness shows the need to emancipate humanity from the destiny of the body (Lewontin et al. 1984). More importantly, however, while the organic principles are clearly visible in the work above, the general theory of systems is said to have an insignificant role in modern social theory (Giddens et al. 1987). Rhetoric aside, it is apparent that these fields persist in pursuing systems (abstract, symbolic, and idealistic) as the content for humanness. Some (e.g., Luhmann) acknowledge their theoretical premises in the organic paradigm, others (e.g., Giddens) appear to apply organic premises while not acknowledging the influence of general systems theory in their work. Many are not clear what their theory for humanness is (e.g. poststructuralism) and yet others do not wish to discuss underlying assumptions at all (e.g., Geertz).

At first sight, it appears that both social and natural sciences have a rich multilevel theory of change. Such is the number of levels of analysis in both fields. As discussed in the previous sections, however, this apparent wealth of models of change is more obvious when compared with the current theory of change in general. At all levels of analysis, be they social or natural, the theory of change dictates either a mechanistic or an organic stance. Thus, the level of scale, the unit of analysis or the origin of the system in nature or society makes no difference in how change is seen to proceed. Because both paradigms of change are in fact patterned after objects (simple machine and cell), it is possible to claim that current mainstream social and natural sciences share a paradigm of inanimate microlevel change. Both fields treat change as if it occurred to things—systems with no way of influencing their trajectory beyond executing a program. These objects do not have self-awareness or awareness of others. Although appealing and useful, in the strictest sense, any application of the current change paradigms to people must be considered misguided because of the failure at the axiomatic level of systems. No theory of objects should include humans.

It is not exaggerated to conclude that nearly all change, be it social or natural, is perceived to be gradual, continuous (and in social sciences progressive) by nature. This perception is based on beliefs rather than proof. Natural evolution is a phenomenon too long in duration to be scientifically proved or disproved (Eldredge and Gould 1972). Thus, whether evolution at all levels and units of analysis is actually Darwinian remains a controversial question. We submit that reductionism has been a restricting stance in understanding the nature and evolution of humanness. Consequently, it has limited organizational and information systems designs. At even higher organizational design levels, such as those that impact societies and culture, the assumption that gradual change will yield progress is often mistaken.

To summarize, the central claim of this paper is that the current human system design principles do not include humanness because they reduce humanness into an idea of a gene or an organism. Specifically, at the assumptions level, the current foundations of modern synthesis in biology and, consequently, the social systems do not distinguish a social level of natural evolution for Homo sapiens from the individual level. Thus, in social sciences, a near universally accepted belief— at all levels of analysis—and in nature or social systems, evolution occurs according to the organic paradigm. We propose that because of this uniformity of the theory of change, any natural tendencies of the human species, such as colony formation and maintenance, as a product of millennia of natural evolution, are not included in social theory, organizational or computer-based information system design principles. The mechanistic and the organic models are essentially individual or microlevel models because they were born from observations of things, not from observations of social systems (Figure 1.)

The implication is that any phenomenon explained by applying mechanistic or organic premises is going to be about things not about living beings. We submit that providing alternative theoretical foundations for social systems requires an understanding of what is essential in humanness. It seems natural to suggest it must be lifelike. ## 4. Colonial Systems

## 4.1. Colonies

In a strict biological sense, whether plant or animal life, a colony is a society of organisms that are highly integrated, either by physical union of the bodies or by division into specialized individuals and groups, or by both (Wilson 1980). In the biological vernacular and even in some technical texts a colony can, however, refer to almost any group of organisms, especially if they are in a fixed locality. In Wilson’s Sociobiology (1980), the colony notion is best restricted to the societies of social insects, together with tightly integrated masses of sponges, siphonophores, bryozoans, and other “colonial” invertebrates. In these highly controlled colonies, mobility is often nonexistent. Thus, colony refers to sharing physical parts and, consequently, translates into certain death to soloists. Some colonies are so tightly integrated that they resemble a single organism. Hence, these superorganisms have learned how to make organs out of individuals. According to Beklemishev (1969), three complementary trends occur in the formation of invertebrates into physically coupled colonies:

(1) weakening of the individuality by physical continuity, sharing of organs, decrease in size and life span, specialization into simpler, highly dependent heterozoids;

(2) the intensification of the individuality of the colony, by means of more elaborate, stereotyped body form and closer physiological and behavioral integration of the zooids; and

(3) the development of cormidia, or “colonies within colonies.”

Banta (1973) has concluded that coloniality is first increased by division of labour, then by physiological interdependence and, finally, by structural interdependence. In later stages, all three processes proceed concurrently.

Physically coupled colonies conform to the organic paradigm. The organic paradigm may also be an appropriate frame of reference for physically coupled colonies since they truly consist of organisms within organisms within organisms. Although these kinds of colonies seem to provide analogies for human social systems, in the local context of humans, colonies formed by mobile individuals who can choose to stay or leave the colony are of more interest.<sup>3</sup>

A striking quality of mobile colonies is that they are as diverse as the species that portray this social behavior (cf., Wilson 1980). In some colonies, individuals have highly specialized tasks (insect colonies) that may last for a lifetime (wasp colonies) or relatively short periods of time (ant colonies). Males (dolphins) or females (wasp colonies) may start colonies. Colonies may portray hierarchical relationships (wasp colonies), or they may be leaderless individuals acting in concert (schools of fish). Individuals join some colonies for life (termite colonies), others are joined on a temporary basis (schools of fish). For some colonies nomadism has been determined as a necessary condition (schools of fish), while in others individuals carry on diverse activities such as grooming and playing (whiptail wallaby). Some colonies portray altruistic cooperative behaviors (dolphins). Some colonies have only few individuals, while others are known to have thousands (dolphins). Some colonies travel far (dolphins) and others stay near the parent colony. Many colonies occur in plains and savannas among nonterritorial herds dividing casually while traveling (vicuria). Others build permanent immobile homes (termites). Some colonies form around matriarchs while males lead solitary lives throughout their lives (elephants). Chimpanzee colonies are known for their high degree of cooperation, while orangutans don’t form colonies at all. Some colonies exchange their members with other colonies they encounter (chimpanzees); others do so only reluctantly (elephants). Mobility seems to add to the diversity of form and content of the colonies. Different colonies of different species portray vastly differing behaviors. All colonies, however, are based on being together over time. We propose that the characteristics (e.g., dolphinness, fishness, or humanness) that most describe a species occur at this level. Instead of dictating a set of unchanging qualities as humanness (such as in sociobiology), we propose that colonies are systems that facilitate the formation of collections of interdependent members and evolution of characteristics that maintain collective stasis and can create or respond to change through collective awareness. Like animal colonies in nature, human colonial systems may form spontaneously and evolve like a species. Such specieslike evolution exhibits periods of both radical change and stasis. These two modes are influenced by the history of the colony, the context of others, as well as by the context of environment and technology in a pervasively inclusive way that can be called humanness.

## 4.2. Colonial Systems

Defining human organizational principles based on living systems would be easy if we knew exactly how to define a living system. Unfortunately, even after the birth of the organic paradigm, the nature of life remains a mystery (Mayr 1982). It is only possible to build upon life’s known aspects. Particularly, we are interested in those aspects not adequately included in the mechanistic or organic models. The author proposes that a useful set of aspects of living systems to supplement the mechanistic and organic models is studied in evolutionary biology, paleontology, and existential philosophy. These supplemental properties include:

(1) history as presented by Mayr;

(2) species level evolution as presented by Eldredge and Gould; and

(3) humanness as presented by Heidegger, respectively.

We propose that, from these premises, it is possible to infer some characteristics of living systems that may be consequential only at the species level in the formation and maintenance of the identity of a species (e.g., humanness, dolphinness, fisheness). In reverse, we submit, it may take the formation of colonies for a species identity to form and evolve. The first step toward supporting colonies is to include history into the system concept. Without their past, colonies are reduced to temporary groups. The meaning of history for a species is a central foundation of the field of evolutionary biology.

4.2.1. Colonial Systems as History (Mayr) Evolutionary biology is based on the realization that life is the outcome of its own past (Mayr 1982). According to Mayr, “Every organism, whether an individual or a species, is the product of a long history, a history that dates back more than 3000 million years” (p. 69). The fact that no two individual lines of ancestry have evolved under the exact same circumstances has led to immense diversity in life’s manifestations. Every instance of life is time bound and space bound. Every such instance is a link of changing forms with no permanent validity (Delbruck 1949 in Mayr 1982). There is hardly any structure or function in an organism that could be understood, unless it is studied against its historical background. At the individual level of an organism, this means that living beings conform poorly to specific laws (e.g., no two individuals are exactly alike). At the colony-level, the unique history means that each colony is unique because its humanness, carried by its members, is a product of its own local history. In other words, the humanness that a colony portrays is unique among colonies, but shared between its members. This means, for example, that some colonies may portray highly altruistic behaviors, while others are closed from or act hostile toward their environment. Based on this principle it would be impossible to claim, as Wilson (1980) does, that the nature of humanness is to be individualistic (selfish) because an opposite conclusion can be drawn from the same premises. Because individuals grow under the influence of different colonies, some learn collaborative and some individualistic ways. Moreover, the culture of collaboration or individualism is passed on from generation to generation as stories, customs, values, and behavior. It is designed into organizations and information systems as rules of conduct.

In biology, the emphasis on sameness led to the classification of individual organisms into species (vertical evolution). This ongoing effort is accompanied with an ever present suspicion that only a fraction of potential species have been classified into vertical evolutionary columns. On the other hand, diversity can be viewed by tracing a single species over many generations (horizontal evolution). In cultural sciences, the emphasis on human diversity at the social level has led to stories, essays, and documents of unique but locally shared human behaviors. This yields the conclusion that uniqueness, not sameness, is the norm of humanness (cf., local knowledge [Geertz 1973]). This fundamental realization about the variability of life’s instances has caused biology to split into two fields: functional biology and evolutionary biology. While functional biology is based on the organic paradigm and reductionism (vertical evolution), evolutionary biology (horizontal evolution) has opened new territory for history-based multilevel theories of life and, thus, humanness.

If history is accepted as a central characteristic of life, human designs such as organizations and computerbased information systems need to be based on the history of the participating colonies—not on the universal rules about human nature as imposed by the reductionist dogma. Human designs should be free from the kinds of rules which artificially impose sameness. Accommodating the history of the actual participating people into social, organizational, and information system designs as a fundamental property of humanness is a significant change from today’s organizational and information system design principles. These design principles are typically structural and ahistorical. They manifest mechanistic/organic values which support beliefs such as general archetypical human norms (vertical evolution).

4.2.2. Colonial Systems as Evolution (Eldredge and Gould) According to an alternative viewpoint presented by Eldredge and Gould (Eldredge and Gould 1972), significant fossil finds such as Burgess Shale (Gould 1989a) and others (cf., Gould 1989b) seem to support evolution at the interindividual (species or social) level, which, they maintain, is not Darwinian by nature. Their model, called punctuated equilibrium, is based on the assumption that change in nature is nonreductionist and multiform. Specifically, Eldredge and Gould propose that the evolution of species consists of two alternating states: stasis and punctuation. While it may be that the evolution of genes or individual organisms is Darwinist by nature, the punctuated equilibrium model presents another scenario for the evolution of humanness at the level of species comparable with colonies.

Eldredge and Gould hold that the normal state of a species is not to change (stasis). The normal stage is stasis. A strict Darwinian viewpoint would suggest that species should gradually change through time. However, the fossil records indicate that most species do not change—even when viewed over very long periods. For example, stromatolites are colonies that were among the first known species on dry land. Found on the coasts of Australia, these colonies are the same today as their fossilized ancestors 3,800 million years of age. Darwinian gradualism is not able to explain fossil finds such as these. Contrary to the accepted gradualism doctrine, some of the oldest species have probably never changed in any meaningful way, and many ancient fossils bear a striking resemblance to species that exist today.

Moreover, according to Eldredge and Gould, when a species changes, it does so rapidly, unexpectedly, and unevenly. The punctuation or sudden change may be a response to the environment or it may be a result of a successful genetic mutation. During this radical change—: called speciation—a new species originates from a small number of representatives of the species that have become isolated from the mainstream physically or because of their characteristics (Mayr 1982). The new species will share the history with its ancestor species but cannot and will not share the future.

We propose that speciation can be viewed as a form of natural prototyping. In the natural prototyping process, during their stasis phase, species continually create new prototypes (mutations) within the environment. These prototypes are not products or things but colonies formed by descendant groups of representatives of the species. These colonies—evolving independently of their parents—are effectively experimental prototypes. They can become populous or extinct in their own right in their own immediate surroundings. They rely primarily on their own history for survival. If their historical characteristics are coupled with an advantageous local environment, the new lineages may become dominant. Thus, a new colony becomes populous and forms a new species.

While the Darwinian evolutionary theory is founded on reaction (adaptation) and competition, Eldredge and Gould hold that although microlevel change (mutations in organisms) may be adaptive, the way groups of organisms (e.g., colonies of human beings) change, is likely to be more proactive and less competitive as previously assumed. Thus, systems formed by groups are able to direct themselves in ways not included in the mainstream theory of natural evolution. This means that it may also be possible to design social, organizational, and computer-based information systems which are directly supportive of spontaneous, proactive, emancipated, collaborative groups capable of radical actions which stem from their past, not from their immediate environment. The current mechanistic/organic design principles do not support such designs at any level of analysis. Therefore, in periods of revolution, our current social organizations and their computer-based information systems do not change rapidly and radically enough to be useful.

Gould and Eldredge’s punctuated equilibrium model can replace the Darwinian-Spencerian model of change and continuing progress. Their model notes that:

Natural selection is a theory of local adaptation to changing environments. It proposes no perfecting principles, no guarantee of general improvement; in short, no reason for general approbation in a political climate favoring innate progress in nature. (Gould 1992, p. 45)

In their species-level model, change is a product of stasis and radical change resulting from the interaction of a group with the environment. Though the change may be precipitated by genetic mutation (e.g., genetic inheritance of tendencies toward certain behaviors continues to be a topic of controversy), the resulting expansion or contraction of the colony is more importantly based on the relationship of the colony (species) to the environment at large. For the purposes of colonial systems, this provides an alternative metaphor for change. It may be that humanness changes at the social level based on a change mechanism that transforms colonies of humans rather than transforming individuals.

The punctuated equilibrium model is about colonies of animals which become isolated from the rest of their species and subsequently develop in a different direction. The classical cause for isolation is a mutation in an organism. According to modern biology, however, environmental changes such as unintended physical isolation, may also cause a species to split (Mayr 1982).

We hypothesize that it is not the cause—be it biological or environmental—but the effect of being isolated from the rest of the species whether by accident, force, or choice, that is the premise of social change as colonies. We further propose that this social change is not determined by the changes in organisms that create the isolation as the organic paradigm has lead us believe. Rather, social change may follow the laws of punctuated equilibrium and occur at a different level from the organisms themselves.

Today, there are no organizational or information systems designs which accommodate this viewpoint of humanness as evolution of the colony. To understand what it may mean for humanity to evolve as colonies, it is essential to illuminate what exactly evolves at the level of species. Colonies can be concerned about the evolution of humanness. But what is humanness? Neither the mechanistic nor the organic models have successfully characterized it. For a colonial system, humanness must be redefined in terms of existence.

4.2.3. Colonial Systems in Context (Heidegger) As demonstrated by a long line of disciplines (cf., Porra 1996), the efforts to clarify humanness beyond biological life lead either to a list of sociobiological traits (e.g., Wilson) or they lead to bodiless, psychological, cultural, and social ideas (e.g., Geertz). As established earlier, the fundamental problem with the social science approach to humanness is that modern social science does not recognize biological life as a factor (cf., Giddens and Turner 1987, Alexander and Seidmann 1990, Marshall 1994). The shift in beliefs away from evolutionism meant that humanness was no longer considered in the context of the body and biological evolution, but could decide how it transforms its own fate independently of its natural history. Therefore, humanness could also freely choose the designs of its social, organizational and computer-based information systems. The social science consensus today is that humans are not affected by any natural tendencies (such as social tendencies to join others) the species might possess. During this century, only a few attempts to mend the gap between biology and humanness have taken place (cf., Luhmann 1982, 1995, in social theory). As mentioned earlier, even the most recent of such attempts (e.g., Luhmann’s) build on the traditional organic design principles.

We suggest a reevaluation of the role of nature in human designs. Although humans undoubtedly are able to transform human-made designs at will, it is possible that, naturally and socially, humans design new organizational forms when spending long time periods in one another’s company. We propose that these natural designs may be based on the kind of being Homo sapiens has evolved into rather than on modern social labels of humanness as structure (mechanis tic designs) or recursive functions (organic designs). It is this kind of humanness that Martin Heidegger (1962, cf., Heidegger 1977, 1985, Sheehan 1981, Sass 1982, Dreyfus 1991) described during the formative years of modern organicism early this century. He warned against the computer-inspired thinking (cognitivism) because it mistook information processing, structures and functions for humanness. But how is humanness different from these cognitive approaches? Once again we turn to nature for answers. In animal colonies, members of species share habitats and food and care for their young in symbiotic or even altruistic settings. In the most general sense, animal colonies are defined as “the degree to which are developed the features or characteristics by all colonial animals but lacking in solitary animals” (Banta 1973). In nature, colonies form when individual organisms spend extensive time periods in each others’ proximity. In nature, colony refers to sharing of features, qualities, or characteristics; being familiar with or knowing one another. Developed over millions of years of evolution and generations of individuals, colonies exhibit cooperation, caring, and sharing in ways not included in the current geneticbased explanations of species. In human colonies we call this kind of behavior humanness. We suggest this kind of humanness is like Heideggerian human kind (Dasein’s kind) of being-in-the-world. For existential philosopher Martin Heidegger, humanness (Dasein’s kind of being-in-the-world) means “innan” (Heidegger 1985, p. 158). Innan is “to dwell”; “ann” stands for “I am accustomed,” “I am familiar with,” or “I take care of something (the Latin colo in the sense of habito and deligo).” This human kind of dwelling, says Heidegger, “is also taken here as taking care of something in intimate familiarity, being involved with.”

From the viewpoint of colonies, Heidegger’s characterization of humanness can be presented in four principles:

(1) Humanness occurs spontaneously and only once. It cannot be recreated or repeated once it has occurred. Humanness only takes place under actual circumstances in the real world in primordial (historical) time in an actual context of human beings, things, events, and other organic life. Humanness cannot be organized into processes or into an unchanging structure. In the best case, humanness only temporarily visits them. By the same token, any general statements about humanness are bound to be wrong whenever compared with actual people. Any context-free statements about human nature are similarly erroneous because any characteristic is true only with certain humanness at a certain time in a specific context.

(2) Humanness is never separate from its context. By nature, humanness is aware of its environment. It is difficult to determine exactly where humanness ends and its environment begins, because it is difficult to determine the boundaries of the awareness of an individual human being or a group of people. Thus, humanness does not lend itself to notions such as “subjects,” “objects,” or “relations” between these. Consequently, humanness cannot be grasped, defined, or pushed around like a thing, nor can its destiny be determined by outside forces (determinism). The context dependence also means that humans are influenced by every other human they come in contact with. They are also changed by events, things, abstract, and symbolic systems, ideals, and other life none of which “are” humanness but which may all become part of it when in contact. Because of this openness, humans can never be for themselves in a way touted by extremist individualists or determinist evolutionists.

(3) Humanness always belongs to itself. The environment cannot direct humanness. It cannot be forced into a form by imposed designs. Humanness holds endogenous power (all power over itself and little power over its surroundings). It survives or fails because of itself. Humanness cannot be used. This means that, in the long run, humanness has opportunities to act itself out. Humanness always belongs to itself. It is ultimately intrinsic and self-referencing. The environment cannot totally direct humanness but it can influence it.

(4) The future potential of humanness is always bound by its history. Humanness directs itself based on the understanding it has of itself at the time. This understanding is founded on the unique history of the instance of humanness. This history includes all that this humanness has come in contact with throughout its existence. Humanness is not present in ahistorical circumstances. It is not present in structures or processes (such as in DNA). Rather, humanness continuously includes and is redefined by its surroundings: things, events, people, and other life forms, which all become part of humanness evolving through as a colony of human beings.

Heidegger opposed the 20th century trend of objectifying humanness and treating others as if they were temporary, context-free means to an end. For him, humanness is a subjective, pervasive, and lasting experience of other human beings and surroundings at the level of self. At this level, humanness is not limiting, controlling, or consciously selective. It includes everything and everybody it comes in contact with over its existence. This means that in the end, humanness is as diverse as the environment it has evolved through. Heidegger’s viewpoint of humanness is the antithesis of evolutionism. His viewpoint makes every human being we encounter over our lifetimes important, because every human being we spend any time with influences our humanness. It is because we spend time with one another that we become somewhat alike. Humanness is influence. It is shared in words, images, and behavior. Heideggerian humanness is an antithesis of artificially created classifications. Genes, genders, or races (vertical evolution) do not close in humanness. Humanness knows no human-made boundaries.

After stating Mayr’s, Eldredge and Gould’s, and Heidegger’s views we can extend the current mechanistic and organic system principles with three ideas.

1. Mayr’s proposition that life is a product of its own, local history.

2. Eldredge and Gould’s proposition that life evolves at several distinct levels. At the level of species evolution seems to manifest itself as proactive groups capable of radical change while at the same time able to maintain the colony’s stability.

3. Heidegger’s proposition that humanness is the spontaneous and unique occurrence of sharing of being over time which evolves through time thoroughly intertwined with its environment.

These three stances provide some additional insight into humanness at the axiomatic level. Using these axioms we can define the extensions to the mechanistic and organic models that capture the nature of colonies.

4.2.4. Defining Colonial Systems With the insights from Mayr, Gould, and Heidegger, we can derive an alternative to the existing mechanistic and organic paradigms. A colonial system is defined as a collection of individuals who share a history and an environment and who cooperate directly or indirectly for the maintenance of the colony. This definition clarifies: (1) the scale of a colony (it is a group not an individual entity) and (2) its evolutionary mechanism (it is a species-level phenomenon existing both in periods of stasis and radical change).

While individuals within a colony may exhibit characteristics of a machine or an organism, the essential nature of a colony is a collective phenomenon. As such, a colonial system has an axiomatic foundation which differs significantly from the mechanistic and organic viewpoints. The specific differences can be shown by enumerating for colonial systems the same properties outlined for the two existing paradigms in Table 1 (see § 3.1: Properties of Mechanistic and Organic Systems).

## 4.3. The Properties of Colonial Systems

4.3.1. Colonial Systems’ Phylogeny, Ontogeny, and Change At the species level of humanness, a colony of humans is the unit of analysis. At this level of analysis, phylogeny is the primary mode of change. This means that colonies of humans evolve through both stasis and punctuation. Corresponding to the model of punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould 1972), humanness evolves through:

(1) splitting of lineages—a group of humans distinguishes itself from the rest of the humanity geographically or otherwise;

(2) rapid development of these lineages—the group rapidly develops characteristics distinct from the rest of the humanity;

(3) speciation from the resulting subpopulations—distinct humanness develops through the formation of new colonies; and

(4) speciation from small parts of the geographical distribution—the evolution of humanness is based on specific colonies not on uniform changes in the entire species.

Since colonial systems are not dependent on any of their individuals for existence, they do not have ontogenies (lifetimes). At the species-level, there are no distinguishable phases comparable with birth, adolescence, maturity, decay, or death. A human colony does not: begin with an individual being born into it; end with an individual dying in it; start by the joining of an new individual; or terminate with an individual leaving the colony. At the colonial level, all change takes place according to the principles of phylogeny. All other change, although meaningful for individuals, from the colonial viewpoint, is just fluctuation. This fluctuation may include both the organic paradigm’s Darwinian mutations in the genes and continuous change such as the aging of the organs at the individual level.

4.3.2. Colonial System Boundaries Unlike formal organizations, which are based on career and monetary contracts, colonies are formations whose causes remain unknown. They are based on seemingly spontaneous acts and founded on bindings such as geographical proximity, long-lasting common interests, family relations, or friendship. Because of the nature of the colonial system called reach (potentially everything in the reach of the senses of its members is included into the colony’s humanness), the spatial boundaries of colonial systems are unclear. It is difficult to say exactly where the colony is, who is included and to what extent. Also being a member of a colony does not exclude being a member of other colonies. Thus, being a member is a matter of degree. The strength of the membership is a function of time spent in the proximity of the colony.

Colonial systems also have temporal boundaries. The temporal boundaries of a human colony potentially reach back in time to the origins of life, more than 3 billion years ago. All individuals who have spent time in the colony or its ancestor colonies over the existence of their life are included in its humanness to a degree of the relative duration of the influence. The temporal boundaries also reach to the future, potentially until the colony’s extinction (the death of the last member). This means that in each human group, exists all its past. Respectively, all the future humanness is already present in the colonies of today as potential.<sup>4</sup>

4.3.3. The Complexity of Colonial Systems If a human group were a mechanistic or an organic system that contains all its past and all its future, it would contribute to unforeseen complexity (Pagels 1989). According to organic principles, this complexity would have long since become unmanageable (entropy), and humanness would have died, rendered mission impossible. Unlike organic systems, colonial systems are adept at using variation in their own complexity as a tool for self-preservation through radical change. In colonial systems, including all past and future contexts in themselves, self-preservation occurs through at least two notions.

(1) Complexity through phylogeny means that older human colonies have more potential for simplicity than younger ones. Since colonial systems own their history as experience, it is likely that a richer history (more tested ways) is a simpler and more efficient form than a younger colonial system.

(2) Complexity in relation to punctuations means that, in general, a punctuation stabilizes a colonial system into a simpler form than it had during the preceding stasis. During a punctuation, a human group reacts in order to reduce complexity and, thereby, attain higher efficiency without jeopardizing its humanness. Occasionally, a human system may assume a more complex form as a result of a punctuation. Such a drastic act may be out of necessity (e.g., loss of key individuals or turbulent environment). Increasing complexity is energy-consuming, and it is preserved by the system only as long as necessary for the shift to the desired, simpler form.

Although longer history generally means the ability to generate more possibilities for the future, “old equals simple” does not apply as a universal law. Complexity is a consequence of the system preserving its humanness while it evolves. This means that stages of simplicity are followed by stages of complexity as the system searches for an efficient way of perpetuating itself.

In general, increasing complexity in a colonial system implies that the system is in an unfamiliar or rapidly changing environment. As the surrounding situation becomes more familiar or calm, the system simplifies itself (in a punctuation). Simplicity, as the means of preservation of species has turned out to be extremely useful (Wilson 1992). Therefore, it is likely that human groups strive on the simplicity of form and content. However, since each instance of humanness is irreplaceable, it is also possible that complexity is used for redundancy for security. Rendered too simple, the past of Homo sapiens has no meaning.

4.3.4. Colonial Systems’ Structure Since structure is a term related to things not humanness, from the perspective of humanness, all structure is temporary by nature. The structure of humanness, formed by people and machines, is a mere vehicle for the evolving humanness. This means that the structure is changed in the course of phylogeny to efficiently preserve the past for the future. Since structure is the container of history, all structure is not replaceable at any one time without jeopardizing the group’s unique humanness. Replacing all people in a colony changes it into another colony, since no carrier for humanness is maintained. Thus, buildings, records, and technology do not make a colony, just as human bones (fossils) do not make a human being (living organism). But all structure (people and machines) can be replaced over time. Replacing a few people every now and then maintains the necessary vehicle for inherited humanness of a colony. Since all structure is temporary, however, in some cases a punctuation may change the group beyond recognition (equivalent to moving all the people of the village to another location) when, in fact, the colonial level preserves the long history of a colony through this change by: spending time with one another; explaining; telling stories; and providing interpretations about the colony. In more abstract design terms, a colonial system exists under the following structural conditions:

(1) the possibility of the colony changing its structure (openended change over long time spans) unrestricted by the current structure;

(2) the possibility of an evolutionary mechanism developing and evolving in the colony;

(3) the possibility of including the history of the colony in its structure; and

(4) the possibility of the colony having an awareness of its environment and the ability to exercise the first three conditions.

At any point in time the colonial system is its structure. This means that, in some way, humanness exists in the individual’s biology as self. If the colonial (species) level forms the basis for human designs, then humanness occurs naturally under circumstances of sharing, caring, collaboration, open communication, and trust. In reverse, this would mean that humanness is unlikely to evolve in modern corporations, their information systems, or in applications of artificial intelligence, because their structure is designed to prevent humanness from occurring.

4.3.5. The Growth of Colonial Systems Yet another way of illuminating the dimensions of colonial systems is through their growth. As can be inferred from the previous discussion, colonial systems are not restricted by the growth of an organism. Thus, a colony has no maximum size or rate of growth. It is unlikely, however, that it is efficient for humanness to grow a large structure (group of people and their machines). Despite this degree of freedom, excessive growth is not desirable for colonial systems. Thus, the colonial perspective differs from the industrial revolution era’s growth ideal. Because colonies are founded on physically being in one another’s company, they call for a size that best supports such existence. Although it is possible to share colony-like experiences over distances using tools such as print media, broadcast media, and the Internet, it is unlikely that the quality of sharing in populous systems supported by these means is comparable with genuine colonies because the experience is too limited for humanness to evolve.<sup>5</sup> Colonial systems use their size as a tool for preserving their unique humanness over time.

Instead of continuous growth, the shape of a colonial growth curve follows a recursive pattern of stasis followed by a punctuation. While the system may grow following stasis, it normally diminishes during punctuations. Depending on the circumstances, however, other kinds of growth patterns are possible. A punctuation may lead to a larger system, which will gradually diminish in size during the stasis phase, finally reaching a size smaller than the system prior to punctuation. Moreover, under unknown or threatening circumstances, the system may choose to punctuate into a larger size system and maintain its size through stasis. Since a punctuation may lead to a system smaller, equal, or larger in size than the punctuating system, and since the system may slowly diminish, remain the same size, or grow during the stasis, colonial systems may grow dramatically or diminish in size equally over relatively short time periods.

Colonial growth curves can be described by Gould’s “onions,” human experiments that begin small, yet may involve large numbers of people relatively inconspicuously and, from the environment’s viewpoint, suddenly. Human-made constructions (sharing structure such as buildings or neighborhoods) do not guarantee or cause the kind of sharing which would lead to the common humanness of the inhabitants. Thus, shared humanness, such as in colonial systems, is only possible from within and over time.

4.3.6 The Goals of Colonial Systems A fundamental characteristic of colonial systems is that they may be sizable, yet they appear leaderless without clear authorities for goal setting, power, or control. This does not mean that such notions do not exist for humanness. Rather, it entails the lack of hierarchy and individual-centered leadership. It is proposed that a colonial system’s goals extend beyond the lifetimes of participating individuals and beyond categories familiar from the current paradigms. Mechanistic authoritarianism, organic finality, or simple feedback mechanism allowing adaptation (teleonomy) do not explain colonial-level goals. On the other hand, colonial goals are likely not to be conscious choices of free wills. Since the goals of the system set the colony on a trail of commitment toward attaining the goals, such random behavior is too risky from the viewpoint of preservation of the system. Rather, the goals of a colonial system are produced by the colony based on:

(1) its history of contexts available as “experience” to the system;

(2) the potentiality of the system for the future; and

(3) the system’s evolutionary mechanism.

Colonial systems are teleogenic (goal generating) systems with goals as diverse as life. Their goals may be as vague as ideals, ideas, dreams, and wishes, or they may take the form of more concrete visions, objectives, or goals. In all cases, there is a width and depth to all these, comparable with the humanness which created them. Thus, colonial goals are the ability to imagine the seemingly impossible from the premises of the history accompanied by the potential to achieve the imagined.

4.3.7. Power in Colonial Systems Colonial systems hold power over themselves. This means that no outside system is able to impose its power on humanness without its consent. Since a colonial system consists of smaller colonial systems, the same applies to all sublevel systems (individuals or subcolonies). No colonial subsystem is able to impose its humanness on other participating subsystems. Colonial power is endogenous authority. Endogenous power means complete authority over oneself and no authority over others. The only influence a colonial system has over other colonial systems is through being in their context for long time periods. Power, if involved at all, is through the long term influence of spending time in one another’s company.

An extreme expression of endogenous power is the authority to surrender or retain complete authority over oneself. By surrendering authority to its environment, a colonial system becomes invisible. It appears as a part of its environment rather than as an independent human system. This form of being is called “latent.” Latency preserves crucial energy in evolution through hard times, because the humanness of the system does not evolve. During the latent period, the colonial system concentrates on preserving its unique past. As soon as the circumstances improve, the colonial system retains authority over itself and directs its evolution toward its goals. Latency is a necessary yet dangerous form of existence. Giving authority to the environment resembles organic power in which adaptation is the goal. During latency, the prospects of the colonial system are at the mercy of the environment.

4.3.8. Control of Colonial Systems The last property of humanness to be considered in this paper is control. Colonial systems are feedback-feedforward systems. These terms (from cybernetics) describe a system that is able to adapt to its environment and which possesses the primitives required for the system to set its own goals (Coulter 1968, 1975, Locker and Coulter, 1976, Parks and Steinburg 1978). Cybernetics as a discipline describes microlevel control mechanisms of both mechanistic and organic character. This means that feedback and feedforward describe a microlevel system of biology, which a colonial system, by definition, is not. It is likely, however, that mechanisms analogous to feedback and feedforward are also used by colonial systems.

Colonial level feedback-feedforward mechanisms are a product of the unique evolution of the colonial system. Instead of being immediate and reflexive as in organic systems, colonial control mechanisms are delayed, complex, and varied like the colonies that portray them. Unlike in an automatic organic control system, the impact of feedback and feedforward is always at the discretion of the colonial system.

## 4.4. A Continuum of Systems

Most social theory premises qualitatively differ from the colonial systems at the assumptions level in two fundamental ways: (1) they are mostly organic; and (2) they are reductionist. Within the current mechanistic and organic paradigms, five levels of scale are shown in Figure 1 (see § 3.5: The Unit of Analysis). This implies that the paradigms have been applied to societies, organizations, groups, individuals, and even computer-based information systems at all levels of scale. The literature of these five levels is replete with machine and cellular models being applied to entities which exhibit none of the basic qualities expressed in the axioms of the models (Porra 1996).

To illustrate the situation, the current premises of human designs can be related to a continuum of systems (see Figure 1). A continuum of systems from mechanistic to organic organizational forms has been suggested before by authors such as Burns and Stalker (1961). They observed that mechanistic organizational forms were suitable for stable environments while “organic” organizations did better under unpredictable circumstances. Burns and Stalker characterized “organic” organizations as systems of: open ended change; rapid progress in turbulent environments; constant exploration; and constant change in the pattern of authority. These kinds of organizations may be more successful in radically changing environments but we know now they are not organic. Specifically, organic systems are not capable of such open-ended flexibility. Thus, organic organizations do not do well in turbulent environments. Burns and Stalker are no doubt correct in their observations about the need to reconsider organizational forms in stable, moderately changing, and turbulent environments. However, their continuum does not effectively specify the system theoretical premises of the new “organic” organizational forms.

Mintzberg has used nature as a metaphor for categorizing organizations to machine bureaucracies, divisional forms, professional bureaucracies, simple structures, and adhocracies (Morgan 1986). According to him, these five organizational types are like five different species. In Mintzberg’s classification, adhocracies are farthest from mechanistic systems. According to Bennis, the inventor of the term, adhocracies are suited for complex and uncertain tasks in turbulent environments. They involve teams that come together to perform a task only to disappear when it is over. Discussions about adhocracies do not specify the theoretical foundations that support the behavior. They conform with the modern view of a group as a temporary arrangement for goal attainment. But what are the system assumptions that support their success under uncertainty and turbulent environments? Mintzberg labels mechanistic and organic organizational types as species because they seem to portray different characteristics (vertical evolution). But in Mintzberg’s view, all species and thus all organizations participate in Social Darwinist evolution. For him, the organic paradigm is the universal explanation of change.

We propose another kind of a continuum of systems based on horizontal evolution. The purpose of our continuum is to illustrate the relative ability of a chosen system theoretical foundation to explain species level humanness. Since the intent is to look for more natural human designs, it would be plausible to call this measure a continuum of “life.” Thus, mechanistic designs would have no life. Organic designs would appear to the right of mechanistic systems since they show some lifelike qualities. Thus life cannot be equated with a thing, structure, function, program, process, force, or substance (Mayr 1982).

A more precise way of relating mechanistic and organic design principles with humanness would be a systems continuum of “context dependence of time and place.” Mechanistic systems are context independent. Organic systems include primitive exchange of material with their environment. According to this analogy, the more human the system, the more context dependent it would be. The problem with this solution is that the notions “time” and “place” are already included in the organic paradigm. In either the mechanistic or organic paradigm, however, time and place in and of themselves, do not guarantee humanness no matter how they are defined. On the contrary, if no other measures are present, complete context dependence means replacing the system with its background in a way atypical for self-aware systems, such as living systems.

We propose a continuum called being-in-time to illustrate where the colony viewpoint of human system differs from the current system paradigms. Being-intime is based on our discussion of Heideggerian humanness (Dasein’s kind of being-in-the-world). This living being (existence) evolves through time under actual, unique circumstances and is profoundly influenced by its immediate surroundings of things, events, humans, and other organic life. For mechanistic systems, time or place (environment) have no meaning. They exist through time, closed from any influence by their environment (cf., determinism and evolutionism). For organic systems, time is an axis against which the duration of its functions can be measured, and place (environment) exists for controlled input-output streams with predetermined consequences for the system (cf., progress in Social Darwinism). For humanness, we suggest that time is primordial time (historical time) which is always inseparably bound to the humanness in the participating real human beings (colony) in an actual place (context). This means that the humanness the colony’s humans portray only exists because of its contextual history. Context refers to the events, people, other life, and inanimate things that have touched the colony of people over the colony’s existence. Thus, humanness as being-in-time refers to existence: Humanness is all that happened to all the people who participated in a colony during their lifetimes. We propose that this kind of existence be produced by natural evolution at the level of species. At this level, individuals are blended in with one another’s humanness in a way that makes it impossible to separate an organism from another—one human from another. At this level, a colony of representatives of a species develops a distinct colonial identity, proactive stance in relation to the environment, sharing, caring collaboration, and trust between members.

In the left part of Figure 1 (see § 3.5: Unit of Analysis), the scales have been modified to reflect the appropriate biological scale, which applies to the mechanistic, organic, and colonial models. It should be noted that claims for mechanisms and organisms are only applicable at the most microlevel. Colonial systems exist only at the species level and have little to contribute to the microlevel explanations or theories, which advocate mechanistic or organic premises. None of the paradigms—mechanistic, organic, or colonial—have little basis for describing large-scale systems such as ecosystems or politics, religious or economic systems.

## 5. Punctuated Prototyping—the Evolutionary Mechanism of Colonial Systems

The previous sections have shown that the mechanistic and organic system paradigms are pervasive as foundations in theories about societies, organizations, and groups. Unfortunately, those organic descriptions often lead to inappropriate conclusions about how systems grow or evolve through time. The conclusions that “gradualism” or “progressive improvement” are provided by organic feedback through “survival of the fittest” are incorrect. Eldredge and Gould have clearly stated how the mechanism punctuated equilibrium operates.

Extrapolation of gradual change under selection to a complete model for the origin of species fails to recognize that speciation is primarily an ecological and geographic process. Natural selection, in allopatric theory, involves adaptation to local conditions and the elaboration of isolating mechanism.

Phyletic gradualism is, in itself, an insufficient picture to explain the origin of diversity in the present, or any past, biota. (Eldredge 1972, p. 97)

The punctuations occur because of the geographic and temporal separation of some variant of the original colony. Whether the origin of this isolation is a conscious creation of the isolated group or an outcome of an external event, the success of the new colony requires independence from the parent colony. We will designate this isolation as punctuated prototyping. The method of punctuated prototyping can then be summarized as three processes, which form three loops at three different levels of evolution. These three elements can be seen as the three processes, which make up the continuum of systems in Figure 1 (see § 3.5: The Unit of Analysis).

Using Figure 1, we show the evolutionary mechanisms of the mechanistic, organic, and colonial paradigms in the appropriate (1) cells of scale (micro, species, and macro) and (2) being-in-time (humanness). We thereby acknowledge that mechanistic, organic, and colonial systems coexist; but they operate at different levels and with different assumptions about their human context. Figure 2 thus places both mechanistic and organic systems at the microlevel of systems (lower row of Figure 2) and colonial systems at the level of speciation (middle row of Figure 2). We can now show how the three kinds of systems interact by virtue of the kinds of processes that occur within in each paradigm.

## 5.1. The Microlevel

The interior, ontogeny, or stasis loop can best be compared with organic ontogeny. It represents the stasis or maintenance phase as a microlevel refinement loop. The mechanistic system is portrayed in Figure 2 as a hierarchy of predefined rules. The mechanistic processes are shown as sequences of tasks designed or genetically inherited for accomplishing a predefined task. To the right of the mechanistic processes, the organic model is shown. Using the computer analogy of the organic paradigm, the organic component provides feedback corrections to the predefined processes of the mechanistic systems that execute the modified programs. The organic process is viewed as being ontological and, thus, representing a birth-to-death process (a lifetime or ontogeny). On the being-in-time scale of humanness, the organic model creates a better representation of lifelike systems by providing direction and stability, but it ultimately fails to survive through gradual adaptation.

## 5.2. The Species or Colonial Level

The middle, punctuation, phylogeny, or colonial loop is the level of processes where punctuations occur. This loop represents the central premise of colonial systems. This colonial process lies between the machines and cellular organisms of the microlevel and the selfactualization of the macrolevel. The model assumes that the local colony has the power to change the goals, structures, or processes of the system based on the colony’s perception of itself in relation to its history and current predicament. This self-directed change is passed down to the microlevel as either:

(a) stasis—the colony perceives no need for change and allows the microlevel to maintain its current form or process, or

(b) punctuation—the colony perceives the need to radically alter its structure and/or processes. This colonial self has the prerogative to ignore the macrolevel pressures and reinterpret them according to the local circumstances.

For a natural colony, the choice between stasis and punctuation may not be made by the self-aware colonists, but be the result of an external agent or event. In evolutionary biology, these catastrophic events play crucial roles in explaining the sudden change in environments that may precipitate species-level change. Though human systems are also at the mercy of natural catastrophes, they additionally possess the selfaware skills to anticipate the need for change. In the context of humanness, the antecedent clues that precipitate change may come from more macrolevel processes, such as at the ecological level (i.e., a macrolevel shift). They may also appear from the microlevel processes, when the maintenance of stability begins to fail. Independent of the source of the perturbation, as a selfreferencing entity, a colony must proactively choose courses of actions that guarantee survival. Systems that continue to execute programs of action, even though they are clearly producing detrimental results by this very definition, fail to possess self-awareness.

The central premise of the colony is shown in the operation of the middle or speciation loop. The colony survives by collective adaptation to both ecological and microlevel pressures. This independence or autonomous action provides the basis for determining how efficient the colony is in correcting for both errors in history (the old ways no longer work) and in anticipation (the feedforward anticipation for the need to change). In natural colonies, these traits are often attributed to self-awareness, when they may be only effective genetic chance. These two extremes (pure genetic apparatus versus self-aware choices) are not mutually exclusive. Moreover, it is not the intent of this colonial presentation to argue for either nature or nurture. The autonomy of the colony allows responses to both such macro- and microlevel pressures.

Whether the need for change is imposed or by conscious choice, the creation of isolated alternatives must occur. It is this assumption by Eldredge and Gould that can be used to specify how change is to occur. Change occurs by the adoption of a successful prototype (i.e., an alternative created in isolation). In the genetic extreme, this may mean that an extremely efficient mutation arises by chance. In the self-conscious sense, this may mean experimentation or the creation of an alternative that lies outside historical assumptions. This process is named punctuated prototyping to emphasize the roles of creation (whether by chance or design) and fundamental difference from previous incarnations of the system.

The punctuated prototype is more than a local adaptation of history to the local environment. The prototype must exhibit at least one essential characteristic that doesn’t exist in the current environment. The empowerment to employ radically different solutions has been shown to provide survival value in biological systems. This capability extends perhaps even more importantly to self-aware systems such as human colonies. Knowing when and how to alter behavior is perhaps the reason that human colonies have dominated nature recently. We will later use this colonial operational premise as a reference paradigm for developing punctuation methodologies that can be used to explain and design systems that reflect humanness.

## 5.3. Macrolevel of Ecologies

The operations relating to system representations of macrolevel evolution cannot be discussed with respect to colonial systems. As Figure 2 (see the beginning of § 5: Punctuated Prototyping) shows, no current system paradigms explain the macroevolutionary level. Even though most social and organizational models speak about entities of this size, they are in fact based on the micro or genetic level of analysis (Porra 1996). In this section we have shown that colonial systems describe species-level evolution and do not attempt to provide any explanation about the ecological or macrolevel environment. Though an ecological interaction certainly exists, trying to define or organize the specific aspects of the ecology is quite beyond the scope of this discussion. We have included this level to indicate the separation between: the microlevel of the mechanistic and organic; the speciation level and the larger context on the ecological environment. Though the ecological label implies a biological interpretation, for human colonies this larger context would include any larger social, economic, or political unit that interacts with colony.

## 5.4. Punctuated Prototyping

The colonial model reveals a complex addition to systems described as mechanistic or organic. The middle colonial loop creates a dynamic control mechanism finer than the older paradigms. Rather than a simple feedback model generated by an exogenous higherlevel designer, a colonial system is embodied by its members as participants in the creation of their own processes and structures. The omission of this local colonial reality in our current methods can be blamed for the failure of our existing systems to change or responsiveness to the current local circumstances.

During both stasis and change, a colonial system continuously creates experiments to test the new realities of the environment. It forms new colonies of people and technologies (e.g., computer-based information systems). Minimally, the colony can completely carry its history to a successful experiment (prototype) in the form of a member or members who is/are the colony’s history. As one or several of the experiments turn out to be successful, the colony can voluntarily change itself into these new forms. In the punctuation, the colonial system (1) changes the microlevel structure, and (2) reorganizes the colonial level history of its humanness into the new structure. This may translate into departing or joining of people, disposing of useless systems, and making geographic shifts from one location to another. It is possible that, in such a shift, what the colony does with technology also changes radically. The new form is thus always a derivative of the humanness of the parent colony and yet offers some additional structure that allows the colony to interact with the environment differently.

The purpose of this process in the humanness sense is to create new colonies of people and technology. This viewpoint dramatically differs from current organizational change and computer-based information system design methodologies based on a product metaphor (microlevel). The punctuated prototyping model outlines a living, ever-evolving three-level interaction of anticipation, punctuation, and stasis. Since all levels are connected forming a dynamic construction, simple predefined sequential step-by-step explanations fail to capture the nature of the colonial operation. One must place the whole framework in motion, in time and space, to correctly capture its essential operation. For example, when viewed from the outside, the colony appears to operate in relative stability for an extended period of time. There may be local fluctuations, but the system is homeostatic. From within this stability, alternative structures arise. This occurs either through mutation in the biological sense or through self-awareness in the humanness sense. The successfully adopted alternative then quickly becomes the dominant structure for the colony. Then the new form maintains its stability for long periods until another successful prototype breaks the continuity. Repeating this cycle again and again indicates why we observe a discontinuous fossil record in nature and a similarly broken trail of organizations and empires throughout human history.

The clear advantage of the punctuated model over previous representations lies in the addition of the middle or colonial control loop. This additional locus of control is normally not captured at the organic feedback loop level, which only explains local fluctuations. Similarly, the exogenous goal mechanism supplied from outside the system (the exterior loop) assumes a congruence between the local mechanistic processes and the goals supplied from the outside. This middle loop demonstrates the existence of a powerful local mechanism that can embody or ignore the higher level inputs. The colony’s ability to create isolated prototypes that differ from the existing structures is the colony’s key operating characteristic. Recognizing the necessity to dispense with the current structures and create a new alternative is one possible way to generate significant change. This approach is considerably more flexible than expecting organic gradualism to alter operations during times when only significant change will assure continuity.

For self-aware systems such as human colonies, the creation of the isolated prototypes may represent revolutionary activity when viewed from the formal structures of the machine or organism. Yet it is this very type of activity that guarantees long-run survival. With perfect hindsight, we can always identify the radical change that took place during a punctuation. Yet, we are always foiled in our attempts to find the source of this change inside predefined rules or adaptive change. For radical change to occur, it must occur outside of the previous assumptions about the system. The colonial method of creating an isolated prototype can be used to explain how such radical change occurs. Change occurs because there is a mechanism that can create viable solutions in a turbulent environment.

This colonial focus then may also explain the oftencited goal incongruence between mechanisms of systems and their higher origins. Colonial systems then exhibit the ability to use local information, which is often more reliable than exogenous inputs from above and strategically more important than a microlevel local fluctuation.

## 6. Some Implications for Design

Suggesting that colonial systems have fundamentally different characteristics from previous organizational paradigms creates two expectations: the ability of the paradigm to explain previous observations and the ability to provide guidance in the design of new alternatives.

The colonial paradigm can be used to explain the failure of systems to respond under conditions of radical change. In simplistic terms, these failures occur because radical change is not built into mechanistic and organic interpretations. In the case of the mechanistic model, there is no a priori rule or procedure that contains descriptions of new alternatives (prototypes). This is also true of the organic system, where the rules for operation are embedded in predefined responses to local feedback. After the system has failed or collapsed, the alternatives that replaced it are often completely different. We typically explain that the system: “has failed to be responsive,” “has become brittle,” “no longer meets our needs,” “is out of date,” or “was replaced by a better alternative.” All of these explanations indicate that the system had no internal mechanism available to survive the turbulence. This is not surprising, since neither mechanistic nor organic systems have any such built-in mechanisms. We are also likely to observe that the new system that replaced the old was created outside the domain of the previous system. Although we often appeal to revolutionary or creative explanations for these occurrences, in the case of humanness, it is likely that they were created with the conscious recognition that they are based on the past yet are in some way fundamentally different from it. With the colonial viewpoint, we can recognize that the source of change lies outside the original system boundaries, and is created by colonists with the perceived need and permission to create a viable alternative.

For example, in the field of information systems, the failure rate in information system design projects remains high (Lyytinen 1987, Hirschheim and Lyytinen 1987). This is not surprising, given the mechanistic and organic basis of the information systems field. Using the colonial viewpoint, one could anticipate system failures because of the lack of an appropriate change mechanism. This failure to understand change is welldocumented. “It is no secret that research on information technology and organizational change has produced conflicting results and few reliable generalizations” (Markus and Robey 1988, p. 596). Burns has made a similar observation about the lack of explanatory capability in organizational theory (Burns 1994). Using the colonial model, one could predict the appearance of successors which began as viable or successful prototypes. Yet to mechanistic or organic explanations, these changes appear as revolutions, creativity, or the metaphysical—explanations that all lie outside the system under study. A colonial explanation may be used to describe how our human systems have evolved through stasis and radical change by the creation of viable alternatives by the participating individuals.

In addition to historical explanations, colonial systems may be expected to provide specific instructions about how to design new systems that portray the richness of behaviors, such as the colonies that in nature have enveloped our natural world. There are hundreds of models or templates available for the designers of computer systems, groups, and organizations. These design methodologies all include the specific rules and steps necessary to develop effective systems of people and technology. To these methodologies, the colonial systems metaphor would add the implicit requirement for the creation of alternative systems that lie outside the prevailing structure. Additionally, the participants must be empowered to recognize the need for such prototypes to be created and possess the ability and authority to transform the old into the new when necessary. Such empowerment and autonomy as suggested by this colonial viewpoint is not likely to be quickly incorporated into methodologies predicated on conformance to predefined rules and continuous improvement. But without the express ability to discover and implement successful alternatives, human systems should be expected to fail with regularity. We suggest that colonies are constructed with the seeds of their own destruction built into the process. In addition, they contain the mechanism of punctuated prototyping to provide a successful new structure that will become the norm in the future.

## 7. Conclusions

Colonial systems have been developed in this paper by showing that: (1) all humanness theories rely on a systems model; (2) historically, only the mechanistic and organic system models have been used (and misused) by computer, organizational, and social theorists; and (3) that there is also a natural process that occurs at the species level (colonial), which may be used as an alternative model to explain change, growth, structure, etc.

Central to these three discussions are the foundations that one uses to establish a conceptual viewpoint about systems, in general, and humanness, in particular. The considerable discussion in §§ 2 and 3 assigns almost all literature on humanness and systems to the category of organic or mechanistic systems (see Figure 1 in § 3.5), based upon their stated (or unstated) use of the premises essential to those paradigms. This classification can be examined by isolating the fundamental assumptions being used by authors to express a model or theory. As we have shown, no existing system literature can escape this microlevel classification thus far.

As an alternative, the colonial paradigm has been presented, which appeals to Eldredge and Gould’s, Mayr’s, and Heidegger’s observations about alternative forms of evolution and being. This representation of colonies that can survive equally well in periods of stasis and punctuation is a viable alternative to the mechanical and organic models. Built on foundations at least as firm as the machine and the single-cell organism, the colonial metaphor appeals to what readers know about themselves and their environment. They know they and their human relationships are neither machines, nor do they operate like a cell or a thermostat. The colonial paradigm is presented in an attempt to elevate the viewpoint upon which we reflect our models from the microlevel to the colonial or species level. Beyond enhancing the scale of our analysis, the colonial viewpoint provides an alternative explanatory mechanism that shows how colonies generate stability and survive catastrophe through the use of history and self-determination. This punctuated prototyping is a succinct explanation that acknowledges how machines and local organic feedback can be used to control machinelike environments when the fluctuations are small. But in the larger context of the colony, its historic self-determinism assures survival even during severe punctuations, when the colony abandons the mechanistic and organic structures implemented for homeostasis and adopts a successful alternative.

The viability of the colonial model presented in this paper will be determined by its utility in providing alternative explanations about existing system phenomena. The colonial model can be used to identify and explain why some current systems fail or fail to perform according to expectation or theoretical prediction. This paper only begins to address this reevaluation of the deficiencies in system models. The cursory examination of the information systems, organizational theory, and social science fields presented in the previous sections demonstrates that there is significant misuse of the notion of evolution and a hearty lack of awareness about the assumptions underlying the models that are employed. We expect that for the foreseeable future, the primary role for this colonial model is to offer an alternative explanatory model for how human systems grow, evolve, and persevere. The clear objective of this exercise will be to show that successful (i.e., persistent) systems operate like a colony, whereas unsuccessful (i.e., rigid) systems fail because they are predicted on machine or cellular processes.

In addition to explaining existing phenomena, colonial systems will be evaluated by the viability of systems, which are designed according to colonial principles. This involves providing a methodology (i.e., punctuated prototyping) that is clearly better than the sequential processes of the mechanistic paradigm and the closed-loop feedback processes of the organic model. Only a skeletal methodological specification has been presented here. We expect more complete methodologies to evolve as we learn from applying the colonial model to realistic circumstances.
