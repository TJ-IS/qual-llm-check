# Nonlinear and Noncompensatory Models in User Information Satisfaction Measurement

Vikram Sethi • Ruth C. King

CIS Department, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, Missouri 65804 vikram@world.std.com

College of Commerce and Industry, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, Illinois

his study applies nonlinear and noncompensatory models to examine how users evaluate their satisfaction with their information systems (IS) environment. Several instruments have been developed in the literature to measure user information satisfaction (UIS). These instruments measure user satisfaction by asking respondents to rate their satisfaction/dissatisfaction with a variety of IS attributes; e.g., EDP services, EDP staff, information product, and involvement in IS development. These responses are then combined linearly to develop a surrogate measure for UIS satisfaction. This linear model is derived from Anderson’s information theory (Anderson 1981) and based on the assumption that each attribute judgment has a conditionally monotone relationship with the UIS evaluation. However, the literature on attitude formation and decision making suggests that other nonlinear and noncompensatory models are available to decision makers for combining information and are used frequently in attitude formation. In this study, we use two sets of data to examine the linear model and five nonlinear models of decision making to evaluate whether nonlinear models are more effective in predicting a user’s overall satisfaction with information systems. First, responses from faculty members at an academic institution were used to test each model. All the nonlinear models were more efficient predictors than the linear models. In addition, two nonlinear models—the multiplicative and the scatter models—best represented the data with square multiple correlations of 0.69 and 0.68, as compared to the linear model which had an ${ \bar { R } } ^ { 2 }$ of 0.61. Second, data from a previous study (Galletta and Lederer 1989) were analyzed to examine whether nonlinear models were more efficient. Data for this study were collected using the short version of the Bailey and Pearson (1983) UIS instrument. Results of the analysis from the full and cross-validation samples show that nonlinear, noncompensatory models performed at par or better than the linear model.

## 1. Introduction

Many of the phenomena observed in information systems (IS) studies, including behavioral responses such as user information satisfaction (UIS), have been mathematically modeled assuming linear information integration. For example, in order to understand how individuals assess their satisfaction with their information systems environment, Bailey and Pearson (1983) weighted the utility of a set of IS factors and then combined them to arrive at an overall value of UIS. User satisfaction is, therefore, postulated as the sum of a user’s weighted reaction to a variety of IS factors. Similar linear models have been used by Joshi (1990) and Lane et al. (1994).

The linear regression model is usually used in information integration because it is considered to be extremely powerful. As noted by Yntema and Torgerson (1961), the linear model provides an excellent approximation to data even when there are interactions or nonlinear relationships in the data. In the case of information integration, Anderson’s classical theory of integration is also based on an “averaging model” (Anderson 1981), which is a variant of the general linear model.

Researchers in psychology and attitude formation have, however, noted that individuals are more likely to use nonlinear strategies in decision making and attitude formation. For example, Kort (1968) found that, in the case of workmen’s compensation claims, multiplicative combinations improved predictive power over the linear model. Brannick and Brannick (1989) conducted two performance evaluation studies and determined that performance evaluation tasks involve nonlinear judgment strategies in individuals. Past research has also shown that decision makers themselves believe that they do not use linear models in their cognitive activity. This has been shown in the case of evaluations of political candidates (Einhorn et al. 1972), medical judgments (Einhorn 1972), and the evaluation of college success by highschool counselors (Mertz and Doherty 1974). In each study the focus was on the modelling of judgment.

The above studies have noted that alternative models of judgment, such as the conjunctive and the disjunctive models, are better representations of judgmental processes (Coombs 1964, Tversky 1972). The conjunctive rule implies that the criterion is a function of the minimum of several independent variables. For example, it would not be practical to “hire an applicant for the position of a pilot who was blind though the person was best on ‘aeronautical principles’ and best in ‘coordination’ and ‘dexterity’” (Ghiselli et al. 1981). The disjunctive model implies the opposite: The dependent variable is a function of the maximum of the independent variables. Thus in selecting a football player, for example, we might want to select someone who can kick or run or pass. The conjunctive processes, therefore, follow the “and” rule, while disjunctive processes are based on the “or” rule. For this reason these models, unlike the linear model, are not considered to be additive.

The types of variables (and situations) that induce nonlinear noncompensatory strategies in general have been described by Ganzach (1993). First, when judgment becomes complex (i.e., the number of input cues increases), or when less deliberation is devoted to judgment, noncompensatory strategies become dominant, since these strategies serve to simplify the judgment process (Billings and Scherer 1988). Second, authors such as Simonson (1989) have noted that a judge’s goal affects his or her judgment strategy. For example, if a judge seeks to award a higher evaluation, he or she will seek out reasons to be lenient and rely more on positive cues (a disjunctive strategy). However, when the goal is to reach a stricter judgment, there will be more focus on negative cues (a conjunctive strategy).

In the current study, our goal is to describe combinatorial rules other than those used in linear models and examine whether they apply to the judgment of UIS. We, therefore, examine models which have been shown to be representations of conjunctive decision making (the multiplicative model), the interactive model, a variant of the conjunctive model (logarithmic), and two models based on profile information (scatter 1 and 2 models). We are interested in understanding if the judgment of UIS can be modelled through the use of conjunctive strategies or can be inferred from profile scores (also nonlinear strategies).

We propose that user information satisfaction (UIS), as a behavior response “to a variety of factors related to the delivery of information products and services” (Bailey and Pearson 1983, Ives et al. 1983), is also amenable to nonlinear modeling. In measuring UIS, a judge (the user) responds to several pieces of information (IS attributes) and configures these responses into a single unit (the UIS score). Processes involved in this configural process have been previously assumed to follow the linear weighted average model. However, as noted by Ostrom (1989),

It is possible, of course, that people do not aggregate their thoughts at all when expressing or acting on attitudes in everyday settings. Instead they may just sample one or two of their thoughts and base their overt attitudinal responses on that sample. People may select the most salient, the most important, the most extreme, the most representative, or the most bland thought they have about the attitude object and base their response on that one thought alone (p. 27).

Ostrom suggests that information combinatorial rules used by individuals to judge UIS may not always be additive and/or linear. In this study, therefore, we compare the linear regression model with five nonlinear, noncompensatory models that are considered to better represent evaluation strategies. The objective is to compare different models with respect to their prediction power for UIS calculated from user IS attribute evaluations.

Below we review the previous analytical treatment of UIS and then present an alternate formulation of UIS based on attitude research. Two sets of data are used in the current study to examine if nonlinear, noncompensatory models can be employed in the measurement of UIS. In the first study, data were collected from 50 faculty members at a midwestern U.S. university and analyzed to examine different UIS models. In addition, we also examined previously published data from a study that was conducted to measure user satisfaction through the use of the Bailey and Pearson short-form questionnaire (Galletta and Lederer 1989). The details of data analysis for both studies are described below.

## Analytical Treatment of UIS

The analytical treatment of UIS has followed the Bailey and Pearson (1983) formulation of UIS wherein

$$
S _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i j} W _ {i j}\tag{1}
$$

where $R _ { i j } =$ the reaction to factor j by individual $i ;$ and $W _ { i j } =$ the importance of factor j to individual i.

These models postulate that a user’s attitude (in this case UIS or S ) can be determined by his or her evaluation of a set of relevant system attributes $R _ { i j }$ weighted by his or her perceptions of the importance of these attributes $W _ { i j } .$ Alternate models have dropped the importance scale to use a model where UIS is a summation of attribute evaluations only; e.g., Doll and Torkzadeh (1988), Joshi (1992), and Miller and Doyle (1987). In these cases, UIS is measured as:

$$
S _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i j}\tag{2}
$$

The critical elements of these models are the components employed and the compositional rules assumed; i.e., the manner in which the model assumes that individuals combine the various pieces of information measured. The UIS model assumes the following.

• The contribution to the effect for each attribute is multiplicative; $\mathrm { e . g . , } \sum _ { j \mathrm { = 1 } } ^ { n } R _ { i j } W _ { i j } .$

• The powers of the terms in the product for each attribute are assumed to be 1.

• The product terms $\sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i j } W _ { i j } \operatorname { o r } \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } R _ { i j }$ add over attributes to form an overall effect measure.

The above assumptions have been accepted in UIS research, and no studies have been performed to assess their validity. The above models are based on the theory of cognitive integration which deals with the question of how people derive an overall attitudinal disposition from an array of knowledge and beliefs they hold about an attitude object. Anderson’s Information Integration Theory (Anderson 1981, Fishbein and Ajzen 1975, Rosenberg 1956) provides the framework for cognitive integration in the form of two concepts: valuation and integration.

Valuation refers to the process of determining the evaluative scale values and the weights assigned to each item of information that contributes to the attitudinal judgment. The scale values vary from positive to negative and are considered independent of context and other cognitive elements. The weight parameter reflects the influence of the cognitive element in determining the overall attitude. It can vary from zero to one with its value influenced by context.

Integration refers to the process of combining the information units into an overall attitudinal judgment. In Anderson’s theory, the integration process is one of algebraically combining the weight and scale value parameters into a single judgment. The algebraic rule that appears to best describe evaluative judgments, such as person impressions, is averaging: The numerator contains the sum of the products of weight times scale value (across all cognitive components) and the denominator is simply the sum of all weights. Information theory, however, allows for the possibility that other rules may be used for different judgment domains $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , summation might describe the value of a stock portfolio, and multiplication may be best suited for job performance evaluation).

There are several assumptions implied by the above theory. For example, Devine et al. (1994) note that the theory, as stated, postulates the following: (1) there exists a memory store in which weight and scale value parameters are stored for each cognition; (2) the parameters for each relevant cognition are retrieved; (3) the requisite algebraic operations are performed on those parameters; and (4) the resulting implicit evaluation response is used as the basis of an overt response.

Such an analysis raises a variety of questions. Where do the weights and scale values come from? How can there be a store large enough to contain all the weights and scale values for the cognitive elements, especially given that each cognitive element must have two parameters for each judgment dimension? How accurate is the “synaptic calculator” that conducts the needed algebraic manipulations on the parameter values? Is the implicit judgment discarded upon overt response, or is a decision made whether or not to store it for future use?

In response to the above questions, Devine et al. (1994) suggest that perhaps there are other mechanisms used for attitude formation. These mechanisms are derived from the broader set of cognitive activities potentially involved in producing attitude responses. For example, Stone (1960) suggests the random walk model under which individuals may randomly sample knowledge and beliefs about the attitude object, extracting a valence from each sampling. A positive response is generated when the positive count reaches a specified number, or a negative response when a specified negative count is obtained. The first of these two thresholds reached will trigger a response.

## A Review of Alternate Judgment Models

In general, there are two classes of judgment models: compensatory and noncompensatory (Green and Wind 1973). Compensatory models are those in which changes in one attribute can be offset by opposing changes in another attribute. The best known of the compensatory models is the additive utility model, which states that the utility of an object U(x) is equal to the sum of the utilities of its parts $U _ { i } .$ Thus:

$$
U (x) = U _ {1} (x _ {1}) + U _ {2} (x _ {2}) + \ldots + U _ {n} (x _ {n})
$$

In a multiple regression formulation, the utility of the alternatives x is assumed to represent a linear combination of the arguments.

Noncompensatory models do not allow any tradeoffs between attributes. These models do allow a comparison on an attribute by attribute basis without collapsing profiles into a single utility number. Several nonlinear, noncompensatory models have been proposed in the literature as representations of the judgment process (Einhorn 1970). These models are listed in Table 1.

The linear compensatory model is shown as Eq. (1).

Equation (2) is a multiplicative model developed by Einhorn (1971) as a diagnostic for conjunctive combinations. Under the conjunctive model, when an individual surpasses some threshold, then the outcome measure $Y$ is dependent on all attributes $x _ { i }$ being greater than a minimum cutoff value. Dawes (1974) called this the minimum evaluation function because of the minimum cutoff requirement on all attributes which implies a multiple cutoff procedure rather than a linear compensatory procedure.

The model in Eq. (3) is a variant of the regression model—the interactive model—proposed by Pedhazur (1982). The additional term (PX ) is the product of the independent variables, and a hierarchical test is used to determine if the interaction term increases predictability. As noted by Brannick and Brannick (1989), under the Einhorn model $( \operatorname { E q } . 2 )$ , nonlinearity is assumed but not tested. A separate slope is estimated for each independent variable in Eq. (2), while in Eq. (3) a separate slope is estimated for each X variable as well as the product of all X variables.

Equation (4) is the logarithmic model proposed by Goldberg (1971) as a result of critiques of the Einhorn model. These models are based on the idea that more information is contained in the pattern of dimension values than is contained in a weighted sum of these values. The first two terms in Eq. (5) are the ordinary linear regression coefficients, with the intercept a and the b slopes. The last term is the profile scatter term proposed by Cronbach and Glesser (1953).

Equation (6) differs from Eq. (5) in that the scatter term is now weighted with the importance of each attribute. This model suggests that individuals attend to a few cues and then estimate the dependent variable. Then they examine the consistency of the important cues. If the cues are consistent, the initial estimate of the dependent variable is retained. If the cues are inconsistent, the estimate is revised downward. This model differs from Eq. (5) in that the Scatter 1 model suggests that individuals attend to the consistency of all cues, not just a few important ones.

## Study 1

In this study, the data to test alternate models were collected using a mail questionnaire sent to faculty members at a midwestern academic institution. The questionnaire was mailed to 75 faculty members of the

College of Business Administration. Fifty questionnaires were returned (67%). All respondents were assured of confidentiality and anonymity.

The questionnaire was designed to elicit responses related to: (1) overall UIS with the IS function at the university; and (2) factors or components of IS that are important to the respondents and the degree of satisfaction/dissatisfaction of the respondents relating to each of these factors.

Prior to questionnaire development, interviews were conducted with 15 faculty members on campus. In addition, interviews were conducted with six departmental chairs and four deans. Each interview lasted about one half hour and was recorded and transcribed. Based on these interviews, 16 factors were identified as being relevant to the measurement of UIS. These factors are shown in Appendix 1, and relate to satisfaction with hardware, computer labs, networks, computer support staff skills and responsiveness, and training programs available to faculty (Cronbach’s alpha 4 0.856).

Finally, all respondents were asked for an overall evaluation of computer resources in the university. This question was on the first page of the questionnaire and measured overall UIS on a 1–5 scale, with 1 being extremely dissatisfied and 5 being extremely satisfied.

## Model Estimation

Guidelines for the estimation of the various models described above are provided by Brannick and Brannick (1989) and Einhorn (1971).

Equation (1) is simply a regression model with separate slopes estimated for each of the IS attributes. For Eq. (2), the logarithm of both dependent and independent variables is taken and used in an ordinary linear regression to fit the slope and the intercept. Similarly, in Eq. (3), linear regression is used to estimate one slope each for the attributes and one for the product of the attribute terms.

In the Scatter 1 model (Eq. 5), the profile scatter term is calculated first. The predictors are standardized with zero means and unit variance across each response. The average z¯ is then computed for every individual, and the scatter around the profile mean (z –z¯) is calculated. This procedure results in a scatter score for each individual, which is then included in a simultaneous linear regression with the full set of independent variables.

For the Scatter 2 model, a two-step procedure is used to calculate the scatter term. First, an ordinary linear regression with all variables standardized is computed on the independent variables without the scatter term. The scatter term is then calculated using information from the regression analysis. The estimated value of Y $( Y ^ { * } )$ is then used to calculate the scatter term. $Y ^ { \ast }$ replaces the z¯ in Eq. (6). In addition, the slope estimates $b _ { i }$ obtained in the linear regression are then used as weights for each profile. A second simultaneous regression is then computed to reestimate the slopes for the independent variables and a single slope for the scatter term.

The $R ^ { 2 }$ values of the linear model are then compared to the $R ^ { 2 }$ obtained from Eqs. (2), (3), (4), (5), and (6). If there is any increase in predictive power from the use of nonlinear models over the linear model, then we can state that users employ nonlinear processes in reaching a judgment regarding their overall satisfaction with information systems.

## Study 2—Data from Galletta and Lederer (1989)

One of the primary difficulties with Study 1 (described in the previous section) is that it develops a new instrument to measure UIS. This was considered necessary because the available instruments, developed by Bailey and Pearson (1983) and Doll and Torkzadeh (1988), were not considered to reflect an academic computing environment. However, the results of Study 1 should also be applicable in cases where data are collected by instruments such as those by Bailey and Pearson (1983) and Doll and Torkzadeh (1988).

Galletta and Lederer (1989) have previously published the results of a study which collected data from 105 managers and executives using the 13–item version of the Bailey and Pearson (1983) instrument. Data from that study were provided to us by the authors and used to assess the efficiency of the nonlinear, noncompensatory models described above.

The 13 items (2 scales for each item) in the UIS questionnaire were grouped into three factors—product factor, support factor, and involvement factor—as described by Ives, Olson, and Baroudi (1983). These factor scores along with the overall UIS scores for each respondent were used in the analysis.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The results in Tables 2 and 3 taken together suggest that users’ judgment can be represented by nonlinear, noncompensatory models, or a combination of judgment strategies when determining their satisfaction with their IS environment. Thus, in the first study, the squared multiple coefficients for the linear model were

0.616, the interactive result was 0.617, the log result was 0.650, the scatter 1 result was 0.676, the scatter 2 result was 0.685, and the multiplicative result was 0.692. In Eqs. 3, 5, and $^ { 6 , }$ the $b _ { k + 1 }$ was significant, confirming that nonlinear models are more efficient. Similarly, in the second study, the squared multiple coefficients for the linear model were 0.2401, 0.2653 for the multiplicative, 0.2594 for the interactive, 0.2699 for the logarithmic, and 0.2402 and 0.2653 respectively for the two scatter models.

It can be argued that, in models with more parameters, larger coefficients may be expected. However, (a) the $b _ { k + 1 }$ in all cases is significant; and (b) the larger coefficients for the nonlinear models in crossvalidation samples preclude a chance effect. In addition, if the $b _ { k + 1 }$ did not account for additional variance, lower average coefficients than in the linear model cross validation sample would result (Bock and Jones 1968).

The analysis in this study is motivated by a need to understand how to capture or represent the way users integrate information regarding IS attributes into a global UIS index. What is of interest is the processing of information that precedes and determines individual judgment, especially with regard to UIS. We have focused on one element of this processing: the cognitive operations performed on attribute information in order to yield an overall attitude score (UIS score). In all models, discrete information regarding IS attributes is combined by the user. In this study we examine how this is done.

The Bailey and Pearson (1983) UIS research stream has followed the information integration or the linear model approach. This model has also been referred to as the regression or the correlation paradigm (Slovic and Lichtenstein, 1971). The broader approach requires a judge to make quantitative evaluations of a number of stimuli, each of which is defined by one or more quantified cue dimensions or characteristics. The focus is on describing a judge’s method of combining and weighting information by developing mathematical equations of the combinatorial process. Information integration theory is one example of this philosophy (Anderson 1981). As described above, integration theory is concerned with two problems: first, the scaling of cues; and, second, the weighting parameters. Each piece of information is evaluated and assigned a weight to obtain a user’s global judgment.

While this model of judgment and attitude formation has been widely used, another consistent theme in the literature has been the assertion by judges that their judgment processes are complex and configurable. For example, Wiggins and Hoffman (1978) used 861 MMPI profiles to show that quadratic equations better described the judges in the sample. Similarly, studies in which judges predicted the effects of foreign policies (Summers and Stewart 1968), rated the attractiveness of gambles (Slovic and Lichtenstein 1971), and evaluated the quality of patient care in hospitals (Huber et al. 1968) found improvements in predictability by the use of nonlinear models.

In the fields of judgment analysis, psychology, and marketing, researchers have noted that despite the ease of use of linear models, they may not be theoretically correct representations of some psychological phenomena (Birnbaum 1974, Dawes and Corrigan 1974, Hoffman 1960). In fact, Oglive and Schmitt (1979) observe that a good deal of judgment research fails to consider the possibility of nonlinear or configural cue usage simply because the linear model has proven both so potent and so parsimonious. The issue is understanding attitudinal judgment formation (including UIS) and how people integrate information to arrive at an overall judgment or understanding of “what cognitive processes underlie judgment” (Einhorn et al. 1979). The emphasis here is on the nature of the cognitive processes, as opposed to the adoption of more complex statistical models. Cooksey (1996), while examining the issue of the use of statistical models based on noncompensatory decision-making, notes: “While one might not be able to show statistically significant increment to judgment prediction using a configural model over a linear model, the configural (nonlinear) model may be more congruent with the judge’s actual thought processes.”

Following this line of reasoning, we have examined several nonlinear models to determine if they better predict a user’s satisfaction. Similar to research conducted by Einhorn (1971), we were interested in whether nonlinear models are more efficient predictors of UIS given a set of attribute evaluations. Therefore, in this study we selected several nonlinear models which have been used in the past to assess judgments and evaluations (Brannick and Brannick 1989). The results of our study confirm that nonlinear models improve prediction of the judgment of user information satisfaction.

The models in this study represent mechanisms to predict judgment in the case of UIS. As such, they provide guidelines to understand alternate mechanisms that may be used in users’ evaluations of their information systems environment. Although there are several models presented in this study, their use was motivated by our objective to examine whether nonlinear, noncompensatory models may provide more insight into user judgments. The adoption of a specific model for use requires a more extensive theoretical examination of when each model may be used. For example, we believe that the number of cues or scales presented as a part of the UIS instrument will affect the judgment model used. Thus, when the number of cues or factors increases, the decision model will change to one of the simpler nonlinear models.

In order for the current research approach to progress, we suggest the use of controlled field studies, where control is exercised on the number of factors presented; the pre-judgment outlook of the users to their environment; involvement of the users with information resources; and the users’ tendency toward lenient versus strict decisions. An evaluation of decisions in such controlled studies can be used to isolate models for specific measurement contexts.
