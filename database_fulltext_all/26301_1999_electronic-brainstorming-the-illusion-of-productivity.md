## Electronic Brainstorming: The Illusion of Productivity

While group work has become increasingly important in organizations, it is often considered to be inefficient and unproductive, especially for the purpose of generating ideas (Hackman and Kaplan 1974, Hymowitz

1988). Indeed, empirical research in both group psychology and social psychology has consistently shown that people generate fewer and lower quality ideas in face-to-face brainstorming than in nominal brainstorming (i.e., when group members generate ideas by working alone, without interacting with other group members) (Chatterjea and Mitra 1976, Diehl and

Stroebe 1987, Gruman 1968, Mullen et al. 1991, Paulus et al. 1995, Renzulli et al. 1974, Stroebe and Diehl 1994, Sutton and Hargadon 1996).

Electronic brainstorming (EBS) was offered by MIS researchers as a solution that alleviates the problems inherent to group brainstorming (e.g., production blocking, evaluation apprehension) while enhancing its strengths. Although research has shown EBS to be superior to face-to-face brainstorming, empirical evidence supporting its superiority over nominal brainstorming is weak. The present paper addresses this issue. To do so, the paper first analyzes the literature in group and social psychology in order to explain why face-to-face brainstorming has been found to be less productive than nominal brainstorming. It then proceeds to discuss the reasons why EBS was expected to surpass nominal brainstorming and provides explanations as to why the existing empirical evidence does not support this expectation. Subsequently, a laboratory experiment that tests some of these explanations is described and the results presented. Finally, the study implications and directions for future research are discussed.

## EBS Theory and Research

Past EBS research has principally focussed on three problems inherent to face-to-face brainstorming (i.e., when generating ideas verbally in groups) to explain its low productivity. First, groups may suffer from evaluation apprehension. That is, group members may feel reticent in expressing their ideas because they may fear retaliation or they may worry that they will be evaluated or judged by others (Camacho and Paulus 1995, Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Jablin and Seibold 1978, Lamm and Trommsdorf 1973, Mullen et al. 1991). In fact, groups have been found to generate fewer ideas when an authority figure was present (Mullen et al. 1991) and when group members were anxious about social interactions (Camacho and Paulus 1995). Second, groups may suffer from production blocking. In face-to-face brainstorming, group members frequently have to wait for others to finish talking before they can express their own thoughts. This waiting time impairs productivity because ideas might become irrelevant as discussion progresses, people might forget their ideas, or, to avoid forgetting them, individuals might rehearse ideas rather than generate new ones (Diehl and Stroebe 1991, Paulus et al. 1997, Stroebe and Diehl 1994). Finally, it is often argued that groups may suffer from free riding. This refers to individuals intentionally limiting their efforts and contributions by relying on other group members to accomplish the brainstorming task at hand (Diehl and Stroebe 1987). The free riding phenomenon typically occurs in groups when individuals perceive their effort to be dispensable (Harkins and Petty 1982, Kerr and Bruun 1983), or feel that the responsibility for generating ideas is diffused (Latane´ et al. 1979, Williams et al. 1981), or because of social or cognitive loafing (Albanese and Van Fleet 1985, Diehl and Stroebe 1987).

During the past decade, EBS has been offered as a solution capable of overcoming the shortcomings of the traditional face-to-face brainstorming approach. With EBS, participants generate ideas using a network of computers that facilitates the entry and sharing of ideas among group members. Typically, EBS groups use a specially designed room and EBS software to enter their ideas on a keyboard, and each member can see the ideas generated by the others on his or her screen.<sup>1</sup> Further, group members can generate ideas in parallel and enter them simultaneously into their workstations. With such a setup, EBS is expected to help reduce the problems inherent to face-to-face brainstorming described earlier (Dennis and Valacich 1993). For instance, parallelism helps reduce production blocking losses since participants no longer have to wait for others to express their ideas (Gallupe et al. 1991, Jessup et al. 1990). Parallelism can also diminish free riding since members do not have to compete for speaking time and everyone’s contribution is facilitated. In addition, the EBS software usually enables a form of anonymity whereby each participant can read on his or her screen the ideas generated by other group members without knowing from whom they originate.

This may make participants less concerned about producing ideas that other group members disapprove of. As a result, EBS is also thought to reduce evaluation apprehension losses (Connolly et al. 1990, Gallupe et al. 1991, Gallupe et al. 1992).<sup>2</sup>

EBS is also expected to introduce several advantages over nominal brainstorming. These can be grouped into two categories: efficiency advantages and stimulation advantages. With nominal brainstorming, participants do not see the ideas produced by the other group members. Thus, the same idea may end up being generated by more than one participant. On the other hand, brainstorming group members using EBS can see other members’ ideas (or a subset of these) on their respective screens and are therefore less prone to waste as much time generating redundant ideas as members of nominal groups. Thus, EBS is thought to be more efficient than nominal brainstorming (Gallupe et al. 1991). In addition, it is also argued that a group as a whole has more information than any one member, that it provides synergistic effects, and that working in a group encourages individuals to perform better (Nunamaker et al. 1991). Thus, by allowing participants to see others’ ideas on their screens, EBS is thought to inspire them to think in novel directions and to spur new ideas. This is thought to render EBS groups more creative and productive than nominal brainstorming groups.

Based on the above presumed theoretical advantages, EBS is often expected to outperform not only face-to-face brainstorming, but also nominal brainstorming (Dennis et al. 1996). However, existing empirical evidence in this regard is mixed (Cooper et al. 1990, Dennis and Valacich 1993, Gallupe et al. 1991, Gallupe et al. 1990, Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1990, Valacich et al. 1994). Figure 1 presents a summary of past studies that compared EBS to other forms of brainstorming.

The results of these studies can be summarized as follows. For groups consisting of less than nine members, EBS groups were never found to generate more unique ideas than nominal brainstorming groups (Cooper et al. 1990, Dennis and Valacich 1993, Gallupe et al. 1990 and 1991, Valacich et al. 1994).<sup>3</sup> For larger groups, EBS was found to be superior to nominal brainstorming in two studies (Dennis and Valacich 1993, Valacich et al. 1994). It should be noted that in these two studies, the productivity of EBS groups was compared to that of pooled nominal groups<sup>4</sup> which were artificially formed after the experiment by randomly pooling together the ideas generated by individuals working independently and asynchronously. On the other hand, EBS groups generated ideas simultaneously while being in the same room. Being part of a group is claimed to instill a certain “group identity” and to generate synergy (Dennis and Valacich 1993). Because they lack this synergy, pooled nominal groups are not directly comparable to EBS groups. Thus, even for very large groups, the evidence supporting the superiority of EBS to nominal brainstorming is not clear. Overall, the main consistent finding emerging from past research is that participants using EBS are generally more satisfied than participants using other forms of brainstorming (Cooper et al. 1990, Dennis and Valacich 1993; Gallupe et al. 1990 and 1991, Valacich et al. 1994).

To summarize, five studies have investigated the idea generation productivity of EBS versus nominal brainstorming. In every study, for one treatment or more, nominal brainstorming was found to be at least as productive (and possibly more productive) than EBS. For groups of size three (one experiment), four (four experiments), six (two experiments), and eight (one experiment), nominal brainstorming was at least equal in productivity to EBS. In one experiment it was found to be superior to EBS for groups of six. EBS was found to be superior only to pooled nominal brainstorming and for groups larger than nine. Thus, the conclusion that can be drawn from the existing empirical evidence is that EBS has not fulfilled its original promise. Nominal brainstorming groups appear to be at least as productive as EBS groups.

## Why EBS Has Not Been Found Superior to Nominal Brainstorming: A Process Gains and Process Losses Approach

In line with Steiner (1972), this paper argues that the actual idea generation productivity of a given brainstorming technology stems from the combined positive effects of its process gains (factors that increase or enhance groups’ idea generation productivity) and the negative effects of its process losses (factors that decrease or inhibit groups’ idea generation productivity). Thus, the overall idea generation productivity of a group will be increased by a brainstorming technology when the technology’s process gains exceed its process losses. In contrast, the group’s productivity will be diminished when the brainstorming technology’s process losses exceed its process gains (Dennis and Valacich 1993, Hill 1982, Steiner 1972).

Although EBS has clearly been shown to be more productive than verbal brainstorming, contrary to expectations, the empirical evidence demonstrating its superiority over nominal brainstorming has been mixed. A comprehensive examination of the different process gains and process losses identified in group psychology and social psychology research reveals several gains and losses that past EBS research has failed to consider, thus providing clues to a potential explanation of this situation. Figure 2 lists the different process gains and losses identified in group and social psychology and applies them to four brainstorming technologies examined in this paper.

These process gains and losses can be grouped under three types of mechanisms: procedural, social psychological, and economic (Camacho and Paulus 1995, Mullen et al. 1991). As shown in Figure 2, a simple count<sup>5</sup> of the process gains and process losses of the four brainstorming technologies indicates that verbal brainstorming appears to be the least productive approach (with four gains and eight losses, giving a net score of 1 4), while nominal, EBS-anonymous, and EBS-nonanonymous brainstorming seem comparable (with respective net scores of 1, 1, 0). Verbal brainstorming benefits from four main process gains (cognitive stimulation, observational learning, social recognition, motivation/arousal) and suffers from eight process losses that impair its productivity (production blocking, cognitive interference, evaluation apprehension, negative productivity matching, pressure for cognitive uniformity, personalization of issues, social influence, free riding). Nominal brainstorming benefits from the advantages of task process separation, task orientation, and motivation/arousal. However, nominal group members are more likely to generate duplicate ideas and embark on single trains of thought. While EBS-anonymous combines the advantages of both verbal and nominal brainstorming (separation of task processes, cognitive stimulation, task orientation, motivation/arousal), it may also suffer from three process losses (cognitive interference, negative productivity matching, free riding). Similarly, EBS-nonanonymous benefits from six process gains (separation of task processes, cognitive stimulation, observational learning, social recognition, task orientation, motivation/arousal), but it also suffers from six process losses (cognitive interference, evaluation apprehension, negative productivity matching, pressure for cognitive uniformity/conformity, personalization of issues, social influence).

As discussed earlier, the EBS literature claims that EBS introduces process gains by reinforcing groups assets (i.e., task orientation, efficient communications, and group processes) while generating few process losses, thus enhancing groups’ productivity in some circumstances. However, Figure 2 indicates that the number of process losses inherent to EBS may actually be greater than those typically encountered in the EBS literature, and that EBS’s net gains over nominal brainstorming may only be marginal.

Such an analysis helps explain why nominal, EBSanonymous, and EBS-nonanonymous have been found to be equally productive and to outperform verbal brainstorming. While EBS benefits from the process gains associated with verbal and nominal brainstorming, it also introduces new process losses that attenuate its productivity to the level of nominal brainstorming. Thus, using a process gains/process losses approach, an important EBS research issue then becomes the identification of different factors or conditions under which EBS productivity may surpass that of nominal brainstorming. In the present paper, we identify and test three such factors.

Group History, Contextual Cues, and Topic Sensitivity as Factors Increasing EBS Productivity Research in group psychology and social psychology suggests that factors such as group history (Krayer 1988, Mennecke et al. 1992), motivation potential of the brainstorming topic (Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Paulus and Dzindolet 1993, Gallupe et al. 1996), and the use of contextual cues (La Greca 1980; Paulus and Dzindolet 1993, Paulus et al. 1993) can affect the productivity of brainstorming groups. These factors can help improve EBS groups’ productivity to the extent that they increase the process gains of EBS and/or reduce its process losses. These three factors, as well as how they are expected to affect the different process gains and process losses identified in Figure 2 (for verbal, nominal, EBS-anonymous, and EBS-nonanonymous brainstorming technologies), are described below.

Group History. “Group history” refers to the extent to which a group has previously worked together prior to an experimental session. To date, most EBS research has been conducted with ad hoc groups formed by subjects randomly brought together for the purposes of an experimental session. These ad hoc groups can be thought of as having zero group history, since their members have not yet worked together, do not know each other, and have not developed norms of group behavior. Such groups are immature, tend to be less task oriented, and often rely on a leader to structure their group processes (Wheelan and Hochberger 1996). Moreover, their communication efforts are heavily oriented toward defining the group’s operating procedures and toward establishing each member’s position regarding the topic to be discussed (Krayer 1988, Lacoursiere 1980, Mennecke et al. 1992, Tuckman 1965). On the other hand, members of groups with a meaningful history of working together already know each other, have established norms of group behavior and performance, are used to working together, and are able to communicate more effectively, with most of their efforts in a group session being oriented toward resolving the problem at hand (Bennis and Shepard 1956, Bion 1961, Slater 1966, Tuckman 1965, Wheelan and Hochberger 1996).

Chidambaram et al. (1991) reviewed the literature on group development and identified four characteristics of a mature, well-developed (established) group: (1) it is a well-knit, cohesive unit, with members working toward the same goal; (2) its members learn to handle major conflicts productively, while maintaining differences of opinion; (3) the group is capable of productive work, its members are creative, efficient, and effective; and (4) group think, group myopia, and other disrup tive forces are at their lowest levels. In a study of “real” ongoing groups (groups with a history), Zack (1993) suggested that members of such groups assume that other group members understand them and that they share their views concerning issues group members are familiar with. Thus, it could be argued that in situations where group members are familiar with the task at hand, the shared understanding of established groups can limit group productivity (since group members assume that they all share a common viewpoint and that they are very knowledgeable about the topic, they might judge their personal contribution to group work to be dispensable, thereby reducing their personal effort). However, in situations where group members are unfamiliar with the task (e.g., the experiment described below), such a shared understanding would not exist, thereby having no diminishing effect on the productivity of established groups. On the other hand, members of established groups are likely to be more efficient than members of ad hoc groups when it comes to establishing group norms or explaining complex statements or ideas. As such, they are likely to be more productive than ad hoc groups. In a paper synthesizing development processes in decision-making groups, Poole and Baldwin (1996) found that welldeveloped groups tended to follow a variety of paths during decision making and were more creative in their interaction processes.

Because established group members know each other well, the process gains associated with observational learning and social recognition (shown in Figure 2) are likely to be enhanced in the case of established groups using verbal and EBS-nonanonymous brainstorming technologies. In addition, established groups are likely to reinforce the task orientation already present in EBS (both anonymous and nonanonymous). Finally, because established groups have been found to experience little social loafing (Erez and Somech 1996), they are not likely to suffer from free riding either. Thus, any productivity advantages EBS may have over other brainstorming approaches ought to be more readily observable when studying established groups than when studying ad hoc groups.

Social Sensitivity of Topic. Working in groups has been found to stimulate individuals to perform better (Lamm and Trommsdorf 1973, Shaw 1981, Zajonc 1965). However, participants in past EBS experiments may not have been sufficiently motivated to perform as well as they could. If participants are not sufficiently motivated to generate ideas, any advantages EBS may have over other brainstorming approaches will be harder to observe (since the productivity levels of the groups for all approaches will tend to be generally low). Research indicates that when generating ideas about a topic considered to be socially less sensitive (i.e., not controversial), individuals are likely to be less motivated (and therefore less productive) than when generating ideas about a topic that is socially more sensitive (Fiske and Taylor 1991, Karau and Williams 1993).<sup>6</sup> Having mainly employed socially less sensitive topics for their idea generation tasks, past EBS research may have suffered from such a lack of participant motivation. Thus, it is expected that socially sensitive topics will reinforce the motivation/ arousal process gains associated with working in groups (verbal, EBS-anonymous and EBS-nonanonymous) and will introduce this process gain in nominal brainstorming.

On the other hand, socially sensitive topics may also increase the evaluation apprehension and anxiety levels of group members and their felt pressure for cognitive conformity (around socially acceptable trains of thought), which could reduce their productivity. However, these process losses are likely to be significantly reduced, if not eliminated, when participants are allowed to generate ideas anonymously (Pinsonneault and Heppel 1998).

Further, high anxiety participants have been found to deal with their anxiety by looking at the ideas generated by others before venturing their own (Cialdini 1993, Jackall 1988). Seeing others express risky or extreme ideas, especially in the case of socially sensitive topics, could encourage EBS group members to express some ideas they would not have otherwise ventured. As such effects are absent in nominal brainstorming, any self-censorship that socially sensitive topics may induce should be alleviated to a greater extent with EBS than with nominal brainstorming. Consequently, any productivity advantages EBS may have over other brainstorming approaches ought to be more readily observable when studying groups generating ideas on socially sensitive topics (anonymously) than when studying groups generating ideas on socially less sensitive topics (regardless of anonymity).

Contextual Cues. Empirical evidence suggests that contextual cues (i.e., supplemental environmental stimuli that can provide hints or clues which help participants in performing the task at hand) of a vague nature can stimulate individual creativity (Sobel and Rothenberg 1980). Environments rich in vague cues (e.g., where superimposed pictures and other objects provide imprecise clues to possible responses) have been found to foster cognitive stimulation and improved creativity (Friedman et al. 1978, Higgins and Chaires 1980, La Greca 1980, Paulus and Dzindolet 1993, Paulus et al. 1993, Sobel and Rothenberg 1980, Ward 1969). Displaying other participants’ ideas on one’s screen might be more typical of providing precise cues than vague ones. As such, the creativity and productivity enhancing characteristics of this EBS feature may be limited. Increasing the stimulation level of EBS groups with contextual cues of a broad nature could be expected to reinforce stimulation (a process gain), thereby increasing the productivity of groups using EBS. In nominal brainstorming, providing contextual cues is likely to introduce a cognitive stimulation process gain (shown in Figure 2), but this effect is likely to be less pronounced because each participant will be stimulated by the same set of ideas that triggered individual idea generation processes. This may incite some group members to generate more ideas or different ideas, but the effect will be localized and not shared with other group members (until the ideas have been generated). On the other hand, in EBS groups, the stimulation process engendered by contextual cues will be shared by all members of the group. As a result, the process gains of vague contextual cues are likely to be more pronounced in EBS groups than in nominal groups. Thus, any productivity advantages EBS may have over other brainstorming approaches should be more readily observable when studying groups generating ideas in environments with vague cues than when studying groups generating ideas without such cues.

## Study Hypotheses

According to the process gains/process losses framework of Figure 2 (and assuming equal weightings for all gains and losses), groups using nominal brainstorming would be expected to be as productive as groups using EBS (since both have equivalent net scores). A different result would suggest that the positive or negative impacts different factors have on idea generation productivity are not equal. Thus, the primary hypothesis of this study is:

Hypothesis 1. There will be no difference between the productivity (in terms of number of unique ideas) of groups using EBS-anonymous and groups using nominal brainstorming.

However, the three factors discussed above are expected to influence the process gains and process losses of different brainstorming technologies. Specifically, group history (i.e., established groups), social sensitivity of the idea generation topic (i.e., sensitive topics), and contextual cues (i.e., presence of vague environmental stimuli) are each expected to increase the productivity of EBS groups. Thus, the secondary hypotheses of the present study are:

Hypothesis 2. Established groups using EBS (anonymous or nonanonymous) will generate more unique ideas than established groups using nominal brainstorming.

Hypothesis 3. For socially sensitive topics, groups using EBS-anonymous will generate more unique ideas than groups using nominal brainstorming.

Hypothesis 4. Groups using EBS-anonymous with contextual cues will generate more unique ideas than groups using nominal brainstorming with contextual cues.

In order to test these hypotheses, an experiment was conducted where EBS (anonymous and nonanonymous) was compared to two alternative brainstorming approaches (face-to-face and nominal) under varying conditions of group history, social sensitivity of the brainstorming topic, and vague contextual cues.

## Method

## Research Design

The experimental design of the study was an unbalanced repeated measures model with three withingroup factors: technology (four levels), topic sensitivity (two levels), contextual cues (two levels); and one between-group factor: group history (two levels). The main dependent variable was the number of unique ideas. Sixteen six-member established groups and 16 six-member ad hoc groups were randomly assigned to treatments. Groups of six individuals were used because there is substantial evidence indicating that groups of five to six are more effective with EBS than groups of three to four (Benbasat and Lim 1993, DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987, Gallupe et al. 1992, George et al. 1990, George et al. 1988, Hare 1992, Valacich et al. 1994). Larger group sizes (e.g., 12 or more) were not used since, as mentioned earlier, evidence supporting the superiority of EBS to nominal brainstorming for groups of 12 or more is inconclusive and has not been widely replicated. In addition, our experience suggests that groups of 12 or 18 members are less common and less important in organizational settings than groups made up of fewer than ten individuals.

The four technology levels, the two topic sensitivity levels, and the two contextual cues levels represent a total of 16 treatments (4 2 2 2 2). Each group was subjected to four of the possible 16 treatments because it was felt that asking each group to brainstorm 16 times would be both impractical (e.g., too time consuming) and ineffective (e.g., fatigue or boredom influencing late treatments).<sup>7</sup>

## Variables and Measures

Independent Variables. Four brainstorming technologies were used in the present study: face-to-face (verbal), electronic nominal (E-nominal), electronic anonymous (EBS-anonymous), and electronic nonanonymous (EBS-nonanonymous). In the verbal sessions (this baseline condition was included in order to enable a direct comparison between the results of this study and past EBS research), group members generated their ideas orally and these were keyboarded by a facilitator as the discussion occurred. In this way, the ideas generated were typed in all experimental conditions, eliminating the potential confounding effect of typing versus other means of recording the ideas.<sup>8</sup> In E-nominal sessions, group members generated ideas by typing them using a software in which access to others’ ideas was not allowed. In the EBS sessions, individuals typed their ideas and could see a randomly selected subset of other members’ ideas on their screen. In the EBS-anonymous condition, no identifiers were associated with the ideas generated, whereas in the EBS-nonanonymous condition, the software automatically appended the names of the individuals to their respective ideas, as they were generated. All sessions were recorded on audio tape. GroupSystemsVt, developed at the University of Arizona, was used in all experimental sessions.

Topic sensitivity was operationalized following Gallupe et al. (1996). Four topics were used in the experiment, two being socially sensitive (“How can the spread of AIDS be reduced?”, “How can violent crimes be reduced?”) and two being less sensitive (“How can tourism be improved in Montreal?”, “How can the university parking situation be improved?”).<sup>9</sup> All 16 ad hoc and 16 established groups generated ideas on the four topics in a counter-balanced order. Each topic was used eight times with each of the four technologies, 16 times with contextual cues present, and 16 times without contextual cues. Four practice topics were also used in the experiment: “What benefits and difficulties would occur if everyone grew an extra thumb on each hand?”, “What are some uses for a brick?”, “What are some uses for a knife?”, “What are the impacts of a longer university year?”.

Contextual cues was an experimental condition operationalized through the presence or absence of additional vague environmental stimuli, labeled idea seeding. In the seeded condition, four different broad ideas, previously generated by the research team on the brainstorming topic of the session, were displayed on a public screen for the duration of that session. The ideas displayed (shown in Appendix 1) reflected different dimensions or general approaches that could be adopted in finding a solution to a given problem. For example, for “How can violent crimes be reduced?” these might include ideas revolving around punishment, positive reinforcement, or control. It was expected that presenting multiple ideas along multiple dimensions would provide a context with vague environmental stimuli, similar to the superimposed images used by Sobel and Rosenberg (1980). In the experimental sessions, participants were instructed to use these ideas as inspiration in generating new ideas. Non-seeded groups were not provided with any such stimuli.

Group history refers to whether groups were established or ad hoc. As in previous studies, ad hoc groups were formed by randomly assigning second- and third-year undergraduate students to 16 six-member groups. Sixteen six-member established groups were recruited from the executive committees of student associations at the E<sup>´</sup> cole des Hautes E<sup>´</sup> tudes Commerciales, E<sup>´</sup> cole Polytechnique, and the Sociology and Industrial Relations departments of the University of Montre´al. To ensure that all subjects were motivated to perform well in the experimental sessions, they were each paid twenty dollars.

Pre- and post-session questionnaires indicated that members of established groups, on average, had been working together for eight months (as opposed to zero for ad hoc groups, $p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , and had three friends in the group (as opposed to an average of less than one for ad hoc groups, $p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . Further, members of established groups had, on the average, been friends for more than 19 months, whereas this average was only eight months for those members who knew one another in ad hoc groups $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . Also, established group members had worked as members of a group an average of 33 times versus 19 times for ad hoc groups $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ .<sup>1</sup>0 There were no significant differences between established and ad hoc groups in terms of typing speed, computer usage experience, and level of scholarship. All 192 participants had a working familiarity with microcomputers, but none had experience in electronic brainstorming. The 16 ad hoc groups consisted of 67 men and 29 women and the 16 established groups consisted of 51 men and 45 women. All groups were randomly assigned to treatments.

Depending Variables. The main dependent variable was the number of unique ideas generated by a group, measured according to the procedure described by Bouchard and Hare (1970) and Cooper et al. (1993). All ideas generated by a group were recorded by the software during each session (in the verbal condition, the facilitator typed them as they were generated) and later printed. Coders were provided with a detailed coding guide that explained how to identify redundant ideas and to calculate the number of unique ideas. Two independent coders, both hypothesis and treatmentblind, were used. The number of unique ideas per group calculated by the two coders was highly correlated (r 4 0.91). The score for the dependent variable was the average number of unique ideas calculated by the two coders. This approach should provide a more reliable assessment than using the scores of only one arbitrarily or randomly selected coder.

In addition to the number of unique ideas, group member satisfaction with the brainstorming process and outcome, group member interest in the brainstorming topic, and group member involvement with the brainstorming topic were also assessed.<sup>11</sup>

## Experimental Design and Procedures

Each of the 32 groups brainstormed under four of the 16 different experimental treatments. The sequence of treatments to which each group was subjected is shown in Appendix 2. This design ensured that each group brainstormed (i) using all four brainstorming technologies, (ii) on two socially sensitive and two less sensitive topics, and (iii) under two seeded and two nonseeded conditions. Thus, the four experimental tasks performed by each group were quite varied: in each of its four brainstorming sessions any given group generated ideas on four different topics while using four different technologies. The groups were assigned to the different treatment sequences randomly. The four observations obtained from each of the 32 groups resulted in a total of 128 observations, providing four observations for each of the 16 treatment conditions.

All sessions were structured identically and conducted by hypotheses-blind facilitator with excellent typing skills and knowledge of the software used. A detailed experimental procedure package was prepared to provide precise instructions to the facilitator on how to conduct each session. Upon arrival at the experimental site, the facilitator presented herself, and members of the group were asked to introduce themselves. A name tag, visible to everyone, was placed on the table in front of each member. After the subjects had signed a consent form and an agreement of confidentiality, they were asked to fill a presession questionnaire assessing their program of study, education level, age, brainstorming experience, work experience, group work experience, typing speed, and computer experience. In the case of established groups, the questionnaire also asked the respondents to indicate the number of months the group had been working together, each member’s position and tenure in the group, the number of friends they had in the group, and the duration of their friendships.

The facilitator then described the sequence of events that would take place in the session. The following procedure was used for each of the four experimental tasks. The facilitator distributed and explained the brainstorming instructions and rules to the participants (Bouchard and Hare 1970, Osborn 1957): the more ideas the better, the wilder the idea the better, don’t criticize, and be as clear and concise as possible. The subjects then brainstormed for three minutes on a practice topic with the technology they would use in the coming experimental task. At the end of this warmup period, the facilitator ensured that everybody felt comfortable with the technology. The group then brainstormed on one of the four experimental topics for 15 minutes. Following the 15-minute session, subjects responded to a post-session questionnaire. Each group repeated this procedure four times, once for each of the four different experimental conditions to which they were subjected. The three-minute practice topics were randomly assigned to the four experimental topics. At the end of the fourth session, the facilitator thanked the participants and conducted an open discussion on the experiment.

Following an initial test with one ad hoc group to assess the sequence of events and time constraints, the entire experimental procedure and questionnaire was retested with four ad hoc groups and, subsequent to some minor changes, resulted in the procedure and questionnaire described above.

The general modeling approach to the analysis of repeated measurements is described by Vonesh and Chinchilli (1997). The Unbalanced Repeated Measures Models with Structured Covariance Matrices (5V) procedure of BMDP (Dixon et al. 1990) implements several such models, including the model used in the present study and described in Appendix 3.

## Testing the Secondary Hypotheses of the Study

The fact that none of the interaction terms were significant means that none of the three secondary hypotheses of the study (Hypotheses 2 to 4) could be supported. According to the secondary hypotheses, it was expected that EBS would lead to higher group productivity levels than E-nominal for established groups (Hypotheses 2), for socially sensitive topics (Hypotheses 3), and in the presence of vague contextual cues (Hypotheses 4). The lack of significance for any of the two-way, three-way, or four-way interactions suggests that these three treatments, alone or in combination, do not help increase EBS groups’ productivity beyond the productivity of E-nominal groups. In fact, a visual comparison of the relevant means in Table 4 suggests that, regardless of group history, topic sensitivity, and contextual cues, E-nominal means were always higher than for the other three technologies.

The fact that neither EBS technology was able to surpass E-nominal brainstorming in any of the experimental conditions examined is further accentuated by the overall lack of relative enthusiasm and interest the subjects expressed for E-nominal brainstorming. The post-session questionnaires included questions on the subjects’ satisfaction, task interest, and involvement. When subjects’ average satisfaction, task interest, and involvement scores were compared across the four brainstorming technologies studied, strong technology main effects were observed (all $p ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s } < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ with subjects’ average satisfaction, task interest, and involvement scores being lowest with E-nominal brainstorming. Thus, despite the fact that subjects were less satisfied, less interested, and less involved with Enominal brainstorming (compared to either EBS brainstorming technology), their idea generation productivity was higher than with either EBS technology. It is also interesting to note that the correlation between satisfaction and the number of unique ideas generated was significant and negative $( \mathbf { r } \ = \ - 0 . 3 1 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ Thus, perceptions seem at odds with reality: individuals were more satisfied with the process in which they actually performed the worst.

These results cast doubt on the validity of the process gain/process loss arguments found in the EBS literature in support of the claimed superiority of EBS over nominal brainstorming. The efficiency argument (i.e., that groups using EBS will generate fewer redundant ideas than groups using nominal brainstorming), while plausible, was not supported in the present study. The average total number of ideas generated with the four brainstorming technologies was: E-nominal (97.00), EBS-anonymous (81.80), EBS-nonanonymous (74.70), and Verbal (48.10). As expected, E-nominal groups produced more ideas in total, but according to the efficiency argument, they should have produced less unique ideas than either EBS group (in both absolute and proportional terms). This was not the case. Groups using E-nominal produced significantly more unique ideas than groups using EBS-anonymous, EBS-nonanonymous, or verbal brainstorming technologies (earlier reported means of 67.70, 55.36, 52.56, and 40.13, respectively). Moreover, the relative efficiency of the four technologies can also be examined by comparing group efficiency scores of each technology (calculated as the number of unique ideas divided by the total number of ideas, generated by a group when using a particular technology). High values of this ratio reflect a large proportion of unique ideas to total ideas, thus indicating a high level of efficiency (i.e., low redundancy). In this study, the following average efficiency scores were observed for the four brainstorming technologies: E-Nominal (0.70),

EBS-anonymous (0.69), EBS-nonanonymous (0.71), and Verbal (0.84). A comparison of these scores indicated that verbal groups were significantly more efficient than groups using the other three brainstorming technologies $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , and that no significant differences existed between E-nominal and either EBS technology $( p < 0 . 9 2 0 )$ . Thus, the efficiency argument is not supported.<sup>14</sup>

In this study, enhancing the EBS environment with vague environmental cues was not found to increase group productivity. This result also implies a lack of support for the stimulation argument (i.e., that the ideas generated by other group members will spur EBS participants’ creativity), suggesting that the stimulation effect thought to be caused by the exchange of ideas among group members using EBS may be overrated. In fact, Connolly et al. (1993) found that nominal groups exposed to rare or common ideas were not more productive than groups without such stimuli. This is also consistent with Amabile (1983) who argued that it is much easier and more effective to try to avoid conditions that undermine creativity than it is to enhance it.

To summarize, the main finding of this study is that, while EBS technologies were found to improve groups’ idea generation productivity over face-to-face brainstorming, they did not do so over E-nominal brainstorming. In fact, the results indicate that, overall, even if groups were more interested, more involved, and more satisfied when they used EBS, they actually were more productive with E-nominal brainstorming than with any of the other three brainstorming technologies studied. Further, even under the three conditions expected to render EBS groups more productive, their productivity never exceeded that of groups using nominal brainstorming. Thus, taken together, the results of this study suggest two possible conclusions: (i) as discussed earlier, the process gains claimed for EBS may actually be considerably less than commonly thought, and/or (ii) EBS contains some heretofore unexplored, but important, process losses.

## Discussion

The results indicate that the conditions (group history, topic sensitivity, contextual cues) expected to reduce some of EBS’s process losses, thereby increasing EBS groups’ idea generation productivity beyond that of nominal groups, did not make much of a difference. It is possible that these conditions do in fact affect the process gains and losses as expected, but their impact may not be large enough to overcome the various process losses inherent to EBS. Our observation of, and debriefing discussions with the members of the 32 groups that participated in our study suggest at least five additional process losses the EBS technology seems to introduce into the idea generation process. First, there appears to be a distraction effect associated with group members reading ideas. The fact that, with EBS, the ideas generated are listed on group members screens leads some members to become overly interested in viewing others’ ideas at the expense of entering new ideas themselves. In such cases, individuals end up spending too much time reading others’ ideas rather than thinking about new ideas, thus inadvertently limiting their productivity. Such an effect might be difficult to eliminate in EBS. One possibility would be to provide only a few ideas on EBS screens in the hope of reducing reading time. However, this approach is also likely to substantially decrease the benefits of idea sharing. An alternative approach might be to provide only a few ideas on any given screen and to allow scrolling. However, scrolling screens to access ideas on other pages while handling a technology thus rendered more cumbersome is also bound to create additional time losses and inefficiencies.

A second potential EBS process loss can be labeled attentional production blocking. This occurs when synchronous idea generation “. . . prevents an individual from paying attention to others’ contributions while he or she is formulating and typing in his or her own ideas . . .” (Straus 1996, p. 137), thus reducing an EBS group’s productivity. A third potential EBS process loss, striving for originality, occurs when EBS group members, in trying not to replicate the ideas already generated in a session, become too focused on others ideas which at times inhibits their ability to come up with original ideas. In a sense, the ideas generated by other group members draw their attention so much that they have difficulty thinking up new ideas, thus “blocking” their productivity.

A fourth process loss appears to stem from the greater cognitive complexity that the EBS idea generation process entails compared to nominal brainstorming. An underlying premise of EBS’s advocated advantage over nominal brainstorming is that group members are spurred to generate new ideas when exposed to ideas generated by others. However, with EBS, the cognitive process of idea generation includes the accomplishment of additional cognitive tasks such as reading ideas as well as understanding and interpreting them. These tasks are absent in nominal brainstorming where group members follow a much simpler sequence consisting of generating ideas and, occasionally, reading their own previously generated ideas. Thus, the more complex cognitive process inherent to EBS may be slowing down the rate of idea generation, thereby reducing the productivity of EBS groups.

Finally, the idea sharing feature of EBS appears to foster a group process that can be labeled cognitive dispersion, characterized by frequently changing trains of thought. In nominal brainstorming, individuals work independently and without being interrupted or disturbed by external stimuli. As such, they are usually able to generate many ideas along a given line of thought (e.g., different ideas along the “punishment of criminals” theme for reducing violent crime). In contrast, when reading others’ ideas on an EBS screen, EBS group members are simultaneously exposed to several ideas along different lines of thought. This can make it more difficult for them to fully exploit potentially fertile lines of thought, thus impairing their idea generation productivity. A related phenomenon is that EBS group members sometimes embark on a line of thought which significantly deviates from the topic at hand, leading to fruitless brainstorming and the generation of irrelevant or useless ideas. While it is possible for individuals working in nominal brainstorming groups to be affected by such a phenomenon also, the fact that nominal groups do not share ideas prevents the propagation of such inefficiencies. This process loss is thus likely to have influenced fewer individuals in nominal brainstorming than in EBS.

In addition to such supplementary process losses that appear to be present in EBS, another potential explanation for the results of this study might be that the different process gains and losses identified in Figure 2 may be of unequal magnitude. If so, the results of the present study would imply that the magnitudes of some of the process losses associated with EBS are larger than the magnitude of some of its process gains.

## Why is Group Brainstorming Still Popular in Organizations?

The results of this study, along with others (Mullen et al. 1991, Stroebe et al. 1992), raise the question of why group brainstorming (face-to-face or EBS) remains popular in organizations. One reason may be that people enjoy working in groups more than working alone, regardless of their productivity. In fact, the results of the present study did show that participants were more satisfied, interested, involved, and aroused when they generated ideas in groups (using EBS) than alone (E-nominal). Another reason why group brainstorming continues to be popular could be that, frequently, group work is used more for purposes of obtaining consensus, creating a group dynamic, esprit de corps, group understanding, or buy-in than solely for productivity or efficiency purposes. However, while such an explanation is plausible, research has yet to demonstrate that EBS can help attain these alternative objectives.

A third explanation for the popularity of group brainstorming might be the presence of the perception that interactive brainstorming approaches are more productive (Paulus et al. 1993, Stroebe et al. 1992). In fact, research has shown that individuals believe they generate more ideas when working in a group than alone, even if in actual fact they do not (Paulus et al. 1993). Paulus et al. (1993) and Stroebe et al. (1992) identified three factors that can account for such an illusion of productivity. First, there could be a baseline fallacy whereby, because more total ideas are generated by groups than by an individual alone, people having worked in a group could be under the impression that they produced many more ideas than they could have achieved alone (Diehl and Stroebe 1991; Stroebe et al. 1992). A second reason might be that individuals attribute a disproportionate amount of ideas to themselves, thus inflating the perception of their own productivity (Paulus et al. 1993, Ross and Sicoly 1979, Stroebe et al. 1992; Wicklund 1989). Individuals might have difficulties in differentiating their own contributions to the group from those of others, and, because of a selfserving bias, they could be overestimating their own contributions to the group performance. In fact, Stroebe et al. (1992) found that individuals who generated ideas in group brainstorming identified a significantly larger number of ideas as “suggested by Me” than they had actually produced. Consequently, individuals may be under the impression that they produce more ideas when working in groups than alone.

Finally, the illusion of group productivity might also result from a social comparison experienced during the social interaction among group members (Paulus et al. 1993). In other words, when generating ideas in a group, individuals might realize that their performance is comparable to that of others, and thus, might perceive their own productivity more favorably compared to working alone. Goethrals and Darley (1987) argued that people with modest abilities maintain a positive evaluation of their productivity level based on a limited comparison of the productivity levels of other group members. Moreover, Paulus et al. (1993) found that being able to compare one’s performance to that of the other group member (in a dyad) positively influenced one’s perceived task productivity level.

## Implications for Research and Practice

This study has several implications for EBS research. First, the presence of an “illusion” concerning group productivity levels (empirically observed in group psychology and social psychology research) suggests that EBS researchers should be cautious when drawing conclusions regarding EBS productivity based solely on perceptual measures of productivity. Further, as the results of the present study show, drawing any productivity inferences from perceptual measures of satisfaction would also be inappropriate. Second, much of published EBS research has not considered nominal brainstorming as an alternative to EBS (e.g., Dennis et al. 1996). This suggests that, as group participants themselves, many researchers may also presently be under the illusion that EBS is superior to all other brainstorming approaches, when in fact this is not supported by empirical evidence. Perpetuating such an illusion may mislead researchers into thinking that the remaining research issues lie in fine-tuning EBS. Our findings suggest that a more fruitful research stream would be one that focuses on determining the contingent factors and conditions under which groups using EBS may actually outperform groups using nominal brainstorming.

Third, the process gains and process losses framework proposed here broadens our thinking about EBS and how it can affect brainstorming productivity. Rather than simply focusing on the three process losses traditionally discussed in the literature (i.e., production blocking, evaluation apprehension, free riding), research needs to address the other process gains and losses suggested here. Following Steiner (1972) and the framework presented in this paper, it could be argued that the productivity of a group generating ideas is equal to the sum of the potential productivity of all the group members generating ideas when working alone (i.e., nominal brainstorming), plus any process gains and minus any process losses associated with the fact that people communicate and share information and are placed in a group context. Hence, to better identify the process gains and losses and their effects on idea generation, future research designs need to enable productivity comparisons of the same individuals when generating ideas alone (i.e., not as part of a group), and when generating ideas in groups, while using different brainstorming technologies (e.g., the design used in this study). Currently popular designs, in which different groups generate ideas under different experimental conditions, introduce group biases that not only make it impossible to estimate each group’s potential productivity, but also render the assessment of different process gains and losses more difficult.

This research also has several implications for practice. First, the finding that E-nominal groups outperformed EBS groups overall, and performed at least as well as EBS groups even under conditions expected to favor EBS, indicates that, so long as the number of unique ideas generated is the criterion of choice, nominal brainstorming should retain its place as the primary idea generation technique in organizations. On the other hand, if participant satisfaction were the important criterion, then EBS would be a more appropriate brainstorming tool. The second implication of this study’s findings for practice is that asynchronous meetings may actually be better than synchronous group meetings for generating ideas. Overall, the key message this research sends to managers is that they should be careful in choosing a brainstorming technology. If only one technology were to be chosen and productivity was of concern, nominal brainstorming should be favored over others.

## Study Limitations and Future Research

One limitation of this study is that the contextual cues used in the experiment might not have been vague or broad enough, or sufficiently strong as a manipulation, to stimulate productivity and creativity (Sobel and Rothenberg 1980). Thus, further research is needed to determine whether other forms of environmental stimuli can enhance EBS productivity. In addition, the stimulation provided by EBS and different contextual cues might not have affected all group members similarly. Past research indicates that contextual cues are particularly efficient in enhancing the creativity of highly creative individuals, but that their influence on less creative individuals is more limited (Ward 1969). Future studies could assess whether or not the stimuli inherent in EBS and the additional stimuli provided by supplemental cues are more stimulating for highly creative individuals than for less creative ones.

Another study limitation may stem from the fact that, even though all ad hoc groups were formed by randomly selecting students from different years and courses, some subjects who were not complete strangers ended up being randomly assigned to the same ad hoc group. This limits our ability to generalize the study findings to “pure” ad hoc groups. However, it also means that the ad hoc groups of this study better reflect the ad hoc groups typically encountered in organizational settings where some group members are likely to be acquainted with each other. Another potential area that can be investigated in future EBS research concerns the effect that different levels of group history (i.e., on a continuum from ad hoc groups made up of complete strangers to established groups made up of closely acquainted individuals) have on EBS effectiveness. Other group characteristics of interest that may be investigated include the extent of group members’ past cooperation, the level and nature of conflicts experienced by the group members, and evaluations of their experience of working together.

This study was conducted with business students and, although established groups had a long experience of working together on real projects, great care should be taken when generalizing the findings to other populations. In particular, it would be useful to replicate this study in organizational settings.

Another limitation of the present study is that the presence or absence of the different process gains and losses were not measured (with the exception of production blocking and evaluation apprehension). The results call for a careful analysis of the group processes that occur when groups are generating ideas either with EBS or nominal brainstorming technologies. Future research needs to investigate the extent to which each process gain or loss influences group productivity in brainstorming. Additional research is also needed to compare the cognitive processes used in EBS and in nominal brainstorming in order to determine whether they are different and whether they impact productivity differentially. Finally, more research is needed to further test and refine the process gains and process losses framework proposed in this study. Research is also needed to determine whether or not the different gains and losses are of equal strength and how best to accentuate the gains while attenuating or eliminating the losses of group brainstorming. While the present study provides new research avenues that might help refine our thinking about EBS and some preliminary answers, it also raises new questions regarding how to best use and apply EBS in organizations as well as how to study idea generation in groups.<sup>15</sup>

## Appendix 3: The Unbalanced Repeated Measures Model with Structured Covariance Matrices: The 5V Procedure of BMDP

The approach used here enables the analysis of designs where each subject is observed at several times under different experimental conditions by using a maximum likelihood approach to obtain estimates of the regression and covariance parameters. The model for the present experiment is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i j k l m} = \mu + \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {j} + \chi_ {k} + \delta_ {1} + (\alpha \beta) _ {i j} \\ \qquad + (\alpha \chi) _ {i k} + (\alpha \delta) _ {i l} + (\beta \chi) _ {j k} + (\beta \delta) _ {j l} + (\chi \delta) _ {k l} + (\alpha \beta \chi) _ {i j k} \\ \qquad + (\alpha \beta \delta) _ {i j l} + (\alpha \chi \delta) _ {i k l} + (\beta \chi \delta) _ {j k l} + (\alpha \beta \chi \delta) _ {i j k l} + e _ {i j k l m}, \end{array}
$$

where

i denotes the level of the technology factor,

j denotes the level of the group-history factor,

k denotes the level of the topic sensitivity factor,

l denotes the level of the contextual cues factor, and

m denotes the brainstorming group.

The terms $\alpha _ { i } , \beta _ { j } , \chi _ { k } , \delta _ { l } ( \alpha \beta ) _ { i j } , ( \alpha \gamma ) _ { i k } ,$ etc. are fixed effects that sum to zero over all $\mathrm { i } , \mathrm { j } , \mathrm { k } ,$ , and l. The terms $\left( \alpha \beta \right) _ { i j } , \left( \alpha \chi \right) _ { i k } , \left( \alpha \delta \right) _ { i l } , \left( \beta \chi \right) _ { j k } , \left( \beta \delta \right) _ { j l }$ and $( \chi \delta ) _ { k l }$ represent the two-way interactions, $( \alpha \beta \chi ) _ { i j k } , ( \alpha \beta \delta ) _ { i j l } , ( \alpha \gamma \delta ) _ { i k l }$ and $( \beta \chi \delta ) _ { j k l }$ represents the three-way interactions, and $( \alpha \beta \chi \delta ) _ { i j k l }$ represents the four-way interaction for the four factors.

In the 5V procedure, the expected values of the responses are described as arbitrary linear functions of a set of regression parameters, and the within-subject covariances modeled as functions of a set of unknown covariance parameters (Dixon et al. 1990). In modeling incomplete repeated measures data 5V assumes that there exists an underlying set of complete data with each subject having a fixed number of repeated measurements. Thus, the observed data is treated as a subset of the complete data (Dixon et al. 1990). Here, the within-subject covariance matrix is assumed to have compound symmetry structure, i.e., a matrix with equal diagonal elements and a constant off-diagonal (Dixon et al. 1990). Accordingly, a withinsubject correlation matrix and the regression parameters for each main effect and interaction are calculated, and a Wald-type Chisquare statistic computed for testing the null hypothesis that the term can be left out of the model.
