# Research Report. Can EDI Benefit Adopters?

Ho Geun Lee • Theodore Clark • Kar Yan Tam

Department of Business Administration, College of Business and Economics, Yonsei University, Seoul, Korea

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management,

Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Hong Kong, PRC

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management,

Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Hong Kong, PRC

lthough the potential for EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) to improve performance of firms involved in industry value chain is widely known, little evidence regarding improved performance for the entire supply channel has been reported. Some researchers have found that EDI networks can benefit EDI champion, but it remains largely unclear whether EDI adopters—who are often coerced to implement the electronic networks by the champion— gain similar payoffs from their EDI investments. To measure impacts of EDI investments for the EDI adopters, we have investigated the performance of 31 grocery retail chains (EDI adopters) that implemented EDI networks with Campbell (EDI champion) for a supply channel reengineering innovation known as “continuous replenishment process.” Analysis of daily data on inventory and stockouts levels for the 31 retail chains demonstrates that these EDI adopters have achieved a significant increase in their inventory turns while simultaneously reducing stockouts as a result of this EDI-enabled supply channel reengineering. This paper thus provides empirical evidence that EDI adopters can achieve dramatic performance improvements if EDI networks are used for interfirm process reengineering.

(Electronic Data Interchange; Interorganizational Systems; Channel Process Reengineering; Continuous Replenishment Process)

## 1. Introduction

The potential for EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) or IOS (Interorganizational Systems) to improve firm performance and to change industry structure has been studied extensively, as linkages between supplier and customer have become an important source of competitive advantage. EDI/IOS enables firms to create new opportunities to optimize the total industry value chain across multiple organizations (Cash and Konsynski 1985, Clemons and McFarlan 1986). Information integration with trading partners helps firms not only improve channel efficiencies (Bakos 1991) but also reduce transaction risks (Clemons and Row 1992). Keen (1990) asserts that the economics of EDI are so compelling that EDI is rapidly becoming one of the “must do” applications. A buying firm can manage its relationships with suppliers by asking each vendor to adopt EDI to create very tight coupling between buyer and supplier. Managing forward in the value chain, a supplier may connect its operations with those of buyers to create disincentive for customers to seek other suppliers.

However, existing studies of positive EDI impact fall largely on the EDI champion sides (Mukhopadhyay et al. 1995, Srinivasan et al. 1994, Whang and Seidmann 1995). EDI adopters are relatively small in size and the EDI champion is often their primary customer.<sup>1</sup> With a dominating position in the negotiation process, the EDI champion is able to convince or even force their small partners to invest in EDI capabilities. Many authors have noted that EDI can benefit its champion but doubted that EDI adopters, who are often coerced to implement the EDI by the champion, can gain similar payoffs from their EDI investments (Iacovou et al. 1995, Riggins and Mukhopadhyay 1994). Several studies of small organizations implementing EDI capabilities indicate little or no impact of these systems on organizational performance (Wallace 1988, Carter 1990, Eckerson 1990, McCuster 1994). Some authors even assert that the EDI champion realizes the benefit at the expense of the EDI adopters (Clemons and Row 1993, Hendry 1993).

Prior research works regarding actual benefits realized from implementing EDI raise the following questions: Can EDI benefit adopters as well? If EDI networks offer benefits to both champions and adopters, under which conditions can EDI adopters achieve productivity gains from their EDI investments? To answer these research questions, we investigate the effect of Continuous Replenishment Process (CRP) which merges EDI and process innovations to improve channel efficiency in the grocery industry. In the CRP innovation, large manufacturers serve as EDI champions, helping their retail grocery chains adopt EDI networks. Retail grocery chains then utilize EDI links to transmit data on daily sales and inventory levels to manufacturers, who use these data to determine product shipment quantities required to maintain adequate inventory levels at retail buyers. By analyzing data on inventory performances of 31 retail firms that implemented CRP with one of major manufacturers (Campbell Soup Company), we provide empirical evidence that EDI adopters can realize significant productivity gains from their EDI investments.

The central claim of this report is that EDI adopters can achieve significant levels of benefits from EDI implementation if EDI is merged with changes of interfirm processes and policies. The development of EDI networks between manufacturers and retailers to drive CRP innovation can be regarded as IT-enabled BPR (Davenport 1993, Venkatraman 1994, Lee and Clark 1996). On the BPR side, CRP represents a dramatic change in the way the inbound logistics of the retailers value chain is managed. CRP calls for vendor management of a retailer’s inventory and eliminates the traditional ordering process by allowing the manufacturer to manage the retailers’ inventory. This is a dramatic change from traditional industry practice and obviously falls within Hammer’s definition of radical process change (Hammer 1990, Hammer and Champy 1993), because retailers “outsource” procurement and inbound logistics decisions to manufacturers who become responsible for minimizing inventories and stockouts at retailer warehouses. On the IT side, CRP implementation requires setting up EDI links between manufacturers and retailer chains. Since the total data volume transmitted is much greater in CRP than in traditional ordering processes, there is simply no other economically justifiable means of data transmission other than using a high-speed and low-cost electronic link.

The importance of merging EDI with interfirm process changes has been widely known. Prior research on EDI implementation suggests that only through business reengineering can EDI’s full potential for improving company and channel performance be realized (Swatman et al. 1993, Clark 1994, Clark and Stoddard 1996). Many authors have also noted that EDI must involve changes in business processes to realize the savings enabled by this technological innovation (Emmelhainz 1993, Riggins and Mukhopadhyay 1994). However, our study differs from previous works that focus largely on benefits for the EDI champions. This study uses established theories, but measures benefits for EDI adopters rather than EDI champions.

## 2. Research Design and Methodology

## 2.1. Theoretical Framework

We adopt the model suggested by Mukhopadhyay (1993) on EDI impact as the guiding theoretical framework to study CRP impact on retailer performance. Instead of measuring final performance variables such as profit or return on investment, our focus is on the intermediate variables that are identified as the more direct locus of value for this channel innovation. The model, as depicted in Figure 1, consists of three factors that are hypothesized to have an impact on retailer performance variables.<sup>2</sup> Two dependent variables—inventory level and stockout level—are used to measure the performance level of the retail customers. Details of each factor specific to the context of this study are summarized in Table 1.

IT Context. EDI is essential for implementing the new CRP program. In traditional self-managed inventory management, most products were ordered on a weekly basis by retail firms either by fax or EDI. CRP dramatically increased the total volume of data transmitted between manufacturers and retailers with daily transmission of information on all products indicating sales volume, warehouse inventory levels, orders in transit (delivered but not yet received), and product shortages. The need for improved data quality to avert serious errors in CRP implementation has also increased the importance of EDI. With the elimination of excess inventory buffers from the channel, any errors due to data-entry keying problems (involved with manual or fax transmissions) could immediately result in retailer stockouts under the new CRP processes. Thus, many retail firms that preferred fax to EDI had to switch to electronic links to adopt CRP because manual (fax-based) CRP was not economically viable.

Process and Policy. Before the advent of CRP, some retail chains had deployed EDI to replace fax for placing orders to manufacturers. However, CRP is significantly different from the traditional EDI-based ordering. With conventional use of EDI, retail firms generally placed orders using the same processes that had been in place prior to EDI implementation, except that data were transmitted directly between computer systems. EDI usage did reduce paper and data-entry errors in the channel, but did not change the basic ordering responsibility or processes between retailers and manufacturers (Clark 1994, Clark and Stoddard

To isolate the effects of CRP on retailer inventory performance from these factors, it is necessary to take into account these exogenous factors.

## 2.2. Research Hypotheses

The research is designed to test the impact of CRP on inventory performance of EDI adopters (grocery retail chains). The research model tests the following two hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. Retailers investing in EDI capabilities for CRP innovations with manufacturers will reduce the inventory level at their warehouses.

Hypothesis 2. Retailers investing in EDI capabilities for CRP innovations with manufacturers will reduce the stockouts level at their warehouses.

Inventory levels and stockouts (generally defined as a percentage of demand that was ordered by stores but could not be filled) are not mutually independent variables. As illustrated in Figure 2, inventory levels and stockouts could be described as performance tradeoffs, with inventory reductions increasing stockouts (and vice versa) if all other factors remain constant. Both inventory and stockouts levels have cost implications for retail firms. Managers theoretically seek to minimize total costs of inventory (handling, storage, damage, obsolescence, and financing costs) and stockouts (opportunity costs associated with lost sales and customer dissatisfaction) at their warehouses. Retailer inventory levels could be reduced at the expense of increased stockouts, or stockouts levels could be lowered by increasing inventory levels. Hypothesis 1 and 2 presented above suggest that retail firms can simultaneously reduce both inventory levels and stockouts with the adoption of the CRP, thus shifting the cost tradeoff curve (see Figure 2).

For the purpose of the analysis, retailer inventory level is measured in terms of inventory turns, which is sales over inventory levels per year. The retail industry has traditionally used inventory turns, rather than inventory level, as an indicator of warehouse performance. Inventory turns provides a more appropriate measure than inventory level since it is relatively independent of sales volumes (reduction of inventory levels may result from decreased sales volume as well as from improved inventory management). For stockouts, retailers are more concerned with the frequency of shortage than the quantity of shortage. In general, retail managers seek to reduce the probability of shortage in the channel to minimize lost sales and consumer dissatisfaction. The stockouts indicates percentage of shortage. Thus, 2 percent of stockouts level (or 98 percent of service level) means that 98 percent of store orders were filled completely; two percent were either not filled or only partially filled.

## 2.3. Data Collection

To analyze the impact of CRP on inventory performance of retail firms (EDI adopters), data were collected from computerized historical records at Campbell Soup Company (manufacturer). The source data contained pre- and post-CRP information on inventory and sales at 31 retail grocery chains that implemented CRP with Campbell (EDI champion) between 1991 and 1994. The source data included daily information on inventory levels, sales, and cuts (shortage quantity) for each of the 732 products replenished by CRP at 31 retailer firms. The data consisted of 4.8 million records transmitted from retailers to Campbell, with each record corresponding to daily information on a single product.

Rigorous and careful control procedures were employed for extracting and aggregating the sample data in an attempt to exclude exogenous factors that might cause bias in the analysis. Exactly the same number of weeks was used for pre- and post-CRP data sampling from each retail customer, with an average of 11 weeks. The post-CRP data samples were drawn from the source data recorded after 12 months lag from the pre-CRP data for two reasons. First, most Campbell products, including its flagship product line “red-andwhite cans of condensed soups,” have seasonal fluctuations in sales. Thus it was necessary to maintain a 1-year gap between pre- and post-CRP to eliminate demand variations due to seasonal fluctuations. Second, interviews with several retail firms revealed that the benefits of CRP did not follow its implementation immediately, and could be felt only after some learning period (at least six months). The 1-year lag served to accommodate the learning time necessary to measure CRP impacts. In addition, daily data were averaged across products within each week to eliminate daily demand variations. The sampling and data aggregation process resulted in 688 data points, with each pre- and post-CRP sample consisting of 344 data points for the 31 firms investigated.

## 3. Results of Analysis

## 3.1. Mean Value Analysis

The comparison of the mean value between pre- and post-CRP data reveals a dramatic improvement of inventory performance for these retailer firms. Of the 31 retail chains that implemented the CRP innovations with Campbell, 28 retail firms improved both inventory turns and stockouts levels after CRP adoption. Only one retail chain showed decreased inventory turns, but its stockouts were also significantly reduced. The remaining two retail firms had significantly higher inventory turns with slightly higher stockouts levels. Thus, more than 90% of retail firms improved both inventory turnovers and stockouts; the remaining retail chains improved either inventory turns or stockouts at the expense of the other performance variable after implementing CRP with Campbell.

Table 2 exhibits key measures for pre- and post-CRP inventory performance, including minimum, maximum, and mean value of inventory turns and stockouts of 31 retail firms. The inventory turns is calculated by dividing annual sales volume with average inventory levels. Thus, the data shown approximate annual inventory turnovers. As we can see in this table, retail firms demonstrate wide ranges of inventory performance both before and after CRP implementation. This wide range in inventory performance comes from specific market and logistics environments of each retail firm, which are explained in the exogenous factors in the conceptual framework. The annual inventory turns has increased from 17.18 to 26.05, indicating more than a 50% improvement after CRP implementation. This mean value difference between pre- and post-CRP is statistically significant at 0.01% level (t-test). The stockouts has also been reduced to less than half the previous level: the percentage of stockouts decreased from 1.92 percent to 0.77 percent after CRP innovations. This mean value difference implies more than 60% improvement in stockouts rate and is statistically significant at 0.01% level (t-test).

## 3.2. Two-Stage Least Square Regression

The purpose of the regression model is to measure the impact of CRP (a combined effect of IT and the Process/Policy in the conceptual model) on retailer inventory performance, rather than trying to uncover all explanatory variables for differences in inventory turns and stockouts. To isolate the impact of CRP, we need to control for the effects of exogenous factors, which are independent of the CRP implementation but influence the level of inventory turnovers and stockouts. Sales amount and company-specific indicator variables (dummy variables) are introduced to exclude the effect of these exogenous variables in this research design.

$\alpha _ { \mathrm { T } }$ and $\alpha _ { \mathrm { T } }$ are intercepts, and $\geqslant _ { \mathrm { T } }$ and $\partial _ { S }$ represent random error terms for Eqs. (1) and (2). We expect the effects of CRP for the inventory turns to be positive, and $\mathrm { C R P } { \mathrm { { s } } }$ impact on stockouts to be negative as CRP implementation helps retailers to increase inventory turns and reduce stockouts. TURNS and STOCKOUTS are expected to have a positive relationship with each other (in both equations), as specified in Figure 2. The models also include SALES as a control variable, since the inventory performance at retailer warehouses is affected by sales variations in retail stores. All other retailer-specific exogenous factors (market/weather conditions, geographical locations, and effectiveness of inventory management, and so on) are represented by company-specific indicators $\mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { i } }$

Since TURNS and STOCKOUTS are jointly endogenous variables for Eqs. (1) and (2), an Ordinary Least Square (OLS) analysis will produce inconsistent parameter estimates due to simultaneous equation bias. A critical assumption of the OLS method is that the regressors (independent or explanatory variables) are uncorrelated with the random error terms. When endogenous variables appear as regressors in other equations, this assumption is violated and the OLS parameter estimates become biased and inconsistent.

One approach to remove the simultaneous equation bias is a two-stage least square regression (2SLS), which replaces the endogenous variables on the right-hand side of (1) and (2) with predicted values that are uncorrelated with the error terms. These predicted values can be obtained through a preliminary, or first stage, instrumental variable regression. Instrumental variables, which are uncorrelated with the error term, are used as regressors to model the predicted values for

TURNS and STOCKOUTS.<sup>3</sup> The parameter estimates are then obtained by a second regression, using the predicted values of the regressors. The 2SLS regression method solves both equations simultaneously to eliminate the simultaneous equation bias and to achieve consistent and unbiased estimates for parameters (Judge et al. 1985).

The results of the 2SLS analysis, which uses 688 data points obtained through data sampling and aggregation, are presented in Figure 3 (coefficient estimates $\delta _ { \mathrm { T i } }$ and $\delta _ { \mathrm { S i } }$ for company specific variables $\mathrm { R _ { i } }$ are not shown for simplicity). The model fit is statistically significant at the 0.01% level for both Eqs. (1) and (2), with adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ of 81% and 43%, respectively. The importance of company-specific indicators as control variables is verified by the statistical significance of their coefficient estimates $\delta _ { \mathrm { T i } }$ and $\delta _ { \mathrm { S i } } \mathrm { : }$ Most of these coefficient estimates are highly significant.<sup>4</sup>

In Eq. (1), the direction of impacts of CRP, SALES, and STOCKOUTS on the inventory turns are positive, as expected. The null hypothesis of particular interest is that the coefficient for CRP $( \beta _ { \mathrm { T 1 } } )$ is zero. This hypothesis is rejected at the 0.01% level, indicating that CRP implementation does significantly affect the level of inventory turns. The coefficient for STOCKOUTS $( \beta _ { \mathrm { T } 3 } )$ is also positive and statistically significant, suggesting that the inventory turns increase (or inventory levels decrease) as the stockout levels increase. This result is consistent with the trade-off relationship between inventory and stockout levels in Figure 2. The effect of increased sales on the inventory turns $( \beta _ {  { \mathrm { T } } 2 } )$ is positive at a significance level of 0.01% as the sales growth results in increased inventory turnovers. Although all three regressor variables—CRP, SALES and STOCKOUTS—have positive impacts on the inventory turns, the effect of CRP appears the highest among them. This implies that $\mathrm { C R P }$ innovation provides retail firms with a higher marginal increase of inventory turnovers than would have been possible either through dramatic increase of sales or at the expense of increased shortages (STOCKOUTS). The regression analysis of the Eq. (1) thus strongly supports our first hypothesis that retail firms increase inventory turns (or reduce inventory levels) as a result of the EDI implementation for CRP innovation.

Our model for the stockouts in Eq. (2) assumes that CRP will have a negative relationship, and TURNS will have a positive relationship with stockouts. ## 4. Discussions

As an initiator of EDI networks for CRP innovations, Campbell significantly reduced its production and inventory costs as a result of this new EDI-enabled, interfirm process reengineering (Clark and McKenney 1994). The purpose of this research is to investigate whether CRP innovations benefit retail firms (EDI adopters) as well. While the EDI champion (Campbell) had strong incentives to implement the EDI network for CRP innovation, it remained unclear whether its adopters (retail chains) would benefit equally from EDI implementation. This study provides empirical evidence that EDI can benefit not only the EDI champion but also EDI adopters, if the electronic network is used as an enabler for interorganizational process reengineering designed to provide benefits to both firms in the value-chain relationship. Retailers who adopted this new procurement innovation with Campbell dramatically improved their inventory performance. Inventory and logistics managers at retailer chains had previously managed trade-offs between inventory levels and stockouts to balance their inventory management: Inventory levels could be reduced at the expense of stockouts, and vice versa. The analysis of this study demonstrates that, if EDI is combined with BPR, it enables EDI adopters to reduce both inventory levels and stockouts simultaneously.

We believe there are three major contributions of this study. First, by focusing on performance measures of EDI adopters (retail firms) rather than the EDI champion (a large manufacturer), we demonstrate that the adoption of EDI can create values across the entire supply channel rather than simply shifting value from coerced EDI adopters to the EDI champion. Second, a longitudinal analysis based on daily transaction data allows us to separate effects of IT-enabled BPR from those of exogenous environmental factors. Third, IT is examined as an enabler of the new process innovations. Unlike other empirical works on EDI, we viewed the performance improvements as indirect benefits of EDI investments, rather than as direct gains of deploying EDI.

We suggest two reasons for the dramatic improvements of inventory performance by retail chains adopting the CRP innovation. First, the “vertical information integration” between manufacturers (EDI champions) and retail firms (EDI adopters) enabled to monitor the inventory performance more effectively through the channel. CRP using EDI networks provides manufacturers with much more timely and detailed information on product movements and stockouts than traditional ordering processes. Much of the information available to manufacturers with CRP had not been available prior to the implementation of this new process: Retailers had provided only data on quantities of goods required (order information), not sales, inventory, or stockout data. Receiving these detailed data allowed manufacturers to reduce their inventories and better manage production at their facilities. This timely information enabled manufacturers to manage inventory and stockout levels at their trading partners (retail firms) more effectively. Lee et al. (1996) have developed an analytic model showing that coordinating a channel inventory based upon order information alone can create the bullwhip effect, which distorts real demands across the supply channel and misguides channel partners in inventory decisions. Their model demonstrates that information sharing of sell-through and inventory status can alleviate the detrimental impact of the bullwhip effect. This research provides evidence of their analytic model. With information sharing of sales, inventory, and stockout status through vertical information integration, firms can achieve higher channel performance than with traditional channel processes, in which trading partners coordinate their operations based on order information alone.

Second, CRP has required that retail firms shift their procurement policy from traditional promotional deals to EDLC (every-day-low-cost) purchasing. Promotional deals have been used widely by retail firms since the 1970s, during which the combination of high inflation, relatively low-interest costs, and large promotional discounts made the economics of forwardbuying very attractive for retail firms. Retailers typically purchased products from manufacturers during the promotional period and kept them in their warehouses to sell later at high prices. Retailers had become dependent on forward-buying as an important source of profits, and had emphasized “buying-for-profit” rather than “selling-for-profit.” However, reliance on promotional programs resulted in high demand artificially created by forward-buying and increased inventory levels in retail warehouses. The combination of promotional pricing and forward-buying increased channel lead times and exaggerated demand variations, creating dramatic inefficiencies in the channel.

The new EDLC pricing provided retailers with an average purchase price equal to the average price paid using the traditional pricing structure, including all discounts realized through forward-buying. Campbell provided this new EDLC pricing in order to enable retail buyers to achieve a competitive product purchasing cost without forward-buying. As a result, CRP streamlined actual customer demands with retailers procurements, eliminating artificial demand fluctuations created by retailers’ forward-buying. Thus, CRP innovation has made demand more predictable and enabled vendors to manage inventory and stockouts levels at retailer warehouses more effectively.

The improvement of inventory performance reported in this study is a compounding effect of IT (EDI), process change (vendor-managed inventory), and new pricing policy (EDLC pricing): EDI enhanced timely information-sharing, the vendor-managed inventory process eliminated excess inventory buffers in the channel, and the EDLC pricing improved channel efficiency by reducing forward-buying. With available empirical data from Campbell, it was not possible to isolate individual effects of these factors. However, qualitative discussions with retailers that adopted EDLC with other suppliers without using CRP indicated that the gains available from CRP adoption were more than twice the magnitude of the gains from shifting from promotional pricing to EDLC without CRP. The effects of the pricing change (EDLC) were also difficult to separate, as none of Campbell’s customers were willing to adopt EDLC pricing without also adopting CRP, even though the new pricing alone would have provided some cost savings.

Many retailers resisted EDLC pricing as this pricing alternative was the completely opposite of the promotional pricing strategies that most retail firms had used to increase sales and profits in the past. In addition, buyers who had the primary relationship with suppliers were threatened by EDLC, as their role in the company became much less critical under an EDLC or CRP procurement approach. When EDLC pricing was made available for all retailers by Campbell, no retailers were willing to adopt the EDLC without the new process being bundled with the new pricing. Thus, the performance variables in this study measure real market decisions that were generally not desegregated by retail customers. However, the separation of EDI effects from process/policy effects is an interesting research issue and can be approached using different methodologies and data. We leave this issue for future research works.<sup>4</sup>
