# Research Report. Learning from Goal-Directed Error Recovery Strategy

Department of Decision Sciences and Information Systems, University of Kentucky, Lexington, Kentucky 40506 Santhan@pop.uky.edu

esearch on training has traditionally viewed errors made by trainees as detrimental to errors from occurring during training. Recently, some researchers have adopted a different perspective: that errors may provide a learning opportunity for trainees. What has been investigated less is the specific mechanism through which errors can foster learning. The objective of our research was to investigate and possibly reconcile these differing viewpoints by examining the error recovery process. We found that, in some situations, errors enhance learning when the trainee adopts an error recovery process that emphasizes the goal structure of the task. We suggest several ways of coaching trainees in training sessions to adopt such error recovery strategies.

## 1. Introduction

When new users are trained on a system, they invariably make errors. An error occurs when planned sequences of mental or physical activities fail to achieve their intended outcome (Reason 1990). Because errors are considered frustrating and disruptive to learning, the traditional approach in training has been to prevent trainees from making errors during the training period (see Carroll 1990 for a review). Strategies, such as a “training wheels interface”, have been developed to minimize the chances of errors (Carroll and Carrithers 1984).

Recently, other researchers have espoused the view that errors during training may have beneficial aspects (Frese et al. 1991, Seifert and Hutchins 1992, Frolich et al. 1994). In an experiment, Frese et al. (1991) assigned difficult tasks to one group of subjects that resulted in their committing errors. The control group received training tasks that prevented errors from occurring.

Subjects in the error group scored better than the control group on several performance measures. The opportunity to learn from errors has also been advocated in the minimalist approach proposed by Carroll (1990). The purpose of this approach is to make errors “less traumatic and more pedagogically productive” (Carroll 1990, pg. 7).

The different perspectives seem to suggest opposite views of dealing with errors. The traditional or detrimental outcome view calls for error avoidance or automatic intervention from the system to correct errors when they occur. The beneficial view regards errors as learning opportunities and calls for training methods and systems design that should encourage errors and emphasize error management. What is less clear is how novices learn about the system from errors. Frese et al. (1991) did not determine why subjects in the error training group performed better, but speculated that perhaps the strategies employed by subjects to recover from errors may have influenced their understanding of the system.

We feel that a closer examination of the role of errors in the learning process is essential to reconcile these disparate views. Specifically, we propose that following a goal-directed strategy of error recovery can lead trainees to a better understanding of the system. To test our contention, we analyzed data collected in a laboratory study as part of a larger, ongoing research program on training methods.

## 2. The Error Recovery Process

Making errors compels a user to focus on an area of a system that needs attention and to reflect on the system or ones own knowledge about it (Winograd and Flores 1986). The error recovery or repair process represents an opportunity for the user to learn more about the system (Brown and Newman 1985, Carroll 1990, Frolich et al. 1994). To identify error recovery strategies that can facilitate learning, we look at the theory of Adaptive Control of Thought (ACT), proposed by Anderson (1983).

## 2.1. Goal-Directed Learning

ACT proposes that learners first acquire declarative knowledge (facts) about the domain/system and then procedural knowledge (how to use knowledge). They finally apply the learned and compiled procedures together to understand the system. The learning process is goal related (McKendree 1990). Learners decompose the learning task into goals and subgoals and fashion procedures around these goals. In learning about a computer system, focusing on the goal structure of each task allows a learner to relate the procedures required to accomplish the task with their declarative knowledge of the system. It thus improves their understanding of the system. In a study, McKendree (1990) found that subjects learned best when provided with specific goal-directed feedback through a tutoring system. Other tutoring systems, such as GUIDON (Clancey 1986), built around the principle of providing goal-directed feedback have been found successful.

In the absence of goal-directed feedback from the system, learners themselves may generate goals and subgoals in trying to recover from errors. We call such an error recovery strategy “goal-directed.” Selfgenerated strategies lead to elaboration about the system to be learned. Research in cognitive psychology based on the level of processing theory suggests that a self-generated process of elaboration is conducive to learning (Craik and Lockhart 1972, Anderson 1990). Anderson explains that elaboration leads to improved understanding by increasing the redundancy of interconnections among the material and efficiently organizing the information for later retrieval. We can thus argue that a goal-directed error recovery strategy can lead to increased learning about the system.

## 2.2. Task and Error Types

A trainee may make errors in any task, but certain error types and task characteristics may provide more opportunities for engaging in a goal-directed recovery process. Errors have been classified into three types: slips, rule-based, and knowledge-based (Norman 1981, Reason 1990). Slips occur because of lapses of attention; rule-based errors are caused by application of a wrong set of actions (or misapplication of known rules); and knowledge-based errors occur because of a lack of knowledge to complete the task (Reason 1990). The likelihood that a trainee may commit any one of these error types depends on the nature of the task.

After training, trainees interact with the system by engaging in near-transfer or far-transfer tasks. Neartransfer tasks are those for which trainees have been specifically trained or are very similar to training tasks. Trainees are most likely to commit slips or rule-based errors on these tasks. They can repair them relatively easily, because they have procedures in their knowledge base to handle them (Reason 1990). Far-transfer tasks are novel tasks for which trainees have not been specifically trained. To perform them, trainees have to use the knowledge provided in the training session, reason about the system components, make inferences and decisions about which procedures to use. They are more likely to make knowledge-based errors in such cases.

Recovering from knowledge-based errors is very challenging because trainees do not have stored procedures that can be used. Error detection and error recovery become problem-solving situations. This requires trainees to construct explanations for the system behavior and update their mental models of the system (Rizzo et al. 1987). Trainees engaging in a goal-directed error recovery process can relate the structure of the task to action sequences and system components. It enables them to acquire more declarative facts about the system, and understand how specific procedures relate to these facts. By contrast, when trainees do not engage in goal-directed recovery, they continue to randomly cycle around stored procedures without relating the task goal to the system structure and procedures. They use no new plan of action nor do they revise mental models of the system. Based on this discussion, we formulate our hypothesis.

Hypothesis. Novice users who engage in a goal-directed error recovery process while performing far-transfer tasks will gain a better understanding of the system compared to users who do not engage in a goal-directed recovery process.

## 3. Method

We analyzed data collected as part of a larger research study that examined various aspects of training. The findings are reported elsewhere (Santhanam and Sein 1994). Here, we briefly summarize the experimental procedures that are relevant to this article.

## 3.1. Experimental Procedures

Subjects were 77 students enrolled in an introductory information systems course in a large state university in the United States. In Phase 1 of the study, they completed a background questionnaire and were randomly assigned to conceptual model or procedural method training groups. The conceptual model described the components and functioning of a VAX mail system as an analogy (office filing cabinet) and an abstract model (hierarchical diagram). In the procedural method, system components were not specifically described, but common procedures such as sending and receiving mail and managing folders were fully described. Subjects studied the materials for 15 minutes after which they were given a quiz in which they had to describe the system. We scored the accuracy of these descriptions to assess their initial mental models, and classified them into “conceptual” and “procedural.”

In Phase 2, subjects performed a variety of hands-on training tasks such as sending, reading, and moving messages. When they made an error, the trainer helped them recover by providing the correct procedure. In Phase 3, subjects performed six experimental tasks (four near-transfer and two far-transfer). They were free to stop working on a task and proceed to the next one. The entire interaction was recorded on session logs. When subjects committed errors, they had to recover without assistance. We classified the error recovery strategies for each task into goal-directed or nongoal-directed by comparing the session log protocols to benchmarks developed using the widelyaccepted GOMS (Goals, Operators, Methods, and Selection rules) model. A detailed description of our classification method is provided in §3.2. The strategies served as the independent variable. In Phase 4, subjects completed a five-item, paper-and-pencil system comprehension test constructed to tease out their conceptual understanding of the system. Each item described a scenario of a user interacting with the system. Subjects were asked how the system would respond, what error occurred, and what the correct response should be. Accurate answers on this test would indicate a good understanding of the working of the system. The development and validation of this measure, specifically where electronic mail is the target system is provided in Sein and Bostrom (1989). Subjects’ score on this system comprehension test was the dependent variable.<sup>1</sup>

## 3.2. Classifying Error Recovery Strategy Using GOMS

GOMS proposes that in performing a task, a user has a specific goal in mind; these goals (which may be decomposed to subgoals) can be accomplished through a series of operators, such as commands or mouse clicks (Card et al. 1983). To reach a goal, a user employs a method that is made up of one or more operators. When subjects are faced with alternative methods to accomplish the same goal, selection rules determine the optimal choice (For a good overview of GOMS, see John 1995).

To perform the task successfully, subjects had to recall that “wastebasket” was a folder and figure out a method to move the deleted message from this folder back to the original folder. This task was far-transfer because subjects were not taught explicitly how to retrieve a deleted message but were trained on all the subgoals of selecting a folder and reading, deleting, and moving a message.

We examined the session logs of the subjects to identify errors. An error was coded for a situation in which a subject wanting to achieve a subgoal gave a command to which the system responded with an error message, or where the subject achieved a wrong goal to which the system gave no error message. Next, we determined if the error was a slip. An example of a slip is as follows: A subject who types “SELECS Fol6” instead of “SELECT Fol6” to select a folder called Fol6, will get a system response “SELECS is an invalid keyword”. This is a slip if the subject immediately recognizes the error and types the correct command. We did not use slips to identify error recovery strategy since a strategy cannot be identified when a single correction command is used.

We classified the recovery strategy from all other errors into “goal-directed” (GDR) or “nongoal-directed” (NGDR). An example of coding an error recovery strategy is as follows: A subject may try to delete the second message in a folder by selecting a folder and typing “DELETE”. The goal would not be achieved because a message has to be read before it could be deleted. The system would respond, “you are not reading a message.” If subjects tried to recover from the error by using the operators/methods listed in the GOMS framework (first reading the message and then deleting it), we classified the strategy as GDR, even if they did not completely recover from the error. If subjects used irrelevant commands such as “LIST” and “COPY” or tried commands randomly, we classified the strategy as NGDR, whether they recovered from the error or not. To be classified into the GDR group, a subject had to engage in goal-directed behavior in either of the two far-transfer tasks. Cases of GDR and NGDR, involving the task shown in Figure 1, are shown in Appendices A and B, respectively. Both are excerpts from session logs.

The GOMS model of the tasks represented the “ideal” way of performing the experimental tasks. However, we were not concerned with how well the subjects’ process matched the “ideal.” Rather, our focus was on whether they proceeded toward the specified goals and subgoals during the error recovery process. Thus, we considered even a suboptimal process as “goal-directed” if our analysis of the session log indicated that the subject was moving toward a specified goal. This is a case of “goal supported by inefficient method” (John 1995, p. 87). In a few cases, subjects did not fully recover from the errors, but exhibited substantial goal-directed behavior. We classified them also into the GDR group.

Using the method described above, the two authors separately analyzed the session logs to code independently the subjects’ errors and classify the error recovery strategies. When we compared our classifications, we found that they were identical in 90 percent of cases. In the remaining cases where there was a difference, we came to an eventual agreement through discussion.

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. Preliminary Analysis

Of the 77 subjects, 14 were excluded from data analyses either because of equipment problems or because they did not generate session logs. Our usable sample size was thus reduced to 63, consisting of 36 females and 27 males. The average age of the subjects was 23. We conducted a Pearson correlation test and found that prior experience with computers was not significantly correlated to the dependent variable $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 1 4 )$ nor were other demographic variables, such as age (p $< 0 . 1 2 )$

Near-Transfer Tasks. As we expected, subjects committed very few errors in the near-transfer tasks. Twelve cases of slips occurred from which subjects recovered immediately by using just one command. There were only four cases of other types of error and all were classified as GDR. These subjects also engaged in goal-directed behavior in the far-transfer tasks.

Far-Transfer Tasks. Four subjects either did not commit any errors or engage in any error recovery process, possibly because they did not recognize an error. Six subjects committed slips only and recovered immediately. As our focus was on error recovery process, all 10 were excluded from further analyses. Of the remaining 53 subjects, we classified 25 into GDR and 28 into NGDR groups.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Impact of Errors

In this study, we proposed that the seemingly conflicting perspectives on the effect of errors on learning could be reconciled using a closer examination of the types of errors and error recovery strategies in the learning process. We found evidence to support our hypothesis that goal-directed error recovery is one mechanism that leads to learning. We have determined that learning occurs in a situation where trainees perform far-transfer tasks (tasks requiring inference beyond training tasks), commit errors, and then recover in a goal-directed manner. Our finding supports the beneficial view of errors.

However, we cannot rule out the detrimental outcome view as we did not investigate the negative aspects of errors. Nor did we look at extremely severe types of errors, types that shut down systems completely or are irrecoverable. Many of our subjects did commit such errors. In an experimental setting, the outcome was benign. In the “real world,” the outcome could be severe. These issues needs to be addressed: What type of errors should be blocked? Up to what stage of learning should they be blocked? For what type of users should they be blocked? We have established, however, that all errors should not be blocked. Instead, our results suggest that trainees would benefit from some types of errors if they could be trained to adopt goal-directed error recovery strategies.

## 5.2. Training for Error Recovery

In our study, we did not specifically train our subjects in error recovery strategies. Subjects generated such strategies on their own. The cognitive psychology and training literature suggests that self-generated strategies are more conducive to learning (Carroll 1990). This is the cornerstone of the exploratory learning approach. However, this approach also leads the trainee to make errors; trainees need to be aware of proper coping strategies. Helping trainees develop selfdirected error coping strategies can be achieved in several ways.

One approach is to provide an explicit training script on how to follow a goal-directed error recovery path. GOMS analysis of a few tasks (e.g., Figure 1) can be used as training material to explain how it can help in error recovery. By understanding the goaldecomposition process, trainees may be more inclined to break down a task into goals and subgoals and move towards subgoals when confronted with errors. While the efficacy of this approach has to be tested, prior research on training suggests that this method may be effective. During tests of the minimalist manual training approach, subjects trained on use of specific error recovery procedures consisting of keystrokes such as “Cancel” and “Reply” used it 60 percent more often than subjects not trained to use these procedures (Carroll 1990). Charney and Reder (1986) and Charney et al. (1990) found that when subjects were given problem-solving practice on a spreadsheet, they were able to apply these general techniques in other tasks at a later date. Designers are trained to conduct GOMS task analysis before starting on any design activity (Beard et al. 1997). These results indicate that trainees can obtain knowledge at a metalevel on how to move towards a goal when they have to deal with an error situation.

Trainers can also use intervention to guide trainees to follow a goal-directed error recovery path. As an illustration, retrieving a deleted message in our study required the knowledge that deleted messages went into a wastebasket folder and could be moved just like messages in any folder. Many of our subjects could not infer this. A trainer may guide such a trainee toward a goal-directed recovery process by saying, “Look in the directory of folders. Do you see any folder that is relevant?” This may cue the trainee to discover a folder called WASTEBASKET that sounds relevant, examine its contents, realize that the message is there, and then retrieve it. The above is more meaningful than a trainer saying, “Type SELECT WASTEBASKET, then type READ and then type MOVE foldername.” Constant intervention from the trainer may get trainees habituated to following a goal-directed approach to dealing with errors.

This type of trainer intervention is in some ways similar to the feedback given by intelligent help systems such as Personal Planner that can lead trainees on an error recovery path (Carroll 1990). While these help systems are useful in supporting error recovery, they work best for simple and specific isolated tasks and cannot deal with unexpected errors. Furthermore, they require a lot of software overhead. Therefore, it is important to help trainees develop the skills necessary to recover from errors so that they are not dependent on the system to guide their error recovery process.

A third approach that does not follow directly from our findings could also be used to help trainees develop self-directed error coping strategies.<sup>2</sup> Because errors present opportunities to learn, training approaches could be designed to induce errors and then used as learning tools. Behavior modeling could be used in this approach, based on Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 1986), the premise of which is that people learn by observing another person perform a specific behavior and imitating the behavior. Behavior modeling has been applied successfully to software training. (Gist et al. 1989, Compeau and Higgins 1995). To use this approach, a trainer, while demonstrating a task, may intentionally commit an error and then recover in a goal-directed manner. By observing this behavior trainees may inculcate goal-directed error recovery strategies.

## 5.3. Limitations

We examined the effect of simply adopting an error recovery strategy, but not the effect of actual recovery. As our study was premised on learning through adoption of goal-directed behavior, actual recovery is not important. However, it may have motivational implications. Although our analysis of subjects’ error recovery strategies was based on GOMS, a well-validated model, the final classification (goal-directed or nongoal-directed) was categorical. A particular subjects’ strategy may have heavier goal orientation than that of another. This fine distinction was not captured in our classification.

## 5.4. Future Research Directions

One avenue for research would be to test the effectiveness of various training methods in helping trainees develop goal-directed error recovery patterns. It is possible that some of the suggested training approaches in combination will be most effective. A logical extension is to determine if inducing errors during training is an efficacious learning method. The negative consequences of errors and how they affect user learning and performance is an interesting area of study. Another avenue of research would be to investigate what types of errors should be blocked initially and for what types of users.

It is interesting to determine what causes goaldirected behavior. Is it a behavioral trait? We can speculate that perhaps the NGDR subjects had the knowledge but not the motivation to persevere with the error recovery process. Recent studies in the MIS literature also suggest that managing motivation levels leads to better learning outcomes (Compeau and Higgins 1995, Olfman and Bostrom 1991, Webster and Martocochio 1992). As part of our ongoing research, we are currently investigating whether highly motivated learners have a propensity to engage in goal-directed behavior. Users spend almost one-third of their interaction time with the system on repairing errors (Frolich et al. 1994). A study of the above issues will help us train end-users more effectively on error management strategies and reduce the time spent on repairing errors.
