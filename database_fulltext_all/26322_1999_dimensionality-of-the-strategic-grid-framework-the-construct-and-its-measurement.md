he recent dramatic impact of information technology (IT) on organizational performance pacts firms in different ways, and management needs a clear and systematic understanding of both the current and future relevance and impact of its IT before selecting management tools and approaches. The strategic grid framework, developed for the purpose of helping management gain this understanding, is one of the most highly recognized and quoted conceptual frameworks in information systems literature. Despite such recognition, valid operational measures of this construct are not available for use in empirical research studies. The research reported in this paper is an attempt to develop and validate operational measures for the dimensions of the strategic grid, with the understanding that the availability of such measures will promote future empirical studies. Data for this study consist of the responses of 231 IS executives to a survey instrument. Operational models of the grid dimensions are specified and the measurement properties of the models are assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) within the LISREL framework. Measures resulting from the analysis are shown to meet the requirements of rigorous tests of measurement properties. The CFA results also show that the current portfolio dimension of the strategic grid is unidimensional, while the future portfolio dimension has three factors: management support, differentiation, and enhancement. (Management of Information Technology; IT Planning; Strategic Grid Framework)

## Introduction

Over the last 25 years the industrialized world has been making the transition from an industrial economy to an information economy, and for the next several decades, information—rather than land and capital—will drive the creation of wealth and creativity (McGee and Prusak 1993). Information technology (IT) has dramatically altered the ways organizations perform their operations and design and market their products, and has become a strategic, competitive resource for many organizations (Porter and Millar 1985, Wiseman 1988). It has been suggested that as the role of IT changes within an organization, leadership, organization design, and management processes also change. To fully realize the competitive advantages created by the strategic use of IT, organizations must have appropriate IT management strategies that accomodate their culture, technology status, and business strategy. This contingent nature of IT management has drawn much attention in IT literature (Cash et al. 1988). Given the importance of proper alignment of IT management processes with the organizational role of IT, organizations need to have a clear and systematic understanding of the strategic relevance and impact of their IT, before developing IT management strategies appropriate to that role.

A framework for delineating the differences in the strategic relevance and impact of IT was proposed by McFarlan and McKenney (1983). This framework identifies four different IT environments which are referred to as categories of strategic relevance and impact, or simply as the strategic grid. The grid has been extensively quoted in IT management literature as a useful framework for understanding the positioning of an organization’s IT, and as the basis for designing appropriate planning and control systems for IT management (Ives and Learmonth 1984, Lucas 1994, Senn 1990, Sprague and McNurlin 1993). While there have been extensive references to the usefulness of the grid framework, the discussions have mostly been at a conceptual level. Very little attempt has been made to empirically test the several contingent relationships proposed in the literature between IT management processes and the strategic relevance and impact of IT as represented on the grid.

Empirical validation of conceptual models is an important contribution to both academic and practitioner literature. However, such empirical studies are only feasible when operational measures of theoretical constructs are available. Prior IT research does not offer an operational measure of the grid model that has been subjected to rigorous tests of measurement properties. The present study attempts to develop such a measure by investigating the dimensionality of the strategic grid framework and proposing operational measures of the dimensions. Development of such measures should fill a gap in IT strategy-related empirical research stream by facilitating the empirical validation of extant theories linking the organizational role of IT to IT management strategies and processes. The next section provides a frame of reference for this study, briefly discussing the focus and level of analysis of the study within the context of the existing literature.

## Frame of Reference

IT has played a strategic role in significantly changing the nature of competition, creating strategic opportunities for firms, and enhancing their sustainability in today’s dynamic business environment. This has been widely recognized by researchers and practitioners alike (e.g., Parker and Case 1993, Applegate et al. 1996; Wiseman 1988, Porter and Millar 1985). The strategic impact of IT has been discussed on at least two levels: (1) industry level, and (2) organization level (both interorganization and intraorganization). Porter and Millar (1985), for example, describe how IT can change the nature of competition by changing industry structure (industry level impact) and creating competitive advantage and opportunities for new businesses (organization level impact). Similarly, Bakos and Treacy’s (1986) discussion of IT-enabled strategy identifies business portfolio strategy at the industry level, and competitive and internal strategies at the organizational level. Competitive strategy focuses on aspects of IT that influence the competitive advantages of a firm visa\`-vis other firms (interorganizational). Internal strategy focuses on the development of efficient and effective organizational structures and processes within a firm (intraorganizational).

The most recent work in the first stream of industrylevel analysis is the study by Segars and Grover (1995). Their study analyzes empirically the industry-level impact of IT and finds significant changes in structural characteristics in three industries after the introduction of strategic IT. Research in the second stream of organization-level analysis focuses mainly on sustainable competitive advantages provided by the strategic use of IT in firms. A recent example of research in the second stream is the study by Sethi and King (1994). Their study offers an operational measure to assess the extent to which IT provides competitive advantage. The present study can be classified within the latter stream of organization-level research in that the strategic grid provides a framework for analyzing internal IT management processes that are appropriate to the organizational role of IT. The framework identifies four different IT environments and suggests that each environment requires a different IT management approach. Earl (1988) refers to it as a positioning and scaling framework because it helps to indicate both the positioning (i.e., importance) of IT in a business and the scale of management challenges involved. The next section describes the framework and its dimensions.

## The Strategic Grid Framework

Two aspects of IT have great importance for the management of IT in an individual organization. To quote Applegate et al. (1996):

For some firms, the second-by-second, utterly reliable zerodefect quality of its operations is crucial to the survival of the firm. Even small interruptions in service or disruptions in quality may have profound impact. In other firms, it would take significant disturbances in IT operations over an extended period to have a major impact on the firm’s overall operations. The second aspect . . . is that whereas new IT development initiatives are of great strategic importance for some firms, for other firms, IT is useful but not strategic. Understanding an organization’s position on these two aspects is critical for developing an appropriate IT management strategy (52–53).

Thus, while IT activities may represent an area of great strategic importance for some organizations, they may play only a supportive role in others. Furthermore, the IT function that does not have strategic importance today may have great strategic significance in the future, and vice versa. Based on the current and future impact of IT, McFarlan and McKenney (1983) describe two aspects of strategic relevance and impact of IT. These two aspects of IT impact are represented on the grid in Figure 1 as the strategic impact of applications development portfolio and the strategic impact of existing systems, and are hereinafter referred to as the future and current portfolios, respectively. The strategic relevance and impact of IT for a firm can be positioned anywhere on a continuum ranging from low impact and relevance to high impact and relevance along each of these dimensions of the grid. McFarlan and McKenney (1983) propose that understanding an organization’s position along these continuums is critical to developing appropriate IT management strategies. The combination of future/current portfolio with high/low impact presents four types of IT environments and is represented by the four cells (strategic, turnaround, factory, and support cells) of the strategic grid. The following is a brief description of each IT environment.

• Strategic. Organizations in this cell are critically dependent on the smooth functioning of IT activity. This dependence relates to existing systems as well as to applications under development (i.e., to both the current and future portfolios). Banks, insurance companies, and financial brokerage companies are generally identified with this cell. For these firms, the IT function has a high level of strategic impact on the firm’s performance in terms of both current and future systems. IT planning must be closely integrated with corporate planning, and a considerable degree of senior management commitment to IT activities is required.

• Turnaround. While existing applications may provide operational support for organizations in this cell, they may not be critical to the organization’s current operations. However, the impact of the applications under development is considered vital to achieving the organization’s strategic objectives. An example would be a manufacturing firm that has been using IT for routine data processing but is undergoing rapid growth through planned introduction of advanced manufacturing technologies such as computer-aided design and manufacturing.

• Factory. These firms are heavily dependent on their existing, day-to-day IT operations, and even a temporary disruption in service could have severe operational consequences. However, the applications under development for the future are not critical to the firm’s ability to compete successfully and are mostly maintenance projects. Firms that fall into this cell are not necessarily manufacturing firms or traditional factories; IT operations in such firms support everyday activities in a factory-like way. The airline industry which is noticeably dependent on its IT for its daily operations is typically identified with this cell.

• Support. In these firms, neither current nor future IT activities are critical to the smooth operation of the firm. IT is viewed as playing the traditional backoffice-support role without any potential for dramatically altering an organization’s current or future operations and/or direction. Firms in process-type industries such as a chemical company would fit into this cell.

McFarlan and McKenney (1983) propose that each of these four categories of IT impact represents very different organizational needs for information systems and technologies. For example, in support situations, IT budgets may be smaller, IT planning shorter term, IT management lower profile, and IT managers lower status than in a strategic context. Turnaround situations may require a small revolution, with stepped increases in funding, education, and management support and involvement. Factory contexts are more likely to require consistent and steady management attention, with careful budgeting, sound project and operations control, and emphasis on reliability and efficiency (Earl 1988).

The strategic grid framework, in recent times, has been one of the most widely quoted and frequently discussed frameworks for the assessment of the strategic significance of IT to an organization (Ives and Learmonth 1984, Davis and Olson 1985, Ahituv and Neumann 1990, Zwass 1992, Revenaugh 1994). Despite the importance given to this framework and its suggested usefulness in the empirical analysis of several IT management-related issues, the literature does not provide us with an operational measure which could potentially have great use in such analysis. Research by Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1990) and Premkumar (1992) provided some preliminary evidence of empirical support for the framework. Their studies, however, did not employ methodological rigor in operationalization, such as the application of strict tests of measurement properties as recommended in the literature. The present study is an attempt to develop operational measures for the two dimensions of the grid framework using a data set that was tested to meet the requirements of content validity. The data were then analyzed using the structural equation modeling approach and subjected to rigorous tests of construct validity.

## Operationalization

The future portfolio and current portfolio dimensions were operationalized using multiple items. These items were modeled after the illustrative questions presented in Cash et al. (1988) and adapted by Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1990). The future portfolio dimension was measured by 11 items and the current portfolio dimension was measured by eight items. Since the previous research reported low reliability for current portfolio, measured only with three items, more items were added to improve the reliability of that construct. All items were measured on fivepoint interval scales. The appendix presents the questionnaire items. To ensure the content validity of the items, the questionnaires were sent first to two IS researchers who checked the items for appropriateness and relevance. Two IS executives of major organizations were also requested to complete the questionnaire and then to comment on the clarity and appropriateness of the questions.

## Choice of Analytical Procedure

This study attempts to develop operational measures of the dimensions of the strategic grid framework. A traditional approach to establishing construct validity is to use exploratory factor analysis (EFA). EFA is useful in discovering potential latent sources of variance and covariance in observed measurements. Items with good measurement properties should exhibit high factor loadings on the latent factor of which they are indicators and small factor loadings on the factors that are measured by differing sets of indicators. Therefore, such results provide evidence of convergent and discriminant validity of the measurement items (Segars and Grover 1993). However, one major limitation of EFA is that the approach initially assumes that the measurement errors of the items are uncorrelated. In practice, there is always some degree of error correlations among certain items that EFA is unable to detect. More recently, the structural equation modeling (SEM) approach has been gaining increasing popularity in IT research due to its robustness and flexibility in establishing construct validity. One of the most widely used SEM software is Joreskog and Sorbom’s (1989) LISREL. Using LISREL, it is possible to specify, test, and modify the measurement model; the model fit indices provide guidance in assessing construct validity. This study uses LISREL analysis as the primary construct validation tool.

## Assessment of Measurement Properties

The literature has conceptualized the future and current portfolio dimensions of the strategic grid framework as two individual constructs. These two constructs were initially specified in the present study as independent single-factor models. The measurement properties of these two models were assessed using confirmatory factor analysis within the LISREL framework. There is general agreement in the literature that tests of unidimensionality (and convergent validity), reliability, and discriminant validity are important for establishing construct validity (Ahire et al. 1996, Sethi and King 1994, Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1994, Venkatraman 1989); a detailed description of these tests, as performed for the present study, is set out below.

Assessment of Unidimensionality (and Convergent Validity). The unidimensionality (and convergent validity) criteria require that there be one single latent variable underlying a set of measurement items (Anderson et al. 1988). The two dimensions of the strategic grid framework were initially conceptualized as individual unidimensional constructs and specified as two single-factor models. The goodness of the overall fit of a model is indicated by the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) and the Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI). The GFI indicates the relative amount of variance and covariance jointly explained by the model; the AGFI differs from the GFI in adjusting for the number of degrees of freedom (Byrne 1989). Both range from 0 to 1. Values of 0.9 or more are considered evidence of good fit (Dillon and Goldstein 1984, Hair et al. 1995). The ratio of chi-square to degrees of freedom provides information on the relative efficiency of competing models in accounting for the data. Researchers have recommended using ratios as low as 2 or as high as 5 to indicate a reasonable fit (Marsh and Hocevar 1985).

The “current portfolio” model had a GFI of 0.92 and AGFI of 0.86 (Table 3) indicating that it can be regarded as a single factor construct. Following Sethi and King (1994), iterative modifications were then made by observing modification indices and k coefficients, to improve key model fit statistics. Further, as recommended by Joreskog and Sorbom (1989) only one item was altered at a time to avoid overmodification of the model. This iterative process continued until all model parameters and key fit indices met recommended criteria. Section A of Table 4 presents the details of this modification process and the items constituting the current portfolio construct.

The single-factor model specification of the “future portfolio” construct had a GFI of 0.69 and AGFI of 0.54 (Table 3). These low values of the indicators suggested that a single-factor model was, perhaps, not adequate in explaining the correlations between the items specified in the model. Anderson and Gerbing (1988) and

MacCallum (1986) have suggested that this situation could arise when fewer than the required number of factors is specified in the confirmatory model. A further investigation of this construct was carried out as follows. As stated earlier in the operationalization section, the items in the future portfolio are based on the items developed by Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1990) in their exploratory study. With their data set made available for use in the present study, an exploratory factor analysis was performed. The analysis led to the identification of three factors. These three factors provided the basis for respecifying the future portfolio model, to have three sub-factors, in a confirmatory factor analysis (using the data collected for the present study). ## Discussion

As initially conceptualized by McFarlan and McKenney (1983), the grid framework was expected to be used primarily for identifying mismatches and guiding the choice of appropriate IT management tools. To identify the positioning of an organization on the grid in terms of whether it is in the strategic, turnaround, factory, or support environment, operational measures are required; this study provides such a set of measures. The following paragraphs discuss the possible uses of these operational measures of the grid from the perspective of both IS management and IS research.

The location of an organization’s position on the grid can facilitate its management’s assessment of whether a mismatch exists between the current position of the organization and where management believes the organization should be. This mismatch can occur, for example, when management wishes to use IT strategically in the future, but all the applications under development in the future portfolio indicate that the organization is only in the support quadrant in the grid. Evidently, management’s goals for IT in this organization have not been put into action through appropriate IT policies and plans.

A mismatch can also occur when a particular cell position on the grid is characteristic of the industry as a whole, but this organization finds itself positioned in a different cell. In this situation, management has to decide whether the current position is where it wants to be or whether it wishes to move into another cell. This decision is an important one because it depends not so much on where the other firms within the industry are positioned but, perhaps, more so on whether the firm wants to be the leader or the follower in its use of IT vis-a\` -vis its competitors. Applegate et al. (1996) provides an example from the retailing industry which, a decade ago, was considered to be in the support cell. The retailer, Wal-Mart, was, even at that time, positioning itself to be in the strategic cell. Its competitors then paid little attention to what Wal-Mart was doing; now, most of those same competitors are trying to play catch-up in an industry in which IT is increasingly being considered to be a strategic force.

The use of the operational measures from this study for identifying an organization’s position on the grid can also help management better understand the importance, the particular character, and the inherited situation of IT for their business, and can provide guidance on how IT should be managed in their particular organization. For example, there may be differences, even among individual managers, in their perceptions of the present and/or future positioning of their IT. By having these managers respond to the grid questionnaire it may be possible to clarify these perceptions and attempt to build consensus among executives on the current and future role of IT. The three-factor solution for the future portfolio that emerges as a better alternative to a single-factor solution suggests that the portfolio is perhaps more complex than conceived by its original authors (McFarlan and McKenney 1983). It is possible that this complexity is an indication of the dramatic changes taking place in the economic, competitive, and technological environment around us. While the original and simpler two-dimensional conceptualization allowed for easier mapping of an organization’s position on the grid, the proposed model increases the complexity of the mapping by increasing the number of dimensions. However, the proposed model could serve as a better diagnostic tool in understanding an organization’s positioning in relation to its IS.

A possible limitation of the three-factor solution for the future portfolio is that, while the enhancement factor is conceptually a distinct factor, the managerial support and differentiation factors are, conceptually, somewhat difficult to distinguish from each other. For example, the “new decision support information” in the managerial support factor could be used for differentiation; likewise, “application of new technologies” in the differentiation factor could be used for managerial support. Future research, by adding or refining the items comprising the operational measures of the portfolios, and by revalidating the constructs, can help strengthen the diagnostic value of the model. Another interesting future research direction will be to gather data about firms’ leadership, organizational design, and management processes to see if these characteristics actually change across different sections of the strategic grid.

## Conclusion

This study offers operational measures of the dimensions of the strategic grid framework. These measures are shown to meet the requirements of rigorous tests of measurement properties. It is hoped that these measures of the relevance and impact of IT would be useful to IT researchers interested in the empirical validation of the many theoretical relationships linking IT management strategies and processes to the organizational role of IT.<sup>1</sup>
