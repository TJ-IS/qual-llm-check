## 1. Introduction

One of the basic tenets of the Decision Support Systems (DSS) literature has been that DSS are designed to assist decision-makers in making better decisions. It is generally assumed that a decision-maker wishes to behave rationally, but cannot because of cognitive limitations that inhibit the complete and accurate assessment of available information. Decision support technologies are designed to provide decision-makers with the additional capabilities to, in effect, extend their bounds of rationality. However, the results concerning the impact of DSS on improving effectiveness are equivocal at best (Benbasat et al. 1993, Eierman et al. 1995, Sharda et al. 1988). The issue of whether the provision of a DSS would lead to greater decisionmaking effectiveness, efficiency, or both, is still an open question.

Given the empirical findings to date, it is unclear the means by which, and the degree to which, DSS designers could and should intervene, influence, and direct the decision-maker towards the desired behavior of effectiveness. DSS design is often based on one of two loosely grouped “schools of thought” (Stabell 1986). The first suggests that DSS, as technological interventions, should assist in the implementation of normative decision-making strategies. While few would dispute the value of these normative models, their implementation is often problematic (Zachary 1988). Decision-makers often resist the use of models that are based on complex procedures. This approach, which emphasizes technology and methodology, sometimes overlooks the role of the decision-maker in the DSS design process (Zachary 1988). The alternative school of thought suggests a behavioral approach to DSS design with the aim of extending the capabilities and overcoming the limitations of decision-makers. The intent of this approach is to guide and direct the decision-maker towards a better solution (Silver 1988, 1991).

The research described in this paper intends to provide answers to the two major issues raised above: 1) the conditions under which DSS use leads to effectiveness or efficiency outcomes, and 2) the means by which the DSS designer could influence the behavior of the user, and what the extent of this intervention should be. We argue that these two issues are closely linked and can be understood by examining the effort and accuracy trade-offs faced by the decision-maker when using a DSS to complete a decision task. Such tradeoffs can be influenced by the nature of the decision tasks, the types of decision support provided and the motivation of the decision-maker. This paper develops an integrated model combining these factors and presents the findings of an experimental test of the key elements in the model. In addition, the work discussed here extends the factors tested in a series of previous studies (Todd and Benbasat 1991, 1992, 1993, 1994a, 1994b, forthcoming) by manipulating a new variable, incentives, to reflect the consequences associated with making a good or bad choice.

The paper proceeds as follows. In § 2, we describe how the models guiding research in DSS have evolved over time leading to the integrated model described in this study. This is a model that explains the relationship of DSS to decision strategy and decision performance from an effort-accuracy perspective (derived from the behavioral decision theory literature) and incorporates the effects of decision-making incentives. Section 3 situates the model in the context of the particular task and decision strategies of interest to this study. Section 4 describes the DSS employed in the study. Section 5 presents the specific hypotheses about the impact of DSS and incentives on decision strategy selection. Section 6 presents the results, and § 7 discusses the implications of the findings for DSS researchers and designers. Methodological details are included in Appendix A.

## 2. Literature Review and Model Development

Eom (1996) has catalogued some 944 articles written between 1971 and 1993 that examined various aspects of decision support, including empirical studies that tested the relationship between DSS and decision performance. Despite this large base of research, the nature of this relationship is still not well understood (Benbasat et al. 1993, Eierman et al. 1995, Sharda et al. 1988). The reasons for this are unclear, but it may be, in part, a result of the inadequate and inconsistent application of research methods to study the impact of DSS on performance (Sharda et al. 1988). There is an extensive set of DSS capabilities that can be tested, across a multitude of task settings, with a wide variety of performance measures. This complicates any attempt at integration. In addition, the equivocal relationships may result from a failure to examine decision processes. These processes are believed to mediate the influence of DSS on performance (Jarvenpaa 1989, Silver 1991, Todd and Benbasat 1987, 1991, Vessey 1991). Failure to explicitly articulate and integrate these processes into comprehensive theoretical models has also been identified as an important stumbling block to advancing knowledge in DSS (Eierman et al. 1995). Building on this notion, we describe the evolution of DSS research models over the last 20 years, culminating in the model we have tested to investigate the influence of DSS on strategy selection and decisionmaking performance in the context of various incentives.

## 2.1. Understanding the DSS–Decision Performance Relationship

Models 1 (a–e) show the evolution of our theoretical understanding of the DSS–performance relationship by postulating a set of mediating and moderating variables that explain how and why a DSS influences decision performance.

Model 1a suggests the simplest relationship—DSS capabilities directly influence decision performance. It implicitly suggests a direct effect that is robust across types of DSS, tasks, users, and work environments. The model lacks explanatory power, as evidenced by the number of studies testing this relationship which have found largely equivocal results (Benbasat et al. 1993, Eierman et al. 1995, Sharda et al. 1988).

Model 1b shows the most commonly tested model of the “DSS–decision performance” relationship. It suggests that the nature of the task and the type of decision support provided individually and jointly influence decision performance. This is a tasktechnology fit perspective (Benbasat et al. 1986, Goodhue and Thompson 1995, Goodhue 1995), i.e., the influence of a DSS on performance is contingent upon the degree to which the capabilities provided by the system match the requirements of the task. The greater this alignment, the more effective the DSS.

In the area of information representation and decision support, the fit notion has been extended by Vessey (1991) to incorporate the decision-maker’s internal representation of the problem. Vessey and Galletta (1991) suggest that the task and technology fit is based on the cognitive demands of the problem and its relationship to the DSS, combined with the internal problem representation of the decision-maker. They argue that for performance to be enhanced, users must develop an appropriate mental model for the effective completion of the task (Vessey and Galletta 1991).

The model developed by Vessey (1991) applies most specifically to lower level spatial and symbolic tasks that involve extracting information from alternative information representations such as line graphs and bar charts. In such instances, there may be a clear solution and strategy that directly lead to that solution. For tasks that are more complex, there may be a greater variety of strategies and determining an optimal strategy may not be straightforward. The model represented in Model 1c accounts for such tasks. It suggests that task and DSS capabilities jointly influence the decision strategy employed and that this strategy, in turn, will influence decision performance (Jarvenpaa 1989). In other words, the way in which the DSS is appropriated by the decision-maker to handle the task will influence decision performance.

The design of the DSS restricts the decision-maker to certain decision processes that are embedded into the system (Silver 1990). Within this restricted design space, DSS designers may guide users, both directly and indirectly, toward certain types of decision strategies. They could use restriction and guidance to direct users towards a normative approach or to prevent them from employing undesirable strategies (Silver 1988). Model 1c is silent about the mechanism(s) by which strategy is determined: Given a DSS that can support a variety of strategies for a particular task, how does the decision-maker determine which one to employ? Implicit in the DSS literature is the somewhat simplistic notion that the decision-maker will choose a strategy that leads to the best performance. Building on the behavioral decision-making literature (Payne et al. 1993), this view can be extended, suggesting that the decision-maker will choose a strategy based on the trade-offs of its perceived effort and perceived accuracy.

This perspective is outlined in Model 1d. Building on the earlier models, it suggests that perceived effort and perceived accuracy moderate the effects of DSS and task on strategy selection. Trade-offs between effort and accuracy have been demonstrated to lead to strategy adaptation in a variety of studies; Payne et al. (1993) provide a comprehensive literature review of the cost-benefit framework of cognition and associated empirical work. According to this framework, the joint objectives of a decision-maker are to maximize accuracy (or decision quality) and minimize effort. As these objectives often conflict, trade-offs are made between the two. Overall, the empirical (Russo and Dosher 1983, Christensen-Szalanski 1980, Bettman et al. 1990, Bettman and Kakkar 1977, Johnson et al. 1988), simulation (Thorngate 1980, Johnson and Payne 1985, Payne et al. 1988, 1990), and conceptual (Beach and Mitchell 1978, Shugan 1979, March 1978, Jungerman 1985, Einhorn and Hogarth 1978, Kleinmuntz and Schkade 1993) literatures indicate that effort is the more important factor influencing strategy selection.

This emphasis on effort extends to strategy selection in the context of DSS usage where decision-making effort plays a moderating role in the proposed relationship between a DSS, strategy selection, and decision performance for a given task. If a DSS provides support in such a way that makes a more accurate strategy at least as easy to employ as a simpler, but less accurate, heuristic, it may induce that more accurate strategy and as a consequence improve decision performance. Otherwise, a DSS may only influence decision-making efficiency by allowing the same strategy to be executed with less effort. This occurs because decision-makers appear to use decision support in a way that minimizes their overall level of effort expenditure.

## 2.2. Understanding the Role of Incentives

Model 1e, which forms the basis for the empirical work reported here, shows the extended model incorporating the role of incentives. Performance-based incentives have the effect of motivating individuals to work harder to achieve a high level of performance by increasing the decision-maker’s sense of involvement with the task. Involvement is defined as the perceived personal relevance and hence the commitment an individual has to completing a particular task (Celsi and

Model 1e Integrating the Role of Incentives  
![](/api/attachments/UYRSK7KU/fulltext/images/443e0b2e2102902a84204f812603dff22fcc987e15fae3e9622694cd08573064.jpg)

Olson 1988, Barki and Hartwick 1989). Increasing involvement has been found to influence the amount of information processing effort that is devoted to a task (Greenwald and Leavitt 1984, Petty et al. 1983, Celsi and Olson 1988, Beatty and Smith 1987, Gensch and Javalgi 1987).

Given the motivating effects of incentives, it is commonly assumed that they will also lead decisionmakers to employ strategies that should lead to greater accuracy, though at the cost of expending greater effort (Payne et al. 1993). However, findings in experimental economics suggest that this widely held belief is not always supported (Beattie and Loomes 1997). Similarly, empirical results from the behavioral decisionmaking literature indicate that incentives may increase, have no effect on, or at times actually decrease performance (Payne et al. 1993, Wright and Abdul-Ezz 1988). For example, performance-based, extrinsic incentives have been observed to result in the use of more normative strategies (Creyer et al. 1990) and lead to higher performance levels (Wright and Abdul-Ezz 1988, Stone and Ziebart 1995). On the other hand, Stone and Schkade (1994) found that explicit incentives could motivate speed but not accuracy. In short, incentives do not necessarily lead to the use of optimal strategies, nor do they improve decision performance in all situations.

To explain these contradictory outcomes, Payne et al. (1993) distinguished between working harder and working smarter. This is the difference between putting more effort into the same strategy versus changing to a strategy that will lead to a better decision or outcome. Thus, increasing incentives could lead decision-makers either to work harder or work smarter, but it is difficult to predict which and, consequently, to predict the effect of incentives on decision performance. These effects are indicated by the arrows in Model 1e from incentives to the path leading into strategy (suggesting the effect of changing strategies or working smarter) and from incentives to the strategy-performance relationship (suggesting working harder).

To more fully understand the effects of incentives, Payne et al. (1993) suggest that for incentives to have a positive influence on strategy selection and decision performance, the decision-maker must:

1) know that his/her current strategy is inadequate in terms of the desired performance;

2) know that a better strategy exists; and

3) have the cognitive resources required to execute the better strategy.

There are several ways by which a DSS could assist a decision-maker to act on these conditions and increase the likelihood of executing a normative strategy in the presence of performance incentives:

1) By providing the additional cognitive resources required to execute normative strategies (point 3 above), decision-makers are made capable of responding to increased incentives.

2) Through restrictiveness, that is, by withholding certain decision support capabilities that are needed to reduce the cost of executing a particular strategy in comparison to others, and thus providing guidance towards a better strategy (Silver 1988; 1990). This supports point 2 above indirectly, since the decisionmaker is influenced in a non-directed way towards the better strategy.

3) Through learning or development effects (Barr and Sharda 1997). The structure provided by a DSS may supply the decision-maker with additional insights into the problem. Further, the more the DSS frees the decision-makers from more mundane computational tasks, the more they can study the problem and develop a greater awareness of alternative strategies for solving it, supporting points 1 and 2 above.

Our study focuses primarily on items (1) and (2) discussed above.

## 3. Choice Strategies and Effort

In this study we examine the role of incentives and decision support capabilities in influencing strategy in the context of a multialternative, multiattribute preferential choice problem. Such tasks require decisionmakers to choose one of a number of alternatives, each of which is described by a common set of attributes (Keeney and Raiffa 1976). There are a variety of strategies that may be used to complete such tasks. For the purposes of this study, we chose to operationalize decision support for two such strategies, described in detail below.

## 3.1. Description of Preferential Choice Strategies 0

Two of the more common strategies for dealing with such problems are: Additive Compensatory (AC) and Elimination by Aspects (EBA) (see for example, Payne 1976, Olshavsky 1979, Svenson 1979, Biggs et al. 1985, Sundstrom 1987, Jarvenpaa 1989). While there are numerous other strategies (Svenson 1979), we focus on these two in detail because they represent prototypical additive and elimination approaches. Each strategy is relatively easy to automate, and the AC strategy, in particular, can be automated to varying degrees, permitting us to examine the sensitivity associated with different levels of support that require different combinations of individual effort and computer-based calculation.

Using the AC strategy, a decision-maker evaluates one alternative at a time along all relevant attributes. Each attribute is assigned a weight, with larger weights indicating higher value or importance. For a particular alternative, the decision-maker reads the first attribute value. The attribute value is then combined with its weight. This process is repeated for each attribute. A score for each alternative is determined by summing the products of the attribute values and weights. Once these computations are completed for each alternative, the one with the highest weighted score is chosen.

The Elimination by Aspects (EBA) strategy is based on a comparison of attribute values to some threshold level and the elimination of any alternative that does not meet the threshold level for any one of its attributes. To evaluate a single attribute a decision-maker must read the attribute value, compare the attribute to a threshold, and eliminate the alternative if the attribute fails to meet the threshold. Attributes are chosen for evaluation based on their relative importance to the decision-maker, with the most important attributes being most likely to be chosen first. This process is repeated until only a single alternative remains.

In general, AC requires more effort than EBA, regardless of factors such as information load, alternative similarity or other task characteristics. This occurs for a number of reasons. First, AC is more process intensive, requiring seven operations to process a single attribute value, compared to four for EBA. Second, the type of operations required by AC are more complex in that they tend to comprise computations rather than simple comparisons (Payne et al. 1988). Finally, using AC typically requires an exhaustive examination of all available information, unless the values of attributes are highly correlated or certain important attribute values are extremely poor. For EBA, alternatives are often eliminated prior to the consideration of all attribute values. Though more it requires more effort to implement, AC should lead to a better outcome than does EBA because it considers all available information, weighs attributes according to their importance to the decision-maker, and is compensatory, allowing high values on some attributes to offset low values on others (Keeney and Raiffa 1976).

## 3.2. Identification of Strategies and Operators

The dependent variables of interest in this study are the operators employed by the decision-maker as they carry out a strategy (e.g., Todd and Benbasat 1994a). The particular operators of interest are: i) independent evaluations that are characteristic of EBA processing, ii) elimination statements that are characteristic of EBA processing, iii) compensatory statements characteristic of AC processing, and iv) total operations characteristic of overall information processing that should be higher when the AC strategy is employed. Detailed definitions of the individual operators are included in Appendix A. For each operator, we examine the proportion of statements in the protocol devoted to that operator. The proportional measures for the individual operators indicate the change in the mix of operations. In addition, examining the total number of operators shows how total effort expenditure changes across treatment groups.

## 4. The Decision Support System (DSS)

An experimental DSS was developed to provide support for various strategies associated with multialternative, multiattribute preferential choice problems. Such tasks require decision-makers to choose one of a number of alternatives, each of which is described by a common set of attributes. The specific task used in this study was a consumer choice task, choosing an electronic organizer (see Appendix A for full details about the task). Each organizer was described in terms of attributes such as storage capacity, display quality, ease of learning, portability, backup capabilities, keypad quality, ease of data entry, and ease of information recall (see Figure 1).

The DSS capabilities provided in this experiment and their syntax are described in Table 2.<sup>1</sup> The interface for the DSS used in this study was command based. Information was presented to subjects in a matrix form, similar to a spreadsheet (see Figure 1). The information in the matrix could be manipulated in a variety of ways as allowed by the commands provided.

For this experiment, different combinations of commands provided different levels of support for the additive compensatory strategy. Support for AC was manipulated at four levels, defined as none, low, moderate, and high (see Table 3). In the no support condition participants were provided only with the OPEN, CLOSE, and DROP, and CONDITIONAL DROP commands, that together automate the key processes for the EBA strategy, but provide no support for the AC strategy. The CONDITIONAL DROP is the key command supporting EBA. To use the CONDITIONAL DROP, the decision-maker specifies an attribute (row) in the choice matrix and a threshold to apply in dropping alternatives from the choice set. For example, to eliminate all alternatives in Figure 1 where display quality was less than 5, the decision-maker would enter DR 2 - 5. This process could be repeated for each attribute.

The three levels of support for AC, beyond the no support condition, were low, moderate, and high (Table 3). As the level of support increases, the AC strategy becomes easier to use. Those in the low AC support condition had access to the CREATE and CALCU-LATE commands. CREATE allows a user to create a column representing a vector of weights to indicate the relative importance of each attribute. The CALCU-LATE command allows the user to perform arithmetic operations on pairs of rows or columns. Thus, in the low AC support condition, the user could CREATE a weight column indicating the importance of each attribute and then use the CALCULATE command to multiply the weights with the values of an alternative (column). This is defined as low AC support since the system provides only partial support for the strategy, leaving the user with a significant number of mental operations to perform.

The moderate level of AC support included all the commands described above for low AC support plus a ROW TOTAL command. The ROW TOTAL command can be used to sum the values for each alternative in the choice set across all rows. Thus, by using a combination of the CREATE, CALCULATE, and ROW TOTAL commands, the users can derive weighted scores for each alternative and then select the alternative with the highest score. To fully execute the AC strategy in this case would require one CREATE command, ten CALCULATE commands, and one ROW TOTAL, for 12 in total. This is defined as a moderate level of support since it takes the user through the steps to the final choice, effectively automating all computation, but requires the user to execute a relatively large number of system commands to do so.

The high level of AC support adds the GLOBAL command to those available in the moderate AC condition. The GLOBAL command can be used to multiply the matrix of attribute values (all 10 columns), by the vector of weights developed using the CREATE command, to yield a matrix of weighted scores. Thus, the GLOBAL command can potentially replace the ten separate CALCULATE commands. After applying the GLOBAL command, the ROW TOTAL command then provides a weighted total score for each alternative. The preferred alternative is the one with the highest weighted score. This is defined as the high AC support condition because the commands provided virtually automate the AC strategy.

## 5. Hypotheses Concerning DSS, Incentives, and Strategy Selection

## 5.1. Hypotheses Concerning Influence of DSS on Strategy

Based on theory and earlier findings, we know that if a DSS provides support in such a way as to make a more accurate strategy at least as easy to employ as a simpler but less accurate heuristic, then the use of the DSS induces behavior that is consistent with that more accurate strategy (Payne et al. 1993, Todd and Benbasat 1991, 1992, 1993, 1994a, 1994b, forthcoming). Our expectations about strategy to be used in the different support conditions are summarized in Table 4. As the level of support for AC increases across the four treatment levels the AC strategy becomes less complex to employ and should be used to a greater extent since it is a more accurate strategy than EBA. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. As the level of AC support increases, subjects will be more likely to follow the AC strategy. Thus, subjects with higher level of AC support will make

a) proportionally fewer independent evaluations,

b) proportionally fewer elimination statements,

c) proportionally more compensatory statements, and d) more total statements,

than those with lower levels of AC support.

A DSS facilitates the identification of alternative strategies by restricting decision-makers to specific DSS capabilities. With the guidance provided by a DSS, decision-makers may have additional points of reference from which to evaluate the adequacy of their approach. Finally, and most directly, the DSS provides the decision-maker with the capability to execute strategies that may be otherwise too cognitively demanding. Thus, with a DSS, decision-makers are in a better position to identify and execute normatively oriented strategies. Further, a DSS may make the use of those strategies. In the low incentive condition, when support for AC is not available (see Table 4), we expect that decisionmakers will have neither the motivation nor the capability to implement a normative strategy; this prediction is consistent with our earlier findings in cases when DSS were not available. Those given higher incentives might be motivated, but cognitive constraints make the implementation of better strategies not feasible. Thus, without AC support, we would not expect a movement towards the AC strategy.

When high support for AC is provided, decisionmakers have the capability to implement the normative strategy. In an earlier study, we observed that subjects with AC support followed an AC strategy even when support for other strategies was also available (Todd and Benbasat forthcoming). High AC support influences (reduces) the threshold level of incentives that is required to follow the AC; that is, in this case we do not expect incentives to influence strategy selection.

To summarize, behavior in the no AC support conditions will not be influenced by the level of incentives since cognitive constraints dominate. In the high support condition, the normative strategy is made so easy that it will be used regardless of the level of incentive provided. It is only in the moderate AC support condition that there may be differential implementation of strategies depending on the level of incentive provided. In these circumstances higher levels of incentive may make the implementation of the AC strategy more likely.

Hypothesis 2. Higher levels of incentives will lead to higher use of the AC strategy when partial support for AC is provided, but will have no influence when either complete or no support for AC is provided. Thus, as the level of incentives increases, subjects with partial AC support will make

## 6. Results

Methodological details are presented in Appendix A. Forty-two subjects performed a consumer choice task, choosing from among a set of ten electronic organizers. Each subject completed the task four times. The first was a practice problem followed by three experimental choice tasks. For all subjects the first experimental choice task involved making a hypothetical selection. The second and third experimental tasks involved making choices based on which the subjects would receive one of the organizers they chose. While engaged in the choice process, each subject verbalized his or her thought processes. Transcriptions of these verbalized protocols form the basic data used in the analyses reported below.

MANOVA was used to test for overall effects, and univariate tests were used to test the individual effects of AC support and incentives, as well as the interaction between the two. Scheffe tests were then used to make pairwise comparisons between the multilevel treatments. Prior to presenting the results a manipulation check on the level of incentives is evaluated.

## 6.1. Manipulation Checks

The effect of the different levels of incentives were assessed in a post-study questionnaire, that asked the subjects to rate how important and how easy it was for them to make a good choice in each of the three tasks. Participants reported that they felt that it was more important to make a good choice in the \$60 task (mean response  2.75 on a 7 point scale where 1  important, 7  unimportant) than in the \$20 task (mean  3.14; t  2.61; p  0.01), but that there was no difference between the hypothetical (mean  3.42) and \$20 task (t  1.5, p  ns). Consistent results were reported with respect to the ease of making a choice. Thus the manipulation check suggests that the subjects were more highly engaged in the \$60 task than in either the \$20 or hypothetical task, and that their level of engagement in the hypothetical and \$20 tasks was equivalent.

This outcome is consistent with our earlier contention that a DSS may affect the way that a decisionmaker thinks about alternative problem solving approaches (Todd and Benbasat 1991). These subtle effects of decisional guidance merit further investigation.

## 7. Concluding Comments

DSS are intended to help decision-makers to improve their performance. Such improvement requires a change from the current approaches to decision making, either towards the better execution of an existing decision strategy (i.e., working harder) or towards a better decision strategy (i.e., working smarter). In fact, Silver (1990) states that much of the DSS literature could be characterized as developing agents of change. A macro view of change focuses on implementation, i.e., the examination of the processes and factors that lead to successful adoption of DSS by individuals and organizations. A micro view of change focuses on the nature of the DSS, i.e., the tools and features it contains, and how these interact with task characteristics to influence decision strategy. How best to create such changes in order to achieve both effectiveness and efficiency has been an issue of much discussion in the DSS literature (Silver 1990). The effort-accuracy perspective we present in this research provides one avenue by which to guide such change at both the macroand micro-design levels.

Guidance, if properly applied, should lead to improvement in decision-making effectiveness and efficiency. Optimal designs are those that will make it easier to employ the best strategies. This goal has been the raison d’etre of designing DSS. However, it has long been asserted that accepting the relationship between DSS use and decision effectiveness as an “article of faith” may limit the development of knowledge in the field (Keen 1979; Stabell 1983). The equivocal nature of the DSS-performance relationship reported in the empirical DSS literature supports these conjectures. This can be extended to the examination of the acceptance of new information technologies. For example, a series of studies by Davis (1989) and others has suggested that assessments of ease of use and usefulness are essential determinants of the use of information technology. At the core of the usefulness concept is the idea that the decision-maker will improve their performance. The assessment of usefulness is partly determined by perceptions of ease of use. It is clear that “engineering” effort considerations as a conscious part of DSS design is central to the notion of guiding the decision maker towards the objective of “working smarter.” How can such guidance be provided?

The answer derived from our research, supported by the cost-benefit theories of cognition and empirical research in behavioral decision making, is that it can accomplished by manipulating effort, i.e., by making available, and/or restricting, certain DSS features in order to alter the costs associated with alternative problem solving strategies. As Silver (1988) notes, restriction and guidance are closely related because a designer can influence the behavior of the user by either means. It appears from our studies that the effect of DSS in terms of their influence on decision quality cannot be understood without taking into account the way the DSS influences the effort required to use alternative strategies. Together, the studies suggest that decisionmakers appear to use the DSS in such a way as to maintain a low overall level of effort expenditure and will employ a particular strategy if the decision aid makes it easier to apply relative to competing alternative strategies. Thus by attending to the relationship between DSS and decision making effort it becomes possible for the DSS designer to influence the way in which information is processed.

Furthermore, this current study clearly suggests that it is possible to induce more normatively oriented strategies when the effort relationships are changed sufficiently in favor of those strategies. Inducing such behaviors has been an elusive goal of DSS research. What is important to recognize here is how low the effort balance needs to be tipped in order to facilitate a strategy shift. The effects of the support for the additive compensatory strategy were only observed when the level of support was sufficient to fully automate the strategy (the moderate and high conditions). Thus, it appears that the influence of the decision aid will be most pronounced when it makes the use of a desired strategy very low in terms of effort relative to alternative strategies. Furthermore, this result was consistent even in the face of relatively high incentives to perform well.

Such incentives have long been asserted to influence whether the decision-maker will use the DSS to achieve effectiveness or efficiency (Moore and Chang 1983). In this study, we extended our earlier research by investigating the role effort and DSS play in the presence of incentives. Based on notions developed by Payne et al. (1993) on the three conditions under which incentives will have effects, we were able to make predictions about how effort and DSS capabilities manifest themselves in the presence of incentives. We postulated that, in cases where the DSS did not provide the capability of executing a more normativelyoriented strategy requiring a high expenditure of effort, incentives would not lead to more normative strategies. In cases where full support was provided for more normative strategies the provision of higher incentives again would not lead to a change in strategies, because the decision-maker had easy access to the most appropriate strategy.

It is interesting that in the cases where we predicted incentive effects would be strongest—the moderate AC support condition—where the subjects only needed to take a few additional steps to complete the

AC strategy, they did not do so. Rather, they used an elimination-oriented EBA strategy that was automated fully and thus was easier to implement. In effect, these results are consistent with many other studies that failed to observe incentive effects. We explain the lack of incentive effects in the following way: Without a DSS, the effort needed to implement better strategies dominates incentive considerations; with a DSS, the effort reduction made possible by a DSS has already influenced the decision-maker to use normative strategies, in which case incentives do not play a key role. In both instances, effort considerations override the potential incentive effects in determining how the DSS is used. This is consistent with our earlier findings that differences in problem size also had no influence on decision strategy when using a DSS (Todd and Benbasat 1994b). Together, these results suggest that the manipulation of effort provides a powerful lever for DSS designers to apply for guiding the use of decision strategies, a guidance strategy that can overcome commonly observed effects of task on strategy (Payne et al. 1993). This points the way towards an engineering approach for DSS design that may be invariant across a variety of task characteristics.

Our research has focused on the effort associated with employing different choice strategies and the role of decision aids in changing those strategies with an eye towards inducing decision makers to work smarter. The implications for this at the micro level have been to develop some fundamental techniques for the analysis of DSS. As part of this we have outlined a decomposition method for DSS designers to estimate the potential effects of DSS use on effort and strategy selection (Todd and Benbasat 1991, 1994a, 1994b) to allow them to direct behavior by manipulating restrictiveness and providing guidance via relative effort. Such micro-level approaches to the cognitive engineering of DSS design are important not simply because they make a DSS easier to use, but because such ease of use is critical making DSS more useful. Making technologies more useful is critical to user acceptance and assessment of the value of IT. The cognitive engineering perspective that we have provided in the context of our “decision support–decision strategy–decision performance” model provides a basis from which to build DSS that balance effort and accuracy trade-offs in such a way as to guide decision makers towards more normative decision strategies and better decision outcomes.

## Appendix A. Details of Methodology

This appendix provides details on the dependent variables, task setting, subjects and procedures used in the experiment.

## Dependent Variables

Independent evaluations are statements that compare the value of an attribute for a given alternative to some externally identified standard or reference point. For example, statements such as “Apartment F is expensive” or “Apartment G is too far from campus” are independent evaluations. Independent evaluations are characteristic of the EBA strategy. In performing an independent evaluation a decision-maker is, in effect, executing a series of processes that include, moving to an attribute value, reading an attribute value, retrieving a threshold value from memory, and comparing the threshold to the attribute value.

Elimination statements indicate that an alternative has been explicitly dropped from consideration prior to a complete evaluation of an alternative. This occurs when an alternative is eliminated after it has been found deficient on some attribute. For example, “The rent is too high so I am going to drop Apartment C.” Elimination statements require the decision-maker to store in memory, or track, the fact that the alternative has been eliminated. This operation is characteristic of EBA processing.

Compensatory statements involve the aggregation and/or trade-off of two or more attributes for a single alternative. For example, a statement such as “Rent per square foot is good for Apartment A,” “I am willing to accept the noise level of Apartment M because of its cleanliness,” or “When I add up all these factors it is clear that D is the best choice” are all considered compensatory statements. They are indicative of additive strategies, where a high value on some attribute may offset low values on others. These statements are indicative of additive processing and the AC strategy.

Total statements are indicative of the overall amount of information processed in completing the task. They should be higher when additive strategies, such as AC, are being used than when elimination strategies, such as EBA, are employed, since for the latter some alternatives are usually eliminated prior to all information about the alternative being evaluated.

## Choice Task

Subjects performed a consumer choice task, choosing an electronic organizer. Electronic organizers were chosen as an incentive based on a survey of the potential subject population where they were asked to rank a list of products they would like to receive the most. At the time the study was conducted few students possessed such organizers, early versions of devices such as the Palm Pilot widely available today.

The choice matrix consisted of eight attributes and ten alternatives. The attributes used to describe the organizers were chosen from Consumer Reports (1991). They describe the organizers in terms of attributes such as storage capacity, display quality, ease of learning, portability, backup capabilities, keypad quality, ease of data entry, and ease of information recall. The data used in the choice tasks were based on standardized ratings (on a 1–10 scale) similar to those found in Consumer Reports. The initial set of data was based on information from Consumer Reports and included brands of organizers manufactured by companies such as Fisher, Rolodex, Casio, and the like. The data was converted to 10 point numeric scales and then the values were adjusted so that there were no clearly dominant alternatives. Ratings across attributes were not highly correlated. The participants were not aware of the specific brands of the organizers.

Each subject completed the task four times. The first was a practice problem followed by three experimental choice tasks. Different sets of data were used for each choice task, though in each case the choice data set had ten alternatives and used the same eight attributes (Figure 1). The data sets for the different choice tasks were formed by rearranging the columns and shifting the values in the data set by a constant value. This means that each of the data sets was essentially equivalent, though they appeared different to the participants. For all subjects the first experimental choice task involved making a hypothetical choice from among a set of 10 organizers. The second and third experimental tasks involved making choices that had real outcomes associated with them. The ordering of the real choice tasks was assigned randomly, with half of the participants completing the \$20 choice task first, and the other half completing the \$60 choice task first. Subjects were informed that they would receive one of the organizers they chose in these two tasks, and that it was equally likely they would receive the \$20 or the \$60 organizer.

## Subjects

Subjects were university undergraduate or graduate students in business administration. Participation was strictly voluntary, and each subject was paid \$10 for participating and also received either a \$20 or \$60 electronic organizer at the completion of the study. Forty-two subjects participated, with subjects assigned at random to each of the four DSS support conditions (see Table 3).

## Experimental Procedures

Subjects were run through the study one at a time. Each subject completed three steps: 1) a tutorial, 2) a practice session, and 3) an experimental session, which involved completing the electronic organizer choice task three times with a different set of data each time.

First, a tutorial was provided to explain each of the commands and enable subjects to perform various tasks that required the use of all the commands available to that subject. The tutorial did not make any direct linkages between the commands and choice strategies, but rather guided the decision-maker to use each of the system commands at least once. Subjects did not develop any decision strategy during the tutorials but rather worked through the mechanics of interacting with the DSS. Following the tutorial, subjects went through a practice session and then the three experimental choice tasks. Each choice task dealt with choosing electronic organizers; the attributes of the choice set were the same, but different data were used. While making a choice the subjects were asked to think out loud. The practice problem was intended to give the subjects a chance to familiarize themselves with the task setting, gain further experience with command use, and become comfortable with verbalizing. Instructions were provided to the subjects on how to verbalize, following the recommendations of Russo et al. (1986).

Three experimental trials were performed with different data sets used for each trial representing the hypothetical, \$20 and \$60 choices. The data sets were presented to each subject in a random order. While completing the experimental tasks subjects were told to use as much or as little information as they wished and to use whatever commands, from those available, that they thought were appropriate. Subjects were to treat the problem as if they were making a selection for themselves. A typical session involving completion of the tutorial, practice session and the three experimental choice tasks took approximately 60 minutes. However, no time constraints were imposed or specified to the subjects.

During the entire experiment a lab assistant was present, though out of sight of the subjects. The lab assistant would prompt subjects to “please say what you are thinking” if they were silent for more than 10 seconds while completing the experimental trials. While engaged in the choice process, each subject’s verbalization was audio taped. Subjects were aware that they were being recorded. At the same time the DSS recorded command use unobtrusively. The tape recordings formed the basis for the data reported analyzed previously. After the experiment, the tapes were transcribed and parsed into individual phrases that represented a single thought or action by the decision-maker. The parsed protocols for each choice task were then evaluated by two independent, hypothesis-blind coders who coded the protocols to identify instances of the operators related to each of the dependent variables. The interrater reliability rating, Cohen’s Kappa, for the coding was 0.79. The data used in the analyses were the ones collected during the three experimental tasks.
