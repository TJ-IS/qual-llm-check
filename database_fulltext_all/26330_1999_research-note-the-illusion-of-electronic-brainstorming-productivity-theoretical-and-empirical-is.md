## Theoretical Support for Using EBS With Large Groups

There are at least two reasons why the synergy effect associated with large groups may actually be smaller than what Dennis and Valacich (1999) suggest. First, as social and group psychology research indicates, stimulating creativity and brainstorming productivity is actually very difficult (Amabile 1996). Groups do not seem to respond well to the reinforcement of various process gains. For example, Roy et al. (1996) as well as Connolly et al. (1993) found that groups that shared ideas during brainstorming did not produce more unique ideas than groups that did not share ideas. Consistent with this, our data also indicated that providing additional stimuli to groups in the form of contextual cues (additional ideas they could refer to when generating ideas) did not significantly improve productivity (Pinsonneault et al. 1999). Research in group and social psychology suggests that avoiding or eliminating the process losses that undermine creativity appear to be actually more effective in enhancing group productivity (e.g., the elimination of production blocking and evaluation apprehension inherent to verbal brainstorming by EBS, and the resulting productivity improvement) than reinforcing the process gains (Amabile 1996). Thus, while synergy and cognitive stimulation may increase with group size, the effect of such process gains on productivity is likely to be small.

In addition, as group size increases, other process losses are also likely to increase. For example, research in group and social psychology strongly suggests that free riding (i.e., the motivated, intentional withdrawal of efforts by some group members) increases with group size (Albanese and Van Fleet 1985, Diehl and Stroebe 1987). Further, cognitive interference, (Straus 1996) which occurs when one is so entranced by other group members’ ideas that one’s idea generation is inhibited, is also likely to increase with group size: the larger the pool of ideas, the more likely it will contain entrancing ideas. Moreover, three additional process losses Pinsonneault et al. (1999) identified might also increase with group size: (i) the larger the pool of ideas one can access, the more likely one will become overly interested in viewing others’ ideas at the expense of entering new ideas themselves (distraction process loss); (ii) the larger the pool of ideas, the more likely group members, in trying not to replicate the ideas already generated in a session, become focused on others’ ideas, which may inhibit their ability to come up with new ideas (striving for originality process loss); and (iii) the larger the pool of ideas, the more likely group members will frequently change lines of thought (cognitive dispersion process loss), making it more difficult

To summarize, although large groups can be expected to benefit from small process gains due to synergy, they are also likely to suffer from increased process losses due to at least the five effects described above. Theoretically, then, EBS’ superiority to nominal brainstorming is not established.

## Empirical Support for Using EBS With Large Groups

According to Dennis and Valacich’s classification of past empirical research that compared EBS to nominal brainstorming, six out of eight experiments conducted with small groups (i.e., less than or equal to eight members) found no productivity differences between EBS and nominal brainstorming while two found nominal brainstorming to outperform EBS.<sup>1</sup> For large groups (i.e., more than eight members), all four experiments conducted found EBS groups to be more productive than nominal groups.<sup>2</sup> Based on this, Dennis and Valacich (1999) conclude that, for small groups, any perceptions of productivity gains associated with EBS are likely to be an illusion. We agree. They also consider the empirical evidence for large groups to be compelling and showing EBS to offer clear performance benefits over nominal brainstorming. We disagree. First, there exist only four experiments that compared EBS to nominal brainstorming for large groups. Second, these four experiments came from two studies (three of them from a single study). Third, three of these experiments used pooled nominal brainstorming rather than “traditional” nominal brainstorming, which also limits the generalizability of their findings.<sup>3</sup>

Without additional replication studies conducted with traditional nominal groups of nine or more, the empirical evidence cannot be said to be compelling.

## The Illusion in EBS Research

The results of a literature search of empirical EBS research published in the last five years are summarized in Figure 1. As can be seen, 11 out of 13 studies compared groups using EBS to other groups using EBS. In addition, eight out of ten studies that examined brainstorming in small groups did not investigate nominal brainstorming. As for large groups, there are no studies that compared EBS to nominal brainstorming in the last five years. Such a pattern suggests that researchers may be under the impression that there is no need to compare EBS to nominal brainstorming anymore because it is clearly superior. However, as the theoretical and empirical evidence suggests, EBS is not superior to nominal brainstorming for small groups, and its superiority for large groups is yet to be established. As such, we urge researchers to include nominal brainstorming as a baseline in future EBS research.
