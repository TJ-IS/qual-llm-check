# Assessing User Competence: Conceptualization and Measurement

rganizations today face great pressure to maximize the benefits from their investments in information technology (IT). They are challenged not just to use IT, but to use it as effectively as possible. Understanding how to assess the competence of users is critical in maximizing the effectiveness of IT use. Yet the user competence construct is largely absent from prominent technology acceptance and fit models, poorly conceptualized, and inconsistently measured. We begin by presenting a conceptual model of the assessment of user competence to organize and clarify the diverse literature regarding what user competence means and the problems of assessment. As an illustrative study, we then report the findings from an experiment involving 66 participants. The experiment was conducted to compare empirically two methods (paper and pencil tests versus self-report questionnaire), across two different types of software, or domains of knowledge (word processing versus spreadsheet packages), and two different conceptualizations of competence (software knowledge versus self-efficacy). The analysis shows statistical significance in all three main effects. How user competence is measured, what is measured, what measurement context is employed: all influence the measurement outcome. Furthermore, significant interaction effects indicate that different combinations of measurement methods, conceptualization, and knowledge domains produce different results. The concept of frame of reference, and its anchoring effect on subjects’ responses, explains a number of these findings. The study demonstrates the need for clarity in both defining what type of competence is being assessed and in drawing conclusions regarding competence, based upon the types of measures used. Since the results suggest that definition and measurement of the user competence construct can change the ability score being captured, the existing information system (IS) models of usage must contain the concept of an ability rating. We conclude by discussing how user competence can be incorporated into the Task-Technology Fit model, as well as additional theoretical and practical implications of our research.

## Introduction

As companies continue to downsize and restructure and as they seek to manage their intellectual capital (Stewart 1997), emphasis on productivity is increased (Rosenthal 1990). Hopper (1990) argues that, in the future, successful organizations will be those which learn to maximize the advantages through employing information technology (IT) most effectively, as opposed to those who simply move most quickly to adopt new technology. By the late 1990s, a high proportion of information workers have adopted and are using information technology in their jobs; nonetheless, they may not be using it well (Brynjolfsson 1996). In today’s competitive environment, however, it is clear that maximizing IT’s potential presumes not only that the technology be adopted and used, but that it be used well.

In this environment, an increased emphasis on individual achievement and productivity in the workplace is a major concern (Stewart 1997, Rosenthal 1990). Employee competence is seen by Kanter (1997) as a primary issue, inasmuch as it is competence which enables employees to translate ideas into highly valued products in the marketplace. Competence with IT is especially important because of its effect on workplace productivity (Bowen 1986, Higginbothan 1997, Little 1997, Magnet 1994).

Past studies on individuals and information technology have focused on the adoption and use of IT by end users (e.g., Compeau and Higgins 1995a, Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989, Mathieson 1991, Moore and Benbasat 1991, Thompson et al. 1991). Users’ competence is not, however, part of popular technology acceptance models (TAM: Davis 1989, Theory of Planned Behaviour: Taylor and Todd 1995, IT Impact: Delone and McLean 1992), nor the Task-Technology Fit model (Goodhue and Thompson 1995). Moreover, there is no clear conceptualization, in the literature, of individual competence. User competence (UC) can be defined as the user’s potential to apply technology to its fullest possible extent so as to maximize performance of specific job tasks.<sup>1</sup> Competence is thus an antecedent to performance. UC has been measured via various methods ranging from typologies (e.g., Rockart and Flannery 1983; Rivard and Huff 1988) to paper-andpencil tests that measure certain types of software or hardware knowledge (e.g., Winter et al. 1992) to selfreports of ability (e.g., Blili et al. 1998, Harrison and Rainer 1992, Munro et al. 1997, Nelson and Cheney 1987, Rainer and Harrison 1993). These approaches are not always comparable: different approaches have resulted in different aspects of competence being assessed with little consideration given to how they interrelate.

Fundamental to the problem is that these different approaches are referred to as being the same, even though it may be widely acknowledged—at a basic level—that they differ. The resulting confusion makes comparison of studies difficult. It is also difficult to build a cumulative body of knowledge from which we can draw generalizations. For example, both Nelson (1991) and Evans and Simkin (1989) talk about “computer proficiency,” yet their measures differ dramatically. Nelson used a self-report measure focusing on six dimensions of knowledge (knowledge domains) proposed by Zmud (1983). Evans and Simkin (1989) used both paper-and-pencil and hands-on measures of capability with respect to programming. Only the programming knowledge was considered. These two measures of computer proficiency are clearly not the same.

Similarly, both Gutek and Winter (1992) and Tsay and Solomon (1988) assessed “computer literacy.” Gutek and Winter used both self-report (e.g., “Do you know what RAM is?”) and multiple choice tests of hardware knowledge, while Tsay and Solomon examined broad categories of hardware, software, and foundation knowledge. For example, Tsay and Solomon asked one question in order to assess the desired level of working knowledge about each of these areas: (1) EDP technology, (2) database concepts and techniques, and (3) general systems concepts. Again, these measures are not equivalent.

We do not suggest that it is inappropriate to use different approaches to assess competence; different approaches will always be necessary to fulfill different practical and/or research objectives. What is critical, however, is clarity in defining—specifically—the type of competence (in terms of conceptualization, measurement, and domain) being assessed and in drawing conclusions regarding competence based upon the types of measures used.

The purpose of this paper is to synthesize alternative approaches to the assessment of UC. First, we present a framework in which competence measures are categorized in terms of their conceptualization, measurement method, and the knowledge domains in question. Within the framework, existing studies that have examined competence are reviewed. This is followed by a report on an empirical study that examines the relationship between two different conceptualizations and two measurement approaches of competence with two software packages. Implications of the findings are then discussed. We also discuss how competence should be incorporated into IT usage models and provide other practical advice for researchers and trainers. We conclude with a discussion of future research questions and conclusions.

## User Competence Framework

A broad review of the literature about user competence included a number of disciplines: information systems, education, psychology, organizational behavior, and training. We observed three factors differentiating the measures of competence: conceptualizations of competence, measurement methods, and knowledge domains (Figure 1). Below, we discuss the literature pertaining to each of the three factors in Figure 1.

## Conceptualizations of Competence

Kraiger et al. (1993) identify three different outcomes associated with learning: cognitive outcomes, skillbased outcomes, and affective outcomes. These outcomes, in a sense, represent different conceptualizations of an individual’s competence and can be used to understand differences in the effectiveness with which people use technology. The three outcomes are explained below and followed by a review of the literature regarding each.

Cognitive outcomes refer to the knowledge users have about what a technology is and how to use it. This is referred to by Kraiger et al. (1993) and others (e.g., Anderson 1980) as declarative knowledge.

Skill-based outcomes are associated with the move from verbal knowledge to compilation and automaticity (Kraiger et al. 1993).<sup>2</sup> During these phases of knowledge acquisition, learners develop their ability to generalize procedures to novel tasks, and to speed up performance by moving beyond the step-by-step processes first learned and into more fluid and efficient processes. For example, those learning word processing might proceed from the knowledge that applying bold formatting to text can be accomplished by highlighting the text and then selecting “bold” from a menu or toolbar, and that applying underline is accomplished in the same way, to the recognition that most character formatting is applied in this fashion. This process of generalization makes it easier to store the commands in memory and makes performance more fluid (Kraiger et al. 1993). According to Kraiger et al., skill-based outcomes might include observations of performance to see whether users have moved beyond mechanical performance of tasks to the more automatic performance associated with highly skilled individuals.

The final category of outcomes reflecting learning are affective outcomes. While Anderson (1980) and others focus on the cognitive and skill-based outcomes,

Kraiger et al. (1993) also include attitude and motivation (including self-efficacy) as outcomes of learning. They argue that if a learner’s “values have undergone some change . . . then learning has occurred” (p. 319). It should be noted that the term affective, as used in this model, is much broader than the term as used in the information systems literature. The IS literature focuses on affective as a component of attitude (Thompson et al. 1991), while the learning literature uses the word as a higher-level term which encompasses all of the components of attitude considered by IS (cognitive, affective, intentional), as well as other motivation constructs.

Among the studies of user competence reviewed, all of these conceptualizations are represented. Cognitive assessments are the most common. Researchers refer to these measures as ability or skill (Cheney and Nelson 1988, Nelson 1991, Nelson and Cheney 1987), computer literacy (Gutek and Winter 1992, Hurt 1990), expertise or proficiency (Evans and Simkin 1989, Schroeder and Kletke 1990), user competence (Munro et al. 1997), end user computing competence (Blili et al. 1998), or end user computing activities (Rainer and Harrison 1993).

Skill-based assessments are less common in the literature on general end user capabilities (Evans and Simkin (1989) is an exception), but are commonly found in the training literature (e.g., Compeau and Higgins 1995b, Olfman and Bostrom 1990, Webster and Martocchio 1993). Related measures are also found in literature pertaining to expert systems and data base systems. Lamberti and Wallace (1990) measured proficiency in computer systems diagnostics using expert systems; Suh and Jenkins (1992) assessed user performance with a database in terms of data retrieval, query correctness, and writing time.

Affective assessments, specifically assessments of computer self-efficacy, were also dealt with in the literature. Self-efficacy (Bandura 1986) represents an individual’s perception of his or her ability to successfully execute some specific task, in this case, using a computer. In general, IS research has considered selfefficacy to be either an antecedent to or an outcome of competence (e.g., Compeau and Higgins 1995a, 1995b; Davis et al. 1989; Marcolin et al. 1992). Others, however, have used a computer self-efficacy instrument to measure computer skill (e.g., Harrison and Rainer 1992, Rainer and Harrison 1993).

## Measurement Methods

In this section we review the various types of measurement methods found in the literature. These measurement types include self reports, hands on tests, paper-and-pencil tests, and observer assessments.

Self-report measures are provided by individuals assessing their own abilities. Hands-on tests refer to specific problem-based evaluations completed by the individual interacting with the tool(s). Our definition of paper-and-pencil tests includes tests administered using computer-based testing programs as well as those using actual paper and pencils. The distinction we propose has to do with the type of question asked in each. Paper-and-pencil tests ask questions about what can be done and what procedures could be used, while hands-on tests focus on actual accomplishment of tasks. Observer assessments involve ratings of skill by independent observers (often the researchers), based on a variety of methods including interviews and behavior observation.

Our review of existing measures indicates that selfreports are most common (Blili et al. 1998, Cheney and Nelson 1988, Harrison and Rainer 1992, Nelson and Cheney 1987, Nelson 1991, Rainer and Harrison 1993, Schroeder and Kletke 1990, Winter et al. 1992). Handson approaches to testing were found in Compeau and Higgins (1995b), Evans and Simkin (1989), Lamberti and Wallace (1990), Olfman and Bostrom (1990), and Suh and Jenkins (1992). Paper-and-pencil tests (including multiple-choice and open-ended questions) were also located in the literature (e.g., Evans and Simkin 1989, Gutek and Winter 1992). These types of testing are, interestingly, more common in organizational use than in research. Most of the company-specific training programs we reviewed made use of this type of test (e.g., Bushnell 1990, Symons 1987, Buszowski 1989). Commercial packages to facilitate this type of testing have been developed (e.g., Skills Evaluation System 1990). One reason for the popularity of this type of testing is the ease of administering and scoring (especially for multiple-choice tests). Compared to handson tests, which require extensive observation and coding of responses, structured paper-and-pencil tests are much easier to score. Observer assessments are common in the early literature on user competence. Of these, Rockart and Flannery’s (1983) work is perhaps best known. Other examples include Hurt (1990), Panko (1988), Cotterman and Kumar (1989), and Miriani and King (1994).

## Knowledge Domains

Knowledge domains refer to the context of a problem, including specific software, hardware, and task. Competence in a wide variety of domains is assessed in the literature. These domains include software applications (Blili et al. 1998, Hurt 1990, Nelson and Cheney 1987), hardware and related technologies (Hurt 1990, Rosenthal 1990, Klauk 1988, Swope and Mahometa 1987, Industrial Management 1986), programming (Evans & Simkin 1989), and foundation concepts (Carr 1990; De Young and Perlman 1985; Hayen et al. 1990; Hurt 1990, Karten 1987, 1988, 1991; Kraemer and Northrop 1989; Rosenthal 1990). Some studies (e.g., Schroeder and Kletke 1990) have included domains related to the task area (e.g., statistical analysis) as well as those related to computers.

## Framework Cell Combinations

The three dimensions of user competence (conceptualization, measurement method, and knowledge domain) provide a rich variety of contexts in which to explore the construct. While certain combinations are more common, all are possible. Conceptualizations based on cognitive processes most often employ selfreports or paper-and-pencil tests, although many of the early end-user typologies were observer assessment (Rockart and Flannery 1983, Hurt 1990, Panko 1988, Cotterman and Kumar 1989) and hands-on methods have also been used (Gutek and Winter 1992, Winter et al. 1992, Evans and Simkin 1989). Self-report cognitive assessments are often used because the measure is aligned with the authors’ model or is just easy to administer. Skill-based conceptualizations of competence are most often hands-on (Lamberti and Wallace 1990, Suh and Jenkins 1992) or paper-andpencil tests. A paper-and-pencil test, in this instance, would involve individuals explaining what strategy they would follow to solve a technology-related task, as opposed to an actual demonstration. Trainers argue that the hands-on test is the only way to truly capture individual competence, citing that it is the only method which parallels the use situation (Olfman and Bostrom 1990, Compeau and Higgins 1995b). Others note that alternative methods are adequate for achieving their theoretical and research objectives and are therefore appropriate (Evans and Simkin 1989, Bushnell 1990, Symons 1987, Buszowski 1989).

Measuring affective conceptualizations of competence through different methods of measurement is also possible. Self-reports are, of course, the most common method for tapping affective assessments of competence, since only the individual can report on how he or she feels. Indirect assessments of affect can also be made, using observer assessment. For example, Webster (1989) assessed computer playfulness by observing facial expressions and body language. An assessment of self-efficacy could be made by an observer noting physical and verbal cues displayed during performance of a task. These cues could include such things as tenseness, facial expressions, body language, time spent thinking, cries for help, and mannerisms.

Paper-and-pencil and hands-on assessments of affective competence are somewhat problematic. Depending on how one thinks about paper-and-pencil and hands-on tests, it may or may not be possible to construct them for affective competence assessment. Certainly, one cannot construct a paper-and-pencil test that would be scored as right/wrong to determine, for example, self-efficacy. One can, however, construct a self-efficacy measure that asks the respondent to rate their ability to complete a paper-and-pencil test or a hands-on test. Such a measure is still, in some senses, a self-report, but it is different than the types of selfreport measures that we call self-report (those which focus on the ability to complete a job task).

We believe it is useful, then, to distinguish “methods” for affective measures based on the specific object of the measure. If the measure focuses on a general perception, similar to those that are found in selfreports of cognitive and skill-based types of competence, then we would classify it as a self-report. If, however, the measure focuses on a perception specific to a paper-and-pencil test, we would call it a paper-andpencil measure. Similarly, if the measure focuses on a perception specific to completing a hands-on test, we would consider it to be a hands-on measure for our purposes.

With respect to the knowledge domains, our review of the literature found that different knowledge domains are examined across the range of conceptualizations and measurement methods. Domains tend to be broader for self-report measures (e.g., Nelson 1991) and observer assessments $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Rockart and Flannery 1983) than for paper-and-pencil or hands-on tests.

## Summary

The user competence framework described in the preceding sections represents a comprehensive tool for categorizing existing measures of user competence. It shows that competence can be conceptualized in multiple ways (specifically cognitive, skill-based, and affective conceptualizations). It also demonstrates how the different measurement methods can be applied to the conceptualizations and how the measures can be extended across multiple knowledge domains.

Our contention is that while previous literature has discussed user competence without regard to the three dimensions, differences in each of the factors will influence the results of the research and the conclusions drawn. Thus, we must now begin to consider these dimensions as we attempt to build a cumulative tradition.

To illustrate the effects of conceptualization, measurement methods, and knowledge domains, the following section presents an experiment which compares two specific conceptualizations measured through two different measurement methods across two knowledge domains (i.e., software packages).

## Research Design and Experimental Procedures

## Research Model

The shaded areas in Figure 1 show the specific research model that was pursued in this study. Our choice of dimensions to examine within each differentiating factor of the UC framework was guided by theoretical and pragmatic considerations. In general, we attempted to focus on the dimensions most reported in the literature, while ensuring that comparable measures could be created for each such dimension.

For the conceptualizations of competence factor, we chose to focus on cognitive and affective assessments of competence. Cognitive assessments are, as noted earlier, the dominant approach in existing literature. An affective assessment (specifically self-efficacy) was interesting for theoretical reasons, specifically to assess the closeness of the relationship between this and other conceptualizations of competence. Skill-based assessment of competence was not used since (a) it is the most difficult to employ and (b) it is highly dependent on task domain skill in addition to computer skill. Mackay and Elam (1992) found that both task expertise and software expertise influenced the way in which a spreadsheet was used in solving a problem. Individuals with comparable levels of software expertise but different levels of task expertise behaved very differently in their use of a spreadsheet. Since our goal was to compare competence across multiple software domains (discussed next) which have different associated task types, a skill-based measure that confounds task domain knowledge and software knowledge would be inappropriate.

For the knowledge domain factor, we chose two representative software packages: word processors and spreadsheets. These represent two of the most commonly used packages on the market, adding to the practical relevance of the study. Moreover, their interface characteristics are different (the spreadsheet was primarily menu-driven while the word processor, at the time, was function-key driven). Consideration was given to including up to ten different packages, but the complexity in research design would have compromised our ability to interpret the results, and thus this was an unacceptable choice.

For the measurement methods factor, we chose to focus on self-report and paper-and-pencil (specifically, multiple-choice) tests. Our decision with respect to measurement method followed closely our decision regarding conceptualization. Since cognitive assessments of competence are often made using paper-andpencil tests, and hands-on testing is typically reserved for skill-based assessments of competence, a paperand-pencil test was considered appropriate here. The questions were randomly chosen at the intermediate level of difficulty, and focused on syntax. A multiplechoice format was deemed appropriate for this assessment. While one can argue, and we would agree, that syntax knowledge such as we tested here does not equate to ability to use the software in task performance, some degree of syntax knowledge is necessary to operate within the environment. Imagine, for example, trying to navigate a help system with no understanding of the underlying syntax of the software being used. Without knowledge of the key terms and functions it is difficult—if not impossible—to work effectively.

In summary, our model compared a cognitive assessment of software knowledge and an affective assessment (self-efficacy). These conceptualizations were compared across a self-report measure (a questionnaire) and a paper-and-pencil test (a multiple-choice test) for two software packages (word processing and spreadsheets).

## Research Design

Sixty-six subjects completed multiple measures of competence, in a controlled setting. A split-plot factorial ANOVA design was employed and included five treatments for package, method, conceptualization, test-taking order, and SE Specificity (Kirk 1982). The first three treatments (package, method, and conceptualization) involved repeated measures and were within subjects. The remaining two treatments (order and SE specificity) were between subjects factors with subjects nested within one of the combinations. In other words, subjects adhered to the same test-taking order and SE specificity across both software packages. For each of two software packages, each subject completed a questionnaire measure of software knowledge, a questionnaire measure of self-efficacy, a multiple choice test of software knowledge, and a comparable test of self-efficacy.<sup>3</sup> To control for presentation order effects, half of the participants completed the questionnaire first and the other half completed the test first; this treatment was evaluated in the analysis. An additional factor was introduced to control for the specificity of questions in the test of self-efficacy (whether the subjects completed the test with or without the multiple-choice answers). The reasons for this factor will be discussed under Measures.

The data were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance, providing strong statistical evidence regarding the extent to which each of the different measurement approaches (conceptualizations and measures) led to higher and lower ratings of competence. (Appendix A gives a more detailed description of this split-plot factorial ANOVA design.)

This research design was preferred as it facilitates comparisons between knowledge about how to use a specific software package (in the case of this study, both WordPerfect- and Lotus 1-2-3) and self-efficacy with respect to the same software packages, and it also facilitates comparisons between a self-report questionnaire and a paper-and-pencil test.

## Experimental Procedures

Prior to the subjects’ arrival at the testing site, we randomly assigned participants to one of the two order conditions (questionnaire first or test first) and flipped a coin to determine whether the individuals would receive the WordPerfect or Lotus tests first. On arrival, subjects participating in the study were given a brief description of the study’s purpose. That is, we instructed the subjects that we wanted to understand people’s familiarity with information technology. Details of the procedures were also explained, and the subjects were asked to sign the consent form.

At this point, either the questionnaire or the first test was given, depending on the individual’s treatment condition. Subjects completing the questionnaire first were told that it would take approximately 20 minutes and were left to complete it. After the questionnaire, these subjects completed the test conditions. They were asked to complete the self-efficacy assessment for the first software package by viewing the flash cards with individual questions on them. Then they completed the multiple choice questions for that software package. The process was repeated for the second software package.

Subjects completing the test first followed the reverse procedure. They completed the flash-card selfefficacy assessment for the first software package, then the multiple choice tests. They repeated this for the second package, and then were given the questionnaire to complete. At the conclusion of both conditions, the subjects were thanked for their time and paid ten dollars for their participation.

## Subjects

The 66 subjects participating in the study were solicited through advertisements in a university community and consisted of administrators and students from all areas of the community. The criteria for inclusion in the study were that the individuals must be users of both WordPerfect and Lotus 1-2-3, and that these were the packages within the software domain with which he or she was most familiar. No specific level of expertise beyond this familiarity was required.

The subjects’ ages averaged 29 years (std. dev.  6.7). Fifty-six percent were women. They were divided between students (62%) and current members of the labour force (38%). Of those currently employed, 15% held managerial positions, 15% held professional positions, 30% occupied technical positions, 25% held clerical positions, and 15% held other positions which most often indicated self-employment. Seventy-eight percent had obtained at least a university undergraduate degree or college diploma. The remainder had some college or university (11%) or high school (11%). The majority of the students also had several years of work experience.

Seventy-one percent of the participants used computers on a daily basis; 23% used computers on a weekly basis. On average, the subjects in this study used computers for 2 hours and 45 minutes per day (std. dev.  77 minutes).

## Measures

Measures were created for the main constructs of interest, as follows. The questionnaire measure of knowledge was Munro et al.’s (1997) User Competence Depth dimension that assesses how well the respondent knows a particular package. The questionnaire measure of self-efficacy was the eight-item scale used by Compeau and Higgins (1995b) that assesses one’s confidence in completing a job task using a particular software (spreadsheet or word processing) package under specific conditions. The test measure of knowledge was a ten-item multiple-choice test randomly constructed from a commercial test database. In the test measure of self-efficacy, the respondents were shown flash cards of each of the multiple choice items from the ability measure for approximately five seconds. They were asked to indicate whether they felt they could correctly answer the question, and, if so, how confident they were that they could. A similar approach to measuring self-efficacy was used by Schunk (1981) in a study of mathematics self-efficacy. The time was limited to five seconds so that subjects would have time to read the question and determine if they could answer it, but not enough time to actually answer the question.<sup>4</sup>

As noted in the Research Design overview, two forms of this self-efficacy instrument were used in the study. In the first form (the detailed form), the questions were shown to the subjects with the answer categories given. In the general form, the questions were shown to the subjects in a paraphrased form, without the answer categories given. Half of the respondents completed the general form, and half completed the detailed form (assignment of form was determined randomly). Bandura (1986) argued for the use of detailed, highly specific measures of self-efficacy, but in this study we were concerned that providing the multiple-choice answers might bias the results. Although subjects were advised not to answer the question but simply to gauge whether they could, we had no way of controlling (other than time) whether they actually followed this direction. In the specific form, with answer categories given, the respondents might have been able to determine an answer. If so, their selfefficacy scores for the questions would have been either zero (cannot do it) or ten (already figured it out). Providing both the general and specific forms and assessing any differences would address issues of bias. Details of these instruments are shown in Appendix B.

Parallelism of Measures. Comparisons between the measures require them to be parallel, that is, assessing the constructs within the same domain. Review of the measures indicates that, even though the wordings are slightly different, responses are parallel. The test measures of knowledge and self-efficacy deal directly with the software packages used in the experiment in that the syntax questions were specific to either WordPerfect or Lotus 1-2-3. The questionnaire measures of competence asked the respondents to describe their depth of knowledge with respect to the word processing or spreadsheet package that they know best. Since this could have resulted in responses being given for a different software package, it was necessary to ensure that the word processing or spreadsheet which respondents knew best was WordPerfect or Lotus 1-2-3. All of the respondents listed WordPerfect and Lotus 1-2-3 as the word processor and spreadsheet that they knew best. The self-efficacy measures are also constructed generically around word processors or spreadsheets.<sup>5</sup> Again, since the subjects in the study knew WordPerfect and Lotus best, we can assume that the level of abstraction was parallel for these measures, as well.

Nunnally (1978) argues that at least 30 items are needed to ensure high reliability in tests of achievement. Given that our measure included only ten items for each package, the low reliability is not entirely surprising. The important question, however, is the extent to which the lack of reliability of these instruments makes further investigation of their relationship to other assessments of competence problematic.

Lack of reliability in this type of instrument can indicate several things. First, it could indicate a high degree of random error, which would be problematic. Nunnally also suggests (p. 274) that low reliability may indicate that the pool of items used in the test capture different facets of knowledge within the area. Inspection of our items shows this to be the case. The WordPerfect instrument, for example, includes questions dealing with use of the help facility, using graphics, creating macros, and using tables. These are indeed different facets of performance, and it is possible for an individual user to have different levels of skill in different areas. For example, some users may have a great deal of skill using graphics, but relatively less knowledge of using macros within the package, depending on the nature of the work they do. While a truly expert user would be expected to know all of the functions, intermediate level users could easily be more focused in one area than another yet still be considered intermediate users.

Given this situation, we do not believe that the low reliability of the multiple-choice tests seriously compromises the results. The ten items chosen for each package reflect a broad range of software functions, and may thus be considered multidimensional. In spite of this multidimensionality with respect to functions, the overall score still reflects the relative depth of knowledge that the individual possesses with respect to the software. We also considered the possibility of dropping some items from the scale to improve reliability, but since—ultimately—the source of unreliability is probably too few items, this did not seem appropriate: reliability could not be increased by removing any single item from the scale and removal of all items except those pertaining to a specific dimension would have resulted in too narrow a measure.

## Results

The univariate-F values for the analysis are shown in Table 2.<sup>6</sup> Neither of the main effects for the two between-subjects factors (ORDER and SE SPECIFIC-ITY) was significant. Moreover, none of the interaction effects involving SE SPECIFICITY was significant. Subjects’ responses to the self-efficacy items measured using the test questions were not influenced by whether or not they could see the entire question, including answers. Thus, this factor is not considered further.

All three main effects for the within-subjects factors were significant at $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Subjects typically scored higher on both self-efficacy and knowledge measures, with respect to word processing, than they did on these measures for spreadsheets, regardless of how they were assessed (PACKAGE effect). Questionnaire measures of both knowledge and self-efficacy resulted in higher scores than did the test measures (METHOD effect). Self-efficacy scores also tended to be higher than knowledge scores across both packages and methods (CONCEPT effect).

The PACKAGE  METHOD interaction $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ was also significant. Post hoc examination of the means across these cells indicates that the word-processing questionnaire combination was different from all other combinations $( \mathtt { p } \ \le \ 0 . 0 1 )$ . Subjects rated their selfefficacy and knowledge for word processing on the questionnaire measure higher than on the test measure and higher than on the spreadsheet domain using either method. None of the other cells differed significantly. Thus, the combination of word processing and a questionnaire measure seems to produce unique results.

The significant METHOD  ORDER effect $( \mathtt { p } \le$ 0.015) indicates that subjects who completed the test first produced lower scores on the questionnaire measures, suggesting that the test performance served as an anchoring stimulus that resulted in more accurate estimates of knowledge and self-efficacy on the questionnaire measure.

The three-way interaction of PACKAGE, METHOD, and CONCEPT was also significant $( \mathtt { p } \le 0 . 0 0 6 )$ . The means for this interaction are shown in Table 3 and represented graphically in Figure 2. This result provides further information about the complex nature of individuals’ self-efficacy and knowledge and the implications of different measurement methods and knowledge domains.

The top half of Figure 2 shows that word-processing self-efficacy is consistently higher than spreadsheet self-efficacy $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . It also shows that the questionnaire measure produces higher self-efficacy scores than the test measure for both packages $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The same result does not hold true, however, for knowledge measures (the lower half of Figure 2). The questionnaire measure of knowledge is significantly higher for word processing than for spreadsheets, but the test measures across these packages are not significantly different. Thus, while subjects rated their word processing knowledge higher than their spreadsheet knowledge, their actual word processing test performance was not significantly higher.

## Discussion

The results clearly indicate that assessment of user competence is influenced by all three of the factors presented in the research framework. Moreover, these dimensions of competence assessment interact, to produce results that are unique combinations of the conceptualization measured (software knowledge versus self-efficacy), the measurement method (self-report questionnaire versus paper-and-pencil test), and the knowledge domain studied (word processing versus spreadsheets).

## Conceptualizations

The most obvious differences in the results were that self-efficacy and knowledge do indeed differ. One’s confidence in one’s ability with respect to computing is, to some extent, independent of one’s actual knowledge. This finding is consistent with Bandura’s (1986) writings on self-efficacy, which argue that perceptions of self-efficacy can be over- or understated with respect to actual knowledge. He also argues that there exists a reciprocal, causal relationship between self-efficacy and ability, such that, over time, self-efficacy influences the development of ability and that performance on specific tests of ability (such as our multiple-choice test) can serve as sources of information used in the formation of self-efficacy judgments.

## Measurement Methods

The results of this study also provide interesting insights into the impact of measurement methods on scores of knowledge and self-efficacy. Questionnaire measures produced significantly higher scores than test measures under the circumstances studied here, except for spreadsheet knowledge (where questionnaire knowledge ratings are in line with test performance). Thus, questionnaire measures of knowledge, for some types of packages, can be expected to result in higher estimates of competence. While one could argue that the test difficulty may impact this overestimation, it is important to remember that we chose a midlevel of difficulty for our test. Moreover, the subjects represented a broad range of competence. The observed relationship is, thus, not an artifact of the study.

## Package (Knowledge Domain)

The effects regarding package observed in this study suggest interesting implications for interpreting and planning research on user competence. First, the inconsistent relationships of self-efficacy and knowledge (measured either by questionnaire or test) across the domains of spreadsheets and word processing suggest that generalizability of findings across knowledge domains may be problematic.

It is possible that the differences observed here result from picking knowledge domains that are extremely different from one another, relative to other domains. Had we included other software types, such as graphics or databases, the differences would not—perhaps— appear as marked (assuming all the other domains fell in between word processing and spreadsheets, in terms of complexity). However, given the observed differences between the two domains studied here, it is clear that further study is needed into the similarities and differences across a much broader range of knowledge domains.

## Interactions

Among the most interesting outcomes of this study were the interactions which suggest that different combinations of method, concept, and package produce unique scores. The two-way interaction of package by method suggests the word-processing/questionnaire combination produces the most vastly different scores, reinforcing the importance of considering the factors in the research framework when assessing user competence. Word-processing and questionnaire combinations produced much higher, and significantly different, scores than the other three combinations. Consequently, research conducted using word processing may be particularly susceptible to problems of generalizability.

A theoretical issue emerged in the analysis from the interpretation of the remaining interactions. These interactions (discussed below) pointed to anchoring or framing as a possible influence on an individual’s assessment of his or her competence.

First, the method by order effect, which showed that giving subjects the test first resulted in a better matching of questionnaire and test self-efficacy scores, suggests that recent experience can serve as an anchor to questionnaire responses.

Second, the three-way interaction showed that spreadsheet scores were better aligned between questionnaire and test than word-processing scores. When we discussed this finding with experts at the Canadian Skills Development Centre they suggested that wordprocessing and spreadsheet packages provide different anchoring cues to users. Word-processing packages (specifically WordPerfect) enable people to become highly skilled in a small range of tasks without knowing the broad range of possible functions. Thus, the respondents in this study could have easily perceived very high knowledge on the questionnaire if they feel they are very good at the few things they know. On the other hand, spreadsheet packages (specifically Lotus 1-2-3) present the user with the broad range of functions on a prominent menu. This visual cue informs users of the software’s functionality so that they are more aware of the range of options. If the users know a narrow range of tasks, they will be more able to gauge their level of knowledge with respect to other users since the range of functions is presented to them with each task. This interface thus formulates a more complete frame of reference.

Both of these interactions suggest environmental factors that might serve to anchor or frame responses to competence measures. Users’ frames of reference, or their exposure to a number of end-user tools, might help them gauge their capabilities in relation to other users, thus making some people more accurate in their self-assessments than other people.

References to anchoring or framing within the IS literature are scant and buried within many other ideas. For instance, frame of reference is similar to the experience construct, which has been found to be a powerful influence on the use of technology (e.g., Thompson et al. 1994). This finding suggests one of the ways in which the literature on use of technology (i.e., the adoption literature) overlaps with research on the effective use of technology.

The notion that frame of reference serves as an anchor to self-report assessments of competence is more prevalent in literature in psychology. This literature addresses how frame-of-reference training impacts performance rating accuracy, which is similar to the accuracy of self-assessments as discussed in this study. Different measures of performance-rating accuracy have been shown to improve when frame-of-reference training is employed (e.g., Cardy and Keefe 1994, Day and Sulsky 1995, Stamoulis and Hauenstein 1993, Woehr 1994). Performance rating gets better when the rater’s frame of reference, or common conceptualizations across workers, is tuned to a common set of worker behaviours (McIntyre et al. 1984).

Speculation about how this “frame-of-reference” characteristic translates to the more graphical software interfaces results in several considerations. In general, many graphical software interfaces have more cues to their full-range of functionality through the icon task bars of the standard software window. This is true of software that previously had command-based versions, such as the WordPerfect version used here. The icons are now constantly visible, whereas this functionality had been previously hidden. These taskbars can be customized either at setup or by the user. Such editing may, in fact, serve to narrow or broaden the visual cues to the full-range of functionality, changing the frame of reference for the user. Graphical interfaces may, therefore, raise the general frame of reference threshold (for software functionality) but are still likely to exhibit differences which could have similar influences suggested here.

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

A logical extension of the framework and the empirical data is a discussion of theoretical implications. Results suggest that because the methods of defining, measuring, and framing the UC construct change the ability score being captured, existing IS models of usage must, therefore, contain the concept of an ability rating. Users come into contact with technology in the usage environment, and, consequently, the IS models of use are most relevant here. The Task-Technology Fit model (Goodhue and Thompson 1995) is most theoretically consistent with the idea of user competence, given the seeds of the construct already present within the model. Other models, such as the Theory of Planned Behaviour (subsuming TAM) (Taylor and Todd 1995, Davis 1989) and the IT Impacts model (Delone and McLean 1992), are also applicable, but discussion of these is beyond the scope of this paper.

Below, we introduce the TTF model, fit UC into that model, and discuss UC’s theoretical role and contribution. Through this theoretical discussion, we hope to inspire an ongoing discussion of the issues. Perhaps others will generate further ideas which could produce additional future directions.

The Task-Technology Fit model (Goodhue and Thompson 1995) proposes that a fit between the task, technology, and individual characteristics determines an individual’s performance in the situation. Although individual characteristics are included in their theoretical conceptualization of the model, these characteristics are not pursued in the subsequent empirical work and are left for others to study. UC naturally picks up where this part of the TTF model left off, fitting in as shown with the dotted lines.

UC mediates between other individual characteristics and usage or task-technology fit. It is the natural mechanism for this mediation, as Kanter (1997) explains. She argues that competence is the capability through which employees translate knowledge into advantage. Because of this emphasis on “working through,” UC must be a mediator when taken into the theoretical model. However, determining which factors are more or less strongly mediated by UC is a long debate, one which cannot be fully discussed here. It is clear that individual characteristics, such as social participation, greatly increase one’s competence through direct translation: the more one learns from a social network, the more one’s competence increases. It can be concluded that as a mediator UC would be important by the mere fact that individual characteristics exert their influence through this capability.

In addition to the role as a mediator within the TTF model, UC as it relates to user fit has a theoretical role as a measure. Goodhue and Thompson (1995) acknowledge that the individual is important in the tasktechnology fit assessment (p. 218) but only go as far as operationalizing task-technology fit as the individual’s perceptions of the task and the technology. This operationalization does not account for the fact that it might be the individual’s abilities, and not how the task is defined or how the technology is built, which limits the outcomes. Performance might depend just as much on the user’s abilities. Thus, it might also be proposed that the measurement of the task-technology-fit construct be expanded to a three-way assessment, with direct questions for each of task, technology, and individual fit. Currently, there are only direct questions for task and technology, subsuming the influences of individual fit. This existing operationalization restricts the types of research questions that can be addressed. Therefore, adding the measurement of user fit is a logical extension of this construct and has implications for future research questions and research designs that can be pursued.

It is important to note that a theoretical distinction should be made between UC and self-efficacy. Since the cognitive and affective conceptualizations of competence have been shown to operate independently in our results, these conceptualizations should be cast separately within this model. In other words, the temptation to consider UC as self-efficacy should be resisted even though one is capable of conceptualizing a context within this model where that might appear appropriate.

From a practical perspective, the inclusion of UC within the TTF model allows us to address several previously unanswered questions. One could conceive of questions for different job types, which begin to address the age-old problem that the impacts of technology are different for different types of organizational workers. Clerical and professional workers use wordprocessing tools differently, as an example. Some general skills, such as typing ability and knowledge of the formatting options, would improve the performance of both workers, but professional workers would need to use word processing to collect ideas, to organize thoughts, to distribute ideas among colleagues, etc. The first question that springs to mind is, “When are these nonclerical type of word processing skills an asset to a professional worker?” “When are too many clerical skills a detriment?” is suggested by previous findings that some workers waste time fidgeting with nonessential computer tasks, such as formatting. A draft policy document does not have to be completely formatted by a professional worker before being distributed for idea generation. What are the essential word-processing skills for these different types of workers?

Similar examples of research questions can be created for other dimensions in the framework and put together in interesting combinations of the conceptualizations, measurement methods, and contextual differences. When are each of the different measurement methods most appropriate? When are the different conceptualizations of competence (cognitive and affective) most appropriate? When does an individual’s competence interfere with job performance to a point where no amount of task or technology design can fix the problem? How do these and other contextual factors change how we train the most competent workers? The empirical data from studies investigating these types of questions build the knowledge for a cumulative tradition. Theories help define the options under each dimension so investigations are undertaken that make sense for the times.

UC is thus introduced into the TTF model as a mediating factor that can be studied with different measurement methods and contexts in many useful ways. The multitude of the choices that could be made between measurement and context is staggering and highlights the complexity of studying questions surrounding UC. Theoretically, options abound. Researchers, by their selection of conceptualization, methods, and domains, will determine the paths and the questions they choose to investigate.

## Limitations of the Study

Five limitations to these findings should be noted. First, subjects who participated in the research were volunteers, and this may have impacted on the distribution of knowledge and self-efficacy scores, though we took several precautions to produce a good range of ability and to reduce sampling bias.

The second limitation of this study, as discussed earlier, is the low reliability of the multiple test measures of knowledge. Since the items were drawn randomly from a well-designed, commercially available test of knowledge and since the small number of items accounts for at least part of the low reliability, we do not believe the results of the study are seriously compromised. Future research, however, should focus on improving the reliability of test measures by using larger numbers of test items and engaging in more complete pretesting to ensure reliability.

A third limitation is evident in the nonorthogonality of the factors in the research design. Focusing on the comparison between conceptualizations of competence and measurement methods, we have—in essence—a 2  2 comparison. From a classical experimental perspective, these factors should be orthogonal; however, the nature of the competence construct makes this impossible. There are three areas of overlap: (a) the self-report of knowledge, because it is a selfreport, is somewhat confounded by self-efficacy; (b) the distinction between the self-report and the paperand-pencil test for both knowledge and self-efficacy is partly one of measure specificity, since the self-report is more general and the paper-and-pencil test is more specific; and (c) both the self-report and paper-andpencil assessments of self-efficacy require the respondent to provide a self assessment. These three areas of overlap, while problematic in a classical experimental sense, do not change the central message of the paper. With respect to overlap (a), the results suggest that, in spite of the overlap, there is a clear distinction between knowledge and self-efficacy measured by self-report. Respondents’ scores for self-efficacy and knowledge were significantly different across both software packages, which would not be the case if they were truly confounded. Overlap (b) is a confound, but one that is typical in the literature on competence. Most selfreports are quite general, while most paper-and-pencil tests are quite specific. Moreover, the competence literature does not recognize this confound since dissimilar constructs are often called the same thing, and the patterns of significance found in the results apply equally to a confounded factor as they would to a “pure” factor. Overlap (c) deals with the objectivity of the paper-and-pencil test of self-efficacy. As we have previously noted, we do not consider objectivity to be the defining difference between self-report and paperand-pencil measures for self-efficacy. Furthermore, the knowledge and self-efficacy measure are aligned in terms of what is being measured. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the labels are confusing when applied to affective conceptualizations of competence.

A fourth limitation, sequence effects, was introduced by the design. Randomization was used to remove sequence effects on the software tests, and the order of measures (questionnaire first or test first) was evaluated in the analysis. One sequence effect remained and could not be eliminated. For the paperand-pencil test measures, self-efficacy was presented before knowledge. The questionnaire presented the knowledge measures before the self-efficacy measures. However the lack of significant interactions involving order and concept make it unlikely that this was a problem.

The final limitation of the empirical study is the relatively narrow scope of measures relative to the conceptual framework. Within each of the three factors of the conceptual framework there are numerous different dimensions. As a first step, we chose to focus on only two different dimensions within each factor. The generalizability of our results is limited by this choice, and future research is needed on each of the dimensions of the conceptual framework. Additional research focusing on the specific impacts of different framing or anchoring experiences would also help confirm and expand our initial findings.

## Future Research

Several compelling questions remain for future research. First and foremost, the question to ask is, which conceptualizations of competence relate best to task performance and how? Does this differ between job classifications, such as clerical and managerial? It seems likely that skill-based capabilities, typified by fast, skillful execution of tasks, would play a greater role for clerical staff than for managerial staff who might benefit from higher affective levels of competence (e.g., competence in uncovering appropriate technological solutions to organizational problems might be better assessed by high self-efficacy than by concrete task performance).

How, then, can we design training programs to cultivate the most appropriate components of competence? When are self-study, computer-assisted learning, practice exercises, various formal courses, journals, colleagues, and known experts, to name a few, most helpful for attaining desired competence? How can we, as information systems researchers, help managers and companies build their IT-related intellectual capital (Stewart 1997)?

Under what circumstances do affective competency outcomes play a greater role than cognitive or skillbased competencies? More specifically, does selfefficacy get a user through the rough spots when he or she does not have the immediate knowledge to solve a problem? More generally, what is the effect of too much or too little competence or confidence on jobrelated task completion?

To improve upon the current study, broader multitrait/multimethod studies could be conducted using all of the conceptualizations and methods within the framework. Such evidence would provide even more compelling proof of which combinations of competence conceptualizations, methods, and domains are best used together. The current study has shown that certain combinations perform differently than others, but further study is required to extend this knowledge.

## Conclusions

In an effort to draw together and add value to the highly differentiated approaches to examining user competence, this paper has presented a user competence framework. The framework identifies the three factors—conceptualizations of competence, measurement methods, and knowledge domains—associated with user competence. The framework can be used to generate hypotheses for further research and provides a theoretical context into which new discoveries may be placed. In particular, the framework serves as a means for standardizing terminology and developing commonality of purpose in this research area.

Our empirical study has shown that user competence is a multifaceted phenomenon that is differently assessed by different measurement methods. Our findings show that different methods of measuring competence (questionnaire and test) and different conceptualizations of competence (knowledge and self-efficacy) are distinctly different, and that they relate differently across knowledge domains (word processing and spreadsheets). As research moves from the study of use (i.e., IT adoption and use) to the study of effective use, researchers must be cognizant of how their conceptualization and measurement choices influence their findings and, thus, the conclusions they can draw. Through this study, we have demonstrated these differences within a controlled empirical study and presented a framework to bring greater conceptual clarity to this issue.

## Appendix A. Split-Plot Factorial Designs

The split-plot factorial (SPF) design combines the features of both completely crossed factorial designs and randomized block designs. Because certain treatments cannot be administered to the same subject, such as completing a sequence of the same tasks in different orders, groups of subjects must be nested within these treatments and the analysis of variance technique adjusted for the violation to its basic assumption of the independence of error terms (Kirk 1982).

Split-plot improves upon the randomized block design in that it requires smaller block sizes and achieves greater statistical power for the within-subjects factors. However, analyses of the betweensubjects factors of a split-plot design are always less powerful than the corresponding tests in a randomized or completely crossed design. Consequently, split-plot is the best choice when greater precision and power are desired for the within-subjects factors and the between-subjects factors can be compromised.

Five treatments were investigated in the study here as shown in Figure A1. Two between-subjects factors of order (A) and selfefficacy specificity (B), and three within-subjects repeated measures factors of package (C), method (D), and conceptualization (E) were employed. Each treatment had only two levels resulting in a SPF-22.222 design. Package (C) varied between WordPerfect 5.1 for word processing and Lotus 1-2-3 v3.1 for spreadsheets. Data collection method (D) employed both a questionnaire format and a paper-and pencil test. Conceptualization (E) was compared for the two constructs called cognitive knowledge and self-efficacy. The levels of order (A) involved taking the questionnaire before the paper-andpencil test, or vice versa. Self-efficacy specificity (B) applied only to the test measure of self-efficacy and involved two levels, a detailed measure that had multiple-choice answers and a general measure that deleted the answers and paraphrased the questions. Thus, the subjects completed both levels of the within-subjects factors C, D, and E, but could only complete one level of the between-subjects factors A and B. The four blocks of subjects contained 17, 14, 19, and 16 people for a total of 66. Power calculations indicated that this sample size was adequate for our analyses.

One additional procedure was added to our design, to account for the fact that the order of completing the tests for each software package (e.g., Lotus 1-2-3 first or WordPerfect first) might influence our results. Since we did not wish to include order of completing tasks for each software package in our design, order of taking the two software tests was randomized, with 50% doing the Word-Perfect test first and 50% doing the Lotus 1-2-3 test first. Note that the software test order is different from the between-subjects factor of questionnaire/multiple-choice test order. The latter concern is evaluated by the design, and the former sequence concern is removed by random assignment.

This design contains 39 factors: 5 main effects, 26 interaction terms, and 8 error terms. Table 2 in the paper contains a list of 25 factors for our SPF-ab.cde design. The self-efficacy specificity factors were not included because none were significant.

The between-subjects factors are completely confounded in this design because the variance associated with the treatment cannot be separated from that due to the group of subjects nested within it. Group-treatment confounding, as this situation is called, does not affect the interpretation of treatment effects but only lessens the statistical precision, and hence power, of the statistical calculations for the confounded factors only.

## Appendix B. Scale Construction

Questionnaire Measure of Knowledge (Cognitive Assessment of Competence). Munro et al.’s (1997) End User Sophistication questionnaire scale for the depth dimension yielded a self-report knowledge score that was based on an assessment of how well (on a scale from one to seven) respondents knew the particular package with which they were most familiar. These scores were converted to percentages for comparison with other measures in the study.

Questionnaire Measure of Self-Efficacy. Compeau and Higgins’s (1995a, 1995b) scale consists of eight items which ask the respondent to consider whether he or she could complete a job task using the particular software (spreadsheet or word processing) under specific conditions. The eight items each present different levels of support which respondents might have (e.g., “if someone was there to give them step-by-step instructions” or “if they had just the online help facility to rely on”). In accordance with Bandura’s (1986) recommendations, respondents are asked both whether they believe they could complete the task under that condition (YES or NO) and if YES, how confident they are in that judgment (on a scale from one to ten). The result is a score for each question that can range from zero (could not complete the task) to ten (could complete the task and is totally confident that he or she could complete the task under the given condition). The scores were averaged across the eight items and converted to a percentage to facilitate comparison with the other measures.

Test Measure of Knowledge. A multiple choice measure of knowledge was constructed for each package by sampling items from a commercial test database, provided by the Canadian Skills Development Centre (Skills Evaluation System 1990). This database consists of hundreds of items to measure knowledge for each software package, rated according to function covered and difficulty. Intermediate difficulty items were chosen for this study, since all of the subjects were at least somewhat familiar with both packages. All functions of the software were covered. Ten items were randomly selected from the database for each of WordPerfect and Lotus 1-2-3. The number of items was determined in consultation with experts at the Canadian Skills Development Centre, based on their experience administering the test to over 7000 employees in different organizations over a four-year period.

Test Measure of Self-Efficacy. In the test measure of selfefficacy, the respondents were shown each of the multiple-choice items from the ability measure on flashcards for approximately five seconds and were asked to indicate whether they felt they could correctly answer the question, and if so how confident they were that they could do so. The time was limited to five seconds so that subjects would have time to read the question and determine if they could answer it, but not enough time to actually answer the question (determined through pretesting). Each of the ten items yielded a score from zero to ten (consistent with the self-efficacy questionnaire). These scores were summed and converted to a percentage to give an overall score of self-efficacy for the particular package.

As noted in the methodology overview, two forms of this selfefficacy instrument were used in the study. In the first form (the detailed form) the questions were shown to the subjects with the answer categories given. In the general form, the questions were shown to the subjects in a paraphrased form, and without the answer categories given. Half of the respondents completed the general form, and half completed the detailed form (the assignment of form was determined randomly). Bandura (1986) argued for the use of detailed, highly specific measures of self-efficacy, but in this study we were concerned that providing the multiple choice answers might bias the results. Providing both forms and assessing any differences would address this effect.
