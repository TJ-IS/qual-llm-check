# Opening the “Black Box”of Network Externalities in Network Adoption

Robert J. Kauffman • James McAndrews • Yu-Ming Wang

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455 rkauffman@csom.umn.edu

Department of Research, Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 33 Liberty Street, New York, New York 10045 jamie.mcandrews@ny.frb.org

R <sup>ecent</sup> <sup>theoretical</sup> <sup>work</sup> <sup>suggests</sup> <sup>that</sup> <sup>network</sup> <sup>externalities</sup> <sup>are</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>determinant</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>network</sup> adoption. However, few empirical studies have reported the impact of network externalities on the adoption of networks. As a result, little is known about the extent to which network externalities may influence network adoption and diffusion. Using electronic banking as a context and an econometric technique called hazard modeling, this research examines empirically the impact of network externalities and other influences that combine to determine network membership. The results support the network externalities hypothesis. We find that banks in markets that can generate a larger effective network size and a higher level of externalities tend to adopt early, while the size of a bank’s own branch network (a proxy for the opportunity cost of adoption) decreases the probability of early adoption.

## Introduction

With the advent of modern telecommunications capabilities, networks are rapidly becoming a competitive necessity in a wide variety of industries. Networks are different from other technological innovations in that they are subject to network externalities (Economides 1996, Shapiro and Varian 1999). The concept of “positive” network consumption externalities was first recognized in the context of telecommunications networks. There the value of a network to a subscriber increases with the number of its adopters (Oren and Smith 1981, Rohlfs 1974). Thus, the network also becomes more attractive to nonsubscribers as the network grows.

Prior research suggests that network goods, products or services that generate network externalities, have characteristics not shared by many other traditional technological innovations. Many technological innovations and standards, such as computer architectures, operating systems, software suites, middleware solutions, and telecommunications network standards, share the characteristics of physical networks: New adopters confer additional benefits to the existing adopters. Such network externalities have been shown to have strategic implications for technology adoption (e.g., Farrell and Saloner 1985, 1986; Katz and Shapiro 1985, 1986a, 1986b; Shapiro and Varian 1999). However, the literature is largely theoretical, with little rigorous empirical validation of the theoretical model. There has been little empirical research done on the determinants of network adoption, and few empirical studies have reported the impact of network externalities on network adoption. Thus, our objective in this paper is to assess the impact of network externalities and other influences on network adoption decision making and diffusion. This is an important undertaking: Today, many of the most interesting information technologies (including the emerging technologies of electronic commerce, for example) are expected to exhibit various kinds of network effects, and senior managers and academic researchers alike would like to know how best to maximize their business value.

Economic theory on networks and technology standards argues that differences in firms’ ex post adoption network size should generate different ex ante valuations of network adoption. The general idea is that firms expecting a larger network and higher level of network externalities will adopt earlier, assuming all other aspects of the environment for decision making are held equal. Using adoption data for a large regionally shared electronic banking network, we test this hypothesis by estimating a hazard model, which is more appropriate and powerful in studying technology adoption and diffusion. We find that banks in markets that can generate a larger network size and a higher level of externalities tend to adopt early, while the size of a bank’s own network decreases the probability of early adoption because of higher opportunity cost.

We next review relevant literature, focusing primarily on the theory of networks and technology standards. We also discuss other aspects of network externalities in the decision-making context we explore, and we examine some of the context-related variables that are believed to influence the perceived value of network adoption, and thus adoption timing. We then present our formal hypotheses that relate to network externality theory, and the research method and data that allow us to test the hypotheses. We next lay out and discuss our empirical estimation results, and then conclude with the contributions, implications, and limitations of this research.

## Background Literature

The economic approach to studying technology adoption and diffusion views diffusion primarily in terms of cost and benefit. The economics literature posits, ceteris paribus, that the time a firm waits to adopt an innovation is inversely related to the profitability associated with the adoption. The most widely tested economic theory of the diffusion of innovation was advanced by Mansfield (1968), who argued that the diffusion of an innovation throughout an industry follows a logarithmic curve, which is a function of the relative profitability of the innovation and the initial investment required. He also argued that diffusion is more rapid in more competitive industries.

Recent economic research on technology adoption has generated a number of theoretical models of optimizing behavior for technological innovations that involve networks or standards. Farrell and Saloner (1986), for example, presented models that yield a number of useful conclusions. Because of economies of scale and positive externalities, network goods have a greater tendency toward monopoly (or greater concentration) than do services that do not generate externalities. In addition, the strength of the network externalities created as a by-product of an existing installed base may lead to a bandwagon effect and result in choices of inferior network technologies. Finally, they showed that the existence of network externalities has strategic implications for a variety of important economic activities, including technology adoption, predatory pricing, and product preannouncements.

The work of Katz and Shapiro (1986a) and Shapiro and Varian (1999) is also of interest here. The authors argued that because of scale economies and network externalities, the dynamics of network goods are fundamentally different from those of conventional innovations. In the presence of network externalities, the total benefit derived from the network depends in part on the number of consumers who adopt compatible products in the future. Thus, a consumer in the market today also cares about the future success of the products. This line of theoretical research provides us with useful guidance. First, technologies subject to network externalities have unique characteristics and traditional explanations of innovation diffusion (e.g., Brancheau and Wetherbe 1990, Mahajan et al. 1990, Rogers 1983) do not accommodate those properties. Second, network externalities have economic value and strategic impacts on the structure of markets, industries, and organizations.

Recent research in information systems (IS) has recognized the importance of network externalities as an important source of business value. As a result, there are a number of newer analytical models that provide important implications for the adoption and diffusion of technologies that involve network externalities (e.g., Chismar and Meier 1992, Clemons and Kleindorfer 1992, Conner 1995, Nault and Dexter 1993, Riggins et al. 1994, Wang and Seidmann 1995). These build on the theoretical networks literature in economics, and each incorporates network externality theory to some extent. Taken together, they reflect the extent to which a theory of existing “installed base” has achieved prominence as a basis for understanding what we have seen develop in the marketplace.

To date, however, there has been little systematic empirical research focusing on network externalities, and almost none on network adoption. Gandal (1994) estimated hedonic price regression models for spreadsheet software using data on price and features of a set of spreadsheet packages. He found that consumers were willing to pay a significant premium for spreadsheet packages that are compatible with the Lotus platform, which was interpreted as evidence of network externalities in the computer spreadsheet market. Brynjolfsson and Kemerer (1996) also estimated a hedonic regression model, using a new data set that includes data on the installed bases of various spreadsheet packages. They found results that are broadly consistent with Gandal’s regarding the value of compatibility with the Lotus platform. In addition, they found a positive relationship between a spreadsheet’s installed base and its price. Another empirical study by Economides and Himmelberg (1995) examines the fax market in the United States. The authors illustrated that the speed of adoption grows in the presence of network externalities, based on an estimation of consumers’ valuation of the installed base, using aggregate time-series data on prices and quantities in the domestic fax market.

There has been other research outside the IS literature that examines electronic banking technology adoption, albeit without explicitly considering the network externality issues that we address in the context of network sharing. For example, Hannan and Mc-Dowell (1984) used data on the adoption of automated teller machines (ATMs) by banks to examine the relationship between the decision to adopt new technology and the determinants of that decision. They found support for their hypothesis that larger banks and banks operating in more concentrated local banking markets were more likely to adopt ATM technology earlier. Saloner and Shepard (1995) examined the impact of bank size in terms of the value of deposits and the number of branches on the timing of ATM adoption among banks. They argued that there are additional benefits of ATM adoption because the value of a proprietary ATM network increases in the number of locations it serves, in addition to the number of its users. They proxied the proprietary network benefit to a bank in adopting ATMs by the number of its branches and found that the larger the number, the earlier the adoption. They also found that a bank’s date of adoption is earlier the larger the value of its deposits base, a proxy for the number of depositors. These findings are consistent with the results presented by Hannan and Mc-Dowell (1984) as well.

In addition, Dos Santos and Peffers (1993, 1995) have examined some related issues. Their 1995 study, using data from Federal Reserve Bank periodic reports, examined the extent to which investments in electronic banking technologies affect the performance of banking firms. The results are consistent with our interpretation that larger electronic banking networks tend to confer significant benefits upon their owners, in part because of the value placed upon installed base by retail banking customers, and that earlier adopters tend to benefit more than later adopters.

With the exception of Dos Santos and Peffers’ work, the other studies primarily modeled banking competition at a time when there was little sharing of ATMs. One reflection of the timeframe of the other authors data was their focus on the benefits of ATM adoption and the tendency to adopt proprietary network technologies—not network externalities that might accrue from interfirm sharing of technology. Thus, we recognize that even with a network effect, in this earlier timeframe each bank’s own proprietary network benefits tended to be independent of other banks’ actions (up to the point that others began to share). So it was reasonable for earlier work to have assumed that the firms’ network adoption decisions were self-contained, and that no network externalities arose from sharing. The world we will model, however, is one in which the dominant organizational form for electronic banking networks is the shared network. In this context, a bank must evaluate the expected adoption behavior of other banks and the future size of the shared network. This, then, ought to be the source of a shared network effect.

## Theoretical Basis and Research Hypotheses

We next discuss variables influencing the perceived business value of a shared electronic banking network and present a series of hypotheses that are aimed at linking observed decision making in the marketplace with the theory that explains it. In this discussion, we will place significant emphasis on the “typical” installed base model of networks growth and adoption from the economics literature. We will argue that differences in banks’ ex post adoption network size should generate different ex ante valuations by their depositors. Thus, the value of shared network adoption ought to be higher for banks expecting to have a larger shared network. Further, banks expecting to build a larger network and higher level of network externalities will adopt share networks earlier, assuming all other aspects of the decision making environment are held equal.

## Theoretical Basis

The theoretical literature on networks and technology standards suggests that the value of a network increases in the number of locations it serves, typically represented by

$$
a + b (N),\tag{1}
$$

where a is the “stand-alone” benefit an adopting firm derives from the technology, N denotes the size of the network, and b(N) represents the “compatibility” benefit derived from other firms’ conformance to the same standard—in other words, a generalized network effect. Figure 1 depicts the value of a network, which includes two separate sources of value. (See also Farrell and Saloner 1986 for an illustration of the “typical” model.)

In the context of proprietary ATM networks, Saloner and Shepard (1995) also modeled the value of a bank’s network to its depositors as ab(n), where a represents the stand-alone or network-independent benefit from proprietary ATM technology, and b(n) represents the proprietary network effect that is increasing with n, the size of the proprietary ATM network in terms of ATM locations.<sup>1</sup> As such, the value of ATM technology to a bank and thus the probability of ATM adoption increases with the size of the proprietary ATM network expected by the bank. Figure 2 shows the related conceptual model for a proprietary network, P.

In the financial services industry, shared electronic banking networks generate significant network externalities, and thus we expect to be able to observe and test for shared network effects, in contrast to Saloner and Shepard’s proprietary network effects. As new member banks join a network and bring in ATMs, depositors of all existing member banks value more highly their ATM cards because of more locational convenience and enhanced account access. In addition, participation of new member banks brings to the network more card holders who may access their accounts using the ATMs of existing members. This increases the potential for some deployers of ATMs to earn interchange revenues, when ATMs are used by other banks’ customers wishing to access their accounts. As a result, the participation of other banks confers externalities to existing network participating banks, and banks value the shared network more highly as it grows; late entrants at some point in time may find that they must join the network to avoid competitive disadvantage.

The perceived business value of a shared ATM network to bank i can be modeled as

$$
a _ {i} + b (N _ {i} ^ {S}),\tag{2}
$$

where $a _ { i }$ represents the network independent benefit for bank i and represents the separable and posi- <sup>S</sup> b(N )<sub>i</sub> tive network externalities for the shared network, S. $b ( N _ { i } ^ { S } )$ monotonically increases with $N _ { i } ^ { S } ,$ , which represents the effective installed base of the shared network that bank i expects in terms of the number of shared ATM locations from which the bank’s customers are able to access their accounts. Figure 3 shows the related conceptual model for the shared network.

## Research Hypotheses

In the context of shared networks, therefore, a potential adopting bank must evaluate the expected adoption behavior of other banks, and the adoption decision is influenced by the expected size of the shared network. Banks with a similar expectation, however, may value the shared network differently because situation-specific characteristics (e.g., firm and market attributes) also tend to influence the perceived business value of the network.

Now, by adopting a shared network, banks can offer their customers more locational convenience, and thus enhance service coverage and obtain additional benefits through interchange revenues when other banks’ customers’ ATM transactions are switched for a fee. Thus, the larger the shared network installed base perceived by a bank, the more valuable that network will be to the bank. Consistent with this assertion, one expects that firms that value the network more on an ex ante basis to adopt earlier. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Banks in markets that are expected to generate a larger network installed base adopt earlier.

Hypothesis 1 employs the concept of effective network size in the local markets in which banks operate. It recognizes the importance of network growth expected by potential adopters in their adoption decisions. In other words, our proxies for the theoretical variable rely on an implicit hypothesis that network externalities are both present and measurable at the local market level. Because differences in banks’ post-adoption effective network size may generate different ex post valuations by their depositors, the value of adopting a shared network ought to be higher for banks in markets that are expected to have a larger network installed base in equilibrium. (Post-adoption effective network size may differ depending on the local markets in which the banks operate, and we assume, without loss of generality, that it will be known to all potential members in the same banking market.) Hence, we proxy a bank’s perceived level of network externalities by the expected network installed base in the market in which the bank operates.

Banks in markets where an ATM location has to serve more depositors on average (a lower level of service coverage) face a stronger demand for better ATM services. The retail banking industry’s time-line shows that most retail banks enhanced customer service by joining shared networks; they either didn’t add costly new branches or they cut back existing branch operations. In addition, the retail banking industry recognized the new opportunity for charging transaction switching fees. Many banks use the number of card holders served per branch office as an “operationalized measure” of network service coverage. We expect that banks in markets with a lower level of service coverage will value more highly the shared network and thus adopt early. Relating this assertion to the typical installed base model in Expression (2) above, banks in a market with a lower level of service coverage will have a greater value for $a _ { i }$ than banks in markets with higher levels of service coverage, all else equal.

Hypothesis 2. Banks in markets that have more card holders served per branch office adopt earlier.

Note that Hypothesis 2 is an alternative to the hypothesis that the number of network card holders served per network ATM location has a positive impact on the probability of adoption. The reason for having this alternative is that we cannot observe ATM placement in the local markets. But we can observe branch placement from publicly available data. Moreover, we expect there to be significant correlation between the number of ATMs and the number of branches. A similar interpretation is offered by Saloner and Shepard (1995).

For banks operating in the same local market, however, heterogeneity in their characteristics may influence the timing of adoption. The number of proprietary ATMs deployed by a bank may contribute to the opportunity costs of shared network adoption. There are three elements in opportunity cost. First, because banks that have deployed proprietary networks prior to shared network membership already enjoyed internally generated network benefits, there is an opportunity cost of shared network adoption. Second, there may be revenue losses, should the bank decide to stay out of the shared network, as its customers switch to a network-providing bank. The opportunity cost is increasing with the size of the proprietary network, so banks with a smaller ATM network will have lower opportunity costs; while banks that have deployed ATMs in a large number of locations will have higher opportunity costs, especially in early stages of shared network growth. (There are also adoption costs for member banks, such as one-time membership fees, a monthly fee per ATM online with the network, and implementation costs. However, the opportunity costs are far more significant than the adoption costs in this context, according to our field study interview participants.) In other words, banks that have deployed a larger number of ATMs have a smaller value for $a _ { i }$ in Expression (2) than others with a smaller ATM network, all else equal. Finally, it is also possible that banks with existing proprietary networks will need to expend additional costs to shift to new business processes that relate to their retail banking services, migrating away from in-house servicing in which the bank can exercise full operational control. Thus, we expect the number of a bank’s ATM locations to have a negative impact on early shared network adoption and banks with a small ATM network are likely to adopt earlier.<sup>2</sup>

Hypothesis 3. Banks that have a larger number of ATM locations adopt later.

By offering its depositors more locational convenience, a bank should be able to obtain some benefits or avoid some disadvantages in terms of market share or revenue. Banks facing a stronger demand for ATM services (i.e., more depositors demanding ATM services) will find shared network membership more valuable. In other words, the perceived business value of shared network adoption for a bank should be increasing in the number of its depositors. As such, banks with a larger depositor base will have a greater value for $a _ { i }$ in Expression (2) than others with a smaller cardholder base, all else equal. Because the greater the perceived value of the network by a bank, the more likely the bank will adopt earlier, we have the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. Banks with more depositors adopt earlier.

To summarize, we have built on the theoretical literature on networks in economics, in general, and on the “typical” installed base model, to model the business value of shared network adoption for a retail bank. Managerial decision makers at bank i assess a two-part function composed of network-independent benefits, $a _ { i } ,$ and positive network externalities, $b ( N _ { i } ^ { S } )$ . We anticipate that banks operating in markets that are expected to generate a larger network-installed base and have more cardholders served per branch office, banks that have a smaller number of ATM locations, and banks with a larger cardholder base will adopt earlier.

Control Variables. To consider the relative strength of other potential theoretical explanations for shared network adoption, we include in our empirical test three additional variables: wage rate, market structure, and firm ownership. Controlling for their effects enables us to make contacts with prior studies (especially Hannan and McDowell 1984, 1987; Saloner and Shepard 1995), which have shown that wage rate, market structure, and bank ownership all tend to influence ATM adoption.

Related findings offer additional impetus for our control for market concentration. For example, Mansfield (1968) and Romeo (1975) reported evidence supporting the hypothesis that the more competitive the market, the greater the rate of innovation diffusion. Kamien and Schwartz (1982) reviewed the empirical evidence and noted that diffusion does indeed tend to be faster in nonconcentrated industries—diffusion should be faster the fewer firms there are in the region and the greater the similarities among firm sizes. Market structure and other industry characteristics have been hypothesized to influence innovation adoption and diffusion by many other studies, however, the results are inconclusive. Hannan and McDowell (1984) found that banks operating in concentrated markets are likely to adopt ATM technology earlier; thus it is likely that this finding will carry over to the adoption of shared networks.

Hannan and McDowell (1984) also found support for their hypothesis that the higher the prevailing wage rate in the market in which the bank operates the more attractive the adoption of ATM to the bank. The findings by Saloner and Shepard (1995) also suggest that, consistent with the incentive for substituting laborsaving ATMs for tellers, a higher wage rate contributes positively to early adoption. Thus, we expect market wage rate to have a positive impact on early adoption.

We follow Hannan and McDowell (1984), who also used bank ownership as a control variable in their research. The authors found that banks that were members of bank holding company organizations tended to adopt ATM technology earlier. They cited reduced risk exposure in the aggregate business portfolio on the part of larger, more diversified bank holding company organizations as a possible explanation. In our shared network context, the ownership of a bank may or may not lead to earlier adoption. Thus, we did not have a prediction for the direction of the bank ownership effect when we specified the empirical model for this research.

## Method: The Hazard Model

We next discuss the fundamentals of the hazard model and develop an empirical model for testing the hypotheses and examining the impact of network externalities on network adoption.

## The Basics of the Hazard Model

Because our data set includes adoption data indicating the time that each firm joined the shared electronic banking network, the appropriate statistical model should be able to assess the dependence of adoption time on the explanatory variables in our model. In addition, because our observations of adoption times are right-censored (i.e., some firms had not joined the network during the period covered in the study), the estimation model must accommodate censoring.

Hazard models (also referred to as duration models and failure time models) were developed in the statistics and econometrics literature in an attempt to assess the impact of covariates on the duration of an event. Hazard models can explicitly incorporate covariates in the specification of time or probabilities to adopt, so that the population is heterogeneous in its timing of adoption. Mahajan et al. (1990), in fact, suggest that hazard models overcome the limitations of previous work on technology adoption using cross-sectional models, and are particularly important in studying the innovation diffusion process. Interested readers should refer to Kalbfleisch and Prentice (1980), who offer a thorough discussion of the statistics associated with this approach, and Peterson (1991) and Kiefer (1988), who also provide useful background on the use of hazard models when analyzing event histories.

Hazard models have been used to investigate the impact of explanatory variables on the timing of adoption at the individual and firm levels, primarily in the economics literature. Hannan and McDowell (1984) were the first to employ a hazard model to investigate the impact of covariates, mainly market structure and firm size, on the timing of adoption of ATMs. Levin et al. (1987) and Rose and Joskow (1990) applied similar techniques to study technology adoption. These studies have explored the impact of market structure and firm size on the process of technology diffusion. Our study, however, is the first to employ a hazard model to test for a market-wide network externality effect. It also emphasizes the impact of network externalities as a first-order effect that decision makers can recognize as they change, period-to-period, with the growth of the network.

## Constructing the Hazard Model

To gauge the impact of network externalities on network adoption and to test the hypotheses, the general strategy is to construct and estimate a hazard model.

Elapsed Time to Adopt and the Distribution of Failure Times. We assume that the elapsed time to adopt, T, is conditional on the explanatory variables and follows a distribution to be selected by the analyst. The selected distribution should balance the concerns of making contacts with prior literature, faithfully represent the real world, and support the interpretational convenience of the analyst. In our case, T measures the time from the start of observation until the event of interest—the adoption of the shared network—occurs, or the observation period ends.

There are four ways to describe the distribution of observed failure times for an event:

1. The cumulative failure time probability distribution function, F(t)Pr(Tt), specifies the probability that the random variable T, for failure time, is less than some value t.

2. The corresponding probability density function, $f ( t ) = d F ( t ) / d t$

3. The survivor function, $S ( t ) = 1 - F ( t ) = P r ( T \geq t ) .$ , defined as the probability that the firm will not have adopted the network by the end of period t.

4. The hazard function, $h ( t ) = f ( t ) / S ( t )$

Each of the four functions can be used to derive the others, although the hazard function is the popular choice in the economics literature. Specifically, if T is a nonnegative random variable representing the adoption time of an individual banking firm from a population of n independent banking firms, the hazard function, $h _ { i } ( t ) ,$ , specifies the conditional probability that banking firm i will join the shared network at time $t ,$ given that it has not done so by t1.

Distributional Assumptions and the Hazard Function. Because the adoption time conditional on a firm’s observable characteristics is assumed to be a random variable, we consider parametric hazard models, which embody specific assumptions about the distribution of failure time. The Weibull distribution has been widely used in modeling technology diffusion (e.g., Rose and Joskow 1990, Saloner and Shepard 1995) with $h ( t ) = \gamma \alpha t ^ { a - 1 }$ and $S ( t ) = e x p ( - \gamma t ^ { a } ) ( \gamma > 0$ and $\mathbf { \alpha } \mathrm { \Gamma } \mathrm { \simeq } 0 )$ making it the most useful for making contacts with other literature.

The effect of explanatory variables on the hazard function can be incorporated by substituting $\gamma$ with $e ^ { - \beta X i }$ . This is a standard assumption (Saloner and Shepard 1995), resulting in the following hazard function specification:

$$
h _ {i} (t) = \alpha t ^ {\alpha - 1} e ^ {- \beta X _ {i}}.\tag{3}
$$

Here, $X _ { i }$ is a vector of explanatory variables for firm $i ,$ and $\beta$ is a column vector of unknown coefficients to be estimated.

The other functional forms that one sees in the literature are the exponential, the normal, and the logistic distributions. These models also include specific assumptions about the distribution of failure time. The Weibull distribution is a simple generalization of the exponential distribution, which is obtained by setting $\alpha = 1$ . When the exponential distributional is assumed, $h ( t ) = \gamma$ and $S ( t ) = e ^ { - \gamma t }$ . This implies a time invariant underlying hazard, which is inappropriate in our case.

Model Robustness Considerations. The choice of a particular parameterization depends, in part, on empirical considerations (Sinha and Chandrashekaran 1992). Ideally, however, the results ought not to be too sensitive to the specific functional form that is selected. Thus, although we will present the results of a hazard model that uses the Weibull distribution for elapsed time to adopt, we also will consider model robustness concerns. We will present results for hazard models with elapsed time distributions specified according to two other appropriate distributions, i.e., the normal and the logistic distributions.

A Likelihood Function for Adoption. The probability that banking firm i will adopt during a period $T _ { i } ,$ prior to the end of the study period, can be shown to be $S _ { i } ( T _ { i - 1 } ) - S _ { i } ( T _ { i } )$ . Hence, the likelihood of adoption is also a function of the explanatory variables prevailing in each period up to the end of the study period or the time of adoption, whichever comes first. The estimation procedure will yield parameter estimates for $\beta$ that maximize a likelihood function composed of the above probabilities. The likelihood function is expressed as follows:

$$
L = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {1}} [ S _ {i} (T _ {i - 1}) - S _ {i} (T _ {i}) ] \prod_ {j = 1} ^ {n _ {2}} [ S _ {j} (T ^ {*}) ].\tag{4}
$$

In this expression, $T ^ { * }$ is the end of observation period, and $n _ { 1 }$ and $n _ { 2 }$ denote the number of firms that adopted by $T ^ { * } ,$ , and did not adopt by $T ^ { * } ,$ respectively.

The Empirical Model. For illustration purposes, however, we assume that adoption times are distributed according to a Weibull distribution as expressed in Equation (3). For our electronic banking context, $X _ { i }$ is a vector that includes explanatory variables for network installed base, service coverage, proprietary ATM network size, and depositor base. One of the attractive features of the Weibull distribution is that the computation of the effects of the covariates on adoption probabilities from parameter estimates is relatively simple. The estimated values of $\beta$ measure the impact of explanatory variables on $h _ { i } ( t ) .$ , as the constant proportional effect on firm i’s conditional probability of adoption, by applying logarithms on both sides of the equation. With the transformation of $\beta ,$ the value of $\bar { 1 0 0 } ^ { * } ( e ^ { - \beta } - 1 )$ can be given a quasi-elasticity interpretation: It indicates the percentage change in the hazard rate for a unit change in the corresponding explanatory variable (Helsen and Schmittlein 1993).

## Empirical Context, Measures, and Data

We now turn to a discussion of some of the details of the electronic banking environment in which our test of the network externalities hypothesis is conducted. We also discuss the operationalization of the variables in our model, as well as the sources and issues that relate to our use of the data.

## Subject and Scope of Study

The subject of our study is the Yankee 24 electronic banking network, operated by New England Network, Inc. The network was organized in August 1983 by nine banks in Connecticut. In July 1984, the network went into operation and began to seek other banking firm members. Because regulatory policies toward shared ATM networks in Connecticut at the time ruled that ATM sharing was mandatory, membership was open to all depository institutions in Connecticut. As a result, all members shared in the ownership of the nonprofit network organization. Potential entrants could thus become actual members by requesting to join Yankee 24, and they could not be refused. Out-ofstate banks, however, were prohibited from establishing or using ATMs within the state of Connecticut at that time. For each interchange transaction, an interchange fee set by Yankee 24 was paid to the owner of an ATM by a network member whenever that member’s cardholders used an ATM.

In 1985, Yankee 24 became the largest shared electronic banking network in New England in terms of number of ATMs shared and interbank ATM transactions. By 1987, Yankee 24 had over 700 ATMs, and a majority of banks in Connecticut had joined Yankee 24. Around that time, banking firms in other New England states were allowed to join the shared network organization, based on prior approval by the network’s board of directors. By 1991, Yankee 24 had more than 700 network members and some 4,000 ATMs accessible to all members’ ATM cardholders.

The founding members of Yankee 24 seemed to have achieved a minimum viable scale that deterred further entry into Connecticut. In addition, recall that out-ofstate banks were not allowed to establish or use ATMs within Connecticut. Thus, for every bank in a local market, there was little uncertainty regarding the ultimate success of Yankee 24. Instead, for many banks, the decision was when to join the state-wide network. When Yankee 24 entered the Massachusetts market in 1987, its largest competitor was BayBanks, particularly in the Boston area. The other competing network was the New York Cash Exchange (NYCE), owned by a consortium of New York City commercial banks. However, it was not until mid-1987 that NYCE began to establish a significant presence outside its home market. Thus, competition from other networks presumably was not an important factor in Connecticut prior to 1987.

For the following reasons, we chose to limit our sample to include only Connecticut-based banks to focus on the variables of interest. First, banking firms in other New England states were allowed to join the shared network organization only after the second quarter of 1987, and a majority of Connecticut-based banking firms had signed up with Yankee 24 by that time. Therefore, the network installed base and the number of network cardholders were large enough so that many banks would obtain positive net benefits from adoption once Yankee 24 opened up to them. (As a result, the variables of interest in this study may not be able to explain the growth of Yankee 24 outside of Connecticut due to differences in state environments.)

Second, because larger banks with more cardholders and ATMs were more valuable to the shared network organization, it is likely that there were member solicitation and coalition formation activities prior to the date Yankee 24 was officially opened to all banking firms in other New England states.

Third, there was no competition in Connecticut in the timeframe of our study, whereas outside the state, several competing networks began to operate (e.g., BayBanks and NYCE). (The presence of those competing networks would require us to model network membership differently.) The sample was further restricted to commercial banks and savings banks for two other reasons: (1) Credit unions are very different organizations from savings and commercial banks;

and (2) savings and loans are not under the supervision of the Federal Reserve Bank, and hence the relevant data were difficult to obtain.

## Definition of Measures

In the hazard model we employ, the elapsed time until adoption, T, is grouped into quarterly intervals for analytical convenience.<sup>3</sup> All banks joining Yankee 24 by the end of the fourth quarter of 1984 are assumed to adopt in Period 1. Every other bank’s adoption time is determined by the elapsed time in quarters until adoption. In studies involving hazard function estimation, the analyst normally must deal with the problem of right-censored data. In the present study, this occurred when a bank did not join Yankee 24 prior to the end of the first quarter of 1987. Following other research conducted by the Federal Reserve Bank, we define a market to be either a Standard Metropolitan Statistical Area (SMSA) or a county that approximates a local banking market. Using this approach, we broke Connecticut into 18 banking markets. Explanatory variables of network adoption (the vector X in Equation (3) above) and the measures used in this study are described below.

NETWORK\_BASE (Expected Network-Installed Base): a weighted average of market bank branches, a proxy for the perceived ultimate size of the network installed base. Although the size of network-installed base is often used to measure the extent of network externalities, there have been few attempts to operationalize network externalities as we do here. In addition, because the researcher cannot observe future network size (in terms of the number of shared ATMs or ATM locations) expected by potential adopting firms, a proxy must be employed. Saloner and Shepard (1995) argued that it is appropriate to use the number of bank branches as a proxy for the unobservable future proprietary network size of firms. We use the same approach for nonproprietary electronic banking networks for three reasons. First, branch offices are known to be the most common, lowest cost locations for ATMs, and they are placed where direct substitution for teller labor is possible. Second, legal restrictions limited off-premise ATM placement during the sample period for Yankee. Third, survey data in the trade press at the time suggested that banks placed most of their ATMs in their branches. So, we use the number of bank branches in the local banking markets in which a bank operates to measure the expected networkinstalled base. For banks that have branch offices covering more than one market, a weighted average is used. Because banks in markets that are expected to have a larger network-installed base are likely to adopt early, we expect that large values of NETWORK\_BASE will have a positive impact on early network adoption.

SERVICE\_COV (Service Coverage): the number of residents per branch office in the local market, a proxy for the number of cardholders per branch. As discussed earlier, we expect that the lower the level of service coverage (the higher the value for SERVICE\_COV), the higher the adoption probability.<sup>4</sup>

OWN\_NETWORK: the total number of branch offices operated by a bank. Because data on the number of proprietary ATMs or ATM locations were not available for non-Yankee 24 members, we use the number of bank branches to proxy the size of a bank’s proprietary ATM network, following Saloner and Shepard (1995). In fact, using data for Yankee 24 members, we found a correlation in excess of 90% between a bank’s branch network size and the number of ATMs it deploys. This variable contributes to the opportunity costs of joining Yankee 24 as discussed earlier.

DEPOSITOR\_BASE: the log of total demand deposits, a proxy for the number of bank depositors, for which data are not available. The logarithm is used because the distribution of deposits is skewed.

CONCENTRATION: market concentration, measured as the proportion of total market deposits accounted for by the largest four banking firms in the local market, a commonly used measure of market concentration in the literature and by the Federal Reserve Bank.

WAGE\_RATE: salary expenses per employee, defined as salary expenses and employee benefits over full-time equivalent (FTE) employees. This a proxy for wage rate.

BHC: a binary variable indicating ownership of a bank by a bank holding company.

## Data Sources

To determine the impact of the explanatory variables on the adoption of Yankee 24, we put together a data set that includes network adoption dates, bank characteristics, and local market structure variables. Membership adoption data were obtained directly from Yankee 24. Local banking market and population data were obtained from the statistics presented in the 1980 U.S. Census of Population. Banking firm characteristics were obtained from periodic reports, including the Report of Condition and Income (referred to in the banking industry as a “Call Report”) and the Summary of Deposits (SUMD) maintained by the Federal Reserve Board. The Call Reports and SUMD detailed balance sheet and other summary information on banks, providing the basis for some of the explanatory variables. Additional information related to bank branches and locations was obtained from Polk’s Bank Directory (1987).

A list of commercial banks and a list of savings banks that operated in Connecticut were obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia and the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, respectively. These two lists were combined for a total of 104 Connecticutbased commercial banks and savings banks, which defined the universe of commercial banks and savings banks in Connecticut at the time the Yankee 24 electronic banking network was being adopted. This number is very realistic. As of June 1987, there were a total of 120 commercial and savings bank in Connecticut reported by Polk’s Bank Directory. However, only 85 of those 104 were found to have filed Call Reports and

SUMD reports with the Federal Reserve Board in June 1984. Thus, we eliminated 19 banking firms from the sample; presumably bankruptcies, mergers, and consolidations occurred during the sample period, and some data were simply not filed by member banks as the Federal Reserve Bank required. To focus on the adoption and diffusion of Yankee 24 after it was established, we excluded the founding members from the estimation of our hazard model. This process yielded a final sample of 78 banking firms for our estimation.

## Discussion

The coefficients for OWN\_NETWORK and CONCEN-TRATION were positive, and those for DEPOSI-TOR\_BASE, WAGE\_RATE, SERVICE\_COV, NET-WORK\_BASE, and BHC were all negative. Given our formulation of the Weibull model, a positive coefficient implies a negative impact on the hazard rate. In other words, any variable that is estimated to have a positive effect indicates that the larger the value for the associated explanatory variable, the longer the mean time to adoption. All coefficients, except the one for CONCENTRATION, were significant at the 5% level. The hazard function is increasing in time with - - 1, which suggests a hazard rate that is generally increasing over time. (Recall that h(t) t <sup>a-1</sup> from Equation (3).)

This finding tells us that the probability of adoption for banks with mean values of the explanatory variables increases over time, given that they have not adopted the shared network. (For this reason, Table 6 includes not just effects on the hazard rate at the mean values of the variables but also for values of the variables elsewhere in the observed distributions in our data. Illustrating differential effects on the hazard rate is more representative of how the marketplace from which our sample is drawn actually worked.) This finding also is consistent with the idea that the benefits of network adoption grew and the opportunity cost of adoption declined over time. Clearly, as the network grew, the value of the shared network increased, and over time an increasing percentage of those who had not adopted the shared network perceived that it would be profitable to do so.

An important contrast occurs with our measure for network-installed base. The variable NET-WORK\_BASE has a significant and negative coefficient. This implies that the hazard rate is an increasing function of the expected network installed base, a proxy for the extent of network externalities. This supports our hypothesis that banks in markets that are expected to generate greater network externalities tended to adopt earlier. Apparently, because postadoption effective network size may be different depending on the local markets in which the banks operate, banks in markets expected to have a larger network-installed base valued the shared network more highly. This result also implies that the impact of network-installed base may be in the local markets and suggests the importance of the concept of expected effective shared network size.

Next, recall that a coefficient, $\beta _ { j } ,$ associated with an explanatory variable j can be viewed as the variable j’s constant proportional effect on the hazard rate. (This is also the conditional probability that a firm will join the shared network given that it has not done so up to the end of the period our data cover.) So, $1 0 0 ^ { * } ( e ^ { - \beta j } – 1 )$ can be interpreted as the percentage change in the hazard rate for a unit change in an explanatory variable. Thus, the estimated coefficient for NETWORK\_BASE indicates that an increase in shared network-installed base by 10 branches will increase the hazard rate by about 2.8%.

The variable measuring customer service coverage, SERVICE\_COV, has a significant and negative coefficient, which supports our hypothesis that the lower the level of network service coverage in the market as perceived by the bank, the earlier the adoption. The result can be interpreted as follows. Banks in markets that had more customers served per ATM location might have adopted earlier because they can enhance customer service without having to add costly new branches and have a better chance to earn interchange revenues. The coefficient for SERVICE\_COV indicates that each increment of 100 customers served per branch will lead to a 4.6% increase in the conditional probability of adoption. The empirical result also indicates that the impact of service coverage for a bank is in the local market in which the bank competes. Although this corresponds with our assumption that the local market is important, it does not rule out the possibility that some benefits, which are very difficult to measure, are enjoyed across the markets. In that case, the network externalities would be underestimated.

The positive coefficient for OWN\_NETWORK indicates that the more branch offices a bank operated, the more likely the bank would have waited longer until adoption. The coefficient also tells us that a one branch increase will lead to a 2.2% decline in the hazard rate. Because OWN\_NETWORK is a proxy for the size of a bank’s own ATM network, the result supports our hypothesis that banks with a smaller ATM network tended to adopt earlier. Our finding is contrary to that reported by Saloner and Shepard (1995) and clearly distinguishes the effect of network externalities from the proprietary network effect. The result can be interpreted as follows: Banks with large own branch networks were not willing to join the shared network early and share their facilities with other members because of a higher opportunity cost.

With a negative estimated coefficient, DEPOSI-TOR\_BASE registers a positive impact on early network adoption, which is supportive of our hypothesis that banks with more depositors tended to join shared networks earlier, presumably because the benefits of network membership for them were greater. Specifically, a 50% increase in demand deposits will increase the conditional probability of adoption by about 3.4%, all else equal. The finding is consistent with that reported by Saloner and Shepard (1995), i.e., a bank’s date of adoption is earlier the larger the value of its deposit base. The coefficient for CONCENTRATION is insignificant although positive. Hence, in our electronic banking context, we did not find support for the finding reported by Hannan and McDowell (1984) that banks operating in concentrated markets were likely to adopt earlier. The coefficient for WAGE\_RATE is negative, indicating that the salary expenses per employee paid by a bank have a positive impact on the adoption probability. An estimated 5% increase in WAGE\_RATE will lead to a 6.6% decline in the hazard rate. This result supports the idea that shared networks are labor saving and that shared network membership can substitute for labor expenses. A negative coefficient was obtained for BHC, indicating that banks owned by a bank holding company tended to adopt earlier.

## Conclusions

We close this paper with conclusions about its contributions to theory and practice, its implications for other applied settings and for the conduct of empirical research in this area, and potential limitations of this research.

## Contributions

Contributions to the Network Externalities Literature. The rate at which technologies are adopted by firms is a fundamental aspect of the process of corporate absorption of technological change. Accordingly, investigation of the firm-specific and market-specific characteristics that influence firm decisions to adopt technologies has long been recognized as important. This paper goes beyond the widely applied classical diffusion model, which stresses the innovativeness of potential adopters and assumes that everyone has an equal opportunity to adopt. Instead, we examine technological, organizational, and market structure-related attributes, which make an externality-bearing shared network differentially attractive to potential adopters. Our results suggest the importance of expected effective network size as a measure of network externalities. Network owners can look into local markets (or market segments) for potential adopting firms and identify the effective network size that is likely to affect the perceived network business value and adoption decisions.

The most general result of this paper is the finding that firms that adopt externality-bearing technological innovations trade off both anticipated and actual costs and revenues in their decisions about the timing of adoption. We learned that the kinds of cost and revenue drivers that figure in such decisions may be situational, especially with respect to the technology involved, firm-specific factors and market-specific conditions. However, these general types of drivers are likely to be observed across different technology adoption decision-making environments. For example, the typical drivers of future technology-led revenues may be such things as current market share, potential market share, and the leverage that the firm’s new technology can exert in a changing marketplace. Similarly, the general cost drivers may include adoption costs, the opportunity costs of not adopting, and switching costs.

Our switching cost result is especially interesting because it shows the extent to which firms may be subject to over-reliance on a proprietary network, even as their competitive marketplace changes. This is no doubt true for technologies that are organized in physical networks (as are proprietary ATM networks and even shared networks today), as well as proprietary networks of human participants who create value in a business process. We see this “foot-dragging” occurring in many places in the financial services industry as traditional players mull over how to get their timing right for a costly commitment to electronic commerce. For example, recently Merrill Lynch announced that it would implement an Internet-based electronic brokerage network, after holding out from doing so for more than two years, as competitors such as Charles Schwab, E\*Trade, and Ameritrade began to snap up profitable business on the World Wide Web. Merrill Lynch ultimately was forced to face up to the new imperative of Internet-based stock trading and research, but recognized the importance of incorporating its “best in the business” network of financial consultants into a new standard for high-end financial advisory service in the marketplace (Merrill Lynch 1999). We believe that having such general insights in hand creates new opportunities for research that can further extend our understanding of how to strategize with respect to externality-bearing IT adoption decisions.

Contributions to Methodologies for IT Value Assessment. An even more immediate benefit is that this work will prompt researchers in the area of the business value of IT to craft new and innovative approaches to the measurement of the returns to system investments. The “heads up” from our research is that the firm’s situation in its marketplace, and the benefits that it may be able to appropriate from IT investment, should be conditioned upon a richer appreciation of intangible but readily perceived value. Network externalities may be impossible to “touch,” but management decision makers, as we learned in this research, are nevertheless “in touch” with the value they can confer upon the firm. From this point of view, the work of Marschak and Miyazawa (1974) on the “payoffadequacy” of information systems and the inherent heterogeneity that investors and decision-makers exhibit rings true to us. They emphasized the extent to which IT investment opportunities tend to deliver heterogeneous levels of potential value, depending upon the investing organization, the investor’s perspective, the time of investment, the nature of the business problem the technology addresses, and so on. Irrespective of the specific kind of IT investment that is made, one should expect senior managers and investors in IT applications to have a “seat of the pants” sense of how network externalities are “payoff-relevant” (Marschak 1974), and the extent to which they may provide a basis for “payoff-adequacy.” Such insights as these from our work provide important contributions to the IS literature.

Contributions to Our Understanding of Shared Electronic Banking Network Strategies. In the case of the growth and adoption of the Yankee 24 electronic banking network of New England, we learned three things that generalize to a range of electronic network phenomena in financial services. These include electronic brokerage networks, electronic bill payment and bill presentment networks, smart card standards, electronic money solutions, and World Wide Web-based home banking services. Banks operating in markets that could generate higher levels of network externalities tended to adopt the shared network sooner. We also learned that banks that had smaller proprietary networks and larger depositor bases tended to adopt the common network earlier. In addition, we found that market wage rate and bank ownership structure exerted some influence on shared network adoption. Overall, the empirical results of this study support our network externalities hypothesis: Network externalities exist in shared electronic banking networks and they appear to have influenced the mechanics of corporate decision-making in network adoption in a significant way.

In closing, we remind the reader that this paper’s primary contribution is in opening the “black box” of network externalities. Using a variant of the “typical” installed base model from economics to reflect network sharing, we have shown the extent to which a marketwide technology adoption phenomenon can be consistent with an individual firm-level model of decision making. The latter takes into account a variety of firmspecific referents for network business value. This paper also prompts those who are interested in studying technology adoption issues to consider using the hazard modeling approach that we showcase in this paper. We emphasize its power and appropriateness for understanding the drivers of technology adoption and diffusion.

## Implications

The results of this study have implications for other kinds of shared networks in banking and financial services, as well as for a broad range of developments that are occurring in the world of electronic commerce on the Internet. For example, the global shared electronic banking networks PLUS and CIRRUS have been competing for the central position in financial services operations, along with their credit card company and banking industry partners. These networks, on the basis of their large installed bases, have rapidly moved to position themselves in the emerging Internet marketplace for electronic bill payment and presentment, and to set new standards in digital encryption that will define retail payments well into the next millenium. Other new Internet-focused consumer bill services providers, such as Checkfree (www.checkfree.com), Integrion (www.integrion.com), and Transpoint (www.trans point.com), are rushing to capture an installed base of institutional members in the financial services industry as they simultaneously work to educate consumers and bill-presenting firms about the benefits of paying bills without paper checks.

Electronic billing turns out to be a very interesting sector of the financial services industry to analyze using the logic of our modeling framework. The adoption problem that firms must address is analogous to what we see for shared electronic banking—with one important difference: There are three kinds of adopting players. The first is financial institutions that must decide among different electronic bill services infrastructure providers to select (e.g., Integrion or Transpoint). The second is regional firms from whom consumers purchase goods and services (e.g., gas and electric companies, telephone service providers, department stores, gas stations, etc.). The third is consumers who need to decide how to hook up and to whom (if at all). Few, if any, banks are willing to adopt an electronic bill payment processing infrastructure without virtual certainty about the viability of the solution provider as a market standard. The slow rate of adoption we see in the marketplace reveals the extent of uncertainty about what the future standard will be. Trading off perceived and actual costs and benefits, senior managers choose to wait to adopt until their estimate of the variance of potential network externalities that will accrue in the marketplace narrows.

Our research perspective also predicts that regional network externalities from financial services firm adoption of bill payment and presentment standards are likely to be an important driver of the rates of adoption of such services by consumers and merchants. Interestingly, we do not expect these latter two tiers of network externalities to materialize at the same rate: Adoption by financial institutions of a given electronic bill services infrastructure ought to depend to some extent on how well banks can convince regional merchants to adopt. Instead, there ought to be some time lag that must pass before all the market participants can agree upon the “right” externality-maximizing network choice. Once this occurs, of course, consumers still must decide whether they want to receive and pay bills via the Internet. Our model predicts that the revenues that electronic bill payment and presentment generate (and the cost reductions that occur in operations) will be determined by the influence that growing installed bases of system users have on other adopters.

Our interpretation of the drivers of Internet banking is similar because the adoption problem is complicated by network externalities, and technological changes will lead to a transformation in industry structure that will determine the winners and loser in the market (Burnham 1999). Electronic money service providers offer their innovations to banks, Internet and realworld merchants, and consumers. Again, we see a number of competing software and electronic money solution infrastructure providers (e.g., Microsoft, Dell/ Compaq/Millicent (www.millicent.com), and Intuit (www.intuit.com)), as well as the leading commercial banks (e.g., Citigroup, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, etc.), which are investing heavily in proprietary solutions of their own. Which technology to adopt remains unclear at this time, however: There are too many different competing solutions, and building a proprietary solution is viable only if the installed base of captive potential users is a significant chunk of the overall market. In these circumstances, too, retail banking strategists will think in terms of trading off perceived and actual revenues and costs, meanwhile waiting for the right signals to emerge that indicate that the scene is set for one or two standard solutions to reach critical mass. Despite their heterogeneous assessments of value, decision-makers will move rapidly to adopt specific solutions once the network externalities associated with their choice make the overall investment “payoffadequate.”

Our results also have implications for many horizontal interorganizational systems (Cash and Konsynski 1985), automated information systems that are shared by two or more companies, and vertical interorganizational systems that play a significant role in vertical mar kets (Bakos 1991, Gurbaxani and Whang 1991, Malone et al. 1987). Like the telephone networks, horizontal interorganizational systems are subject to significant network externalities. The value of the system to a given subscriber is affected by the number of others joining the same network, and new subscribers confer externalities upon existing network members. Our results are consistent with the findings of several studies of successful applications of interorganizational systems, including shared electronic banking networks (Banker and Kauffman 1988, Clemons 1990), computerized airline computerized reservation systems (CRS) (Copeland and McKenney 1988, Duliba et al. 1998), and wholesale distribution systems (Clemons and Row 1988). Larger installed bases result in higher levels of willingness to pay by customers as quality and service capabilities increase. In fact, over time the network externalities from some of the systems discussed in this literature have grown to such an extent that firms not adopting them have been locked out of a wide range of services and the market at large (Keen 1991). A contrasting vertical interorganizational market example is Priceline.com, a buyer-driven marketplace discussed in Bakos (1998) that brings together price-sensitive consumers and airline ticket suppliers with excess inventory of leisure travel services. Judging from the spate of recent national advertising, all current efforts appear to be devoted to achieving an effective installed base of suppliers and consumer-bidders to ensure that Priceline.com can survive the coming shakeout in its marketplace. But meanwhile, there is no rush on the part of the airlines to provide ticket supply to Priceline. Although its business model is an innovative one, the major airlines have yet to adopt this solution wholeheartedly, thus limiting Priceline’s externality value to the consumer for connectivity with many suppliers.

## Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The first of these involves how we operationalized network externalities in our empirical analysis.

The usual approach is to characterize network externalities as the size of the network installed base. But, direct estimation of network adoption with the current installed base as an explanatory variable—as opposed to the future expected or equilibrium level installed base—fails to adequately account for the role that expectations play in adoption decision making. The reader should recognize, however, that in our research obtaining a direct measure for the future network size expected by potential adopting firms was not possible. The variable was unobservable from the publicly available data to which we had access, even with the participation of the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia and direct access to the operations of the network we studied. Moreover, we could not have surveyed firms for this information; the study focuses on adoption behavior in the past rather than on current expectations about what will happen in the future. As such, we chose a second-best approach: identifying observable proxies for network externalities, based on firm and market-specific characteristics relevant to the period covered in the study, and which we believe were likely to enhance the perceived business value of a shared network. Finally, the proxies we did select all had precedents in the prior literature.

Second, it is also worthwhile to consider the short time-series of observations of adoption used in our study as a possible limitation. For example, an outside observer might view adoption timing during the brief two-and-a-half-year period from the fourth quarter of 1984 to the first quarter of 1987 as a time in which period adoption was occurring for noneconomic reasons (e.g., site-specific implementation requirements, availability of skilled personnel, and so on). But this was not suggested to us by the senior executives we interviewed during our field study at Yankee 24, even though it must have been true in some cases. In fact, Richard P. Yanak, then president and CEO of New England Network, Inc., and Richard Symington, then Yankee 24 network’s vice president of operations, told us that adoption did play out on a “wait-and-see” basis in their marketplace. So the explanations we offer are at least consistent with our case study findings.<sup>6</sup>

Another related alternative explanation is that the data we are modeling reflect just a short early segment of technology adopters who are insensitive to network externalities. Again, the anecdotal evidence we acquired in our field study suggests otherwise.

Third, there are also some threats to the validity of our study that derive from theoretical considerations. Kridel et al. (1993) presented findings that argue that explaining adoption, as we have, in terms of consumer surplus in excess of adoption subscription costs, fails to recognize that shared electronic banking network membership can create “real options” for potential adopters. (For additional development of this perspective relative to the electronic banking network discussed in this research, the interested reader should see Benaroch and Kauffman 1999.)

A fourth concern involves the scope of the decisionmaking model that we think applies in this context. We argued that expectations about network size were limited to regional markets in which a firm was already operating, and that potential adopters were homogeneous in conceptualizing network value. Such assumptions enable the kind of analysis we performed, even though they present a somewhat stylized view of the electronic banking services marketplace, which today involves increasingly national banking firms in a nationwide domestic marketplace.
