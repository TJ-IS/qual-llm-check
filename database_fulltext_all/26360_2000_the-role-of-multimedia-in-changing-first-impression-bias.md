# The Role of Multimedia in Changing First Impression Bias

Kai H. Lim • Izak Benbasat • Lawrence M. Ward

Department of Information Systems, Faculty of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong, and Department of Information Systems, Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio 44106

Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada, V6T 1Z2

Department of Psychology, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada, V6T 1Z4 khl2@po.cwru.edu • izak@unixg.ubc.ca • lward@cortex.psych.ubc.ca

F <sup>irst</sup> <sup>impression</sup> <sup>bias</sup> <sup>refers</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>limitation</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>human</sup> <sup>information</sup> <sup>processing</sup> <sup>in</sup> <sup>which</sup> <sup>people</sup> are strongly influenced by the first piece of information that they are exposed to, and that they are biased in evaluating subsequent information in the direction of the initial influence. The psychology literature has portrayed first impression bias as a virtually “inherent” human bias. Drawing from multimedia literature, this study identifies several characteristics of multimedia presentations that have the potential to alleviate first impression bias. Based on this literature, a set of predictions was generated and tested through a laboratory experiment using a simulated multimedia intranet.

Half of the 80 subjects were provided with a biased cue. Subjects were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) text with first impression bias cue, (2) multimedia with first impression bias cue, (3) text without biased cue, and (4) multimedia without biased cue. The experimental task involved conducting a five-year performance appraisal of a department head. The first impression bias cue was designed to provide incomplete and unfavorable information about the department head, but the information provided subsequently was intended to be favorable of his performance.

Results show that the appraisal score of the text with biased cue group was significantly lower than the text only (without biased cue) group. On the other hand, the appraisal score of the multimedia with biased cue group was not significantly different from the multimedia only (without biased cue) group. As a whole, the results suggest that multimedia presentations, but not text-based presentations, reduce the influence of first impression bias.

## 1. Introduction

One of philosophy’s oldest paradoxes is the apparent contradiction between the great triumphs and the dramatic failures of the human mind. The same organism that routinely solves inferential problems too subtle and complex for the mightiest computers often makes errors in the simplest of judgments about everyday events. (Nisbett and Ross 1980)

There is a rich body of research in information systems investigating how to present information to decision makers so that the best performance (in terms of effectiveness and/or efficiency) can be achieved (Benbasat and Dexter 1985, 1986; Remus 1984; Jarvenpaa 1989; Tan and Benbasat 1990; Vessey 1991). Another stream of research extends the findings in cognitive psychology on the inherent limitations in human decision making, and proposes to use information and decision support systems to compensate for such deficiencies (e.g., Lim 1995, Roy and Lerch 1996, Todd and Benbasat 1991, 1992). While both bodies of research have generated useful guidelines for information system design, the advent of multimedia technology has provided both new opportunities and challenges for researchers. Central among these is whether the existing guidelines or theories from the earlier work are adequate to predict the benefits of richer and more multifaceted means of presenting information.

Despite the popularity of multimedia-based software, more needs to be known about the efficacy of this technology for decision making. Although several researchers have advocated the idea of storing and presenting organizational information using multimedia technology (Rao and Goldman-Segall 1995, Sprague 1995, Stein and Zwass 1995), little empirical work has been performed to assess the potential benefits and drawbacks of such use.

The objective of this paper is to examine the advantages of multimedia technology for supporting decision making in the context of evaluating a person or an event. More specifically, we investigate whether or not information presented in multimedia format has the potential to mitigate or reverse a negative first impression. Research in psychology and multimedia have provided some preliminary guidelines to judge the benefits of multimedia presentations. Drawing from these literatures, we generate several predictions about the benefits of multimedia over text and test them in a laboratory experiment in which subjects are asked to make personnel appraisals.

Research has shown that one’s opinions about an event or a person are very much determined by the first piece of information to which one is exposed. This inherent limitation is called first impression bias (Asch 1946).<sup>1</sup> First impression bias is also closely related to the concept of primacy effect, which suggests that the first few pieces of information presented tend to be better remembered than those presented later. First impression bias has been shown to consistently occur in different contexts (Fiske and Taylor 1991, Jones and Goethals 1972, Lana 1964).

First impression bias can have serious consequences in organizational decision making. With a strong first impression, decision makers will reinterpret information received subsequently to fit their first impressions. If the first piece of information provides an accurate portrait of events, subsequent decisions may not be negatively affected. However, if this first piece of information is incomplete or inaccurate, then the resulting bias may have adverse consequences. This concern is especially apparent for tasks that are unstructured or lack well-defined procedures, because much of the information that is relevant to such situations is ambiguous and may therefore be interpreted in numerous ways (Weick 1979).

In this paper, we show that multimedia is a more powerful means than text for affecting attitudes and judgements. It can be used in a variety of ways to change a person’s impression of an event, including changes that might lead to negative consequences. For example, while multimedia may be used to reduce first impression bias, it may also have the potential to induce biases, either by design or by accident. In this paper, we have chosen to focus on the potential positive effects of multimedia technology in our first attempt to investigate its efficacy.

This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the literature on first impression bias and discusses two related characteristics of multimedia information.

A theoretical perspective, which describes how multimedia can reduce the influence of first impression bias, is then developed. Section 3 describes the research method and the experiment conducted. Statistical analyses and the results are reported in §4. Section 5 discusses the findings and the limitations of the study and concludes the paper by pointing out the contributions of this study and the implications of its findings.

## 2. Theory

## 2.1. First Impression Bias

The scientific basis for the first impression bias was introduced by Asch (1946). In his classic study, he told his subjects that they should try to form an impression of a person based on a set of qualities. Next, he gave two groups of subjects two alternative descriptions of a hypothetical person:

List 1: intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, and envious.

List 2: envious, stubborn, critical, impulsive, industrious, intelligent.

List 1 begins with two positive qualities (intelligent, industrious), proceeds to three qualities that could be interpreted as either good or bad (impulsive, critical, stubborn), and ends with a negative quality (envious). List 2, in contrast, begins with a negative quality and ends with two positive qualities.

Subsequently, subjects were asked to rate the target person using a checklist consisting of 18 pairs of contrasting traits (e.g., generous-ungenerous, wise-shrewd). Subjects who were given List 1 (positive quality first) rated the target person far more positively than those subjects who were given List 2 (negative quality first). When asked to write a short character sketch those given List 1 appeared to interpret the traits “impulsive,” “stubborn,” and “critical” as “spontaneous,” “decisive,” and “discriminating;” in contrast, subjects given List 2 interpreted the same two traits as “reckless,” “obstinate,” and “fault-finding.”

Asch’s study provides clear evidence that the information received first has a disproportionately great effect on judgement. Subsequent studies that examined this phenomenon in more realistic contexts have consistently obtained the same results (e.g., Jones et al.

1968, Kelly 1950, Luchins 1957, Rosenhan 1973). Asch’s work and subsequent studies highlight several facts about the nature of human decision making. First, people readily make judgements on the basis of a very small quantity of information. Although decision makers might consider such judgments to be rational and unbiased, the fact remains that a judgment based on so little information has a high potential for bias. In our study, we simulate a situation in which only partial information is initially revealed to the subjects. Further, we negatively distort the information so that our subjects will form an initially negative biased judgement.

Second, these studies demonstrate that people formulate a strong opinion based on their first impression and that they are inherently biased in evaluating subsequent information. If subsequent information is consistent with one’s first impression, the first impression is reinforced. If it is inconsistent, a person will attempt to reinterpret the new information to fit the original impression. If this reinterpretation attempt fails, one ignores the new information as an anomaly. Only after many inconsistencies have been observed will people start to question the validity of the original impression.

## 2.2. Multimedia

The Cognitive Technology Group at Vanderbilt (1993, p. 118) defines multimedia as the “linkage of text, sound, video, graphics, and the computer in such a way that the user’s access to these media becomes nonlinear and virtually instantaneous.” This section discusses two unique characteristics of multimedia, namely rich language and complementary cues, which are relevant to this study.<sup>2</sup>

2.2.1. Rich Language. Every symbol system has strengths and limitations. While research suggests that a picture is sometimes worth ten thousand words (Larkin and Simon 1987), pictures cannot convey the meaning of conditional events or causes, such as if, nevertheless, because, or no, because pictures do not entail any logical connections (Salomon 1979). On the other hand, as Hansen (1989, p. 8) argues, “many human experiences are encoded in symbolic systems that resist logic descriptions, at least, they are not handy to communicate when translated into verbal (or mathematical) descriptions.”

It is believed that visual information is represented differently in memory than linguistically presented information. Baggett (1989) suggests that visual representations contain more information and are “bushier.” She uses the example of a “red leaf” to illustrate her argument. The phrase “red leaf” when stored in memory contains only the name of an object and a modifier. On the other hand, a mental representation of a “red leaf” formulated from a picture would contain information about its size, color, and shape. Multimedia technology brings together the symbolic and processing capabilities of various media and thus creates a richer symbolic system of communication.

A rich representation is particularly important in conveying information related to social situations and tasks. Daft et al. (1987) found that managers perceive “lean” communication media, such as electronic mail, to be ineffective for tasks that are ambiguous and unstructured. Instead, they rely more heavily on rich media, such as face-to-face interactions, for such tasks (also see McGrath and Hollingshead 1993) because of the ability of such media to convey nonverbal messages using a richer set of symbol systems and hence better preserve the original meanings, thus reducing the chances of misinterpretation.

Research in nonverbal communication suggests that nonverbal messages are complements, not alternatives, to using language. One key difference between nonverbal and verbal language is that nonverbal messages tend to be much more efficient in transmitting attitudes and emotions than spoken languages (Ekman and Friesen 1974). Argyle and his colleagues (1970, 1971) found that when verbal messages are contradicted by nonverbal messages, the nonverbal messages have more influence over people’s judgements. Similarly, in capturing organizational information, video information would preserve the embedded social and interpersonal cues in complex, unstructured organizational activities relevant to decision making.

2.2.2. Complementary Cues. Representing information in multiple formats has been illustrated to lead to better retention and retrieval (e.g., Paivio 1971). At first glance, it appears that multiple representations, such as presenting auditory and visual information simultaneously, may cause interference, since these two sources of information may compete for our limited cognitive resources (Kahneman 1973). Research suggests otherwise. Simultaneous presentation of congruent information in different modalities does not result in competition for cognitive resources. Instead, they complement each other (Wetzel et al. 1994).

For example, empirical findings in audio and visual presentations indicate that people can construct a mental representation using either audio or visual information alone (e.g., Baggett and Ehrenfeucht 1982, 1983; Beagles-Roos and Gat 1983; Hayes and Kelly 1984; Hayes et al. 1986; Motowidlo and Burnett 1995). However, when presented together, each source provides additional complementary information that retains some of the characteristics of each of the original symbol systems (Wetzel et al. 1994).

The complementary effect of multiple representations on learning has also been studied extensively. For example, Levie and Lentz (1982) found that congruent pictures paired with text generally improve comprehension over either text or pictures alone. It is thought that during the learning stage, multiple representations of an object or event increase the number of possible links between information to be stored and existing knowledge in long-term memory, thus leading to better retention in long-term memory.

In summary, there is substantial empirical evidence suggesting that complementary cues increase retention and improve understanding through a more vivid presentation that provides more potential links with existing information in long-term memory. As a result, the information is not easily overlooked or ignored.

## 2.3. The Role of Multimedia in Changing the First Impression

In the previous section, we discussed the two unique characteristics of multimedia: complementary cues and rich language. With these characteristics, multimedia becomes a powerful tool that can be used in a variety of ways to change a person’s attitude and judgment. However, the rich nature of the medium could be a double-edged sword. While the rich information in multimedia could ameliorate a biased first impression, it could also be used to either create—intentionally or unintentionally—a misleading impression.

Research in self-presentation and impression management suggests that people use their nonverbal behaviors as self-presentation tactics (Leary 1995). For example, political leaders often control their nonverbal behavior to convey an impression of being powerful individuals (Korda 1976, Schlenker 1980). Thus, a weak manager may be able to use the rich nonverbal cues in multimedia to tactically self-portray a stronger image and to conceal truth. On the other hand, for a good leader who has poor self-presentation skills, multimedia may create a more negative impression than text-based information. In both cases, multimedia has the potential to create a misleading impression and lead to biases.

In short, multimedia simply offers more information cues (verbal and nonverbal) than text-based systems. However, these information cues only provide potential capabilities; “they are an opportunity rather than a guarantee to benefit” (Wetzel et al. 1994 p. 210).” It is up to the presenter to utilize those cues to manage impression.

Our belief is that, while multimedia technology is a powerful means of presenting information, its potential consequences on organizational decision making are still unclear. In this paper, we investigate only one potential advantage of multimedia technology, namely, its role in diluting the first impression bias. However, the potential benefits and problems of multimedia need to be studied much more thoroughly in future research.

First impression bias is mediated by two specific processes: 1) reinterpreting information to fit the first impression and 2) ignoring disconfirming information. Thus, to reduce first impression bias, these two processes must be suppressed. This effect can be achieved through: 1) presenting information in such unambiguous ways that multiple interpretations are eliminated or reduced (Daft and Lengel 1986), and 2) presenting information in such vivid ways that evidence disconfirming the original bias is difficult to ignore (Nisbett and Ross 1980).

For two reasons, it is likely that presenting information via multimedia will provide a means of changing first impressions. First, by using a set of rich symbolic languages, multimedia presentations can convey the authentic meaning of information in less distorted and less ambiguous ways, thus reducing the potential for misinterpretations. For example, with video images, a multimedia presentation preserves the facial and body language of a speaker, and thus better conveys the original meaning of the message, making reinterpretation more difficult.

Second, by utilizing the embedded complementary cues provided by audio and video, multimedia presents information in vivid ways. The use of complementary cues leads to better retention and retrieval of information because complementary cues create more potential links to knowledge structures in long-term memory. With video and audio capabilities, multimedia makes disconfirming information more evident.<sup>3</sup>

In summary, the theoretical perspective discussed above suggests that multimedia has the potential to reduce first impression bias. Specifically, through the use of an audio and video format, a multimedia system preserves much of the rich, nonverbal semantics and presents information in less ambiguous (Daft and Lengel 1984, 1986) and more vivid ways (Baggett 1989). These characteristics make subsequent information that is inconsistent with the first impression more difficult to reinterpret and/or ignore. However, as was mentioned earlier, capabilities offered by multimedia only provide an opportunity to generate benefits rather than guarantee them. A necessary condition, within the context of our study, is that the person appearing on the video (i.e., the individual being evaluated), should possess good self-presentation skills. Therefore, with the presence of this condition, we hypothesize the following.

## 3. Research Method

This study is the third in a series of three experiments examining media. To enhance realism and the generalizability of the findings, information from a real organization, the British Columbia Cancer Research Centre (BCCRC), was collected and presented to the subjects. In the experiment reported in this paper (Experiment 3), subjects were asked to act as newly appointed members of the BCCRC’s Board of Trustees and evaluate one of the six department heads at the BCCRC.

Previously, subjects had performed two experimental tasks. In Experiment 1, subjects were exposed to organizational information about BCCRC. This allowed them to become familiar with the organization. In Experiment 2, subjects were asked to evaluate the ambiguity of the information presented via text and multimedia.

The materials provided to subjects in this experiment included an interview with a department head, who was the target of the appraisal decision. The interview was divided into several segments based on the topics discussed, and linked via hypertext. Subjects were required to review all available information. Figure 1 shows an example of the user interface. Subjects were informed that they could examine any piece of information at any time by clicking on the underlined and highlighted text.

Depending on the experimental condition assigned, information was made available in text or multimedia format. Subjects using the multimedia system viewed information in a full-motion video format. For example, to learn about the department head’s thoughts on keeping track of his subordinate researchers’ work, subjects could simply “ask” the department head by clicking on a hypertext link that read “keeping track of fellow researchers.” A video clip showing the department head explaining his views on this aspect would then be presented.

Subjects using the text-based system made use of the same hypertext access method but viewed only written transcripts of the video clips. The transcripts were professionally prepared to provide all spoken words (as would be usual for ordinary meeting transcripts). A research assistant used the video clips to verify the accuracy of the transcripts. Thus, the only difference between the two systems was the medium, namely written transcripts versus video clips. In both systems, users could reexamine the information by moving a scroll bar to the desired position.

## 3.1. The Experimental Presentation System

The system used in the experiments was designed especially for this research. It ran on an IBM compatible 90 MHz Pentium PC with a NEC 15” Multisync 4FGe monitor. The system was implemented as a simulated intranet. Subjects used Netscape (version 1.1) to browse the experimental materials stored on an 850 MB hard disk. With this configuration, retrieval of information, including video clips, was almost instantaneous.

The video clips were digitized using Intel’s Smart Video Recorder Pro (1994) at a frame output rate of 20 frames per second. Since most of the clips were interviews, this frame rate provided a fairly smooth replay quality. In the experiment, subjects were approximately two feet away from the monitor. Using the high-resolution monitor, subjects could clearly see the facial expressions of the people in the video clips.

## 3.2. The Experimental Task

The experimental task was to conduct a five-year performance appraisal of a department head. It required subjects to evaluate the department head’s overall performance, including his leadership skills, vision, and communication skills. Subjects were also asked to make a final recommendation on whether to renew or terminate the department head’s contract.

The key information on which subjects based their appraisal was a series of interviews with the department head. In these interviews, the department head discussed and answered questions on specific aspects of managing his department. These aspects range from a high-level view, such as his vision of where the department was heading, to specific details, such as how he would handle unproductive subordinates. Subjects could examine the department head’s view on any one of these aspects by clicking the hypertext link for the specific topic of interest.

An instrument (see Appendix A) that consisted of 11 Likert-type items was used by the subjects in performing the appraisal. Another 3 Likert-type items measured the subjects’ confidence with the appraisals made. To ensure that subjects were motivated to perform diligently, they were also asked to provide a written justification for their decision.

## 3.3. Experimental Manipulation

Figure 2 summarizes the design of this experiment. As highlighted in the figure, there were two experimental and two control groups. All subjects performed the appraisal task described above. The only difference between the experimental and control groups was that the experimental groups (both text-based and multimedia) received a biased information cue prior to examining the interview information.

The biased cue was operationalized as follows. First, we obtained the performance indicators for all six departments within BCCRC over the past five years.<sup>4</sup> The data pertaining to the department under appraisal was then altered to give the appearance that this department was not performing as well as the other five departments.

Second, similar to Asch’s (1946) study, we asked our subjects to perform their appraisal with only partial information. As in Asch’s study, we attempt to simulate a biased first impression by requiring the subjects to make their first judgment based on information lacking the full context surrounding the entity being evaluated. In fact, as will be mentioned later, our pilot subjects expressed that they did not have sufficient information during the first appraisal. Nonetheless, they went ahead and made their first appraisal with only partial information.

Subjects in the experimental groups performed the appraisal twice. ## 3.4. The Independent Variable

System type is the independent variable for this experiment. Two systems, text-based system and multimedia system, were compared. The two systems were equivalent in terms of verbal information content and the method of accessing this information (both through hypertext links).

## 3.5. The Dependent Variables

The two dependent variables were appraisal score and confidence with the appraisal made.

1) Appraisal score. Appraisal score was measured by an 11-item scale adapted from Denison’s (1990) Leadership Index (see Appendix A).<sup>5</sup> The instrument taps into various aspects of leadership skills, including team building, goal emphasis, supervisory support, communication skills, and vision.

2) Confidence in the appraisal made. Confidence in the appraisal made was measured using a 3-item scale adopted from Aldag and Power (1986). It is defined as the extent to which the decision maker feels that he or she has reached a good decision outcome or made a good judgement. Aldag and Power reported that the reliability of the instrument was 0.84.

## 3.6. Experimental Procedures

The experiments were conducted one subject at a time in an isolated room with the help of a research assistant. The two different assistants who were involved in conducting the sessions were not told the objectives and the hypotheses of the study until the data collection phase was completed.

3.6.1. Control Groups. Subjects in both control groups (text-based control group and multimedia control group) began by reading the task instructions labeled as Task Description (Appendix B). Next, they proceeded to examine the interview information for the department head stored either in the multimedia or the text-based system, depending on their assigned experimental condition. The interview information took approximately 15 minutes to examine. After they had finished examining the interview information, they were given the appraisal instrument (Appendix A) to perform the formal appraisal task.

3.6.2. Experimental Groups. Subjects in both experimental groups (text-based experimental group and multimedia experimental group) were given a different instruction sheet labeled as “Task Instruction A” (Appendix C). Compared to their counterparts in the control groups, subjects in the experimental groups were given two additional pieces of information before examining the interview information. The first extra piece of information was the biased cue, which showed performance indicators for the department under appraisal (contrasted with the five other departments in the Research Centre) for the past five years (as explained in §3.3). The biased cue was generated as follows. First, as in other studies examining first impression bias, only partial information was given when the subjects were asked to form a first impression. Second, the “true” data pertaining to the performance of the department under appraisal was altered so that it now appeared to be poor in comparison to the other departments. Pilot tests showed the manipulation was successful; subjects formed an initial impression that the department head had not been managing his department very well.

The second piece of extra information was a “fake” personality profile (Appendix D) of the department head under evaluation. Although the manipulation of the biased cue was successful, pilot subjects mentioned that, with the performance indicator alone, they did not feel that they had sufficient information to make a good appraisal of the department head. To help subjects create a sense that they had sufficient information to evaluate him, a fake personality profile of the department head was given to the subjects in both experimental groups after they had examined the performance indicators.<sup>6</sup> This personality profile is neutral in tone, and the statements are general enough to be true of most people. With this, the “personality” of the department head can be interpreted either positively or negatively depending on the first impression formed. The fake personality profile included four statements adapted from Forer (1949).<sup>7</sup>

After viewing these two pieces of extra information (performance indicators and the fake personality profile), experimental group subjects were given the postbiased-cue appraisal instrument (Appendix A) to perform their initial appraisal of the department head. This appraisal served as a manipulation check to ensure that the experimental subjects were indeed affected by the biased information cue. The 3-item scale that measured the subjects’ confidence with the appraisals they made was also included at the end of the appraisal instrument.

All subjects, whether in the experimental or control groups, were required to provide a written justification of their appraisal. The entire experiment took approximately 45 minutes to complete.

## 3.7. Subject Population

Subjects were recruited through campus advertisements at a large public university. Participation in the study was voluntary. Subjects were provided with three incentives. First, they were paid \$20 for participating. Second, an additional \$30 was offered for each of the three best performers. Third, after participating, all subjects were entitled to attend a free, one-hour World Wide Web tutorial.

Of the 80 subjects participating in the study, 54% were male and 46% were female. Their average age was 25. Half were business students; the remainder majored in the Sciences (27%) and the Arts (23%). Forty-seven percent were undergraduate students, while the rest were graduate students.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Manipulation Checks

Recall that this study is the third in a series of three experiments. In both Experiments 1 and 2 (reported elsewhere), half of the subjects started with a textbased system and the other half started with a multimedia system. Furthermore, half of the subjects performed Experiment 2 first while the other half performed Experiment 3 first. Therefore, statistical tests were first performed on all dependent variables used in the current experiment to ascertain that there were no order effects. Results show no statistically significant order effects on any of the dependent variables used in this experiment (statistical tables available upon request).

The initial appraisal scores (post-biased-cue appraisal scores) of the experimental groups were compared to the post-interview appraisal scores of the control groups as a manipulation check. The t-test results indicate that the manipulation was successful (t(38) - 6.18, $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Control groups had a mean score of 0.89, while experimental groups had a mean score of 0.26, indicating an unfavorable first impression of the department head.

The scores of the two control groups were also contrasted. There were no significant differences between the two groups $( \mathsf { t } ( 3 8 ) \ = \ 0 . 8 1 , \ \mathsf { p } \ = \ 0 . 4 2 )$ . This result suggests that without the initial biased cue, media differences did not cause any differences in judgements.

We also compared the appraisal scores between undergraduate and graduate students using a t-test to see if there were any systematic biases. There were no significant differences between the two groups for both post-biased-cue appraisal $( \mathrm { t } ( 3 8 ) = 1 . 7 6 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 9 )$ and post-interview-information appraisal (t(78) - 0.84, p - 0.40).

The scores on the remaining 10 items were aggregated into one score for further analyses.

A 3-item instrument adapted from Aldag and Power (1986) was used to measure the subjects’ confidence with their appraisal. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84. The scores on these 3 items were also aggregated into a single score for further analyses.

4.4.2. The Written Justification. Recall that at the end of the appraisal task, each subject was asked to provide a written justification of the decision made. To obtain complementary evidence to support our quantitative results, we performed a content analysis of the written justifications collected.

We developed a coding scheme based on the theory that was delineated in §2. That is, first impression bias is mediated by two processes: (1) ignoring disconfirming information, and (2) reinterpreting information to fit the first impression. Each written justification was coded for these two processes. First, it was assigned a code to indicate whether or not the subject ignored the data presented after the initial impression was created.

A “yes” was assigned when the decision was based only on the first impression cue and very little or not at all on the subsequent interview data. A “no” was assigned if the written justification provided evidence that the interview data was taken into consideration (i.e., not ignored).

Second, each written justification was assigned a code to indicate whether or not the subject reinterpreted the information presented in the interviews. A “yes” was assigned if the subjected reinterpreted/distorted the interview data to fit the first impression formed. A “no” was assigned if there was no reinterpretation of what was said in the interview. For example, in one part of the interview data, the department head under evaluation said that he “loves research and it is totally reasonable to devote 10 to 12 hours per day to conduct a clinical experiment.” If in the written justification a subject interpreted the above as “he is not able to balance between his administrative duties and research,” then “reinterpretation” was coded as “yes.” Because in itself the above statement did not indicate whether the department head was able to balance his duties or not, the subject must have reinterpreted the statement negatively to fit the negative impression formed originally, based on the biased cue.

Two raters coded the written justifications independently. The original interrater agreement was 90%, but the two raters were able to reconcile all the differences. The results reported here are based on the coding after the two raters had reconciled the differences.

The content analysis was based on 18 text-based and 19 multimedia subjects, because two subjects in the text-based condition and one in the multimedia condition did not provide any written justification.

1) Ignoring Subsequent Information. Of the 18 textbased subjects, 10 (56%) either did not mention the interview data (i.e., only mentioned the poor performance indicators as their basis of decision made) or only mentioned the interview data in passing. In contrast, only 4 of the 19 multimedia subjects (21%) either ignored or paid little attention to the interview data. This evidence suggests that text-based subjects, as compared to the multimedia subjects, were more likely to ignore information presented after they had formed their first impression.

2) Reinterpreting Subsequent Information. Of the 18 text-based subjects, 9 (50%) reinterpreted the interview data. On the other hand, only 3 of the 19 multimedia subjects (16%) reinterpreted the interview data. This finding suggests that the text-based subjects were three times as likely as the multimedia subjects to reinterpret (i.e., to fit the negative impression formed) the subsequent information presented after they formed their first impression.

Overall, multimedia experimental group subjects expressed a more positive attitude towards the department head. They mentioned his good communication skills, caring, and willingness to listen to employees’ problems, whereas text-based subjects mentioned more negative attributes, such as being inflexible and autocratic. More importantly, most text-based subjects heavily emphasized the bad performance indicators. The following example demonstrates how subjects reinterpreted the information they had seen.

In one of the interview segments with the department head, he was asked how he would go about balancing his own research and administrative duties. He expressed that he would allocate specific hours for administrative duties, but noted that as the head of a research department, his main duty is to lead a group of researchers working on a common goal of finding a cure for cancer. Overall, he expressed a balanced view of his job duties:

I guess my reaction or my approach to combining administration and research is to take what I think is an objective opinion or formulate an objective opinion on what is the minimum amount of time that is required to perform my administrative functions. Now, if that is say 10 or 15 hours a week, then I will dedicate that amount of time plus a little bit more to administrative functions. But what I would try to do is lock that in. I would try to dedicate a given number of hours to administrative functions and hopefully defined hours so that my staff and in fact everybody in the department knows that I’m wearing my administrative hat, let’s say the first three hours of every morning or two or three defined days during the week, whatever. Research, on the other hand, then occupies the rest of the time and, of course, research is openended. If one gets involved in an experiment and excited in their experiment, then 12 or 14 or 16 hours a day to finish an experiment is not uncommon and it’s totally expected.

The multimedia experimental group subjects’ reacted to the above as follows:

I feel that he makes an excellent scientist and tries his best to balance his administrative duties as well.

He is aware of his own weaknesses, i.e., balancing administrative duties with research and has a plan to combat it with setting an initial schedule but keeping it flexible allowing for the goal of important research.

He has a balanced view of his duties. Although his passion is on research, he realizes that he needs to allocate time for other administrative duties.

He is aware of his strengths and weaknesses as researcher and administrator.

The text-based experimental group subjects reacted very differently:

He just wants to do his own research. Why hire him as a Department Head to begin with?

Too much research emphasis and inflexible.

His first love is research, and he seems only willing to allot a number of hours to administration. Research—as many hours as it takes.

A Department Head should be more willing to commit more time for administrative duties. Administrative problems arise at any time. How can a schedule be fixed?

Presumably, the multimedia experimental group subjects were able to sense his commitment and serious intentions from the video clip. These subjects saw him as a reasonable person who recognized his own strengths and weaknesses but was willing to work hard to further the goals of the department. In contrast, the text-based experimental subjects focused on only certain parts of what was said and reinterpreted them in a negative way to fit their first impression.

In summary, subjects’ written justifications provide supporting evidence to our hypothesis that multimedia reduces the effect of first impression bias by (1) making information more vivid and harder to ignore, and (2) presenting information in a less ambiguous way, thus making the information less subject to reinterpretation.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

## 5.1. Discussion

The results show that the multimedia system reduces the influence of a first impression bias. Although the first impressions of both the text-based experimental group and the multimedia experimental group subjects were affected, for the multimedia system the magnitude of change was approximately double that of the text-based system.

First impression bias is maintained through two specific processes: reinterpreting and ignoring subsequent inconsistent information. Two unique characteristics of multimedia presentations, rich language and complementary cues, enable decision makers to suppress or reduce the likelihood of occurrence of these two processes, thus reducing the influence of first impression bias.

First, using a set of rich symbolic languages, multimedia presentations convey the original meaning of information in a less distorted and less ambiguous way. This clarity reduces the potential for misinterpretation and makes inconsistent information less subject to reinterpretation.

Second, multimedia systems present information in vivid ways using audio and video that provide complementary cues. The use of complementary cues leads to better retention and retrieval of information by providing more potential links to knowledge in long-term memory. As a result, the information is better registered in the decision maker’s mind, and inconsistent information becomes more difficult to ignore.

One unexpected result concerns the confidence with which the appraisal was made. Confidence with the decision is affected by the availability of information (Oskamp 1965). Since a multimedia system provides richer information and more cues than a text-based system, we expected that those who used the multimedia system would perceive that they have received more information, and thereby be more certain about their decision. However, the results do not support this prediction. We speculate that this outcome is due to the perceived inconsistency that our multimedia subjects experienced. Multimedia subjects, through the video clips, had vividly seen an image of the department head who possesses most of the good qualities of a leader, but was inconsistent with their first impression created by the bias information cue. Perhaps this inconsistency led to uncertainty and less confidence in their decisions. On the other hand, the text-based subjects maintained a more consistent impression throughout the experiment. Consequently, after viewing the interview information, the text-based subjects felt more confident than before about their appraisal. This explanation is consistent with our argument that the multimedia system helped subjects to debias their first impression bias. In contrast, the text-based system not only did not reduce the biased first impression, but appeared to further reinforce it.

Another plausible explanation to this unexpected finding is that people are less experienced in controlling video than text. Although the subjects could also scroll back in the video clips, they would have had less experience in doing this. In addition, for less experienced users, it would have had taken more time for them to scroll back to a specific location in the video. Although there was no time limit placed on performing the experimental task, it is still possible that the subjects were less likely to scroll back the videos than the text. Therefore, text subjects may have felt more confident that they had a good understanding of the materials. More research is needed to explore this issue.

A specific point that warrants some discussion is the practical significance of the study. Although we observed that the multimedia system (but not the textbased system) was capable of reducing the influence of first impression bias, as reported in Figure 3, the average change in the multimedia subject’s appraisal score was only 1.0 (from 0.33 to 0.67). Since the magnitude of change (in this case, 1.0 on a 7-point scale) was small, the practical significance of this result may be questionable.

However, in interpreting this result one has to consider the “ceiling” effect of the “true appraisal score” for the target person. The appraisal scores given by subjects from the two control groups (in the absence of the influence of the biased information cue) could be assumed to represent the “true score” of the target person. The department head’s ability to manage the department was appraised to be at 0.94 (averaging 0.84 and 1.03, the appraisal scores given by the two control groups, as shown in Table 2). This score represents the “true score” without the influence of a biased information cue. This score was the “ceiling” score (imposed by the information conveyed in the interview information regarding the target person as an effective department head) for subjects in the experimental groups.

Using the same method, the “floor” was 0.27 (averaging 0.2 and 0.33, the two initial scores from Figure 3). Therefore, the actual scale ranges from 0.27 to 0.94 rather than 3 to 3. In light of this, the change in appraisal score of 1.0 was actually fairly substantial (approximately 84% of the range), and arguably has a practical significance.

## 5.2. Limitations

One limitation of this experiment that might have confounded the findings is that is it possible that people tend to identify more with someone, and are more conscientious when performing the task, when they “see” that person. Since multimedia subjects, but not the text-based subjects, saw (in videos) the department head under appraisal, it is possible that the multimedia subjects identified more with the department head than the text-based subjects. As a result, the multimedia subjects might have been more sympathetic or more serious in performing the appraisal, thus potentially creating a secondary bias.

Prior to performing the experimental task, half of the subjects had seen the department head on video in a previous experimental task. If this secondary bias had indeed occurred, the appraisal outcomes for those subjects who had seen the department head would have been different from those who had not seen him. However, a statistical analysis revealed no significant order effect. This rules out the possibility that multimedia system subjects gave the department head a better appraisal because they identified more with him.

A second limitation is associated with the audio cues in the text transcript used in the text-based system. In designing the text-based system, we felt that we should preserve as much detail of what was being said as possible. At one extreme, one could add annotated comments in parentheses (e.g., jokingly, sadly, hesitantly) to the transcript. However, by doing so, we would impose specific interpretation of the speech to the subjects. Therefore, we decided to draw the line at providing as much detail as possible, but without imposing our interpretation.

With this principle in mind, we included everything that was said by the department head, including verbal cues such “Eh,” but added no annotations. However, this is just one specific implementation of the system along the two extremes that were mentioned above. In interpreting the results this operationalization has to be taken into consideration. It is possible the audio cues in text may have made the speaker appear inept.

A third limitation is that the control and experimental groups did not receive equal amount of information. However, this disparity is inherent in the design of the study—we needed to introduce the biased cue to create a negative first impression. Most importantly, the focus of this study is to examine the media effect, i.e., the differences between text and multimedia in the experimental groups. The control groups served the major purpose of showing that the experimental manipulation was successful.

A fourth limitation is the low statistical power associated with some of the tests. Specifically, the estimated power for system type main effect associated with the confidence scores (Table 3, also see Footnote 10) is inadequate. Given the low power, we cannot conclude unequivocally that there was no difference between the two system types used in term of inducing confidence levels. However, the power level for system type main effect associated with appraisal scores shown in Table 1 is not a concern because the hypothesis of interest was the interaction effect (which has an acceptable power level). Further, the analysis in Footnote 9 shows that the actual power of this main effect was 0.64, which is an acceptable value.

Finally, as was mentioned in the Theory section, multimedia can be a double-edged sword. Consider the case where the negative performance indicator is the true representation of a department head’s performance. A manager with good self-presentation skills can take advantage of the technology and create a positive yet false impression of his performance. The contrary may also occur. For a manager with poor selfpresentation skills, multimedia may create an even more negative impression than text-based cues. In such cases, the use of multimedia may have an adverse effect in organizational decision making. This is another area that needs additional research effort.

## 5.3. Conclusions

The psychology literature has portrayed first impression bias as a virtually inherent human bias. The major contribution of this study is to show that the influence of first impression bias can be reduced. The multimedia format presents information in a vivid way so that decision makers are more likely to perceive inconsistencies and less likely to ignore or reinterpret them. However, it needs to be emphasized that in this study multimedia substantially reduced the influence of first impression bias rather than eliminating it totally. Interestingly, however, in the absence of the biased cue there was no difference between multimedia and text groups. We see this research effort as a step toward building our knowledge on understanding this powerful, yet understudied, technology.

Capturing organizational information, providing an organizational memory system of this information in its original or summarized forms, and making it widely available and accessible through means such as intranets is becoming commonplace in many organizations. Although cost and technical difficulties may currently prohibit the widespread adoption of multimedia technology for capturing and presenting organizational information, this is likely to change as organizations become more familiar with its advantages and with the continuing improvements in price/performance ratio of information technology.

This research has several practical implications for the design of organizational memory systems. First, as there are increasing opportunities to retrieve and peruse information in flexible ways, decision makers and systems designers, should be cognizant of the fact that the order in which information is examined could bias one’s assessment of an event or individual. Decision makers should be advised to consider perusing information more than once, in a different sequence, to reduce any first impression bias that might have occurred.

Second, in deciding in which medium to store or present organizational information, designers have to consider how to minimize the effect of first impression bias so as to support a more informed decision-making process. This study has demonstrated that multimedia can be used to further enhance the capability of the current text-based information systems, especially where reduction of original biases is an issue of concern. Third, decision makers and designers should be aware that the nonverbal aspects of multimedia could be manipulated to create a more positive view of an entity or event than the factual information warrants. Similarly, a manager with poor presentation skills may convey a more negative impression than the objective data would imply.
