# Using Repertory Grids to Conduct Cross-Cultural Information Systems Research

M. Gordon Hunter • John E. Beck

St. Francis Xavier University, P.O. Box 5000, Antigonish, Nova Scotia, Canada B2G 2W5 ghunter@stfx.ca

Division of Human Resource and Quality Management, School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 2263 aebjohn@ntu.edu.sg

s more business is being conducted internationally and corporations establish themselves globally, the impact of cross-cultural aspects becomes an important research issue. The need to conduct cross-cultural research is perhaps even more important in the relatively newly emerging and quickly changing information systems (IS) field. This article presents issues relating to qualitative research, emic versus etic approaches, and describes a structured, yet flexible, qualitative research interviewing technique, which decreases the potential for bias on the part of the researcher. The grounded theory technique presented in this article is based on Kelly’s Repertory Grid (RepGrid), which concentrates on “laddering,” or the further elaboration of elicited constructs, to obtain detailed research participant comments about an aspect within the domain of discourse. The technique provides structure to a “one-to-one” interview. But, at the same time, RepGrids allow sufficient flexibility for the research participants to be able to express their own interpretation about a particular topic. This article includes a brief outline of a series of research projects that employed the RepGrid technique to examine similarities and differences in the way in which “excellent” systems analysts are viewed in two different cultures. Also included is a discussion of the technique’s applicability for qualitative research in general and cross-cultural studies specifically. The article concludes by suggesting ways in which the RepGrid technique addresses some of the major methodological issues in cross-cultural research.

## 1. Introduction

More business is being conducted on an international basis and more corporations are establishing themselves globally. The question arises, then, regarding whether the processes developed for one specific location can be transferred to another. Do they survive more or less intact as they cross national and cultural boundaries, or are they changed when they are implemented at different locations? This globalization process suggests the need for more cross-cultural research. This need is perhaps even more important in the newly emerging and quickly changing information systems (IS) field. These issues are not merely of academic interest, but also have valuable practical implications. In an increasingly interdependent world there exists the need for a well-integrated and coherent IS which will coordinate the activity of multinational corporations, international agencies, and governments. Is it likely that common processes will become established worldwide? Projects that investigate these aspects reside within the general category of cross-cultural research.

A series of articles discuss research regarding various cross-cultural issues relating to information systems. On one hand, some articles compare what transpires in each country, employing such quantitative survey techniques as reviews of public data or questionnaires. These articles include: Burn et al. (1993), cross-national dimensions of information management; Straub (1994), use of information technologies; Tan and Igbaria (1994), turnover and remuneration of IS personnel; Slaughter and Ang (1995), IS employment structures; and Ein-Dor et al. (1993), cultural variables affecting information systems. On the other hand, a qualitative approach has been taken in some instances. Case studies have been employed by Barrett and Walsham (1995) to investigate issues surrounding aspects of information technology management of a Jamaican insurance company. Harvey (1997) compared interpretations of information technology theory between Germany and the United States. Also, Hill et al. (1998) employed a field study to investigate information technology transfer in the Arab culture.

While it is acknowledged that these articles have provided a valuable contribution of knowledge to cross-cultural IS research, it remains important to attempt to adopt a research method that reflects a consideration for the project objectives and the specific situation. Perhaps, then, consideration should be given to other research approaches. Indeed, research by Fitzgerald et al. (1985), Klein and Lyytinen (1985), and more recently Hirschheim (1992) suggests that because the IS field is so new, many different methods of investigation should be considered when initiating a research project.

Currently, there exists a growing interest amongst IS researchers in various qualitative research techniques. Since 1991 the annual International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) has held a series of panel discussions on aspects relating to qualitative research. These workshops represent an expanding forum for IS researchers who are considering or actively applying qualitative research. The research results of Lending and Wetherbe (1992) suggest an increase in the application of qualitative research techniques, as indicated by journal publications. More recently, Gallupe and Tan (1999) investigated the types of methods employed when conducting global information management research. They propose a framework in response to their determination that research in the area is somewhat disjointed. As a consequence of proposing their framework, they also challenge global IS researchers to use alternative qualitative techniques, such as ethnography, interpretive epistemology, or grounded theory.

While qualitative research provides some interesting methodological approaches, it is important to ensure the specific technique adopted is one that reflects a consideration for the specific objectives of the project. Galliers and Land (1987) have proposed that IS research “methods must take account of the nature of the subject and the complexity of the real world” (Galliers and Land 1987 p. 901). It is important to note that both qualitative and quantitative research approaches are useful, but for different reasons. In general, qualitative research is considered more appropriate for theory building, whereas quantitative research relates more to theory testing. Within the overall context of a research program the two approaches are complementary. The most important consideration, as subsequently suggested by Galliers (1992), is that the research method adopted be chosen in light of the research objectives.

This article describes a qualitative research technique that, it is proposed, addresses the concerns of conducting cross-cultural IS research. First, some general comments regarding the approach to conducting cross-cultural research are presented. Then, the proposed technique is described as it was employed for a series of research projects. Finally, conclusions are presented regarding the technique and its usefulness for cross-cultural IS research.

## 2. Research Approach

In general, with respect to conducting cross-cultural research there exist two competing hypotheses discussed by Webber (1969), Yang (1986), and Ronen (1986). The first hypothesis suggests that as a result of globalization, industrialized societies throughout the world will converge and become more similar to one another. Technology, education, and training represent examples of the forces that are creating this convergence. In contrast, the divergence hypothesis suggests that cultures tend to be resistant to change as societies strive to retain their cultural distinctiveness. This article does not address the resolution of these competing hypotheses. What is suggested is a technique that should be considered when conducting cross-cultural research within the domain of both hypotheses.

Another issue when conducting cross-cultural research relates to the distinction between an emic versus etic approach. These concepts were originated based on linguistic research by Pike (1954) and have been further explained by Berry (1990) and Headland et al. (1990). In essence, an emic approach adopts a single framework within one culture based on criteria internal to the culture. An etic approach assesses and compares frameworks relative to specific cultures based on universal criteria. The issue of which approach to adopt is further complicated by the introduction of another term, “pseudo-etic” (Triandis 1972), which suggests constructs from a limited number of cultures could be used to logically deduce those constructs and their relationships that are considered universal. Thus, the most effective research approach with respect to this issue will be one that incorporates both emic and etic elements. That is, cross-cultural researchers may replicate emic studies in other cultures from which it may be possible to evolve an etic model of universal constructs. Finally, as Earley and Mosakowski (1995) suggest “What seems to be the most useful approach to conducting IM (International Management) and ICO (Intercultural Organizational) research is to focus on the pseudo-etic approach to develop quasi-universal constructs which may be subsequently challenged to more universal tests of validity” (Earley and Mosakowski 1995 p. 9). These authors further suggest that the ideal cross-cultural research method is one that incorporates both emic and etic elements. This may be accomplished by starting from an emic base and then conducting emic studies in other cultures from which it might be possible to evolve an etic model of universal constructs based on the similarities and differences that emerge from the emic data. It will be shown later that the research technique presented in this document represents an emic approach to conducting cross-cultural research.

Hofstede (1980) has researched the nature and extent of cultural differences in a landmark study. He conceptualizes “culture” as the “collective programming of the mind”. As individuals grow up in any culture, their assumptions about the way a society should be organized are instilled into them. These taken-forgranted assumptions are rarely questioned, unless perhaps there is contact with a different culture that operates on different or incompatible assumptions. In pursuing the implications of his research, Hofstede (1980, 1983, 1988, 1990, and 1993) has shown that certain management practices can be compatible and others incompatible with the culture of a society. He has suggested that cultural incompatibility can undermine the successful transfer of accepted Western managerial practices to developing countries.

The research described in this article compares the differing perceptions of the role of “excellent” systems analysts between samples gathered in Canada and Singapore. In terms of Hofstede’s dimensions, Singapore and Canada are societies that are unlike one another in some important ways. Canada is a highly individualistic society, whereas Singapore is much more collectivist in social relationships. In terms of Power Distance, Canada is a small Power Distance country, whereas Singaporeans are more comfortable with a relatively unequal distribution of power. In Hofstede’s sample, Singapore was the society that had the weakest Uncertainty Avoidance score. However, Canada is also a society that is relatively comfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity. Finally, there is very little difference between the two cultures on the Masculinity— Femininity dimension. Therefore, these two cultures show quite marked differences on two of Hofstede’s dimensions, but lesser differences on the other two dimensions.

## 3. Research Method

The approach to conducting this research takes into consideration those aspects indicated previously regarding cross-cultural, qualitative investigations. A specific technique within the Theory of Personal Constructs has been adopted. The Theory of Personal Constructs was first proposed by George Kelly (1955, 1963), a psychologist, based on his work in attempting to help individuals (clients, as he referred to them) analyze their own interpersonal relationships. He regarded individuals as “scientists” and believed that in order for them to gain control of their environment, it was necessary to be able to interpret and predict (i.e., construe). Thus, Kelly proposed that individuals, based upon their past experience, would devise a personal construct system, which would assist them in dealing with current or anticipated situations. A technique developed by Kelly to study personal construct systems is the Role Construct Repertory Test (RepGrid). While the RepGrid technique was originally devised to assist in counselling his clients, this technique has subsequently been applied in a number of other areas. Some articles discuss the use of the RepGrid technique in general or for specific problem construction and market research (e.g., Bannister and Mair 1968, Corsini and Marsella 1983, Eden and Jones 1984, Eden and Wheaton 1980, Fransella 1981, Shaw 1980). RepGrids have also been used for various aspects of knowledge acquisition in relation to the development of expert systems (Botten et al. 1989, Latta and Swigger 1992, Phythian and King 1992).

The specific projects where RepGrids were applied consisted of 70 research participants at three fieldwork locations in Canada and 17 research participants at one location in Singapore (Hunter 1993, 1994). While opportunity played a part in the determination of the fieldwork locations, the selection process also allowed for variability by geographic region, industry, and culture. Within each location, five groups (see Figure 1) of research participants were identified that, because of their interaction with systems analysts, could provide an assessment of the performance of systems analysts. One-to-one interviews were conducted which maintained confidentiality and allowed the researcher to pursue research participant comments to a fair degree of detail.

At the beginning of the interview the research participants were asked to talk about their experience in working with systems analysts. This discussion allowed the research participants to focus their thoughts on the qualities of systems analysts. Then the RepGrid technique was introduced. A completed RepGrid is shown in Figure 2. The technique was reviewed in general, and related to systems analysts. Each research participant was asked to identify up to six systems analysts with whom he or she had interacted in the current or a previous organization. The names of the actual systems analysts plus the two added elements were transferred to individual recipe cards. With one name or title per card, the research participant was presented with three of the eight cards. The three cards are generally known as a “triad” in the literature. So that each of the eight cards has an equal chance of being included in a triad, the three cards to be included are chosen randomly. For each triad, the research participant was asked to identify, with regards to the skills of an “excellent” systems analyst, how two elements of the triad were alike, yet different from the third. The research participant was encouraged to physically separate the two cards from the third and to verbalize the decision process. These descriptive comments were noted regarding the differentiation within each triad.

This method of “triading” promotes a discussion of similarity and contrast, which is how Kelly viewed all constructs. A construct, it should be noted, is a complex image or idea. Kelly believed that understanding the nature of a construct requires knowledge of both the similarity and the contrast regarding a triad of elements. The word or phrase (verbal tag) used to describe both the similarity and the contrast is determined solely by the research participant. Taken together, the similarity and contrast represent a bipolar description (“dichotomous construct”) relating to one component of the investigation.

Also, interview notes were taken in support of the RepGrid construct. Figure 3 represents the notes, which support the third construct from Figure 2. It should be noted that both audio and video recording had been considered but rejected because of the potentially limiting influence on the participant during the interview. So, handwritten interview notes were taken.

A “laddering” technique (Stewart and Stewart 1981) was employed to encourage the research participant to enunciate the elicited construct. The technique incorporates a series of “how” questions, which results in further detail about the construct and increases the understanding of what the research participant means.

Continuing with the interview, the research participant was then asked to rate each systems analyst and the two added elements in relation to the elicited construct. The research participant was encouraged to physically place all eight cards beside each other, representing relative variation of the elements with regards to the specific construct. Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest this technique helps research participants decide which elements are similar and which are different relative to a specific construct. The research participant was then asked to assign a number to each card based on a 9-point rating scale, where 1 represented the positive end of the construct and 9 represented the negative end. Stewart and Stewart (1981) suggest acceptable scales range from 2 points to 9 points. A 9-point scale was chosen for this research project for the following reasons. First, the scale is composed of an odd number, thus allowing a mid-point, which provides research participants with a reference point when rating systems analysts. Second, a 9-point scale provides sufficient flexibility for rating the eight elements associated with this research project. That is, with a 9-point scale the research participant could, if desired, rate each systems analyst separately. With any smaller scale, the research participant would be forced to give equal rates to some systems analysts. These numbers were entered into the RepGrid as representative of the participant’s differentiation of the systems analysts. The results of this rating process are shown in Figure 2 as the numbers in the cells of the RepGrid.

When the elicitation process was complete, the research participant was asked to rank the identified constructs. The ranking was accomplished using a paired-comparison technique. Ranking the constructs in this way provides information about the research participant’s personal view of their relative importance.

At the end of each interview, when the RepGrid was completed, a preliminary confirmation was obtained regarding which actual systems analysts were considered by the research participant to be most like the research participant’s interpretation of “ideal” and “incompetent”. This process entailed the visual comparison of columns in the completed RepGrid. This technique is similar to what Stewart and Stewart (1981) refer to as “visual focusing” which attempts to identify those elements or constructs which are the most similar or most different. In all interviews, the researcher’s preliminary RepGrid interpretation and the research participant’s interpretation were the same. This comparison seemed to be an appropriate method to confirm, at the end of an interview, that what had been recorded fairly represented what the research participant had stated during the interview.

## 4. Discussion

RepGrids generate a large amount of rich, in-depth, qualitative and narrative data relating to a research participant’s explanation of an elicited construct. The documentation of the research participant’s explanations as interview notes forms the basis of the research data for this project. Detailed comments were recorded for each pole of the elicited construct. The researchers determined a system of hierarchies for each construct that depicted the relationships, within the interview notes, between an elicited construct, at the RepGrid level, and a detailed action statement, at the interview note level.

Extensive interview notes were taken which provided descriptive support for each construct in the RepGrid. The vast amount of rich qualitative data was subsequently analyzed to identify emerging themes. Common or universal themes emerged from the data, which represents the research participants’ interpretation of what constitutes an “excellent” systems analyst. Overall, the relatively more important themes that emerged included communication, attitude, knowledge, investigation, and experience. Further, the relative level of importance of these universal themes reflected a “local” interpretation. For instance, business professionals (represented by users, sponsors, and clients) emphasized content related themes, such as knowledge. IS professionals (represented by systems analysts and business systems managers), however, differentiated on process related themes, such as investigation.

A subsequent analysis of the themes in conjunction with the replication conducted in Singapore has lead Hunter and Beck (1994) to conclude that there are indications of the emergence of an occupational community that transcends cultural boundaries. First, there was a commonality regarding the major themes that emerged from the research data. This suggests that there is a shared concept of the roles and professional qualities of a systems analyst. Further, this evidence suggests that systems analysts have a recognizable professional identity, which supports the emergence of an international occupational community. Second, there were, however, differences in emphasis between the two cultures. The Singapore systems analysts were more likely to take a “technocratic” approach by relying upon their professional attitudes and expertise to influence clients. This suggests the systems analysts in Singapore would become the more dominant participants in determining the direction of the information systems project. The Canadian systems analysts acted more like “consultants” in a partnership relationship with a client. Thus, in Canada, the client is more likely to be encouraged to participate in the information systems design process.

Stewart and Stewart suggest, “At its simplest, Grids provide a way of doing research into problems— almost any problems—in a more precise, less biased, way than any other research method” (1981 p. vii). These authors also suggest the RepGrid technique “enables one to interview someone in detail, extracting a good deal of information . . . and to do this in such a way that the input from the observer is reduced to zero” (Stewart and Stewart 1981 p. 5). Further, Pervin states, “There have been studies of the reliability of the Rep Test and the evidence to date suggests that the responses of individuals to the role title list and constructs used are reasonably stable over time” (1989 p. 271). Thus, RepGrids, as used in this research project, have been proven to be a reliable technique to gather qualitative data.

The research participants determined their own list of elements and also were able to decide on their own constructs as well as indicate their interpretation of the elicited constructs. Common RepGrids were not used because this would have forced the research participants to respond to previously identified (and therefore potentially biased) elements and constructs. While each RepGrid is therefore unique, common themes were identified via the subsequent analysis of the detailed interview notes. Thus it is possible, using the technique described above, to be able to offer the research participant the fullest amount of freedom to comment upon a subject, yet still maintain a structured method to the data gathering process.

In that the initial research and the replication are making a cross-cultural comparison of the perceived qualities of “excellent” systems analysts between a Canadian and Singaporean sample, it is what Berry (1980) and Ronen (1986) define as an “etic” study. However, the methodology used is based on Grounded Theory (Glaser and Strauss 1967), which suggests that categories and their properties should emerge from the data, rather than being influenced by the a priori adoption of a theoretical framework. That is, the RepGrid technique grounds the data within the culture of the research participant. So, in the data gathering stage, researcher bias is reduced (Stewart and Stewart 1981) because the research participant decides on the construct label and through “laddering” provides further descriptive detail regarding their interpretation of the construct. Also, subsequent data analysis, while conducted by the researchers, was based on the content of the comments made by the research participants from within the culture. This kind of approach is more consistent with an “emic” paradigm of cross-cultural research (Berry 1980, Ronen 1986). The cross-cultural comparison is therefore between what are essentially two emic studies conducted in two different cultures.

## 5. Conclusion

The RepGrid technique may be employed in many domains. Therefore, it is important to consider the research objectives in relation to both element selection and construct elicitation. First, element selection relates to identifying the entities, which will ground the domain of discourse for each research participant. The research participants should have sufficient experience within the domain of discourse to be able to enunciate and elaborate upon their chosen elements. Further, each selected element must be unique so that the research participant will be able to compare and contrast mutually exclusive entities. The research reported here employed elements representing systems analysts known by the research participant or concepts of systems analysts as interpreted by the research participant. Depending upon the research objectives, element categories seem to be limited only by the researcher’s creativity. For example, if the domain of discourse is strategy—IT alignment, the element categories could be strategic initiatives. Further, projects could represent element categories for an investigation into project management. The researcher should determine the element categories in concert with the objectives of the project. The specific entities will be contingent on the research participant’s experience within the domain of discourse.

Second, the construct elicitation process should randomly consider triadic elements to elicit dichotomous constructs. The subsequent application of the process of laddering will allow the researcher to probe further into the research participant’s interpretation of the elicited construct.

The research technique described in this document is considered significant regarding the general method of conducting an interview to gather research data. The method adopted has addressed the research objectives to investigate the skills of “excellent” systems analysts. A qualitative approach was employed, which also allowed the consideration of a cross-cultural perspective. It is considered very important to realize that the emergence of the general commonality, as well as the divergent specificity, was made entirely possible through the employment of the RepGrid technique, for RepGrids support the gathering of research participants’ comments in a relatively unbiased manner. It is possible then to analyze the data to identify emerging themes and, in turn, to use the data to also define these same emerging themes. This method is supported by the underlying concepts expounded by Grounded Theory (Glaser and Strauss 1967).
