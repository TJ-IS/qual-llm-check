# How Do We Understand a System with (So) Many Diagrams? Cognitive Integration Processes in Diagrammatic Reasoning

Jinwoo Kim • Jungpil Hahn • Hyoungmee Hahn

Yonsei University, Department of Business Administration, Seoul, 120–749, Korea

University of Minnesota, Information and Decision Sciences, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455

LG Hitachi, Seoul, 150–756, Korea

jinwoo@base.yonsei.ac.kr • jhahn@csom.umn.edu • hmhahn@lghitachi.co.kr

n order to understand diagrammatic reasoning with multiple diagrams, this study proposes a theoretical framework that focuses on the cognitive processes of perceptual and conceptual integration. The perceptual integration process involves establishing interdependence between relevant system elements that have been dispersed across multiple diagrams, while the conceptual integration process involves generating and refining hypotheses about a system by combining higher-level information inferred from the diagrams. This study applies a diagrammatic reasoning framework of a single diagram to assess the usability of multiple diagrams as an integral part of a system development methodology. Our experiment evaluated the effectiveness and usability of design guidelines to aid problem solving with multiple diagrams. The results of our experiment revealed that understanding a system represented by multiple diagrams involves a process of searching for related information and of developing hypotheses about the target system. The results also showed that these perceptual and conceptual integration processes were facilitated by incorporating visual cues and contextual information in the multiple diagrams as representation aids. Visual cues indicate which elements in a diagram are related to elements in other diagrams; the contextual information indicates how the individual datum in one diagram is related to the overall hypothesis about the entire system.

(Diagrammatic Reasoning; System Analysis and Design; Business Engineering; Cognitive Integration)

## 1. Introduction

Diagrams are widely used in everyday life (e.g., Iwasaki 1995, Larkin and Simon 1987). The role and effectiveness of diagrams for understanding a system have been investigated in various areas, such as qualitative reasoning (Forbus et al. 1991), geometric problem solving (Koedinger and Anderson 1990), reasoning about economic markets (Tabachneck et al. 1994), medical diagnosis (Rogers 1996), scientific discovery (Cheng and Simon 1995), and understanding mechanical systems (Narayanan et al. 1995).

Diagrams are abstractions of real systems. They typically focus on only one system perspective to enhance understanding of that perspective. When the system to be represented is complex, multiple, interrelated diagrams are used to represent different perspectives of the system. Construction blueprints, for example, typically use different diagrams for the structural, electrical, plumbing, and heating perspectives of a building. Business system reengineering methodologies (Davenport 1993, Hammer and Champy 1993, Jacobson 1995) typically use multiple diagrams to represent different system perspectives, such as workflows and reporting relationships, to facilitate understanding of how information technology can be used to improve business processes. Information systems development (Jordan and Machesky 1990) and software engineering (Pressman 1992) methodologies similarly utilize multiple diagrams (Booch 1994, Jacobson 1995) to represent the same system from different perspectives (Rumbaugh et al. 1991) or at different levels of abstraction (Coad and Yourdon 1990).

Although multiple interrelated diagrams are used extensively in the real world (Cheng 1996), few studies have dealt with the cognitive process involved in utilizing them. Prior research on diagrammatic reasoning has focused on reasoning within a single type of diagram, investigating how the diagram supports search, recognition, and inference in reasoning, and what cognitive processes are involved in reasoning with it (Larkin and Simon 1987). Studies that have investigated issues arising from the use of multiple diagrams (e.g., McDonald and Stevenson 1996) have used multiple displays of the same type of diagram, each showing a small portion of the whole target (Woods and Watts 1997). Issues concerning the coordination of multiple diagrams of different types, the integration of information dispersed across multiple diagrams representing different system perspectives, and the problem of extracting information from multiple diagrams of different types remain largely unstudied (Woods 1995).

The lack of research addressing the use of multiple diagrams is particularly detrimental to the success of business engineering projects, where different system perspectives are represented in different diagrams and it is crucial for designers to understand each perspective. Moreover, most business engineering projects require a greater number of diagrams than other types of system analysis projects, because one needs to understand and design both the business processes and the information systems that support them (Jacobson 1995, Taylor 1995).

The objective of this study is to explore the cognitive processes used by an individual to gain understanding of a complex system when it is represented in multiple diagrams. This objective is pursued by (1) proposing a theoretical framework of the cognitive processes used to integrate multiple diagrams, (2) using the framework to develop design guidelines for multiple diagrams in business engineering, and (3) conducting an experimental study to examine the cognitive processes of utilizing multiple diagrams and to verify the usability of these design guidelines. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant theories of diagrammatic reasoning within a single diagram and presents our theoretical framework by extending the theory to reasoning with multiple diagrams. We also propose guidelines for the design of diagrammatic representations that facilitate reasoning with multiple diagrams. Section 3 provides details of the experimental study. Section 4 presents the results of the experiment. Section 5 concludes the paper with a discussion of the limitations and implications of our study.

## 2. The Cognitive Integration Process with Multiple Diagrams

## 2.1. Perceptual and Conceptual Processes in Diagrammatic Reasoning

Diagrammatic reasoning is the study of how humans use diagrammatic (pictorial) representations in problem solving and reasoning (Chandrasekaran et al. 1995). The cognitive processes in diagrammatic reasoning consist of perceptual and conceptual processes (Larkin and Simon 1987, Narayanan et al. 1995, Rogers 1996). The perceptual process is a bottom-up activity of sensing something and knowing its meaning and value (Bolles 1991), while the conceptual process is a top-down activity of generating and refining hypotheses (Simon and Lea 1974). In other words, we search and recognize relevant information through perceptual processes and reason by inferring and deriving new information through conceptual processes.

The use of a diagram to support reasoning and problem solving is effective when it assists the perceptual and conceptual processes (Rogers 1996). For example, Larkin and Simon found that a well-designed diagrammatic representation is better than a sentential representation, because the diagrammatic representation helps both the perceptual and conceptual processes in problem solving. In terms of the perceptual process, the diagrammatic representation helps people identify and recognize relevant items, because it groups all relevant information, thus avoiding a prolonged search for elements required for problem solving. In terms of conceptual process, the diagrammatic representation helps people generate and test hypotheses, because it provides a large number of perceptual inference cues, which are extremely easy for humans to use (Larkin and Simon 1987).

To fully exploit the potential of a diagram, it must be effectively utilized in both the perceptual and conceptual processes. For example, Rogers (1995, 1996) compared the cognitive processes of high-performance and low-performance subjects diagnosing possible diseases based on x-ray films. High-performance subjects exhibited a two-way interaction between the conceptual and perceptual processes, which led to a fairly efficient convergence upon a correct solution. On the other hand, low-performance subjects gathered a great deal of perceptual information about the primary symptom, but failed to generate diagnostic hypotheses. Hence, the combination of perceptual and conceptual aspects of the diagnosis process resulted in a correct diagnosis, whereas an emphasis on only one aspect of the diagnostic process led to an incorrect diagnosis.

Simply providing a diagram does not guarantee good performance. Diagrams are effective when they facilitate both the perceptual and the conceptual processes of reasoning. In the following section, we extend our discussion to reasoning with multiple diagrams and propose guidelines for the design of diagrams to facilitate reasoning when the task requires the use of multiple diagrams.

2.2. Integration Processes with Multiple Diagrams As a target system becomes more complex, we need more than a single diagram to represent it. Multiple diagrams depict the same system from different perspectives (Booch 1994), at different levels of abstraction (Coad and Yourdon 1990), or for different functional roles (Cheng 1996). For example, in business engineering, different diagrams emphasize unique perspectives (Curtis et al. 1992). Diagrams from the functional perspective represent what process elements are being performed, while those from the behavioral perspective represent when and how they are performed. On the other hand, organizational diagrams show who and where actions are performed within an organization, and diagrams from the informational perspective indicate the informational entities produced or manipulated by the processes. Another example is that most object-oriented methodologies model a system from the static, the dynamic, and the functional perspectives (Rumbaugh et al. 1991). Models from the static perspective describe the static structure of a system in terms of objects and their relationships in a system. Models from the dynamic perspective describe the aspects of a system that change over time. Finally, models from the functional perspective describe the data value transformations within a system. These three perspectives are orthogonal views of the complete system, which, when combined, create a thorough understanding of a system (Pennington 1987). Similarly, Cheng proposed 12 functional roles for diagrams and argued that multiple diagrams were needed to provide comprehensive coverage of these 12 roles.

Multiple diagrams are designed to facilitate human understanding and communication for each constituent diagram, because each individual diagram organizes relevant constructs from a single consistent perspective. However, ease of understanding individual parts of a target system (i.e., one diagram) does not guarantee ease of understanding the system as a whole (i.e., the whole set of multiple diagrams). As the number of diagrams increases, the problem solver can only see a small portion of the total system at a time or a very small number of the available displays (Cook et al. 1991). This property is referred to as the keyhole effect (Woods and Watts 1997). The keyhole effect complicates perceptual and conceptual processes in diagrammatic reasoning. Considering the perceptual process, the ease of search and recognition achieved by a single diagrammatic representation may no longer apply because information about a particular object is distributed across several diagrams. The problem solver, therefore, has to navigate multiple displays to search and relate items that are dispersed among different diagrams (Woods and Watts 1997). Similarly, with the conceptual process, the activity of generating and refining hypotheses about a target system becomes more difficult because all necessary information for problem solving may not be readily available in a single diagram. Therefore, in order to attain a thorough understanding of the target system, the problem solver must navigate different diagrams in order to search for relevant information and integrate it into a coherent representation of the target system. However, the navigation process cannot be performed randomly or sequentially because not all diagrams convey information meaningful for the problem solver’s current hypothesis and the set of diagrams containing meaningful information about that particular hypothesis is usually a subset of the set of all the diagrams available. When the number of diagrams to investigate exceeds a problem solver’s span of control, the limited capacity of human working memory (Baddeley 1986) makes it practically impossible for the problem solver to retain all information from all diagrams to reason successfully about the target system. We need to devise a better way to assist the reasoning process with multiple diagrams in order to utilize multiple diagrams more efficiently (Woods and Watts 1997).

Reasoning with multiple diagrams involves the integration of information dispersed across multiple displays in order to construct a coherent representation about the target system. We argue that the need for effective perceptual and conceptual processes in reasoning with a single diagram also applies to multiple diagrams. In other words, we need to perform effective perceptual and conceptual processes in integrating information distributed across multiple diagrams. Both the perceptual integration and the conceptual integration processes should be conducted effectively in order to reason successfully with multiple diagrams. With multiple diagrams, the perceptual integration process consists of linking relevant visual items that are dispersed across various diagrams of different perspectives, whereas the conceptual integration process consists of generating and refining hypotheses about a target system with various information inferred from different diagrams.

There are several ways in which diagrams can be designed to facilitate the perceptual and conceptual processes in reasoning with multiple diagrams. In order to perform perceptual integration successfully, we need to identify which diagram to look at and also recognize the relevant items in the target diagram.

Therefore, the visual representation of a diagram must provide effective visual cues that indicate how an item in one diagram is related to other items in different diagrams. These visual cues will help users navigate to a diagram that is related to his/her current hypothesis about the target system. Providing visible cues for diagrams has been suggested as a way of improving “visual momentum,” which decreases the mental effort required to reason with multiple diagrams (Woods and Watts 1997). Second, in order to perform conceptual integration effectively, we need to discern whether the data provided in various diagrams is important to the attending hypothesis. The relative importance of a single datum cannot be judged by itself, but should be put into context to assess its relative value (Woods 1995). One way to provide the contextual information is to provide a “long shot,” which shows the big picture of the entire system (Woods and Watts 1997). Therefore, a long shot diagram with contextual information that indicates how an individual element in one diagram is related to the overall hypothesis of the entire system should be provided. This contextual information will help the problem solver evaluate his/ her current hypothesis in relation to the entire system.

In summary, we argue that both perceptual and conceptual integration processes should be conducted effectively in order to understand a system represented in multiple diagrams from different perspectives. Perceptual integration links relevant visual items across multiple diagrams. This helps relate information across different diagrams. Concomitantly, conceptual integration generates and refines hypotheses about the target systems with the information acquired from perceptual integration. A well-balanced integration in both the perceptual and conceptual processes is essential to a successful understanding of a system represented with multiple diagrams. An experiment was conducted to test the effects of these design guidelines (i.e., visual cues and contextual information) on the cognitive processes of reasoning with multiple diagrams.

## 3. Experiment Design

In this section, we explain the task domain and materials that were used in our experiment, as well as the data analysis method employed in this study.

## 3.1. Subjects

The subjects were senior undergraduate students enrolled in a semester-long business-engineering course at Yonsei University. In order to assess the effects of visual cues and contextual information in the multiple diagrams, subjects needed to be trained in systems analysis and design methodologies using different diagrammatic representations. The experiment was, thus, conducted over a period of two semesters. During the first semester, nine students were taught an object-oriented methodology, which did not support visual cues and contextual information in the diagrams (Rumbaugh et al. 1991).<sup>1</sup> These subjects will be referred to as the control group. During the second semester, seven students were taught an object-oriented methodology, which supported visual cues and contextual information (Rational 1996). These subjects will be referred to as the experimental group. The subjects participated in the experiment at the end of their respective business-engineering course, by which time they had completed several homework assignments and a class project. None of the subjects in either the control or experimental groups had difficulties in understanding the diagrams provided as the experimental material, because the diagrams were similar to those practiced in class.

## 3.2. Task Domain

This study focuses on the use of multiple diagrams in object-oriented business engineering (OOBE). OOBE is defined as the application of object technologies to the restructuring of business processes (Pancake 1995). Business engineering consists of understanding current business processes in order to diagnose the fundamental source of problems in the existing processes and subsequently designing radically new processes to eliminate problem sources. OOBE has been recommended as one of the major methodologies to conduct business-engineering projects (Davenport 1993, Jacobson 1995) because it provides several diagrams that can represent real world business processes and the information system in a unified way (Beedle 1995, Taylor 1995).

One of the most important objectives of OOBE is to provide visual representations that enable business engineers to diagnose the current business processes and identify the fundamental problem sources efficiently and effectively. In order to achieve this objective, the business engineer should not only be able to understand the content of each of the individual diagrams, but also be able to integrate information from multiple diagrams from different perspectives. Although the multiple diagrams in OOBE have been used extensively in actual business engineering practices (Pancake 1995), few studies have empirically investigated the usability of multiple diagrams for OOBE. This study conducts an experiment to explore the cognitive integration processes of reasoning with multiple diagrams where the integration of information from different perspectives is essential to understanding the represented system.

## 3.3. Task Material

A real world business engineering case was used as the experimental stimulus. In order to provide the subjects with a realistic environment, the case was selected based on three criteria. First, the case must describe fundamental problems in the business processes that can only be solved by a radical redesign of the processes. Second, the case should balance the amount of relevant information among multiple diagrams from different perspectives: static, dynamic, and functional perspectives. Third, the scope of the case should not be exceedingly large, since the subjects must be able to understand the entire business system within the given time limits of the experiment. The Taco Bell case was selected because it met all the three criteria (Champy 1995, Hammer 1996, Hammer and Champy 1993).

Taco Bell, one of the biggest fast-food franchises, had fundamental problems in its pre-arrangement and post-order processes. The pre-arrangement process consisted of receiving raw material from suppliers and then pre-cooking it so that meals could be readily prepared when ordered. The post-order process started when a customer made an order and continued until the order was served with the requested meals. Since each outlet had to prepare their respective materials, it took far too long to perform the pre-arrangement and the post-order processes. The company solved these problems by establishing a central pre-processing facility that prepared the raw material so that individual outlets needed only to perform simple tasks to serve the requested orders.

This business engineering case was complex in that the subjects had to integrate information from multiple perspectives in order to correctly diagnose the two fundamental problems. The dynamic perspective was needed to infer that the chef in charge and the assistant chef were exceedingly occupied while preparing the raw material and the individual dishes. The functional perspective showing the actual preparation processes was needed to infer that the preparation process consisted of countless steps and thus took a long time to accomplish. Finally, the static perspective showing that the restaurant was a chain store instead of an individual outlet was necessary to infer that the same time consuming and complex tasks of preparing the raw material and meals were performed simultaneously in multiple outlets. All of the above information had to be successfully integrated to identify the correct problems in the case.

The experimental material was prepared by structuring the business engineering case into two parts: the problem statement and the diagrams. The problem statement was refined as follows. First, the original case was segmented into four parts: background, current situation, source of problems, and new solution. Second, the source and solution parts were removed and the background and the current situation were recompiled so that only information relevant to the stated problems remained. Then, in a pilot study, the problem statement was given to eight subjects in order to verify that it provided enough information to diagnose the fundamental problems. Five out of the eight subjects successfully identified the problems. The problem statement was revised both by removing information that caused subjects to come up with unintended problems and also by supplying additional information related to the intended problems.

The diagrams were drawn based on the problem statement using the following sequence. First, the content of the problem statement was represented in structured language. Second, the structured language description was segmented into the various perspectives (i.e., static, dynamic, and functional). Third, the actual diagrams were drawn based on the segmented descriptions. Fourth, the diagrams were translated back to the natural language descriptions, which were compared with the original problem statement. The diagrams were revised to resolve the discrepancies between them. These steps were iterated several times to ensure that the set of diagrams faithfully represented the original problem statement.

A separate preliminary study was conducted to verify the need for the multiple perspectives (i.e., static, dynamic, and functional) for the current problem (Hahn et al. 1997). Eighteen subjects taking a course on OOBE taught by the first author participated in this preliminary study. None of the subjects had any problems understanding the notation of the diagrams. In order to verify that multiple diagrams were necessary for diagnosing the business, the subjects were randomly divided into three groups, where each group was provided with diagrams from only one of the three perspectives. The first group only received diagrams from the static perspective, the second group only from the dynamic perspective, and the final group only from the functional perspective. It turned out that only one of the 18 subjects could identify only one of the two problems in the business case. This indicates that integration of information from the multiple perspectives was indeed necessary to thoroughly understand the business case. Detailed information about the preliminary study can be found in Hahn et al. (1997).

The OMT provides class diagrams from the static perspective that represent classes and the static relations among the classes. The UML combines the strengths of the various object-oriented methodologies (Booch 1994, Jacobson 1992, 1995, Rumbaugh et al. 1991). Considering the impact the UML would have on business engineering practices, this was taught to the subjects in the experimental group. The UML provides multiple diagrams from two basic perspectives: static and dynamic (Rational 1996). Two sets of diagrams were constructed as the experimental stimuli. One modification was made to provide visual cues that would help increase the continuity across different diagrams. This was done by choosing the object message diagram and excluding the event trace diagram from the experimental diagram set. The event trace diagram, whose purpose is to show the flow of messages between objects, represents objects as vertical lines and messages exchanged between the objects as arrows between the vertical lines. The object message diagram, which is informationally equivalent to the event trace diagram, adopts a node-arc structure (i.e., objects as nodes and messages as arcs), thus making it visually similar to the class diagram. The visual similarity was expected to increase visual momentum and, therefore, help easily relate the class diagram and state transition diagrams, and consequently encourage the subjects to link the static and dynamic perspective (Edelman et al. 1996). Whereas the event trace diagram emphasizes the temporal flow of behavior, the object message diagram emphasizes the relationships between the objects. The object message diagram shows the objects and links just as in the class diagram, with arrows from sender objects to receiver objects. The second modification was made to provide a broader context through which the problem solver would identify the relative importance of individual items with regard to the attending hypotheses. This was achieved by designing a context diagram (Figure 8).

The context diagram can be regarded as a long-shot diagram at a detailed level with use cases decomposed into sub-use cases that show the process flow and the functional dependencies between the sub-use cases. In addition, the use cases in the context diagram are mapped onto the individual object message diagrams, which can be referred to in order to acquire further details about the individual business processes. Thus, the high level grouping of the use case diagram and the low level representation of the context diagram were expected to help the problem solver understand the detailed process diagrams (i.e., object message diagrams) with an overall view of the entire system.

Even though the number of diagrams differed between the two groups, the two sets of diagrams were drawn so that they were informationally equivalent (Larkin and Simon 1987). In order to verify the information equivalence, both sets of diagrams were translated back to natural language descriptions. The translated natural language descriptions were then compared and the diagrams were revised to resolve the discrepancies between the two. These steps were iterated several times to make sure that the two sets of diagrams were equivalent in information content.

In summary, the set of diagrams for the control and experimental groups represented the same amount of information, but used different representations. In other words, the material was informationally equivalent, but not necessarily computationally equivalent (Larkin and Simon 1987). The event trace diagrams that focused on the temporal flow of messages were replaced by the object message diagrams that were hypothesized to provide effective visual cues to connect diagrams from the static and dynamic perspectives. The data flow diagrams and the input-output diagram that focus on the functional flow of data were replaced by the use case diagram and the context diagram that were intended to provide contextual information that would help decide the importance of individual data. ## 3.5. Experimental Procedure

The experimental sessions were divided into four sections. First, the subjects were given instructions about the general nature of the experiment and were told that verbal protocols would be collected. Subjects were trained to “think-aloud” using two standard training tasks (Ericsson and Simon 1993). The subjects were then presented with the experimental diagrams and were asked to diagnose the business system based on the diagrams. Since the diagrams were provided on paper, subjects could spread them out physically in parallel. The subjects were given 90 minutes to understand the diagrams and come up with what they believed were the fundamental problems in the business system. Due to the intended complexity of the experimental task, most subjects worked up until the 90- minute time limit.

## 3.6. Data Analysis and Coding Schema

We used protocol analysis (Ericsson and Simon 1993) to investigate the cognitive process involved in diagnosing the business processes through the use of multiple diagrams. Verbal utterances during problem solving were the major source of data for the experiment. Action protocols were also collected along with verbal protocols, since the use of both verbal and action protocols was expected to provide a more complete trace of problem solving behavior (Rist 1989). Two important considerations for using protocol analysis are to identify an appropriate unit of analysis and to develop an objective coding system for each unit. We opted to use episodes as our unit of analysis considering the volume of data in our study. Episodes are small, selfcontained phases of highly organized activity (Newell and Simon 1972). A preliminary study was conducted to develop an objective coding scheme to ensure that the coding system was not skewed to some specific features of a certain group in the experiment.

The perceptual data was coded based on action protocols. The perceptual data traced the diagrams the subjects were attending to during problem solving. A subject was coded as paying attention to a certain diagram if the title of the diagram was identified or if a certain visual item in the diagram was located by pointing to the visual item or by explicitly mentioning its name. The activity of deliberately switching between two diagrams in order to identify the relations between the two was also coded as perceptual data. Each subject’s perceptual data was summarized into a diagram transition graph that depicts the trajectory of the subjects’ transitions among the multiple diagrams during the entire experimental session. An example of the diagram transition graph will be presented in the results section.

The conceptual data was coded based mainly on the verbal protocols. The conceptual data traced the subjects’ hypotheses about the fundamental problem of the given business process. All verbal utterances that contained the subject’s opinion about a certain process was coded as a hypothesis. For example, a subject may directly suggest a potential problem (“It took four hours to prepare for the meals everyday. That’s too long.”), or ponder the basic rationale about the current process (“Why does she have to input the order only like that?”), or may infer consequences not given in the diagrams (“She must be pretty tired after taking care of all this stuff ”). It was possible that some hypotheses were not well focused to specific problems. The subject could also refine the hypothesis that he/she made earlier. This was also coded as conceptual data. The conceptual data was summarized into a hypothesis behavior graph similar to the problem behavior graph proposed by Newell and Simon. The hypothesis behavior graph traces the subjects’ problem solving behavior in terms of hypotheses generated and refined. An example of the hypothesis behavior graph will be presented in the results section.

The following steps of protocol analysis were used. First, all of the verbal and action protocols were transcribed with their time of occurrence. The transcriptions were verified by the authors. Then, the transcripts of the entire protocols were initially segmented into episodes. Segmentation was guided by a segmentation rule based on the types of episodes described in the coding system. Upon examination of the initial segmentation of the entire protocol, a few ambiguities in the segmentation rule were identified. After revising the segmentation rule to account for those ambiguities, we again applied it to the entire set of protocols. The resulting segmented episodes were coded using the coding system described above. Although a few episodes could not be classified with the current coding system, a re-analysis of the protocols indicated that the time spent by the subjects in these episodes was negligible and did not contain significant information about the subjects’ problem solving behavior. Therefore, those episodes were omitted from further analysis. Finally, the diagram transition graph and the hypothesis behavior graph were constructed as described above for each subject to summarize his/her perceptual and conceptual cognitive activities.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Overall Final Performance

The overall performance was measured by counting the number of correct problems the subjects had identified during the diagnostic process. The results are summarized in Table 2. In the control group, only two subjects were able to identify all of the correct problems, three subjects identified only one of the two problems, and four subjects could not come up with any problem at all. In the experimental group, six subjects came up with the two correct problems while one subject came up with a single problem. These results suggest that the overall performance of the experimental group was significantly better compared to that of the control group (Chi-square - 6.86, p  0.05).

Since the experimental material for the two groups was informationally equivalent, the difference in final performance implies that the difference in how the experimental material was presented to the subjects may have produced the difference in final performance. More specifically, the visual cues and contextual information incorporated in the diagram set for the experimental group may have helped the subjects within the perceptual and conceptual integration processes of diagrammatic reasoning with multiple diagrams. Protocol analysis was performed to examine the cognitive integration processes of the subjects to account for the difference in final performance.

## 5. Discussion

One of the goals of research into diagrammatic reasoning is to help develop better representations that aid problem solving. This paper extends the current research from diagrammatic reasoning with a single diagram to reasoning with multiple diagrams and also presents a theoretical framework for the cognitive processes of perceptual and conceptual integration within multiple diagrams. This paper identifies important design considerations for developing multiple diagrams (i.e., visual cues and contextual information) and applies them to better design diagrams for object-oriented business engineering. The results from the experiment indicate that visual cues facilitated the perceptual integration process, which resulted in more transitions among multiple diagrams and, especially, more transitions between pairs of related diagrams from different perspectives. The contextual information also turned out to facilitate the conceptual integration process, which resulted in more thorough hypothesis refinements and in a more versatile use of diverse diagrams from different perspectives. The ease in both the perceptual and conceptual integration fostered by the visual cues and contextual information helped construct a more comprehensive representation about the target system and also resulted in higher problem solving performance.

The limitations of our study stem from the nature of experimental studies. First, the business case we used in our experiment was customized for experimental use, which includes simplification of the original case so that it could be understood by the subjects within a given time limit. This may have simplified the problem to the point that it may be inappropriate to regard it as a real world business-engineering project. Furthermore, subjects in our experiments were neither real business engineers nor subject-matter experts, but undergraduate students taking a business engineering course. Business engineers or subject-matter experts in the real world might have behaved differently from our student subjects based on their actual field experiences. Given the fact that more and more business engineering efforts are becoming participatory in nature, it will be interesting to investigate how well subject-matter experts or business-engineering experts understand a business system through the use of multiple diagrams. Our future research includes conducting a field study in a real business-engineering project. We expect the field study to add external support in applying the diagrammatic reasoning principles to the development of multiple diagram representations.

Another limitation of this study is related to a frequently occurring problem with most protocol analysis studies. Since the coding process is often impossible to automate, human coders have to manually perform extensive coding of the protocol data. Even though care was taken in the design of the study to maximize the objectivity of the coding system, some bias may have been introduced into the study results because it is extremely difficult for human coders to be completely ignorant of the experimental hypothesis during the iterative coding process. Despite these potential problems, detailed collection and analysis of thinking aloud protocol data has proved to be effective in providing insight into the subjects’ problem solving processes. Experimental studies in the information systems discipline may benefit from this study’s example and employ similarly detailed data collection and analysis methods in the search for understanding of human problem solving processes. Therefore, experimental studies should not only be designed to demonstrate between-group differences, but should also be designed to explain the causes of the differences in terms of detailed problem solving traces.

From a practitioner’s point of view, the design of the experiment may make the results of the study difficult to interpret. Practitioners may wish to see a study that compares the traditional object modeling technique and the newly proposed unified modeling language so that they may decide to choose the OMT or the UML in their business engineering practices. However, due to the design of the experimental material, the results of the study may not be used to compare the OMT and UML directly for two reasons. First, not all of the diagrams provided by UML were presented to the experimental group. For example, the event trace diagram is also offered by the UML but was not presented to the experimental group because the event trace diagram was informationally equivalent to the object message diagram and only the object message diagrams afforded visual cues. Second, some of the diagrams provided to the experimental group were not part of the UML. For example, the context diagram presented to the experimental group is not part of the UML.

Third, this study did not compare the original UML with another UML enhanced with visual cues and contextual information. The UML could not be used for the control group and the enhanced UML for the experimental group because the contextual information could not be completely removed unless the use case diagram was eliminated from the original UML. However, the use case diagram is a representative diagram in the UML, and the experimental group subjects who had already used these diagrams over several months could not accept the UML without the use case diagram. Therefore, the OMT had to be used for our control group, even though this made the results of the study somewhat difficult to interpret. A future study may be designed to conduct a more controlled experiment by developing a third notation, different from both the OMT and the UML.

Despite the above limitations, this study has strong theoretical and practical contributions. Theoretically, this paper presents a framework for the cognitive integration processes in reasoning with multiple diagrams. This paper proposed two dimensions of cognitive integration (i.e., perceptual and conceptual), analytical methods to investigate the dynamic process of the two types of integration (i.e., the diagram transition graphs and the hypothesis behavior graphs), and prescriptive ways to facilitate the integration process in the two dimensions (i.e., visual cues and contextual information). The two dimensions are consistent with the two problem spaces proposed by Kim and Lerch (1997) that support the cognitive activity of programmers in writing programming codes. The two prescriptive ways are also consistent with the two methods that were proposed by Woods and Watts that provide various ways to navigate a large number of diagrams. The practical contribution of this paper arises from the prevalence of multiple diagrams from multiple perspectives in software and business engineering. The use of more than one type of diagram is the norm rather than an exception in modeling information systems and business processes. However, the use of more diagrams does not necessarily increase analysts’ understanding of the target system unless the representation of the diagrams aids problem solving (Good 1996; Green et al. 1991; Vessey 1991). Poor understanding of the target system may result in catastrophic flaws in the design of business processes of large corporations (Hammer 1996). At the same time, the current trends in software and business engineering place more emphasis on design diagrams than on final programming codes, because using design diagrams is more effective for communicating with other team members and maintaining the target systems (Winograd 1995). The increased role of design documentation is also expected to add more significance to this paper, because performance benefits accrue when a problem representation induced from multiple diagrams matches the characteristics of information system developments and business engineering tasks (Vessey and Galletta 1991).
