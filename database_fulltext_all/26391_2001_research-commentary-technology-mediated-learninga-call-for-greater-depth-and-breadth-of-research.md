# Research Commentary: Technology-Mediated Learning—A Call for Greater Depth and Breadth of Research

Maryam Alavi • Dorothy E. Leidner

The Goizueta Business School, Emory University, Atlanta, Georgia 30322

INSEAD, 77305 Fontainebleau Cedex France

maryam\_alavi@bus.emory.edu • dleidner@earthlink.net

niversities and corporate training facilities have been investing in information technologies to improve education and training at an increasing rate during the past decade. Many new companies are emerging to provide tools and services to enable the effective design of IT-based learning solutions. Although research on technology-mediated learning has increased in recent years, it still lags behind developments in practice. This essay suggests potential research avenues in the area of technology-mediated learning. It seeks to motivate greater depth of research into the question of how technology enhances learning. This question requires an explicit consideration of relationships among technology capabilities, instructional strategy, psychological processes, and contextual factors involved in learning. The essay also recommends attention to a greater breadth of research questions, including issues of how technology-mediated learning affects program design and what structures and processes universities can employ to facilitate innovation.

## 1. Introduction

The past decade has witnessed a dramatic increase in the development of technology-based teaching and learning. For example, in the university landscape a prominent change has been the increase in virtual course offerings, otherwise referred to as distance learning. Since 1998, the number of universities offering some form of distance learning has increased by 33%. It is predicted that by 2002, 80% of institutions of higher education will offer some form of virtual education, although the extent and scope of these initiatives will vary (Block and Dobell 1999). Other areas of interest among instructors have included virtual teams (Knoll and Jarvenpaa 1995), improving traditional teamwork with technology tools (Alavi 1994, Alavi et al. 1995), improving classroom interaction with computer use (Alavi et al. 1997, Leidner and Fuller 1997), and improving delivery of instructional material (Leidner and Jarvenpaa 1993).

In keeping with the trend toward increased application of IT to teaching and learning, a plethora of start-up companies have emerged to provide tools and services for the market, such as: Lotus LearningSpace (lotus.com), Real Education Active Learning System (realeducation.com), Blackboard (blackboard.net), Convene (convene.com), and LearnLinc (ilinc.com). These companies provide web-based learning application development tools and services, while Tool-BookII (asymetrix.com), Authorware (macromedia.com), IconAuthor 7.0 (aimtech.com), Quest 6.0 (allencomm.com), and Designer’s Edge (allencomm.com) provide content representation tools.

The boom in the application of technology to education and training underscores a fundamental need to understand how these technologies improve the learning process. While research at the postsecondary level has increased in recent years (with such venues as the Americas Conference on Information Systems and the Hawaii International Conference having a track and a mini-track, respectively, on IT in education, and prominent academic journals hosting special issues on the topic of IT and management education (MIS Quarterly, Academy of Management Journal)), the need for research in this area lags well behind the flux of activity in practice.

The objective of this essay is to motivate future research and dialogue on technology-mediated learning (TML) by suggesting some potentially productive research venues in this area. TML is defined as an environment in which the learner’s interactions with learning materials (readings, assignments, exercises, etc.), peers, and/or instructors are mediated through advanced information technologies. The term “information technology” broadly refers to computing, communication, and data management technologies, and their convergence. The focus of the essay is on learning from instruction in the context of postsecondary educational environments, although the issues are also relevant to corporate training. Learning from instruction refers to situations in which one individual intentionally tries to influence the learning of another by structuring the environment of the learner in such a way that the latter individual will achieve a desired outcome (Shuell and Lee 1976). Other forms of learning—learning by doing, vicarious learning, and learning that occurs as a by-product of daily life and work experiences are not considered here, although very important to an individual’s development.

## 2. Why Should TML Research Be Important to the IS Community?

In their essay on the relevance of IS research, Benbasat and Zmud (1999) suggest that the foremost consideration in choosing research topics should be the future interest that key stakeholders, including journals, colleagues, and practitioners, are likely to hold in the topic. We believe that the field of TML is still inchoate and will continue to increase in importance even as educational institutions allocate significant amounts of resources to the implementation of IT-enabled learning environments. Already the educational sector is among the largest in the U.S. economy with \$700 billion in expenditure in 1998 (Block and Dobell 1999). Currently, and in the foreseeable future, there seems to be a great deal of interest in the design and application of TML environments. An important factor fueling this interest is the increase in demand for postsecondary education by traditional students as well as on-the-job training required in a knowledge-based economy.

The growth in the traditional student demand for postsecondary education is partly due to the “baby boom echo”—an estimated 72 million children of the 76 million baby boomer Americans born from 1946 to 1964 (Merrill Lynch 1999). Furthermore, the transition to the knowledge economy is dramatically changing the quality and quantity of demand for education and learning. In the knowledge economy, there is an increased demand for a highly educated workforce and for the continuous learning and upgrading of employees’ skills. The increase in the quantity as well as quality of demand from both traditional students and working adults combined with the strong penetration rate of information technologies present an extraordinary potential for the transformation of educational and learning processes. Many traditional colleges and universities, individually or in various forms of partnerships, are embracing information technologies to create new learning models that enhance the effectiveness and reach of their programs. Furthermore, forprofit providers are rapidly appearing as the market opportunities expand.

Despite the growing interest and enthusiasm for development and application of TML, there is a paucity of theoretically grounded and rigorous research to guide the development of these environments. The IS field is uniquely positioned to contribute to the intellectual development of TML for several reasons. First, the IS field has a history and tradition of research and development that involves the application of information technologies to cognitive processes (e.g., decision making and problem solving). Research and development in the areas of decision support systems, executive support systems, expert systems, and group decision support represent examples of this genre of work (see, for example, Todd and Benbasat 1987, Vandenbosch and Higgins 1996, and Sheetz et al. 1994). IS scholars can draw upon relevant research to postulate how technology can influence the cognitive processes involved in learning. Secondly, based on a long tradition of research in the appropriate structures and processes to enhance information system success (see, for example, Diedler et al. 1996, Ciborra 1996, Orlikowski and Robey 1991, Robey and Sahay 1996). IS researchers can offer organizational level vision on structures and processes to effectively implement TML initiatives. Thirdly, given that technology can be both an enabler of, or hindrance to, innovation in education, IS scholars can deploy their knowledge of information technologies to help determine appropriate instructional applications.

In the absence of intellectual leadership from the academy, and considering the growing interest in TML applications and the potential for economic gains, profit motives may start to drive the field; as researchers and educators, we may find ourselves on the sidelines of our own game (Reigeluth 1989).

## 3. The State of TML Research

TML research has only seriously attracted the attention of IS scholars in the past decade, although the topic has consistently been of interest to educational psychologists. A wealth of studies from the latter discipline has examined the impact of technology on learning. The results have been mixed: for a review of results suggesting a significant difference in outcomes, the reader is referred to Orr (1997). For a review of results suggesting no significant difference in outcome, the reader is referred to Russell (1999). In this section, we will highlight the recent research primarily from IS scholars, given that the discipline itself may influence appropriate uses of TML, and that we believe IS scholars can make a unique contribution to TML research.

IS researchers have focused on the influence of IT on instructional methods and learning outcomes in both traditional classrooms and virtual classrooms. Research conducted in a traditional classroom setting has examined the various instructional methods enabled by IT. In some early TML research, Leidner and Jarvenpaa (1993) observed several new methods in use including interactive guided learning, informal learning labs, and real-time data manipulation with students taking active roles as participants, rather than observers, in the methods. An important conclusion, however, was that the influence of the IT-enabled instructional methods on learning was moderated by instructor and student characteristics.

The IT-enabled learning method most studied by IS researchers is that of computer-based collaboration. Perhaps because of the long tradition of GSS (group support systems) research with its emphasis on improving process gains and decreasing process losses in collaborative settings, a substantial amount of research attention has addressed the question of enabling collaborative learning by implementing GSS tools. Some studies have explored the use of GSS to foster case discussions in a traditional classroom (Hashaim et al. 1992, Leidner and Fuller 1997). Other studies have examined the use of GSS to enable collaboration in small teams of students in traditional classes. The findings suggest that the GSS supported groups out performed the nonsupported groups (Alavi 1994).

The interest in collaborative learning is based on the belief that collaboration and participation improve learning. This has its roots in research suggesting that participation mediates socioemotional variables and affects cognitive processes (Webb 1982, Baldwin et al. 1997). In fact, Lim et al. (1997) suggest that codiscovery (a form of collaborative learning) forced learners to engage in deeper-level thinking that facilitated and improved learning.

Research has also considered the effectiveness of virtual learning environments. Webster and Hackley (1997) studied synchronous virtual learning and found that the greater the number of locations, the greater the process losses and the less involved the remote students appeared. However, they noted the importance of instructor style in achieving student participation and involvement. Ahmed (2000) found that the students in a virtual environment reported higher selfefficacy than students in a comparable traditional environment, with performance in the two environments being equivalent. Hiltz (1986, 1997) and Hiltz and Turoff (1993) have found that virtual environments are most successful when collaboration among students is achieved. They also note, though, that the environment is more conducive to highly motivated learners than to “average” learners.

Consistent with research in a traditional setting, research in the virtual setting has concluded that student characteristics have an important influence on learning outcomes. Cognitive and psychological characteristics—level of academic ability, motivation, and degree of effort—have been shown to positively correlate with learning outcomes in the asynchronous distance learning environment (Hiltz 1986). Moreover, students’ comfort with their onscreen image was observed to affect behavior in synchronous distance learning environments (Webster and Hackley 1997).

The importance of student characteristics in moderating learning outcomes in both traditional and virtual environments has led to research suggesting that instructional designs should be created that provide various pedagogical options for students depending upon the student’s learning style (Lengnick-Hall and Sanders 1997). The role of IT in enabling individualized learning methods, while not new, has received strikingly little attention.

This brief review of TML research in the IS area, summarized in Table 1, has highlighted the major areas of focus. While great progress has been made in advancing our understanding of the critical enabling role of IT in learning environments, we believe that several major avenues of research are needed to expand our knowledge. This research can also offer valuable guidelines to universities and corporations as they consider the important question of how to improve their education and training programs by utilizing the IT tools at their disposal. More specifically, we would highlight the absence of research considering the interactions of technology, instructional method, and the psychological processes of student learners. On a broader level, research is needed that considers the organizational processes and structures needed to effectively coordinate organizational level (e.g., university level) implementation of TML. Also on this broader level, research is needed that considers the program (such as an MBA program) as the level of analysis, in which case research can focus on appropriate models—part virtual, part traditional—to maximize the efficacy of the learning environment. These areas will be explained more fully in the next section.

## 4. Moving Toward Greater Depth and Breadth of TML Research

In this section, we seek to direct TML research attention to areas of greater depth and breadth. Firstly, we argue for a deeper assessment of TML by considering the underlying structures of TML environments. Secondly, we argue for greater breadth in the field—an examination of program level initiatives made possible with TML, as well as organizational-level factors to foster TML.

## 4.1. Greater Depth of Research: TML and Psychological Learning Processes

Most would agree that the objective of using technology in learning should be to positively influence learning in one way or another; that is, the student should either learn something that he/she would not have learned without the technology or learn it in a more efficient manner. In this vein, most of the existing studies comparing various forms of TML have focused on the influence of technology features (e.g., presence or absence of video or media synchronicity) on learning outcomes. Missing from these studies is consideration of the internal psychological processes through which learning occurs. Psychological processes in this context refer to states within the learner that are involved in learning. They include the learner’s cognitive and information processing activities, motivation, interest, and cognitive structures (memory). Cognition and information processing in this context refers to a range of mental processes that the learner uses to select, encode, and comprehend the information presented externally through the instructional model. Also missing from the studies are descriptions of the link between technology features and instructional variables that might influence learning outcomes. For example, feedback is known to be a key process for certain types of learning. Thus, if a researcher is interested in studying the relationship between media synchronicity and learning outcomes, he or she might look for ways in which feedback processes operate under various conditions of media synchronicity. In other words, to improve the consistency of TML research findings, it is important to conceptualize technology features and attributes in a manner directly relevant to instructional and learning processes.

As argued above, TML should not merely attempt to replicate conventional learning but try to enhance and improve it. An important research question is, therefore: “How does technology enhance learning?” This research question is consistent with Kozma’s (1994) perspective on the role of media in learning. It immediately shifts our attention from comparative studies of TML and traditional learning environments to studies that focus on forming relationships among technology and relevant instructional, psychological, and environmental factors that will enhance learning outcomes (see Figure 1).

Whereas past research has looked directly at the learning outcomes, research is needed that considers the psychological processes of learning; without affecting these processes, one cannot affect learning outcomes. At the heart of such research is an explicit configuration of relationships among technology capabilities, instructional strategy, and psychological processes involved in learning. In other words, as implied by Figure 1, it is not the technology features, in and of themselves, that matter. It is the mutual influence of technology features, instructional strategy, and psychological processes that impacts learning outcomes in a given learning context. The remaining components of Figure 1 are briefly described next to illustrate the need to move beyond simple investigation of surface, features of technology to a study of deeper structures (variable sets and their interactions) of TML environments.

4.1.1. Instructional Strategy. Instructional strategy refers to methods and models for presenting, sequencing, and synthesizing subject-matter content (Reigeluth et al. 1994). According to these authors, presentation refers to selection and display mode and format of content information. Sequencing refers to the order in which different “topics” of the subject matter (e.g., concepts and principles) are presented to learners. Synthesizing refers to establishing relationships among various subject-matter topics. By drawing on learning theories and knowledge of human cognition, instructional strategies aim to facilitate learning by activating the psychological processes required by learners for achieving the desired learning outcomes. Gagne and Briggs’ (1979) classic work represents one of the most comprehensive and influential sets of instructional models. This set consists of five models: one for teaching attitudes, one for teaching motor skills, and three for teaching cognitive skills (intellectual skills and problem-solving strategy). One of the key features of the Gagne and Briggs model for teaching in tellectual skills is the prescription of a set of instructional events (e.g., gaining attention, presenting the stimulus material, and providing feedback about performance correctness). These events refer to instructional activities designed to elicit learners’ relevant psychological processes.

4.1.2. Learning Outcomes. Different types of learning have been identified in the instructional design and educational research literature. Gagne (1977), for example, has identified five categories of learning outcomes: intellectual skills, motor skills, verbal information, cognitive strategy, and attitude. Learning involves the acquisition of knowledge and the capability to take effective action (perform). Knowledge and capability are not directly observable. Only the action and performance that result from learning can be observed and measured. Different types of learning result in different types of performance (outcomes). For example, according to Gagne, the outcome of attitude learning is choosing to behave in a certain way, while cognitive strategy learning leads to originating solutions to problems. Thus, making distinctions among various types of learning in TML research is important for two reasons. First, internal and external conditions required are different for each type of learning. Second, the performance manifested is entirely different for each type, requiring different approaches to the measurement of learning outcomes.

In addition to learning, another set of outcomes may be of interest to TML researchers. Examples include learners’ affective reactions to TML (e.g., satisfaction with the TML experience) and efficiency (cost and learning time) of TML environments.

4.1.3. Information Technology. Technology can provide enhanced capabilities for the execution of instructional strategies (e.g., new forms of content presentations or the efficient delivery of instructional events). This may in turn facilitate the activation and engagement of relevant psychological processes of the learner, leading to more effective or efficient learning outcomes. Technology can also influence learning through the direct support of the underlying psychological processes (e.g., facilitating cognitive informationprocessing activities such as search, scanning, transformation, or comparison of information). For example, King et al. (1990) developed a decision support system (DSS), which was used in teaching a graduate financial management course. The DSS facilitated presentation, access to, and analysis of stock market and company financial data contained in commercial data-bases. The menu-driven DSS was used by students to learn financial concepts like cash flow analysis, capital budgeting, investment, and risk analysis by modeling and interpreting data from real-world companies. The learning outcomes of students who studied in the TML environment (i.e., used the DSS) were consistently superior to the learning outcomes of students who learned in a traditional environment from the same instructor. In this learning situation, dynamic online information display and analytical capabilities of the DSS (technology features) were used to develop rich presentations of the information stimulus (one of the instructional events in Gagne and Briggs’ model of instruction described above) otherwise unlikely to be available to learners. Furthermore, the modular structure of the DSS enabled students to work with progressively more sophisticated and complex financial models. For example, the cash flow model permitted analysis of the cash flows of companies, calculations of net present values, and forecasting of cash flows for future periods in a modular and incremental fashion. It can be argued that, in this example, the use of technology features in the presentation and sequencing of the subject-matter content (two aspects of instructional strategy design) facilitated learners’ comprehension of the phenomenon under study. The students also used the DSS as a simulation tool to understand the relationships between various financial factors such as risk-return trade-offs and the interrelationships between various financial indicators such as beta values and market rate of return. It can be further postulated that DSS provided a forum for learners to generate and test hypotheses, make decisions, and generate responses, thus, directly engaging and facilitating the psychological processes required for learning in this context.

The preceding example illustrates how, in a given situation, technology features (dynamic information display and information processing capabilities of the computer) can enhance learning outcomes by enabling implementation of a rich instructional strategy and by directly eliciting certain cognitive problem-solving and decision-making processes of the learner. In the same vein, TML research studies should reach beyond specifying a simple cause-effect relationship between an observable technology feature and learning outcomes.

To summarize, §4.1 argues for greater depth in the exploration of TML, suggests reframing the TML research question from, “Does technology influence learning?” to “How can technology enhance learning?,” and proposes a framework (displayed in Figure 1) to address this research question. The framework posits the need to consider technology features in relation to the instructional methods that employ them and as they interact with the psychological processes required for learning in a given context.

## 4.2. Greater Breadth of Research in TML: Considering the Program and Organizationa Levels of Analysis

Whereas the previous section highlighted the need for a deeper perspective on the impact of TML on learning processes, there is also a need for research that takes a broader perspective on the impact of TML.

4.2.1. The Program Level. While the research to date has focused largely on the student level of analysis, the popular press has focused on the level of the degree program. The press of late has given extensive coverage to the question of how the Internet will affect degree programs (see, for example, Weber 2000 and Grimes 2000). Business schools have been touting their new e-business courses purported to help inform students and prepare them to work in the dynamic, everchanging digital environment. What is striking is the seeming assumption that the options are either virtual or traditional, with little room for hybrid programs.

For example, rather than applying the principles of the digital economy to their own business programs, business schools are content either to teach the principles in traditional format or in a digital format (online). Why not use the power of modern IT to customize a program for individual students? One could place the core of an MBA program online, allowing students to complete their first year without having to incur the expense of relocating for two full years. Midway through the year, students could be polled to determine what electives they desire to take. The students then arrive in person for their second year, all of which consists of electives that were being developed the previous semester. In this way, a hybrid system that avails itself of the convenience of online learning for fairly standardized services (such as a core) is used to free up the faculty resource to customize the value-added service of any given program (its electives) to the students. Is such a model feasible? Would it improve the overall quality of the degree program?

With such wide-ranging possibilities enabled by modern technologies, we do not pretend to provide answers to the question of what business education will look like in the twenty-first century, but we encourage IS scholars to break the assumptions of current programs (for example, that instructors must physically teach a certain number of hours per year) and develop innovative solutions that rival the best of ebusinesses in terms of service, personalization, and customization.

4.2.2. The Organizational Level. Lastly, at the organizational level of analysis, IS scholars might have insights to provide to the question of how to encourage instructors (in the role of end-users) to incorporate IT tools to improve their instructional design, development, and delivery. From reviewing the research on TML initiatives, it would appear that vanguard faculty members in a given institution pursue what they believe to be an interesting application of IT to learning, learn from their experience, and then possibly share their experience with a few cohorts who might try a similar implementation. However, there appears to be little systematic effort to inform and train instructors on the potential uses of IT in their courses, the notable exceptions being that of the New Jersey Technical Institute, with its long tradition of encouraging innovation in instruction (Hiltz 1986, 1994), and the Instituto Tecnologico et de Estudios Superiores de Monterrey, which has been offering regular degree programs across 26 distance campus sites in the past decade. Decisions concerning both the coordination (or lack thereof) of technology acquisition and support across departments and schools and knowledge sharing on the effectiveness of various implementations need to be made. Such decisions will have a crucial impact on the degree to which the university as an organization embraces TML.

One can envision a simple matrix (Figure 2) to consider the options, often selected unsystematically at a university level, that can have tremendous consequences for the success that any given university as an organization will have with TML. We would suggest that most universities fall in Quadrant I, in which the purchase of specific tools (such as GSS) is made locally, the support provided for the tools is also local, and the knowledge sharing that occurs concerning the classroom use of the tools extends to an individual faculty initiator’s network but rarely beyond. We label this the Experimental Quadrant. Quadrant II would be representative of universities that give great lip service to innovative uses of TML, but leave the purchase and support of technology decisions to local decision makers, thus effectively limiting the use of TML to the departmental innovators. In Quadrant III, which we label Laissez-Faire, are universities that for budgetary reasons coordinate purchase and support but who do not implement policies for knowledge sharing. One would expect to see large duplication of innovation efforts across departments. For a university that desires to truly embrace the digital age, greater coordination of acquisition, support, and knowledge sharing is in order. Thus, in Quadrant IV, we represent universities whose strategic goal is to implement TML to better position themselves to offer the type of learning environments that future generations of technology-savvy students will demand. This Quadrant is simply labeled “Top-in-Class.” With purchasing and support of instructional technology being a priority at the university level, and with efforts to effectuate knowledge sharing across departmental silos, these universities can position themselves to learn, innovate, and change to meet the shifting demands of one of their major constituencies (i.e., students). What we intend to suggest with this matrix is that the university wittingly or unwittingly makes distinct decisions regarding how TML will be encouraged and supported. For universities that do not wish to be left behind, research examining the most effective support and knowledge-sharing patterns could prove relevant and valuable. We believe this to be another potential stream of TML research that has not yet been broached.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

In summary, most of the recent attempts at studying TML in IS research tend to adopt an overly simplistic view of this phenomenon. By asking, “Does information technology influence learning?,” we have primarily focused on the comparison of TML environments with non-TML environments. Moreover, the program and university levels of analysis have been largely ignored. This represents a static view and perhaps an outdated stimulus-response perspective on learning research, which is trying to establish a direct causeeffect relationship between technology (stimulus) and learning outcome (response), while ignoring the larger context within which learning occurs. This essay makes research suggestions that may deepen our understanding of TML phenomenon, inform the design of effective TML environments, and expand the breadth of potential research avenues. These suggestions are summarized in Table 2. TML research, as characterized in this essay, is indeed a complex and challenging endeavor. It involves engineering innovative learning environments while simultaneously assessing their effectiveness. Much detail remains to be specified, but our hope is that the suggested frameworks and research questions provide a basis for guiding future research on technology-mediated learning.
