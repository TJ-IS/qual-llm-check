Richard D. Johnson • George M. Marakas

University of Central Florida, College of Business Administration, Orlando, Florida 32816

BAT Faculty Fellow in Global IT Strategy, Indiana University, Kelley School of Business, Bloomington, Indiana 47405 gmarakas@indiana.edu • rjohnson@bus.ucf.edu

R <sup>ecent</sup> <sup>empirical</sup> <sup>work</sup> <sup>by</sup> <sup>Compeau</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>Higgins</sup> <sup>(1995)</sup> <sup>investigated</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>role</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>behavioral</sup> modeling training in the development of computer skills. Their efforts have provided insight into our understanding of the role of computer self-efficacy (CSE) and behavioral modeling (BM) techniques with regard to training effectiveness. Contrary to their expectations, however, several of the hypothesized relationships were not supported, especially those relating to outcome expectancy. In this paper, an empirically derived model of the CSE construct proposed by Marakas, Yi, and Johnson (1998) is offered to highlight potential theoretical, methodological, and measurement issues which may have contributed to or exacerbated the unexpected results obtained in the Compeau and Higgins study. The empirical work contained herein is intended to both replicate and extend the work of Compeau and Higgins and to assist in resolving several key issues left unsettled by their seminal work in this area. ## 1. Introduction

Using social cognitive theory (SCT; Bandura 1977a, 1977b, 1986, 1997) as their theoretical basis, several recent studies have investigated the role that behavioral modeling (BM) and computer self-efficacy (CSE) play in the acquisition of computer skills (cf. Compeau and Higgins 1995, Gist 1989). While behavioral modeling training methods have found strong support throughout the social sciences (cf. Latham and Saari 1979, Manz and Sims 1981), a recent study conducted by Compeau and Higgins (1995), focusing on the effects of behavioral modeling on computer training outcomes, found mixed results. This groundbreaking research provided the academic community several important insights into the CSE construct, behavioral modeling, and their roles in the realm of computer skills training and development. Further, the study stands as one of the first attempts to apply SCT and the concepts of CSE to the study of end-user computer training. The results provided insights into the mediating role of CSE with regard to the influence of behavior modeling on performance, and it highlighted the potential for applying behavior modeling techniques in applied scenarios. Despite the initial insights gained, however, Compeau and Higgins concluded that “overall, this research does not provide support for the model as presented” (Compeau and Higgins 1995, p. 131). Through a replication and extension of the research conducted by Compeau and Higgins, we offer several perspectives of a theoretical, methodological, and measurement nature which may help to explain the sources that contributed to and/or exacerbated several of their unexpected results. Thus, this research has three objectives: (1) to report findings obtained via a replication of the original Compeau and Higgins (1995) study with regard to the effects of behavioral modeling on performance, (2) to extend their work by providing empirical or theoretical explanations for issues which may have contributed to their unexpected results, and (3) to propose a modified model of the phenomenon which may more accurately reflect both social cognitive theory and the constructs of interest.

## 2. Background

Social cognitive theory is a robust and well-accepted model of individual behavior and has often been used as the basis for many different types of performance domains. Using this theory, Compeau and Higgins (1995) developed a model of computer training from which a number of hypotheses were derived and tested during a two-day study. As depicted in Figure 1, the model contains four independent variables (BM, CSE, outcome expectations (OE), prior experience) and a single dependent variable (performance).

## Variables Defined

As defined in the original study, behavioral modeling is the observation by the subject of another person performing a desired behavior. Within the realm of social cognitive theory, behavioral modeling represents an example of vicarious experience, one of the four primary sources of self-efficacy (SE) formation proposed by Bandura (1977a, 1977b). The strength of vicarious experience (modeling) on formations of SE perceptions is second only to enactive mastery, or direct experience, and has been found to be an effective way to increase both SE and performance in a wide variety of domains (Gist et al. 1989, Latham and Saari 1979, Manz and Sims 1981, Schunk 1981). Further, Bandura (1971) suggests that when subjects see salient others succeed in their behavior, they will behave in a comparable fashion in the expectation of achieving similar successes.

Computer Self-Efficacy (CSE) is defined as “an individual’s perception of efficacy in performing specific computer-related tasks within the domain of general computing” (Marakas et al. 1998, p. 128). A person’s efficacy (belief in ability) estimations are an important predictor of their subsequent performance and can be influenced by a variety of factors including enactive mastery (prior experience in the domain), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal (Bandura 1977a).

Outcome Expectations (OE) are “a person’s estimate that a given behavior will lead to certain outcomes” (Bandura 1977b, p. 193). Citing Bandura (1978), Compeau and Higgins proposed that outcome expectations are derived largely by how well the individual expects to perform. Bostrom et al. (1990) found that the perceived usefulness (a construct similar to OE; Davis 1989) of a software package positively influenced performance. Based on this, Compeau and Higgins argued for the positioning of CSE as an antecedent to OE and the positioning of OE as antecedent to performance in their original model.

Prior experience, which Bandura refers to as enactive mastery, has been found to have the most powerful effect on self-efficacy estimations (Bandura and Schunk 1981, Locke et al. 1984, Wood and Bandura 1989). Compeau and Higgins suggested that prior experience would also affect outcome expectations because people will expect to perform at a level similar to prior attempts, thus suggesting somewhat of a recursive relationship between experience and OE. Finally, Wood and Bandura (1989) found that prior levels of performance were significantly related to future performance levels.

Using this model, Compeau and Higgins trained managers over a two-day period to use both a wordprocessing and spreadsheet package. Contrary to their expectations, the model did not receive consistent support. For Day 1 spreadsheet training, only 5 out of the 9 hypothesized relations were significant in the hypothesized direction with the remainder showing either no support or significance in the opposite direction. For Day 2 training, however, 9 of 13 hypotheses were significant in spreadsheet training subjects. For word-processing, a slightly different set of 5 of 9 hypothesized relations were supported for Day 1 with only 4 of 13 hypotheses supported on Day 2. The majority of the unexpected results centered on hypothesized relationships involving either prior experience, outcome expectancy, or both. Further, the overall results obtained for the word-processing component of the study did not receive as strong a support as the spreadsheet portion.

From a logical perspective, several sources for the unexpected results can be suggested: (1) the model under investigation may be theoretically misspecified, (2) the constructs contained within the model may not be adequately isolated in their measurement, and/or (3) the methodology employed may not completely capture the robustness of the phenomena under investigation. Following a brief discussion of an alternative model of the phenomena, an investigation into such possible sources will be presented.

## 3. Proposed Model

Based upon a review of the self-efficacy and CSE literature, Marakas et al. (1998) developed a model which empirically links over 20 variables to CSE, several of which mediate the CSE-performance relationship. Figure 2 illustrates a subset of this model containing the variables of interest in this study. This subset will serve as the theoretical basis for a proposed alternative model of training intended to provide insight into the findings of the Compeau and Higgins study.<sup>1</sup>

## Differences in the Proposed Model

Outcome Expectancy. One key difference between the proposed model and the Compeau and Higgins (1995) model is the positioning of outcome expectancy. In his early writings, Bandura (1986) suggests the importance of external motivators in an educational/ training context: “incentives promote broad, enduring changes when they sponsor generalizable skills that have functional value, irrespective of time and place” (p. 256). Bandura also suggests that:

People select courses of action within their perceived capabilities [self-efficacy] and sustain their efforts partly on the basis of such outcome expectations. Because of their cognitive capacity to foresee the likely outcomes of prospective actions, people can sustain their efforts by symbolic motivators over a long time perspective (Bandura 1986, p. 230).

To those researchers utilizing SCT, this has suggested the power that positive outcomes (i.e., rewards)

can have to motivate people to not only undertake a task, but also to perform at higher levels than they would otherwise. Thus, outcome expectations are suggested to be a motivational factor contributing to performance outcomes.

Recent research, however, has brought into question the usefulness of outcome expectancy in adding predictive value to immediate performance outcomes. Bandura (1997) suggests that “where performance determines outcome, efficacy beliefs account for most of the variance in expected outcomes. When differences in efficacy beliefs are controlled, the outcomes expected for given performances make little or no independent contribution to prediction of behavior” (p. 24). Empirical evidence has also borne this out in that even when subjects were given monetary incentives (i.e., an outcome expectation) subjects with low self-efficacy did not perform better (Lee et al. 1997).

Instead, Bandura (1997) has suggested, “Outcomes arise from actions. How one behaves largely determines the outcomes one experiences. Performance is thus causally prior to outcomes” (p. 21, emphasis ours). Figure 3 depicts this newest explication of SCT.

At the same time, though, Bandura also suggests a reciprocal relationship between SE and performance and OE and performance. What this suggests is that both SE and OE may serve as antecedents to, as well as result from, performance. Thus, it seems instructive to compare these two models to see which model most accurately reflects the training context of interest.

One additional construct, anxiety, which was not investigated in the original study was included based upon the model of Marakas et al. (1998) and the strong empirical findings from social cognitive research as to its influence (Bandura 1986).

Anxiety. Emotional arousal (or anxiety) is a wellknown antecedent to one’s CSE judgments, since people often base their efficacy estimations on both their physiological and psychological states (Bandura 1977a, 1977b; Lazarus and Folkman 1984). This arousal not only tends to be considered in the efficacy estimating process, but throughout the performance activity, as well. Evidence suggests that a low level of efficacy can result in subjects spending increased time focusing on their arousal rather than their performance (Bandura 1977a, Rosen et al. 1987). Based on this, and other evidence contained in the literature (i.e., Glass and Knight 1988, Meier 1988), the relationship between anxiety and performance was included in the proposed model.

## 4. Experimental Methodology

Two issues arise out of the methodological approach adopted in the original Compeau and Higgins study. First, within the set of predictors of CSE, enactive mastery is known to have the greatest influence (Bandura 1977b). Therefore, ceteris parabis, its effect on CSE should conceivably be larger than the effects of the modeling manipulation. Compeau and Higgins acknowledge that their lack of measurement of CSE both after the manipulation and practice time as a limitation in their study. By measuring CSE only after the modeling manipulation, increases in performance due to CSE may be attributed to modeling, but as can be seen from Figure 4, some of the increase in performance may, in reality, be due to enactive mastery experience and its influence on CSE. To investigate the potential effects of this issue, we chose to measure CSE at both pre-manipulation and post-practice time periods and propose a final hypothesis:

Hypothesis 11. CSE will be higher after the practice session than before.

The second methodological issue of concern is also related to the effects of enactive mastery on CSE estimations. In the original study, the training manipulation provided experimental subjects with a 30-minute training video while allowing control subjects 30 minutes to review their training notes. While this manipulation does differentiate between a modeling approach and a nonmodeling approach, it is nonetheless possible that subjects who had time to review their notes were actually processing syntactical information (i.e., the proper formula for a summation is “@sum(cell1, celln)”), thus engaging in a form of enactive mastery. As a result, any differences due to the manipulation may have been confounded. This could easily account for some of the nonsignificant and negative results found in word-processing training of the Compeau and Higgins study. An alternative manipulation is to have the control group spend time on a nontechnology-related activity. For this study, the control group watched a video not related in any way to technology or to the experimental manipulation while the nonbehavioral modeling group was allowed to review their notes.

Subjects. Subjects were 146 students enrolled in two sections of an introductory information systems application course at a large mid-atlantic university. The majority had little or no experience with Excel and 95.2% of the subjects were between the ages of 17 to 24 years. Gender was evenly balanced within the subject pool (M:F  55:45) and no significant differences on any demographic dimension were found across the experimental groups. Subjects were qualified by both a pre-test subjective assessment of their prior experience with Excel (in a manner similar to that performed in the original study) and also by an objective Excel knowledge demonstration (KD) developed by the authors. The results of both assessments were used to determine whether a subject’s responses should be included in the final data analysis. Given the magnitude of the influence of prior experience on the variables of interest herein, any subject who listed their experience as “fair” or better or any subject who scored greater than 40% on the KD was eliminated from the final data analysis. This exclusion reduced the useable response sample to 116 subjects.

Procedures. The methodology employed in this study parallels that of Compeau and Higgins (1995) wherever possible, but materially diverges in three distinct areas—a reduction in subject training time, an increase in the number of interim measurement points, and the addition of a control group (see Figure 5). Upon arriving at the training session, subjects were randomly assigned to receive a questionnaire containing CSE questions from either the original Compeau and Higgins study (1995) or CSE questions developed using the Marakas et al. framework, as well as measures of anxiety and outcome expectancy. Subjects also completed the same subjective pre-test instrument as was used in the original Compeau and Higgins study, as well as the KD. Following the pre-test activities, all subjects received a 30-minute instructional lecture on Excel. Following the lecture, subjects in the control group (G1) watched a 30-minute video which was unrelated to computers in any way, subjects in the nonbehavioral modeling group (G2) were given 30 minutes to review their notes, and subjects in the behavioral modeling group (G3) watched a 30-minute behavioral modeling video on using Excel. After the manipulation, subjects completed a questionnaire which measured CSE (using the same questions as in their respective pre-test questionnaire), outcome expectancy, and anxiety. All subjects were then given a 15-minute practice session after which they completed a questionnaire measuring the same variables as the previous questionnaire. Finally, all subjects participated in a 25-minute final Excel skill test where they were asked to complete a series of 15 tasks using Excel.<sup>3</sup>

The degree of behavioral modeling was determined via the random assignment of subjects to one of the three training groups. Neither the G1 nor G2 received any behavioral modeling for Excel while G3 viewed the modeling video. The video chosen was used previously by Yi (1998) and was shown to be an effective vicarious experience manipulation for Excel training.

Measures. Prior Experience. Subjective assessment of experience was measured by 10 questions developed by Compeau (1992) which addressed subjects’ previous experience with different software, as well as an objective Excel skills knowledge demonstration (KD) developed for this study (10 multiple-choice questions). A pilot test revealed that those with little or no previous experience performed poorly on this test and those with high experience performed well.<sup>4</sup>

Computer Self-Efficacy. Across all groups, CSE for half of the subjects was measured using the 10 questions developed by Compeau and Higgins (1995) and administered in the original study. The questions ask an individual to rate their ability (on a scale of 1–10) to accomplish a task using Excel, under differing levels of assistance (“if there was no one around to tell me what to do as I go”, “if I had only the software manuals for reference”, etc.). For the remaining subjects, CSE was measured using 9 items developed by Marakas et al. (1999) following the development framework proposed by Marakas et al. (1998).<sup>5</sup> These questions measured individuals beliefs in their abilities to complete tasks using a spreadsheet, such as communicating numeric information and summarizing numeric information. For each question, subjects responded dichotomously (yes/no) to each question. For each yes answer, subjects indicated their confidence in their abilities on a 10–100 scale. Both measures displayed extremely high reliabilities with regard to internal consistency.

Outcome Expectancy. OE was measured by 9 questions developed by Compeau and Higgins (1995).<sup>6</sup> These questions measure outcomes that might occur due to effective use of the computer system. Each question used a five-point scale, with anchors of “very unlikely” and “very likely”.

Computer Anxiety. Anxiety was measured with an 8- item subset of the 19-item Computer Anxiety Rating Scale (CARS; Heinssen et al. 1987). Consistent with previous factor analysis (Harrison and Rainer 1992), the anxiety items were found to load on multiple factors, often cross-loading with outcome expectancy. Eight questions which cleanly loaded, and which still adequately reflected anxiety, were retained as a measure of the construct.

Finally, performance (SCORE) was measured using the subject’s score on an Excel skill task developed by Yi (1998).

## Data Analysis

The data was divided into two sets reflecting the use of the two different CSE measures. For analysis of the two models, conventional structural equation modeling analysis was performed using AMOS 4.0 with a maximum likelihood algorithm (Analysis of Moment Structures; Arbuckle and Wothke 1999). For in-depth investigation of Hypothesis 8 and Hypothesis 10 A and B, regression analysis and ANOVA tests (allowing for unequal sample sizes) were used.

## Behavioral Modeling

Hypotheses 1 through 3 focused on the effects of the behavioral modeling manipulation on several variables of interest. The results obtained paralleled those of the original study with Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 3 finding support and nonsignificant results obtained for Hypothesis 2. It is important to note here that the subjects in the note-review group and the video group did not differ significantly on any of the variables of interest, suggesting that the use of a review period did not provide any type of enactive mastery experience for the subjects. Therefore, for the remainder of the study, data from these two groups were analyzed together as a nonbehavioral modeling manipulation. Using repeated measures analysis, we investigated how CSE changed over time. Results indicate that CSE was influenced by both the manipulation and practice.

## Computer Self-Efficacy

An interesting result occurs upon investigation of H11. As shown in Table 2, using both the Marakas et al. measures and the Compeau and Higgins measures, we find that the subjects’ CSE is higher after the manipulation than before the manipulation. Using the Marakas et al. measures, we also find strong support for H11 (p  0.000). Subject CSE levels were higher after the enactive mastery practice session than before the post-manipulation practice period. Yet for the Compeau and Higgins measures, we do not find support (p  0.450). These results suggest that while both measures are able to capture changes in CSE, the Marakas et al. measures appear to be able to capture them more finely.

## Outcome Expectations

The results obtained in the original Compeau and Higgins study with regard to OE were countertheoretic and often significant in the opposite direction than hypothesized. The results obtained in this study found no support for H6 using the Compeau and Higgins model and the hypothesis was not tested using the Marakas et al. measure. No significant relationship was found between OE and performance until the post-practice measurement. This is easily explained given that the subjects were now well aware of their ability to perform and could, therefore, easily formulate an outcome expectation based upon that experience.

## Prior Experience

In the original study, Hypothesis 7 was tested on Day 2 by using the performance obtained on Day 1 as a measure of prior experience for Day 2. One possible confound to this approach is that the Day 1 subjects received training and experience on a different software package (either word-processing or spreadsheet) than that of Day 2. Implicit in the original design is the assumption that prior experience with a spreadsheet will translate into a new experience with a wordprocessor and vice versa. Results obtained in the original study were equivocal with the hypothesis being supported for spreadsheet but not for wordprocessing.

The influence of prior experience on OE is found through its influence on performance.

The results obtained with regard to Hypothesis 9 may be some of the most revealing of the study. As discussed previously, a material difference between the original study and this one is the use of both a subjective (as used in the Compeau and Higgins study) and objective pre-test measure of experience (KD). By comparing each of these measures to the dependent variable, insight is gained into a possible confound in the original study.

As described by Compeau and Higgins:

Potential participants were first screened to ensure that they had little prior experience with computers. This screening took place during a telephone interview and relied on the judgments of the potential subjects . . . As a check on the effectiveness of the screening, three questions from the background questionnaire were examined . . . overall familiarity with computers . . . familiarity with WordPerfect and Lotus was also assessed (1995, pg. 124).

The results obtained from these screenings were used to determine the suitability of the subject for participation in the study. Of concern is the use of a perceptual measure as a check against a perceptual measure (questionnaire results compared to verbal screening results). To determine the effectiveness of the perceptual measure a comparison was done between the subjective measure employed in the original study and the KD measure used herein. As shown in Table 2, there was no relationship found between the subject’s perceived experience level and the KD (p  0.239). More interesting, however, are the results obtained during the filtering process for this study.

Recall from the subsection describing the subject selection process that any subject who listed their prior experience as “fair” or better or anyone scoring greater than 40% on the KD was excluded from the final data analysis. This filtering excluded a total of 30 subjects from the original sample of 146 (20.5% reduction). Prior to their removal, the relationship between the subjective and objective measures of experience was highly significant (p  0.001). Following removal of the high experience subjects, however, the relationship between the subjective and objective assessments was no longer significant (p  0.239). This suggests that the high experience subjects were better able to provide an accurate subjective experience assessment than the less experienced subjects. This finding is intuitive given the strong propensity for novices to overestimate their abilities (cf. Lichtenstein et al. 1982, Keren, 1991, Spence 1996), because, as Pfeifer (1994) suggests, “(overconfidence) is a natural consequence of imperfect knowledge” (p. 211). Conversely, those with a large amount of experience in a domain are often better to self-assess when they receive immediate feedback as to their ability (cf. Murphy and Winkler 1977, Tomassini et al. 1982). When people use a spreadsheet package, they receive quick feedback as to how well they are performing and of their spreadsheet ability. Thus, it should not be surprising that those with higher amounts of experience using spreadsheets should be much more accurate at calibrating their actual abilities.

Further, this effect was the result of approximately one-fifth of the total sample indicating a relatively strong effect size. Results obtained following the removal showed no relationship between the subjective prior experience assessment and final performance (p  0.803) but a highly significant relationship between the objective assessment and the final performance indicating unequivocal support for H9. While this replicates the results obtained in the original study, it also suggests that the difficulty of inexperienced subjects to accurately assess their experience may have potentially confounded the effects of variables such as BM, CSE, and OE within the Compeau and Higgins (1995) model.

Although not investigated in the original study, the model of antecedents to CSE offered by Marakas et al. (1998) suggests that anxiety has a marked relationship with estimations of CSE and subsequent performance. Results obtained using both measures indicated support for H10a and H10b evidencing the inverse relationship between anxiety and CSE.<sup>7</sup> Strong support was also found for H10c both after the manipulation and before the actual performance. As shown in the table, the relationship between anxiety levels (ANX) and performance (SCORE) was highly significant at all measurement periods following the pre-test measure.

## 6. Limitations

As with any study, limitations with regard to interpretation of the results must be expressly identified. First, the trainer for all sessions was the principal investigator and was, thus, aware of the hypotheses under study. Although this certainly could be considered as a threat to internal validity, the investigator worked from a tightly scripted discourse to insure that the subjects were exposed to the same training content and contact time and all manipulations occurred immediately after the lecture portion of the training. While we acknowledge that a more ideal scenario would have been to conduct the training of all subjects simultaneously, analysis of the key variables revealed no significant differences between any of the sessions. Further, this approach differs from that employed in the original Compeau and Higgins (1995) study where a professional trainer was used. Given the parallels between the results obtained herein and those of the original study, it appears that this difference did not materially affect the study.

Additional limitations are found with the multiple measurement periods. By administering the three questionnaires in a somewhat compressed time period, it is possible that the increases in the responses could be due to a demand or testing effect. Since the training was expected to lead to increases in all the variables which were measured at different times, it becomes somewhat problematic to ascertain whether a testing effect was or was not occurring. Evidence from measures taken after training suggests that this was not occurring, because scores did not significantly change after the performance task. If the testing effect were occurring, then we would expect scores to continue to rise. Admittedly, a counterargument is that the responses may actually stabilize because subjects are remembering past responses, and responding similarly for consistency.<sup>8</sup> Thus, threats from testing effects cannot be completely eliminated.

With respect to external validity, there are also several potential limitations. First, this study utilized undergraduate students in a controlled laboratory setting, and therefore may not generalize to organizational settings. When using student subjects, a way to minimize the effect of this limitation is to use subjects with the same characteristics of the population of interest. It was felt that for the purposes of this experiment, the student subjects should be representative of the professional population of interest. Another limitation is the limited time in training and the coverage. Due to laboratory availability and time constraints, the training given was not able to completely replicate, in environment or time, the training of Compeau and Higgins. Further, the training was only an introduction, not an in-depth study of one software. Although there is no evidence that the effects of modeling and self-efficacy would differ for other software packages, we cannot, based upon this one study, generalize this to all software packages. More complex and in-depth study of the software may have differing effects on both CSE and performance than was found here.

## 7. Discussion

As with the original Compeau and Higgins work, their model did not find complete support in this study either. This is reinforced by the fact that the findings of this replication are very similar to those in the original study when the same measures are used. The revised model of the CSE-Performance-OE relationship proposed herein did find support, however. Consistent across the models is the significant role CSE plays in the acquisition of computer skills. Given the findings in this and the previous work of Compeau and Higgins (1995) and Gist et al. (1989) CSE should be regarded as a critical variable to include in future studies when comparing multiple training methods or measuring any change in acquired skill or acumen.

This research was able to provide a finer granularity of understanding to the role that training methods play in influencing CSE estimations. This addresses an acknowledged limitation of the Compeau and Higgins study:

Subjects’ self-efficacy may have changed following the post measure but before the performance test. Since these possible changes were not measured, the relationship between selfefficacy immediately prior to performance and actual performance may be somewhat different than that shown by the data (p. 135).

By measuring CSE both after the manipulation and again after practice, we were able to see that both the vicarious experience and enactive mastery contributed to CSE increases. This suggests that gains in CSE and performance could not be attributed directly to the training manipulation. In fact, just participating in effective training appears to have an efficacy-enhancing effect as suggested by the results obtained by the nonbehavioral modeling groups. It becomes important for future research using both enactive mastery and other manipulations to measure CSE at all points where it would be expected to change. While this, admittedly, increases the risk of a learning effect as the subjects answer the same questions multiple times, it appears, nonetheless, necessary if we are to isolate the true effect of the training manipulation on the dependent variable.

An additional finding focuses on the role of outcome expectancies. While outcome expectancies have been shown to play an influential role in the decision to use technology (cf. Davis 1989, Thompson et al. 1991), within a social cognitive training model, outcome expectations appear to not add to the explanation of performance. These findings should not be seen as a call for discontinued use of outcome expectancy, but instead as a call to further research as to the appropriate role of outcome expectancies in training and learning.

In this study, two measures of CSE were compared. While both measures were able to capture changes in CSE and both measures performed well within the model, they had differential levels of effectiveness. Each instrument had its advantages in the model. For example, the Marakas et al. measures were more effective at capturing changes in CSE throughout the experiment, it was able to capture the relationship with prior experience to a greater extent. It appears that the Marakas et al. measures are better able to capture changes in CSE due to enactive mastery. On the other hand, the Compeau and Higgins measure was able to predict performance to a greater extent and was influenced more by the modeling training. This suggests that the Compeau and Higgins measures may be more able to capture changes due to vicarious experience. While this paper explored issues as to the development of CSE measures and their predictive ability, more research is needed to address how we develop and validate measures of CSE.

Further research is needed to look at the degree to which the effects of increased CSE in one computer domain are transferable to another. Both Bandura (1997) and Marakas, Yi, and Johnson (1998) suggest that self-efficacy increases in one subdomain may affect self-efficacy in other related subdomains. One of the important training tasks in IS departments is the initial software application training on different packages (i.e., spreadsheet, word-processor, database, etc.). If we can understand how CSE transfers from one subdomain to another, through either skill acquisition or increased coping capabilities, then we can provide training focused on those aspects that accelerate or enhance such transfer.

Given the strength of the relationship between performance and subsequent OE, an interesting question arises as to how the training may influence subsequent levels of OE. Further research investigating the role that training has on performance, post-training expectancies, subsequent technology use/adoption decisions, and continued technology use could provide a valuable link between training and adoption research.<sup>9</sup>

Through replication and extension of the seminal work by Compeau and Higgins (1995), this study represents the next step in the process of understanding the influence of behavioral modeling on computer selfefficacy, and ultimately on the acquisition of training skills. By aligning the theory, measures, and methodology more closely with the underlying reference disciplines, researchers will have a much greater opportunity to provide effective computer training.
