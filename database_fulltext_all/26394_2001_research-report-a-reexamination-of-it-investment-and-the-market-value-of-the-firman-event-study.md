# Research Report: A Reexamination of IT Investment and the Market Value of the Firm—An Event Study Methodology

Kun Shin Im • Kevin E. Dow • Varun Grover

E <sup>valuating</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>effectiveness</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>Information</sup> <sup>Technology</sup> <sup>(IT)</sup> <sup>investments</sup> <sup>has</sup> <sup>always</sup> <sup>been</sup> an elusive but important goal of IS researchers. This study builds on a prior study that examined changes in the market value of the firm as reflected by the stock price in response to IT investment announcements. Data on stock prices were analyzed for 238 publicly traded companies. In addition to the stock price analysis, reaction of trading volume to the announcements was also examined to identify whether IT investment announcements affect investors beliefs about IT value. Potentially confounding factors such as industry, size, and time lag effects were also analyzed. Size and time lag effects were found for all IT investment announcements. Reactions of price and volume were negatively related to firm size and became more positive over time. The positive excess return for smaller firms shows that smaller firms can leverage the lower price/performance ratio of new IT and reap greater rewards from IT investments than larger firms. Also, the result of time lag effect demonstrates that the stock market has recently begun to identify both tangible and intangible benefits of IT investments. For recent IT investment announcements, industry classification and firm size also affected the reactions of stock price to the announcements. This study provides optimism on the stock market reaction to IT investment announcements as well as further insight into the study of IT impacts on organizational performance.

## 1. Introduction

Does information technology (IT) pay off? Many attempts have been made to answer this question, by both economists and IS researchers. Recent evidence indicates that IT has been a very important contributor to productivity (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, 1998, Brynjolffson and

Yang 1997). Despite these findings, the results of other empirical studies relating IT investments and firm performance have been equivocal (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Loveman 1994, Weill 1992, Wilson 1993). These contradictory results have been attributed primarily to the inadequacies of productivity measurement and the time lags due to an IT learning effect, or a time-consuming period of complementary organization changes (Brynjolfsson 1993, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998).

It has been argued that the intangible benefits of IT, in areas such as improved quality, variety, timeliness, and customization have not been appropriately measured. This is primarily due to the use of conventional productivity measurement techniques (Brynjolfsson 1993, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998). Further, because many factors influence firm performance, it is difficult to establish causality between IT investments and firmlevel output performance. Recently, IS researchers have begun to use stock market valuation as an alternative measure of the business performance (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Yang 1997, Dos Santos et al. 1993). Specifically, Dos Santos et al. (1993) investigated the relationship between IT and market value using the event study methodology that uses the stock market’s reaction to announcements as an assessment of how the announcement impacts stockholders’ valuation of the firm. They found no change in stock prices for the sample of IT investment announcements. This result was not different for their two-industry subsamples. However, they found that “innovative” IT investments had positive effects on stock price.

This study builds upon the work of Dos Santos et al. (1993) in three distinct ways. First, it uses a larger and more recent sample of IT investment announcements and investigates the time lag effect of IT investments. Second, it draws from parallel work in other disciplines to examine not only price but also volume reactions of these announcements. Finally, in addition to the time lag effect, it controls for other confounding factors such as size and industry to enhance the internal validity of the findings. In the spirit of building a cumulative tradition of research, this study provides a richer understanding of IT impacts on organizational performance.

## 1.1. The Event Study Methodology

Many IT productivity studies that use conventional productivity measurement techniques fail to consider many of the improvements in economic output brought about by IT (such as faster service, more varied products and services, and better access to business information). To complement these productivity studies, a powerful argument can be made for the use of the event study methodology that has become popular in the accounting and finance literatures.<sup>1</sup> The event study methodology is a powerful tool that can help IS researchers assess the business performance of IT investments using such market-based measures as stock price or trading volume. It obviates the need to analyze accounting-based measures of IT investments’ benefits, which have been criticized because they are often not adequate indicators of the true performance of IT investments (McWilliams and Siegel 1997). This method enables researchers to measure stock price changes that can serve as estimates for the effectiveness of the firm in foreseeing and rapidly adapting to its changing environment (Brynjolfsson and Yang 1996).

In an event study, the objective is to examine the stock market’s response to events that are often related to the release of information to the stock market. According to the semistrong form of efficient market hypothesis (with which empirical evidence is consistent), the market price fully reflects all publicly available information (Fama 1970, 1976; Jensen 1978; Watts and Zimmerman 1986). Therefore, an abnormal stock price effect associated with an unanticipated event should be observed if the event has information content.<sup>2</sup> The event study method focuses on the effect of the event on stock price, which is an unbiased estimate of risk and return on IT investments. It allows the measurement of stock market reactions to the release of information about IT investments. If the market responds by revaluing a firm’s stock to reflect the information in the IT investment announcements, it can be concluded that the investment affects the market value of the firm. In other words, if rational investors value both tangible and intangible aspects of IT investments, a change in stock price should approximate the true contribution of IT to the firm’s value not only in cost reductions, but also in increased variety, timeliness, and quality (Brynjolfsson and Yang 1996). The event study methodology is also relatively easy to implement because the only data necessary are the publicly traded firms’ name, event dates, stock prices, and trading volumes. Despite these merits, this methodology has not yet been widely employed by IS researchers, with the exception of Dos Santos et al. (1993).

## 1.2. Dos Santos, Peffers, and Mauer’s Study

Dos Santos et al. (1993) examined the impact of IT investment announcements on the market value of the firm for a sample of 97 IT investments in the finance and manufacturing industries from 1981 to 1988. In their study, the overall effect of IT investment announcements on excess returns was not found to be significant.<sup>3</sup> Researchers have discussed confounding effects, such as industry and size effects, that may have contributed to the lack of the stock market’s reaction (c.f., Berge et al. 1984; Brealey and Myers 1984; Brown and Warner 1980, 1985; Foster 1986; Madhavan and Prescott 1995; Xiang 1993). On average, a financial firm’s IT investment decision may be a major technological event, while a manufacturing firm’s IT investment decision may not. Because financial firms have more information-intensive processes than manufacturers, IT could have a greater impact on firm performance in the financial firms than in the manufacturing firms (Cash et al. 1992, Jarvenpaa and Ives 1990, Johnston and Carrico 1988, Porter and Millar 1985). Therefore, different responses of the market to IT investment announcements across industries would be expected. Dos Santos et al. (1993) also tested for the industry effect by classifying their sample into manufacturing and finance subsamples and by analyzing the excess returns for each subsample. However, they found that the excess returns were not significantly different from zero in these subsamples. In this study, additional announcements of IT investments are gathered and examined in order to determine whether the stock market reacts differently to IT investments announcements across industries.

A potentially confounding factor (firm size) was not tested by Dos Santos et al. (1993). Securities of smaller firms are traded at lower prices, and the volatility of stock price for smaller firms is often much greater than that for larger firms (Brealey and Myers 1984, Brown and Warner 1985, Fargher and Weigand 1998). This size effect presents difficulties in combining smaller and larger firms in event studies. More importantly, firm size has also served as a surrogate for the amount of predisclosure information (Atiase 1985, Bamber 1986, 1987, Ziebart 1990). For example, Atiase (1985) argues that the amount of private predisclosure information production and dissemination is an increasing function of firm size. The larger the firm and, hence, the more information likely to be available from alternative sources,<sup>4</sup> the less important the formal announcement and the smaller and quicker the reaction to that announcement (Bamber 1987). Thus, ceteris paribus, the amount of unexpected information conveyed to the market by IT investment announcements should be inversely related to firm size. Therefore, it would be expected that IT investment announcements of larger firms have marginally less information content (i.e., less incremental value of the actual disclosure) than those of smaller firms. Hence, the market reaction would be smaller to IT investment announcements by larger firms.

In addition to the industry and size effects, the time lag effect can be considered to explain the lack of the stock market’s reaction to IT investment announcements. It has been argued that to realize the potential benefits of computerization, additional (complementary) investments may be needed in other intangible assets such as worker knowledge, new organizational structures, or interorganizational relationships (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1993, 1998; Brynjolfsson and Yang 1997). There is some lag or adjustment time required to match IT investments and the organizational factors that all appear to be important in realizing the maximum benefit of IT (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998). For example, Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1993) argue that because the changes in business processes needed to realize the benefits of IT may have taken some time to be implemented, it is possible that the actual returns from investments in computers have increased over time. Moreover, they maintain that a pattern of increasing returns is consistent with the strategy for optimal investment in the presence of learning-by-doing. In other words, if the learning curve for a general purpose industrial tool such as IT is long (Ives 1994), shortterm returns of IT investments should initially be lower than returns of other capital. However, the returns of IT investments tend to subsequently rise to exceed the returns of other capital, compensating for the investment in learning (c.f., Lester and McCabe 1993). Therefore, a time lag effect of IT on the market value of the firms is also expected.

Finally, trading volume has also been used as a measure of information content in many event studies (e.g., Atiase and Bamber 1994; Bamber 1986; Bamber and Cheon 1995; Barron 1995; Beaver 1968; Holthausen and Verrecchia 1990; Karpoff 1986, 1987; Kim et al. 1997; Kim and Verrecchia 1991; Lobo and Tung 1997; Verrecchia 1981; Wang 1994; Ziebart 1990; etc.). Trading volume reflects investors’ activity by summing all market trades, whereas stock prices reflect an aggregation or averaging of investors’ beliefs (Bamber 1986). Trading volume could be treated as the number of transactions between buyers and sellers who are randomly paired in the trading period. Trading opportunities arise because both potential buyers and potential sellers revise their expectations (e.g., demand prices) about the value of a stock. It might be expected that information increases trading volume if it causes investors (i.e., buyers or sellers) to revise their demand prices heterogeneously or if the information is partially but not homogeneously anticipated (Karpoff 1986).<sup>5</sup> Therefore, any movement in trading volume in response to IT investment announcements can be interpreted in terms of investors’ lack of agreement on their interpretation of the value of the information. Examination of volume effects can add useful insight to the nonsignificant reaction of stock price to IT investment announcements in the previous study (Dos Santos et al. 1993).

## 1.3. Propositions

The previous section suggests that a more comprehensive approach to event analysis is warranted, which incorporates industry effect, size effect, time lag effect and volume reaction to obtain a more valid assessment of IT’s impact on the firm’s market value. From this assessment, it is possible to estimate the tangible and intangible benefits of IT investment and infer the information content of the IT investment announcements. This study replicates, extends, and enhances Dos Santos et al. (1993) study by revisiting the critical questions of relationship between IT investment and the stock market’s reaction. As such, this study draws from the earlier discussion and addresses three key research questions:

1. Is there an overall impact of IT investment announcements on the market value (stock, price) of the firm? If not, do the announcements affect trading volume?

2. Does industry or firm size affect the response of stock price to IT investment announcements?

3. Do more recent announcements reflect different valuation from older announcements?

First, this study investigates whether the aggregate market’s belief (i.e., stock price) about the value of IT investment is altered when information about IT investment decisions is released. If the release of information about IT investments is unexpected in the stock market, an abnormal reaction of stock market should be observed. Moreover, if the IT investment announcements are good news to investors, an increase in excess returns should be expected. Brynjolfsson and Yang (1997) argue that investments in complementary assets are required to make IT investments profitable. If these “intangible” complementary investments are costly but invisible to the stock market, then the announcement of an IT investment is a signal (i.e., good news) that the firm has made or is making such investments. This would in turn justify a higher market valuation for the firm, even in equilibrium. Accordingly, it is expected that IT investment announcements will lead to an increase in the market value of the firm.

Second, it has been documented that trading volume rises on the days of earnings announcements (Bamber 1986; Beaver 1968; Foster 1973; Kiger 1972; Morse 1980, 1981; Wang 1994). This result suggests that because earnings announcements convey relevant information about a firm, they cause investors to revise their expectations about the firm’s value attributes (Lobo and Tung 1997). If IT investments have an impact on the market value of the firm<sup>6</sup> and IT investment announcements convey this information to the stock market, the information release of IT investments can also give rise to trading. Therefore, it is expected that trading volume will increase when the IT investments are announced. However, because trading volume reflects differences in individual investors’ belief revisions about value-relevant attributes of a security (Bamber 1986, Karpof3f 1986, Kim and Verrecchia 1991, Lobo and Tung 1997), it should be noted that the interpretation of a trading volume increase is ambiguous. The volume increase can indicate (1) that investors interpret the information differently, or (2) that they interpret the information identically but begin with diverse prior expectations (Karpoff 1986).

Third, this study also explores whether the price reactions to IT investment announcements vary across industries and are impacted by firm size. The expectation is that modern IT investments will leverage all industries, but will have a more profound effect on information-intensive firms (e.g., financial firms rather than manufacturing firms). On the other hand, it is found that the unexpected return at the time of earnings announcements is larger for smaller firms (Bamber 1986). This is due to the fact that for smaller firms, the announcement may contain more new information about firm earnings than for larger firms (Bamber 1986, Wang 1994). Accordingly, a negative relationship between the price reaction to IT investment announcements and the firm size is expected. In other words, it is expected that the price reaction will be larger to IT investment announcements by smaller firms than to those by larger firms.

Fourth, there is evidence that firms are beginning to see both tangible and intangible benefits of IT investments (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1993, 1996, 1998). Unlike other researchers who failed to find IT increases output, Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1996) found evidence that

IT has made a substantial contribution to firm output. Because the models they applied were essentially the same as those that have been previously used to assess the contribution of IT, they attributed the different results to the fact that their data set is more current and larger than others explored (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996). Similarly, recent IT investment announcements would be expected to bring more increases of the market value and trading volume of the firm than old announcements. Hence, the expectation is that the responses of price and volume to announcements of IT investments will increase over time.<sup>7</sup>

These expectations can be stated as a core proposition and four subpropositions that reflect the confounding factors:

Proposition: Stock Price Will Positively React to Announcements of IT Investments.

P1: Trading volume will positively react to announcements of IT investments.

P2: Stock price will react more positively to announcements of IT investments by financial firms than those by manufacturing firms.

P3: Stock price reactions to announcement of IT investments will be negatively related to firm size.

P4: Stock price and volume will react more positively to announcements of IT investments over time.

## 2. Research Design

## 2.1. Sample Selection

In addition to stock price data on the 97 IT announcements collected by Dos Santos et al. (1993) for the time period from 1981 to 1988, stock price and trading volume data for the more recent period from 1989 to 1996 was also collected. A procedure for sample selection used by Dos Santos et al. (1993) was followed in this study. PR Newswire and PTS Prompt as well as Business and Industry were searched for news articles on announcements of IT investments over the period 1989 to 1996 by using such keywords as computer, hardware, software, and other terms related to information systems (e.g., DSS, EIS, ES, Internet/Intranet and Client/Server Systems) along with action verbs (such as plan to purchase, install, and develop). SIC codes were used to classify the industries of the sample firms in this study. Over 1,500 newer announcements were analyzed and evaluated based on the following (additional) sampling criteria:

1. Only announcements in daily publications such as business newspapers, national newspapers and regional newspapers were included.

2. Only announcements by firms that are traded on major security exchanges (such as NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ) were included.

3. IT investment announcements which might be confounded by any other information announcements (e.g., dividends, earnings, merge/acquisition, etc.) around the announcement period were excluded.

4. Only IT investment announcements by firms whose data on firm size are available in the COMPU-STAT data file were included.

5. Only IT investment announcements that were significant at the corporate level, rather than at a division or subunit, were included.

These sampling criteria yielded a total of 141 additional IT investment announcements over the period from 1989 to 1996. Along with the data on earlier announcements (Dos Santos 1993), a total of 238 IT announcements were analyzed. The complete sample is listed in Appendix A.

## 2.2. Statistical Methodology and Measures of Variables

Event and Estimation Period. A time line for this study is represented in Figure 1. Selection of the length of event period and estimation period is based on previous event studies and institutional factors. A oneday event period is usually recommended. However, when the event announcement appears in the newspaper, it is unclear whether the market was informed before the close of the market on the prior trading day. Thus, if the announcement occurs after the close of trading on the previous day, any immediate valuation effects will be reflected in the stock price on the day in which the announcement appears in print. On the contrary, if the information is released prior to the close of trading, any immediate valuation effect will be registered on the day before the announcement appears in print (McConnell and Muscarella 1985). The usual way of handling this problem is to expand the event window from one day to two days. Accordingly, this study used a two-day event period, including the day of publication of the announcement and the day preceding it. On the other hand, the period for estimating normal returns typically ranges from 100 to 300 days for daily studies (Kritzman 1994, Peterson 1989). To facilitate comparison with prior studies, this study used a time period of 200 days before the event to estimate the expected return and trading volume.<sup>8</sup>

## 3.1. Price and Volume Responses to IT Investment Announcements

Overall Price and Volume Responses. Average standardized cumulative abnormal returns (ASCAR) and volumes (ASCAV) over the event window are presented in Table 2. As shown in the table, the full sample did not demonstrate that IT investments are related to an increase in the market value of the firm. This result is consistent with that of Dos Santos, et al. Similarly, trading volume was not higher than the expected trading volume over the event period. Because the difference in investors’ response to the same information generates trading (Wang 1994), this result would suggest that all investors interpret the announcements identically (high consensus). ## 4. Conclusions

This study has examined the responses of price and trading volume to announcements of IT investments. We also investigated whether, in addition to the industry and the size effect, IT investments increase the market value of the firm over time. In analyzing the full sample of 238 IT investment announcements, the results indicate no effects of price (and volume). Also, consistent with Dos Santos et al. (1993) financial firms did not have a larger impact of IT investments on their market value than nonfinancial firms. The conclusion might have been that IT investments did not increase the market value of the firm, nor did individual investors consider them when they made decisions on stock trading. However, further analysis indicates that this would have been an incorrect interpretation and might be misleading. The results and their possible interpretations are summarized below.

Consistently, there is no price reaction for larger firms and a positive price reaction for smaller firms: This indicates that smaller firms’ IT investments increased the market value of the firms. This result might be a consequence of dilution due to more predisclosure information in the larger firm, thereby decreasing the impact of the formal announcement. Alternatively, it may indicate that IT investments were disproportionately beneficial for smaller firms. It is consistent with the finding in Brynjolfsson’s paper (1994)<sup>14</sup> that smaller firms provide better incentives for exploiting IT than larger firms.

There is an increase in both price and volume reaction over time: Both price and volume reacted more positively to new announcements of IT investments than to old announcements. The positive time lag effect might indicate that the stock market has begun to react positively to the information content of IT-specific announcements. As investors perceive the increasing importance of IT and companies demonstrate their ability to leverage IT investments through complementary assets, the valuations of IT announcements increase. It is consistent with the findings in Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1993, 1996, 1998).

Volume reactions changed from negative (old sample) to positive (new sample), also suggesting that the information content, and arguably the importance of IT announcements, is increasing, thereby causing investors to take action (buy or sell) in the marketplace. It might also indicate a lowering of consensus among investors as to the long-term interpretation of the announcement, in the increasingly diverse and uncertain technological environment. Therefore, in the earlier sample, while there was greater consensus on IT investments, traders might not have considered them important enough to take action. In the newer sample, the importance might be recognized and traders take action, but the uncertainty and lack of consensus forces both buying and selling, thereby increasing volume.

Both industry and size effects become stronger over time: It was also found that in the subsample of the recent IT investment announcements, the industry and size effects were stronger than those effects in the full sample. Thus, both smaller firms and financial firms in the new subsample had more positive excess return. These results suggest that, particularly for smaller firms and financial firms, the “IT productivity paradox” was alleviated by 1991 (c.f., Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1993).

In the tradition of positivist research on IS effectiveness, the results of this study should be viewed as a positive finding. The reasons for the results could be manifold, not the least of which are the improved technologies and infrastructures, the increased learning effects of older technologies, and the willingness to undertake process and structural changes to leverage powerful IT. Hopefully, recent work on business process reengineering, e-commerce, organization change, and IT productivity can ultimately converge with these results and facilitate more prescriptive implications on IT deployment.

Although a concerted attempt was made to control for confounding variables (see Appendix C), all potentially confounding variables could not be considered in this study. For example, future studies could control for characteristics of IT investments. Infrastructure investments might systematically affect the market value of the firm more than IT investments in special purpose applications. Further, IT investments would affect firm value differently, depending upon whether they are innovative or follow-up investments (c.f., Dos Santos et al. 1993).<sup>15</sup> Similarly, controlling for the amount of IT investment expenditure might be useful for future studies. Also, a more refined examination of industry (i.e., explicitly considering industry information intensity) and the impact of intranets and Internet (i.e., electronic commerce) on organizational performance might provide additional insight.

In conclusion, this study builds on prior work in the spirit of a cumulative tradition of IS research. This empirical work using event study methodology serves to complement the rich case study and other quantitative work, which are beginning to show that IT spending is of value to the firm. In doing so, this study provides further insight into the elusive notion of firm-level IS effectiveness, and it provides a note of optimism in its observation of positive impacts of IT investments in recent years.

## Appendix B. Abnormal Return and Volume

Abnormal Return. Abnormal returns are unbiased estimates of changes in the market value of the firm during the event period, which reflect the price reaction to the event. The abnormal return for the stock of firm i on event day t is calculated as

$$
\mathrm{AR} _ {i t} = R _ {i t} - R _ {i t} ^ {*},
$$

where

$\operatorname { A R } _ { i t } \colon$ Abnormal return of ith firm for time period t;

$R _ { i t } \mathbf { : }$ Actual return of ith firm for time period t; and

R\*: <sub>it</sub> Normal return of ith firm for time period t.

There are several common choices for estimating normal return such as market model and mean-adjusted normal return model. In the mean-adjusted normal return model, the abnormal returns are calculated by subtracting the average return of the firm from the actual returns during the estimation period. However, market model is usually more accepted as the method to estimate normal return. We use market model to estimate normal returns:

$$
R _ {i t} ^ {*} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t} + \epsilon_ {i t},
$$

where

$\alpha \mathrm { : }$ Intercept term,

$\beta _ { i } \mathrm { : }$ Systematic risk of ith firm,

$R _ { m i } \mathrm { : }$ Actual return of market for time period t, and

$\epsilon _ { i t } .$ Error term, with $E \{ \epsilon _ { i t } \} = 0 .$

There is another option that we have to select which market index to use for $R _ { m t } .$ There are two commonly used market indexes: (1) the equally-weighted index; and (2) the value-weighted index. The equally-weighted index is more likely to detect abnormal security returns (Dann and Mikkelson 1984). This is due to the fact that the greater degree of correlation between the equally-weighted index and returns; the greater the precision of the estimated parameters, the more easily detectable are the abnormal returns.<sup>16</sup>

To test whether abnormal returns over the event period are significantly different from zero, we first compute the standardized abnormal return (SAR) for firm i on event day t as

$$
\mathrm{SAR} _ {i t} = \mathrm{AR} _ {i t} / \mathrm{SD} _ {i t},
$$

with

$$
\mathrm{SD} _ {i t} = \left\{S _ {i} ^ {2} \times \left[ 1 + 1 / T (R _ {m t} - R _ {m}) ^ {2} / \sum_ {t = 2 0 1} ^ {- 2} (R _ {m t} - R _ {m}) ^ {2} \right] \right\} ^ {0. 5},
$$

where $S _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ is the residual variance from the market model over the estimation period, and $R _ { m }$ is the mean return on the market index over the estimation period.

The cumulative standardized abnormal return (CSAR) for each firm is computed as

$$
\mathrm{CSAR} _ {i} = \sum_ {t = - 1} ^ {0} \mathrm{SAR} _ {i t} / \sqrt {2}.
$$

Finally, to assess the statistical significance of the average effect of the event on the market value of the N firms, the Z-statistic is defined as

$$
Z = \sqrt {N} \times \mathrm{ASCAR},
$$

where

$$
\mathrm{ASCAR} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {0} \mathrm{CSAR} _ {i} / N.
$$

The significance of the abnormal return based on the Z-statistic test allows the researcher to infer that IT investment announcements have a significant impact on the market value of the firms (c.f., Loderer and Mauer 1992, McWilliams and Siegel 1997).

Abnormal Volume. Similar to previous trading volume studies (e.g., Bamber 1986, Ajinkya et al. 1991, Atiase and Bamber 1994, Bamber and Cheon 1995, Lobo and Tung 1997, Tkac 1999), we define volume measure as the percentage of a firm’s outstanding shares traded on a given day, computed as follows:

$$
V _ {i t} = \text { SHRTRD } _ {i t} / \text { SHROUT } _ {i t} \times 1 0 0,
$$

where

$V _ { i t } \mathrm { : }$ Actual volume of ith firm for time period t; SHRTRD : Shares traded of ith firm for time period t; and SHROU $\operatorname { T } _ { i t } \colon$ Shares outstanding of ith firm for time period t.

The abnormal volume (AV) for firm i on event day t is calculated as

$$
\mathrm{AV} _ {i t} = V _ {i t} - V _ {i t} ^ {*}
$$

where

$\operatorname { A V } _ { i t } .$ Abnormal volume of ith firm for time period t; and

$V _ { i t } ^ { * . }$ Normal volume of ith firm for time period t.
