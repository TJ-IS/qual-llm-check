## Introduction

As organizations face new challenges of survival and profitability in a global, fast-paced economy, they often investigate new work structures. One of these, telecommuting, is defined as “working away from the traditional office using computers and telecommunication facilities to maintain a link to the office” (Belanger 1999, p. 139). This definition is used in the present research. Researchers report a large growth of this phenomenon in recent years, and it is estimated that up to 130 million workers worldwide are telecommuters (Gartner Group 1999). The International Telework Association and Council reported in its 1999 survey that telecommuters worked on average nine days per month away from the office and that there were 19.6 million telecommuters in the United States, representing 10% of the adult population (Pratt 1999). JALA International also estimates that there should be about 25 million telecommuters in the United States in mid-2000 (JALA 1999). For practitioners, the increased interest is explained by the lure of the potential benefits expected from telecommuting. For researchers, the interest is in understanding the impact of this work setting on individuals, organizations, and society.

What separates the more recent trends of remote work from the “cottage” industry and homework is the use of computers and telecommunications links to communicate with an office. An additional differentiation is that today many knowledge workers, whose primary work involves information and who have less clearly defined outputs, are part of this remote work force. To date, these new work arrangements have met with varying success (Chapman et al. 1995, Ruppel and Harrington 1995), and it is still unclear what outcomes organizations and individuals involved may expect. Accordingly, some researchers are calling for more research on the determinants of success in remote work (Hartman et al. 1992) and on technologies and processes that enable individuals to work in groups while being geographically dispersed.

Most of the existing research in this area has investigated the extent of the phenomenon, pilot implementations, perceptions of remote work from nontelecommuters, lists of advantages and disadvantages, or work-family conflict issues. These approaches have not resulted in clear findings and guidance for practice. In addition, there are no clear theoretical underpinnings and few research-based constructs in most of the published work (Belanger and Collins 1998, Chapman et al. 1995, McCloskey and Igbaria 1998). Consequently, the present research explores how the technology available to, and communication patterns of, telecommuters impact outcomes. The following section describes the research model and background literature. This is followed by the description of the study and results.

## Research Models and Theoretical Background

Three main aspects of the telecommuting arrangement are explored in this study: the level of advanced information technologies available to telecommuters, the communication patterns of the telecommuters’ work groups, and how those technologies and communications fit together. The types of outcomes investigated are telecommuter-perceived productivity, performance, and satisfaction. We have taken two perspectives with regard to the possible relationships between the telecommuters’ technological resources and communication demands with work outcomes. First, direct effects of advanced information system and communication technologies and work-group communication on work outcomes are examined (Figure 1). Most of the published studies in telecommuting investigate such individual bivariate relationships (e.g., Duxbury et al. 1992, Venkatesh and Vitalari 1992, Watson-Fritz et al. 1998).

Second, the interactions between work group communication and technologies are explored, using the concept of fit, for impacts on telecommuting success (Figure 2). The model is based on a research framework for distributed work arrangements posited by Belanger and Collins (1998). It suggests that the availability of information system technology, communication technology, and the communication patterns of work groups all interact (“fit”) to affect the success of a telecommuting arrangement. This second perspective is driven by the recognition that organizational changes can rarely be predicted by only one factor, and that it is instead the interaction between multiple factors that may explain these changes (Drazin and Van de Ven 1985, Gutek 1990, Van de Ven et al. 1976).

Availability of Advanced Information Technologies Advanced information technologies are “devices (a) that transmit, manipulate, analyze, or exploit information; (b) in which a digital computer processes information integral to the user’s communication or decision tasks; and (c) that have either made their appearance since 1970 or exist in a form that aids in communication or decision tasks to a significantly greater degree than did pre-1971 forms”(Huber 1990, p. 48). The term has been used to describe a variety of technologies such as electronic communication tools or computer-assisted production systems. Obviously, what are termed advanced information technologies today will not necessarily be considered advanced five years hence.

The focus of this study—the impact of the availability of advanced information technologies on work outcomes—is not based on a notion of technological determinism. The availability of such technology for a worker, taken alone, does not determine outcomes, since individual choices in adapting the technology to tasks can lead to differential results (Mohrman and Lawler 1987). Here the technology available is seen as a resource that can vary between very rich and very poor or minimal resources, and depending on that variation, will enable or limit some work activities. When resources are low, employees’ work on a task that could be supported with technology will be made more difficult or slow. When resources are high, employees are able to perform tasks more easily and/or complete them more quickly, but whether the resources are so used depends on the individual. The extent to which individuals in rich resource settings do not fully utilize the capabilities of the technology available to them will minimize the difference in work outcomes between high and low resource settings.

In this study, advanced information technologies are separated into two components: information system and communication technologies. Advanced information system technologies include features such as type of computer, memory, applications, and peripherals available. Components of communication technologies include transmission technology, modem, fax, and available applications. The combination of the two components can vary. For example, an individual may possess a high level of information system technology (a high-end PC based on the most current microprocessor, with large amounts of RAM and storage, and a laser printer), but a relatively low level of communication technology (slow modem, no fax, no ISDN line). In another situation, high levels of communication technologies may be paired with high levels of information system technologies (e.g., an ISDN line or groupware applications may require higher processing speeds and memory capacity). Table 1 illustrates some possible configurations of telecommuters’ workstations and the relative costs of providing these resources. When telecommuting is rare in an organization, such cost differences should not be a concern, but when significant numbers of workers telecommute, controlling the costs of technology for those telecommuters is likely to be an issue.

The importance of making this distinction between information system technology and communication technology is underscored by previous research. Researchers have found differential impacts for the two types of technologies. For example, in one study, communication technology was not used extensively as a substitute for communication, while computer technology was often used as a substitute for hands-on work, primarily for word processing and information manipulation (Olson and Primps 1984).

Technologies available for geographically dispersed work groups include teleconferencing, videoconferencing, conference calls, electronic mail, voice mail, computer conferencing, and groupware systems to conduct remote meetings. Other technologies include telephones, faxes, electronic bulletin boards, cellular phones, pagers, laptops, ISDN, and the Internet. Despite the array of technologies available in the marketplace, there are still a large number of communication problems that exist for telecommuters, and these must be resolved to increase their overall effectiveness (Gupta et al. 1995). Huber (1990) argues that the use of advanced information technologies increases when it is in the employee’s best interests, leading to increased effectiveness overall.

## Communication Patterns

Communication is one of the processes most affected by telecommuting, and yet it is central to the existence of virtual organizations and distributed work (DeSanctis and Monge 1999). Prior research has shown that telecommuters must adapt their communication methods to fit the situation (Duxbury and Neufeld 1999, Ramsower 1985). Any existing communication problems can be exacerbated when organizational members start working remotely from the office (Katz 1987). In telecommuting, failure to provide some adequate support for these high volume exchanges can lead to decentralization of information and result in duplication of work and increased errors (Gordon 1986). The introduction of new communication technologies affects formal and informal communications of organizational members (Fulk and DeSanctis 1995, Hinds and Kiesler 1995). Previous research indicates that both formal and informal interactions with coworkers are very important concerns of telecommuters (Hartman et al. 1992, Huws et al. 1990). There is also some evidence that telecommuters have improved communications, presumably because the interactions are more structured and planned (Duxbury and Neufeld 1999).

Organizational design researchers have long argued that to deal with environmental change, complexity, volatility, and uncertainty, organizations must process and exchange information (Nadler and Tushman

1988). Communication among group members, however, is easier when they are in close proximity, where they can communicate directly with other members (Shane 1979). Communication in work groups is required for coordination, negotiation, conflict resolution, information sharing, and knowledge transfer, even for groups with no common task or outcome. For example, work groups of sales representatives can each have independent quotas and customer territories. Yet, they may often need to talk to one another to share information on the latest product, instructions on using a new system, or just to brainstorm on new ideas. This explains why early researchers often stated that the best candidates for telecommuting arrangements were individuals who often work independently (DeSanctis 1984, Katz 1987).

With the advent of more advanced electronic communication tools, researchers have investigated their effect on organizational communications. However, most studies of electronic communication focus on electronic mail and computer conferencing (DeSanctis and Monge 1999), while comparing these to face-toface environments. Different theories have been used to conduct research on which media individuals choose to use or which media are most effective for different types of communications, including media richness, social presence, and social information processing. The purpose of the present research, however, is not to look at which medium organizational members choose to use, but whether the availability of the media allows them to successfully perform their work in a remote work setting.

When studying communications, it is important to understand the content or purpose of the communications. Given the nature of this research, content will be defined here as work-related communications. This study uses network analysis to describe work-related communication patterns of work groups that include telecommuters.

Network Analysis. Network analysis provides a useful way to understand the communication patterns in a work group. Examples of network analysis studies in information system research include communication technologies to support global organizations (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1993) and usage of information systems (Burkhardt and Brass 1990). In a communication network, each individual is an actor or a node. Network analysis looks at the patterns of linkages among actors by mapping all the communications of network members with each other. Several network properties can be studied at the individual, group, or complete network level. Some popular metrics include density, reachability, size, and centrality. Centrality, a measure of “the number of direct contacts” an individual has with others in the network (Monge and Eisenberg 1987, p. 316), was selected in this study to describe the communication patterns of individuals in their work groups. Centrality is one of the most used metrics in network analysis, and the normalized degree of centrality allows for comparison of individuals between networks of varying sizes.

## Telecommuting Success

There is a long list of potential impacts of telecommuting in the (mostly anecdotal) literature. For example, it suggests outcomes such as increased job satisfaction and productivity, reduced stress and pollution, and increased loneliness and isolation of workers (Belanger and Collins 1998, McCloskey and Igbaria 1998). While most of these studies are not empirical, some exceptions investigate either perceived or subjective observation of increases in productivity, and changes in satisfaction levels. Two outcomes of interest to organizations, perceived performance and perceived productivity, and an outcome of interest to workers, satisfaction, are used as measures of success in this research.

The perceptual measures of success are used because of the difficulty in creating concrete, objective measures of performance and productivity in a cross-job and organization field setting. Perceptual measures are problematic in that they ask individuals to isolate the contribution of the technology-supported work environment to organizational performance (DeLone and McLean 1992); individuals making judgments about such relationships are prone to errors and biases in those cognitions (Hufnagel and Conca 1994). For example, subjects may tailor their responses to conform to cues about researchers’ intent in the research context (Norenzayan and Schwarz 1999), and there are differences in how managers make attributions of causeeffect relationships between successful and unsuccessful events (Gooding and Kinicki 1995). However, the literature in judgment and decision making points out that the identification of cause-effect relationships is part of everyday reasoning (Hogarth 1980), and unusual or unexpected events—such as new work arrangements—are more likely to elicit causal reasoning and to be recalled with greater ease (Hastie 1984).

Productivity. Reasons often used to justify an expected productivity increase for telecommuters include working at peak efficiency hours, experiencing fewer disruptive interruptions, and having reduced commuting times (Belanger and Collins 1998). Researchers have therefore predicted or reported increases in productivity for telecommuters. The traditional definition of productivity is the ratio between inputs and outputs, but in telecommuting research it has most often been measured in terms of the individuals’ self-reported perceived productivity increases or decreases (Hartman et al. 1992, Watson-Fritz et al. 1998). Telecommuters in mixed work groups (where both telecommuters and nontelecommuters are present) must often perform work that is identical to their nontelecommuter colleagues who have access to more sophisticated office equipment. Unless one’s job does not involve the use of computer technology, it can be expected that telecommuters will require high levels of information system technologies (to perform their tasks as if they were at the office) and communication technologies (to obtain help from colleagues rapidly when needed).

Hypothesis 1a. Higher levels of information systems technology will increase perceived productivity as reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 1b. Higher levels of communication technology will increase perceived productivity as reported by telecommuters.

As discussed earlier, communications require extra effort for the telecommuter because this communication tends to be more planned and structured, and the telecommuter has to adapt to how communications are done in the telework setting. Therefore, as the level of communications required to perform one’s task increases, it is expected that productivity will decrease.

Hypothesis 1c. Higher levels of communication requirements will decrease perceived productivity as reported by telecommuters.

Performance. Performance is often confused with productivity, but it is actually measured by more global variables such as the quality of outputs, job knowledge, leadership, or judgment. In the traditional office, performance is largely established by scanning for employees’ presence and through direct and indirect observations (Ruppel and Harrington 1995). When managers get conflicting information about performance from their visual and nonvisual data, they tend to rely more on the visual data (Kulik and Ambrose 1993), which can be a problem for telecommuters. For telecommuters to be fairly compared to their nontelecommuter colleagues, they must therefore maintain close communications with their manager (through advanced communication technologies). In addition, some researchers have argued that when appropriate levels of advanced information technologies are used, performance may be improved (Huber 1990). This is to be expected when one knows that quality and timeliness of the work produced is aided by having higher levels of information system technologies available. Therefore, performance is expected to be positively impacted by technology.

Hypothesis 2a. Higher levels of information systems technology will increase perceived performance as reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 2b. Higher levels of communication technology will increase perceived performance as reported by telecommuters.

As discussed earlier, telecommuters who have higher levels of communication requirements may have to exert extra effort to adapt their communications to the telework environment and thus negatively impact their productivity. However, when the focus is solely on the nature of the outcome, as is true for performance measures, there is some evidence that these communications are actually improved in telework (Duxbury and Neufeld 1999). A potential explanation for this improved performance is that when working in a distributed environment, teleworkers must plan and structure their communication exchanges more extensively than their office-bound colleagues. As such, those individuals who are required by their work setting to do the most communicating would therefore have the greatest potential performance gain.

Hypothesis 2c. Higher levels of communication requirements will increase perceived performance as reported by telecommuters.

Satisfaction. It is often argued that telecommuting should increase workers’ satisfaction with their job. Satisfaction occurs when an employee “responds with positive rather than negative feelings to his/her job and job-related experiences” (Chapman et al. 1995, p. 232). One study reveals that both telecommuters and nontelecommuters report high levels of satisfaction with their office communications when they have proper information technology support, task predictability, and electronic coordination (Watson-Fritz et al. 1998). It can therefore be expected that if telecommuters are not provided with the appropriate communication technology (to reduce the feelings of isolation and loneliness), and information systems technology (to be as efficient as in the office), they may be less satisfied with their work environment.

Hypothesis 3a. Higher levels of information systems technology will increase perceived satisfaction with the work environment as reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 3b. Higher levels of communication technology will increase perceived satisfaction with the work environment as reported by telecommuters.

Similarly, if one is involved in substantial communications to accomplish tasks (e.g., because of high coordination requirements), then the telecommuter’s many communication exchanges with coworkers should minimize feelings of isolation and loneliness. This should result in this telecommuter being more satisfied with his or her work environment.

Hypothesis 3c. Higher levels of communication requirements will increase perceived satisfaction with the work environment as reported by telecommuters.

## The Concept of Fit

In addition to hypothesizing the direct effects of the nature of the telecommuting environment on success in telecommuting, we also viewed this relationship from a contingency theory perspective. Contingency theory researchers have used the concept of fit to look at the interrelationships of constructs and their impacts on various variables. For example, in information systems, some researchers have looked at the impact of task-technology fit on outcomes (Goodhue and Thompson 1995, Vessey and Galetta 1991). A common issue with researchers using this concept is the lack of specification of the type of fit under investigation (Drazin and Van de Ven 1985). Venkatraman (1989) proposes a conceptual framework to identify six forms of fit, classified using the number of variables in the relationship, the degree of specificity between variables, and the degree of anchoring of fit on a criterion variable.

Belanger and Collins (1998) used Venkatraman’s gestalt type of fit (criterion free) to consider the internal coherence between a large number of attributes (e.g., individual, organizational, work, and technology characteristics) whose interactions could not be precisely formulated. While this research model is based on their framework, fit as moderation is more appropriate for this study because several criterion variables are used to anchor fit (outcome variables measuring telecommuting success). Other criterion-based forms of fit, such as profile deviation (requires a profile from which deviations can be measured) and mediation (a variable impacts a criterion variable through an intervening variable), are not appropriate for the present research. When fit is viewed as moderation, the effects of an independent variable on the criterion variable vary as a function of the moderating variable. Figure 2 was depicted using the schematic representation of fit (moderation) as illustrated in Venkatraman (p. 425). The propositions depicted in Figure 2 are that (1) the interaction of the availabilities of advanced information system technologies and advanced communication technologies will have implications for telecommuting success, and (2) the interaction of communication patterns and the availability of advanced communication technologies will have implications for telecommuting success.

Previous researchers have recognized the important role played by computer and communication technologies in telecommuting. There is no research, however, about the optimal combination of information system and communication technologies. As previously discussed, these two components should be considered separately and may have differing impacts on outcomes. However, their effects on outcomes should also be considered in light of one another. For example, while one may argue that higher levels of information system technology may lead to higher productivity, a higher level of communication technology may be required for this higher productivity to be achieved if an individual is working away from, and must maintain a link to, the traditional office. Since the level of communication technology available may need to match the level of information systems to function (as in the case when an application requires some minimum amount of main memory), it is posited that a “technology match” is better than a “technology nonmatch.”

Hypothesis 1d. The better the fit between the level of advanced information system technology and the level of advanced communication technology, the higher the perceived productivity reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 2d. The better the fit between the level of advanced information system technology and the level of advanced communication technology, the higher the perceived performance reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 3d. The better the fit between the level of advanced information system technology and the level of advanced communication technology, the higher the perceived satisfaction with the work environment reported by telecommuters.

The other contingency investigated in this study is whether the availability of advanced communication media allows telecommuters to successfully perform their work in a remote work setting. “The issue is not one of the technologies driving out the use of richer media, but rather of the technologies enabling communications that otherwise would be unlikely to occur” (Huber 1990, p. 53). Existing communication patterns among organizational members are therefore seen as constraints and/or enablers, which must fit with the available communication technology to ensure the success of telecommuting.

Hypothesis 1e. The better the fit between the level of advanced communication technology available and the communication requirements (normalized degree of centrality), the higher the perceived productivity reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 2e. The better the fit between the level of advanced communication technology available and the communication requirements, the higher the perceived performance reported by telecommuters.

Hypothesis 3e. The better the fit between the level of advanced communication technology available and the communication requirements, the higher the perceived satisfaction with the work environment reported by telecommuters.

## Methodology

## Overview

This study was a two-step field research project. The first phase of data collection involved conducting semistructured interviews with managers of work groups that included telecommuters. The second phase of data collection involved cross-sectional surveys of telecommuters in multiple organizations. The first and second phases were held sequentially for a given group, but concurrently between different groups. Data were collected from telecommuters, their colleagues, and their supervisors, since a thorough look at the interrelationships among individuals in a work group was necessary to understand communication patterns. All predictors and criteria were operationalized at the individual level. For the purposes of this research, the work group was defined as those people who report to the same supervisor (Gutek 1990,Van de Ven et al. 1976). The interviews were conducted with three objectives in mind: (1) to identify the type of distributed work arrangement each group was involved in, (2) to gather general information on telecommuting from the managers’ perspectives, and (3) to obtain the participation of all group members in the study.

## Instrument

A survey instrument was primarily created from a combination of existing measures in information systems, communication, and industrial psychology research. It contained sections designed to collect information on demographics, technologies, communication, and outcomes. In the demographics section, a definition of telecommuting was provided (see Appendix A), and respondents were asked to indicate whether a computer was necessary to perform their job. All respondents with no exceptions indicated that computers were required for their job. Important measures of work-setting variables, such as the number of telecommuting days, were also obtained from this section, in addition to demographic data. The technology section requested respondents to identify the technologies available to them in their home or work offices. The list of technologies was derived from the authors’ experience and the research of Huber (1990) and Grover and Goslar (1993). The communication section was adapted to each group by listing all members of the group and requesting respondents to identify whom they communicated with and how often, for work-related purposes. Outcomes were measured on 7-point Likert-type scales. An annotated copy of the instrument, including sources for items, can be found in Appendix A. The instrument was pretested twice, with groups of 5 and 13 respondents, to reword or eliminate confusing items, and to evaluate estimated completion time.

## Sample

A convenience sample of work groups in North American organizations was used for the study. Initial semistructured interviews were conducted to determine if an entire work group could be surveyed and whether at least some employees in the work group were telecommuters. Eight managers agreed to participate. Members of their work groups received an envelope containing a request-for-participation letter, the survey, and a self-addressed stamped envelope. For network analysis purposes, all members of the groups were asked to answer the survey even if they considered themselves to be nontelecommuters. Two weeks after the initial mailing, a reminder postcard was sent to all participants.

A total of 253 surveys were delivered to eight work groups in six organizations, and 121 surveys were returned for an overall response rate of 48%. Of these, 110 were usable for the overall analysis, including 67 individuals who were categorized as telecommuters, although 119 were usable for network analysis. Individuals were considered telecommuters only if they worked away from the office at least one day a week and had been performing remote work for at least three months. The size of work groups and their response rates are shown in Table 3.

## Instrument Validation

The instrument’s reliability and validity were evaluated using Cronbach’s Alpha and confirmatory factor analysis in SPSS/PC 8.0. Cronbach’s alpha resulted in reliabilities of 0.91, 0.80, and 0.82 for performance, productivity, and satisfaction, respectively. These are well above the recommended threshold (Nunnally 1978). To ensure the unidimensionality of the scales, we used confirmatory factor analysis with promax oblique rotation because there were correlations between the dependent variables. Table 4 presents the results of the factor analysis with the highest loadings for each item bolded.

There were 12 items in the scales, 8 of which loaded properly on single factors. Therefore questions 2, 8, and 12 were retained for satisfaction; questions 3 and 5 for productivity; and questions 6, 9, and 11 for performance. While question 10 loaded properly on performance, it showed a high loading on productivity. To avoid overlap of these two constructs that are theoretically different, but often confused, we did not include item 10 in our final list. The resulting number of items was therefore 3, 2, and 3 for satisfaction, productivity, and performance, respectively. The items with improper loadings were dropped and excluded from further analyses. Ratings were then averaged to create final scores for each variable (Ruppel and Harrington 1995).

## Network Analysis

Individuals were asked to identify the group members with whom they communicated for work-related purposes, and the frequency of those communications. The frequency scale ranged from 0 (never) to 6 (several times a day) (Wigand 1988). Responses were entered into matrices, with the rows indicating who communicates with whom (columns). A zero indicates no work-related communication (or less than once per month) between individuals (nodes). There are two problems to contend with before analyzing this matrix data. First, missing data, a frequent problem in network analysis (Knoke and Kuklinski 1982), was entered according to the ratings provided by at least one informant (ideally, two responses should be obtained for each pair of respondents). Rows where there were still missing data (e.g., no data provided by at least one respondent) were deleted from the matrix. Second, conflicts in the ratings between participants are often dealt with by “symmetrizing” the matrices, especially when analyzing reciprocal communication channels, such as between coworkers (Knoke and Kuklinski 1982, p. 43). In this study, replication was forced by changing one of the two values, a standard approach. Details and examples of the reconciliation procedures are presented in Appendix B. The normalized degree of centrality was computed using the DEGREE procedure in UCINET IV (Borgatti et al. 1992), which calculates the number of links and frequencies for an individual divided by the potential number of such links given the individual’s network size.

## Hypothesis Testing

Hypotheses about direct effects were tested using ANOVA tests of the independent variables on the outcome criteria. Fit hypotheses were tested with multiple regression analyses. Criterion variables were included as dependent variables, and main effect models were tested. We then tested the two-way interaction terms. Prior to conducting these analyses, assumptions of multivariate normal distribution, independence of errors, and equality of variance were tested. The index of communication technology availability and the normalized degree of centrality produced normal distributions, while the index of information system technology availability was slightly skewed. Outcome variables showed upward skewness, with productivity highly skewed. This was expected primarily because these were self-report data. Pearson correlation coefficients revealed that the independent variables were not correlated, and the main effect regression models with variance inflation factors (VIF) confirmed that multicollinearity was not a concern with this data set (1.052 to 1.106). For regressions, outlier influential observations were identified with leverage, studentized residuals, and Cook’s D-statistic. Five observations were found to be both outliers and influential. Further investigation revealed that these respondents rated every question as high, even when the wording was reversed. These five observations were removed from the data set, leaving 62 observations from telecommuters for final regression analyses.

## ANOVA Tests

Table 10 presents the results from the ANOVA tests for the direct effects hypotheses that predicted positive effects of technologies on outcomes. All three (productivity, performance, and satisfaction) showed significant effects of advanced communication technology on outcomes in the expected direction, thereby supporting Hypothesis 1b, Hypothesis 2b, and Hypothesis 3b. Only performance was significantly impacted by advanced information system technology, supporting Hypothesis 2a.

The normalized degree of centrality (levels of communication requirements) was significantly associated with lower levels of performance and productivity, as can be seen by the negative coefficient. This was expected for productivity (Hypothesis 1c), but Hypothesis 2c posited a positive relationship between higher levels of communication requirements and perceived performance. Potential explanations for this unexpected significant result will be presented in the Result section for performance. The coefficient for centrality is very small, but this is explained by the different range of values for this variable. Information system and communication technology variables varied roughly between 0 and 2.0, outcome variables varied between 1.0 and 7.0, and the normalized degree of centrality varied between 20 and 340. As such, small coefficients can have major effects on the models.

When combining the results from Tables 10 and 11, a view emerges of how the technology and communications nature of the telecommuting environment impacts certain telecommuting outcomes.

Productivity. The best model for productivity includes the availability of communication technology, the interaction between the technology variables, the normalized degree of centrality, and the number of days telecommuting. This model explains roughly 20% of the variance for productivity. The communication technology variable has a positive coefficient, while the interaction term offers a mitigating effect (negative, but a much smaller coefficient) that reduces the overall positive effect. The overall positive impact of increases in communication technology available to telecommuters is also reduced to some extent by this interaction, possibly due to the additional effort required for employees to use more complex, as well as more powerful, workstations when more communication technologies are required. The negative beta coefficient for the interaction term highlights the importance of considering this interaction between the two technology variables instead of just looking at each technology variable separately. Considering only main effect terms could lead one to incorrectly expect a larger gain in productivity than what would actually be achieved by an increase in communication technology availability.

Increases in communication requirements lead to lower levels of perceived productivity, as expected. Interestingly, an increase in the number of days of telecommuting leads to increases in productivity. However, one must be very careful in interpreting these results. Since these are perceived measures, the regression results really mean that telecommuters who work more days away from the office report higher levels of perceived productivity.

Performance. The best model for performance accounts for more than 31% of the variance. While no interaction terms affected performance, all main effect variables were significant (information system technology, communication technology, and degree of centrality). The results suggest that both technology variables positively impact perceived performance. Conversely, as the level of communication requirements increases, there is a decrease in the level of perceived performance. Again, the very small coefficient (0.004) can be explained by the larger difference in the range of values for this variable.

The fact that greater communication requirements have a negative impact on a telecommuter’s perceived performance is consistent with the comments of telecommuters, who suggest that one of the main benefits of working at home is an increased quality of work and efficiency attributed to fewer interruptions from colleagues initiating conversations (work-related or not); this, though, is contrary to both our hypothesis and some previous work (Duxbury and Neufeld 1999). Such comments indicate that a lower level of communication is sought by some telecommuters; when they remain central to work group communications and thus must maintain high levels of communication, they may perceive that their performance is negatively effected. Duxbury and Neufeld (1999) found no differences in amount of communication between telecommuters and nontelecommuters. Watson-Fritz and colleagues (1998) also found no differences between telecommuters and nontelecommuters in both informal and formal types of communication. In order to explore this possible explanation, future research will be needed to measure the individual difference of desired amount of communication. Still, in this study, individuals feel that their performance should increase with higher levels of information system and communication technologies, possibly because they have the necessary technology to support the communications they require to perform their work.

Satisfaction. The best model for satisfaction with work environment accounts only for 4% of the variance. The only term that showed significance is the level of communication technology available, suggesting that as it increases, telecommuters are more satisfied with their work environment. This can be explained by the fact that many telecommuters feel isolated, and communication technologies may be the only link they have to their office and coworkers. Another explanation for the small percent of variance accounted by the model for satisfaction with the work environment can be due to the fact that personality variables were not included in the research, or to other problems with the measurement of satisfaction. Some types of personalities are more likely to be satisfied with telecommuting than others (Ramsower 1985).

## Discussion

This research proposed that the fit between work group communication requirements and information system and communication technology affects telecommuting outcomes (e.g., productivity, performance, and satisfaction). Hypotheses posited that main effect terms and two-way interactions (fit) between the independent variables would influence telecommuting success. As described in the results section, several direct effects and only one of the “fit” hypotheses, were supported.

## Advanced Information Technologies in Telecommuting

The results clearly demonstrate that several technology variables impacted several outcomes on telecommuters’ perceived outcomes. The availability of communication technologies had significant impact on perceived productivity, performance, and satisfaction. The availability of information system technologies had significant effects on performance. This leads to an important implication for managers interested in telecommuting for their employees: The technologies, particularly communication technologies, which are available to telecommuters, impact the success of a telecommuting arrangement when it is operationalized by these self-reported perceived measures. Therefore, managers should consider which technologies they want to provide to employees or which minimum equipment configurations will be required for individuals wishing to participate in a telecommuting program.

For three of the main effects, increases in either advanced information system or advanced communication technologies led to higher levels of perceived telecommuting success. Concurrently, it is important to look at the fit between those two variables because it had a significant, negative impact on at least one success outcome (productivity), reducing the overall positive effect of greater levels of communication technologies available to telecommuters.

These findings also demonstrate that it is important for researchers to distinguish information systems from communication technologies. It was clearly shown by the data collected that telecommuters tend to have higher levels of information system than communication technologies available to them. It is possible that individuals buy their own personal computers for use by their family members, and such workstations tend to be very powerful today. However, rarely do these individuals buy themselves an ISDN link to their office.

## Communication Patterns in Telecommuting

Group work requires substantial levels of communications for knowledge workers. Studies have found that formal and informal communications are important to telecommuters (Hartman et al. 1992), that there might be improved communications for telecommuters (Duxbury and Neufeld 1999), but that being a telecommuter may not impact communication with either telecommuter or nontelecommuter colleagues (Belanger 1999b). These studies investigated the effect of telecommuting on outcomes or communication. Building on this research we investigated the effects of communication in telecommuting on outcomes.

The normalized degree of centrality represented the communication patterns of individuals in their work groups. The results show that higher levels of centrality (higher levels of communication requirements) have a significant negative impact on productivity (Hypothesis 1c) and performance (Hypothesis 2c). The more telecommuters had communication links in their network, the lower their perceived productivity and performance. It is logical to think that individuals who must communicate substantially with others in order to perform their tasks, and spend more effort to adapt and structure their communications, feel less productive in telecommuting environments, where such communications may represent greater challenges than in the traditional office.

An important caveat to the present research is that it considered work-related communications only. Including non-work-related communications might change the results of the models. The reason is that most formal communications can be handled via e-mail and phones, but problems arise with informal communications, where such technologies may not be sufficient. Nonetheless, communication patterns, as measured by normalized degree of centrality, should be included in future studies as an important determinant of successful outcomes in distributed work. In addition, information system researchers are encouraged to consider adding network analysis to their methodologies tool kit.

A second suggestion for future research would be to include a measure of the individual’s need for communication. Such an individual difference may moderate the relationship between centrality of communication and performance and productivity outcomes. As discussed earlier, telecommuters may desire fewer communications and have an expectation that the telecommuting arrangement will satisfy that need, yet find that the amount of their communications is no less because of their centrality to communications in the work group.

## Building on the Theoretical (“Fit”) Foundation

One premise of this research was that investigating individual bivariate variables did not provide sufficient information on the effects that these variables had on outcomes, and that therefore, their interaction, or how they fit together, must be taken into account. Two sets of hypotheses were proposed to investigate the fit between our independent variables (Hypotheses 1–3 d and e). One of the “fit” variables (level of communication technology and communication requirements) did not prove significant in any of the models. The effect of the interaction term between the technology variables on productivity was not only significant, but went in the direction of reducing the overall increase in productivity that would be expected from looking only at bivariate relationships. Fit, when operationalized as the interaction between independent variables (moderation), therefore, does provide a richer understanding of the relationships. Thus, when considering communication in distributed work, the role of advanced technologies is to provide adequate support for successful outcomes. While this research measured only within-group communications, it can be inferred that the requirements of high communication technologies for successful outcomes, as discovered in this study, are relevant for supporting other communications of telecommuters as well (out of group, informal, out of organization, vertical, etc.). Given that technologies must fit together for at least one outcome criterion (productivity), potential profiles of fit can be established. Examples of some profiles of fit between technologies in support of communication, based on the results obtained in this study, are detailed in Table 12.

The “adequately supported” type includes telecommuters with high within-group communication needs and high availability of communications technology, as well as telecommuters with low within-group communication needs and low availability of communications technology. The “poorly supported” type is represented by telecommuters with high within-group communication needs who do not have adequate communication technology support. The “oversupported” type of telecommuter has low within-group communication needs and high availability of information systems and communication technologies. There are other variations of these technology match-mismatch profiles, all of which are in need of further empirical validation.

## Implications and Contributions

This research provides several contributions to the fields of information systems and communication research, both in the results obtained and the methodologies employed. One of the most important contributions is the identification of determinants of telecommuting success measured in terms of productivity, performance, and satisfaction. These significant research findings are summarized in Figure 3.

The results indicate that the level of information systems technologies available to telecommuters impacts their performance, and the level of communication technologies available significantly impacts productivity, performance, and satisfaction, either directly or through interaction. It has been suggested that technology is a driving force behind the growth of telecommuting (Belanger and Collins 1998, Cross and Raizman 1986). This study shows that technologies do matter in telecommuting. In addition, the methodology for creating indices for information systems and communication technologies can be reused by others interested in comparing technologies available to individuals. A note of caution is that the ratings obtained here should not be directly reused in future studies given the fast pace of technological change. Instead, the complete process of creating indices should be followed, including obtaining new expert ratings for technologies for the time period under consideration.

The study was designed with the belief that information system and communication technologies impacted telecommuting outcomes differently. The data collected showed that this belief was justified in two ways. First, the levels of the two types of technologies available to telecommuters varied greatly, with an overall higher level of information system than communication technology available to telecommuters and nontelecommuters. Second, their impact on outcomes varied. Thus, researchers should consider using the two technologies as separate variables in future studies.

Many information system researchers are interested in communication technologies. For example, the areas of computer-mediated communications, computersupported cooperative work, and media richness studies focus on the concept of organizational communications. This research tried to capture the communications between individuals in a work group in a nonlinear model through network analysis. While it has its own set of limitations, this methodology helps eliminate some sources of bias in measuring communications by asking two respondents to rate each other (Rogers and Kincaid 1981). Other information system researchers should consider this methodology.

An important finding of the research is the negative effect on two perceived outcomes of increases in communication links within one’s work group. Individuals requiring higher levels of communication to complete their tasks report lower perceived outcomes. Fortunately, this can be somewhat counterbalanced by greater availability of communication technologies. More research is needed, not only to validate the findings but also to further explore their causes and implications. Nevertheless, an evaluation of one’s communication requirements prior to establishment of a telecommuting program might be an important step for practitioners to consider.

## Limitations

Choices made in research often lead to sets of limitations. However, it is important to look at these limitations in light of how these choices allowed the field to progress through the contributions of this research. Some of the limitations of this study are the use of a convenience sample (limiting generalizability), size of groups, the types of communications measured, selfreport (obtaining perceived measures for outcomes is subject to employees’ perceptions of how they perform their work), and recall. While differences in group size are taken into account by the normalized degree of centrality metric, one concern is that two of the eight groups made up the largest proportion of the sample. In addition, it is better for network analysis to have groups of seven or more, and this study included groups of five and six individuals. The study only measured work-related communications. It may be argued that the need for non-work-related communications is an important aspect of individuals’ work life, particularly for those workers with higher social needs. Finally, since the study focused on telecommuters, we measured only their home office technology availability. Future studies should be careful to measure both home and work office technology availability.

## Conclusion

In their review of the literature, McCloskey and Igbaria (1998) identify the three main limitations of existing research on telecommuting: (1) the lack of formal definitions of telecommuting used; (2) methodological weaknesses, especially small and poorly constructed samples; and (3) the lack of control of extraneous variables. They particularly highlight the issues of not controlling for the number of days individuals are telecommuting, the employment status of individuals (part time vs. full time), and the job type of participants, all of which could impact the types of issues telecommuters would experience. In this research we provided a clear definition of telecommuting to respondents, obtained a reasonable sample size, and controlled for the number of telecommuting days. In addition, respondents were full-time employees and professionals, avoiding the pitfall of lumping together clerical and professional workers in studying telecommuting. The findings from this research indicate that while not all the hypothesized interactions of the independent variables impact the telecommuting outcome criteria, there are significant impacts from at least one two-way interaction and several main effect terms.

## Recommendations for Future Research

The next empirical step is to test models elaborated from the variables identified in this study, possibly using the profiles of fit presented in Table 11. Following are some additional ideas for future research. First, additional variables could be investigated. In particular, individual characteristics such as training, support, or skills might prove important determinants of telecommuting success. For example, conflicts between work and family roles of telecommuters can potentially affect telecommuting outcomes (Duxbury et al. 1992, 1998). Other work settings could also be investigated, such as neighborhood work centers, hotelling, or virtual organizations. Second, the formation of cliques in groups in which some employees are telecommuting might provide a different view of the communication patterns of work groups in which not all employees are telecommuters. Do nontelecommuters form closer ties together? Third, a large proportion of the respondents worked only one or two days per week from their home office. The models should be validated for groups of telecommuters who work full-time from home. As discussed, the number of telecommuting days impacted productivity but investigations of mandatory full-time telecommuting arrangements should be conducted. Fourth, investigations of all communications of telecommuters should be done. This would involve looking at all communications within and outside their groups, both for work and non-work related purposes. One way to perform this would be to have a group of telecommuters complete a communication diary for all communications that they perform over a certain period of time. This data could be analyzed by creating rectangular data sets for network analysis, in which extra columns would represent additional nodes of communication outside the work group (Knoke and Kuklinski 1982). As noted earlier, studies that focus on communications should include measures of individuals’ need or desire for communications as a possible important moderator variable. Finally, it might be interesting to investigate in more depth how job categories relate to the fit between communication and technology in affecting telecommuting outcomes.
