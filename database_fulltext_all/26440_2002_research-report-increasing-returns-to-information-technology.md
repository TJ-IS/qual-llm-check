Stephan Kudyba • Romesh Diwan

Null Sigma, Inc., Wayne, New Jersey 07470

his work analyzes firm-level investment in information technology and corresponding productivity through the use of a production function over the period from 1995–1997. The results are then compared to previous studies that utilized similar data and methodologies to compare productivity estimates over time. The analysis indicates that investment in IT enhances productivity over the period in question and has illustrated increasing returns over time. These findings are supported by the corresponding empirical analysis which yielded IT capital coefficients in a production function of (0.12, 0.16, 0.18) and IT flow coefficients in a similar function of (0.17, 0.24, 0.22) for the years 1995, 1996, and 1997, respectively. These results reflect the change in firm output given a one-percent change in the natural log of dollars invested in IT capital and flow, and are statistically significant.

## Increasing Returns to Information Technology

The world is undergoing a technological revolution at the heart of which are information technologies. This new technological era is evident by the increased investment in telecommunications, computer processing equipment, and software over the last decade by companies across industry sectors. As a result of this, analysts and corporate leaders continue to ask the question . . . does the ever increasing investment in information technology result in increased productivity? Empirical work on the productivity of IT spending is just beginning to mature. The following research attempts to respond to this question by quantifying productivity in United States information technology.

This paper investigates how the evolution of information technologies has affected business productivity by applying methods based on economic theory. It analyzes firm-level data on investment in information technology as an input in a standard production function and estimates productivity coefficients for the period from 1995–1997. These results are compared with earlier studies which incorporated similar methodologies to illustrate how advanced computer processing, the proliferation of personal computers, and complementary technologies such as software and the Internet have enhanced the impact information technology has had on business performance.

## Information Technology and Economic Expansion

The evolving Information Age has been cited as a primary source of the recent remarkable economic growth, sustained economic expansion, and wealth creation in the United States. A number of studies and policy makers have attributed the unprecedented sustained macroeconomic growth in the United States with subdued inflation since the mid-1990s to efficiencies attained by United States firms through increased utilization of information technology, (Greenspan 1997,

Engardio 1998). This argument is based on the idea that firms are able to produce greater amounts of output with a given amount of inputs through an effective implementation of IT. As is generally assumed in economic literature on maximizing profits and/or minimizing costs strategies, this has encouraged United States corporations to continuously streamline operations to reduce costs. (Caves and Krepps 1993).

## IT Productivity Studies

There has been some contradictory evidence on whether increased investments in IT enhance productivity. Some older studies such as Morrison and Berndt (1991), Barua (1995), and Loveman (1994) have concluded that: (i) computers add nothing to total output, or (ii) marginal cost of investment in computers outweighs marginal benefits. However, more recent studies such as Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1996a and b), Lichtenberg (1995, 1998) offer an opposite conclusion; namely, investment in IT capital has made a substantial and significant contribution to output. These studies were able to incorporate more detailed firm-level estimates of investment in computers. It could also be that investment in IT has now reached and passed a threshold level so that it can contribute to output positively.

This paper extends the firm-level analysis conducted by Brynjolfsson and Hitt and Lichtenberg by applying it to the most recent period, 1995–1997, for which detailed data are available. This period is also of particular interest not only because it is the most recent but also because it incorporates utilization of such significant IT capital enhancements as advanced computer processing, “state of the art” software applications, and the Internet. In fact, prior to this period the Internet hardly mattered.

## IT Productivity: Two Hypotheses

Microeconomic theory addresses the issue of business productivity. It entails the estimation of production functions in which output is a function of individual factor inputs such as capital and labor. Firms seek to optimize the use of inputs and invest in an input, such as IT, until the output it generates adds no more value than its cost. More specifically, investment is carried out to the point where marginal input cost is equal to the value of the marginal output where marginal output refers to the output generated from the last dollar invested in the relevant factor input. In view of the evolving information age and increased IT utilization, production theory can be applied to measure the impact of IT on corporate output. Accordingly, this paper attempts to test the following two hypotheses:

$$
Q = f (K, L, I T)
$$

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Investment in IT contributes positively to firm output; i.e., $d Q / d I T > 0 ;$ and

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Investment in IT, over time, has resulted in increased productivity over time. In other words, dQ/dIT has been increasing over time.

## Factor Input Calculations

The following definitions and calculations are given for each of the production function variables. Much of the methodology utilizes those techniques introduced by (Lichtenberg 1995).

Capital is defined as Net Property Plant and Equipment (PP&E), which adjusts total plant property and equipment for accumulated depreciation. Net PP&E had to be adjusted downward by the value of IT capital to avoid double counting.

IT capital is the portion of the overall IS budget which encompassed P/Cs and mainframes (hardware) which needed to be estimated for each firm from 1995–1997. The percentage hardware portion of the IS budget as reported in the IW 500 (1994) edition was used to calculate base IT capital estimates which were adjusted by corresponding depreciation rates over the relevant period.

Non-IT Labor and IT Labor involved multiplying the amount of non-IT labor personnel as reported in the

IW 500 survey by the average annual earnings of all private-sector workers, (a weighted average of IT and non-IT labor earnings) as published in the Economic Report of the President for the relevant years. Because the average annual earnings figure incorporated both IT and non-IT labor earnings, the estimate had to be adjusted by the relevant IT to non-IT labor ratio. The IT labor input involved multiplying the number of IT personnel by the average annual earnings of IT workers derived from Lichtenberg’s work and estimates published in ComputerWorld magazine.

Sales Revenue is gross revenue for the corresponding firm.

Value Added refers to gross sales less variable costs where:

$$
\text { Variable   costs } = (\text { Cost   of   goods   sold   and   selling   and   general   administrative   expenses })
$$

Not all variables were available for each firm listed in the IW 500 survey. Therefore, the number of observations for which all data is available varies from year to year. 1995 includes 348 firms, 1996 includes 355 firms, and 1997 includes 188. Summary tables for production function variables are included in Tables 1 and 2, which show a significant growth in investment in information technology capital.

## The Production Function

As previously stated, the term productivity generally refers to efficiency of performance of a business entity or, in other words, measuring the level of output given changes in the amount of inputs used to produce it. The popular economic tool used to measure productivity applies the theory of production and costs and utilizes a mathematical relationship between outputs and inputs used in the production process, commonly known as the production function.

This work utilizes the Cobb-Douglas production function because this form facilitates the estimation of elasticity of production function inputs through linearization of the equation. The Cobb-Douglas function in this study is illustrated by:

$Q = ( I L ^ { \ S } , L ^ { \ S } , I K ^ { \ S } , K ^ { \ S } )$ where Q is output.

(IL, L, K and IK) are IT Labor,

Labor, Capital and IT Capital.

The linearized version becomes:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln (Q) _ {i j} = & \beta 1 \ln (I L) _ {i j} + \beta 2 \ln (L) _ {i j} + \beta 3 \ln (I K) _ {i j} \\ & + \beta 4 \ln (K) _ {i j}, \end{array}
$$

where the (b) values are the parameters that denote the elasticity of each of the input factors. The function is estimated for (i) individual firms over ( j) separate years.

The results are illustrated in Table (3).

The interesting aspect of the results is the coefficients of IT capital, which ranged from 0.122 in 1995 to 0.184 in 1997. These coefficients represent the output elasticities of IT capital, or in other words, the net change to output per a 1% increase in the natural log of dollars invested in IT capital. The values are not only positive and significant, as supported by corresponding tstatistics which infer acceptance well within a 95 per cent confidence zone, but increase significantly (as we will show) over the three years.

An alternate approach which may provide a more appropriate analysis given the flow nature of each of the output measures, (e.g., Sales and Value Added) is to estimate the production function utilizing an IT flow measure as opposed to IT capital. The following analysis incorporates the IS budget, which is the annual expenditure on IT equipment defined above, in place of the IT capital components, (IK in the equation above becomes the investment in IT or IT flow). This flow analysis will be extended to incorporate Value Added as an output measure, where the equation differs slightly from that above and takes the form utilized by (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996). It incorporates three factor inputs in the production function with output measure Value Added. (IK) refers to the flow of IT investment.

Results from a CHOW test are depicted below (Table 9) for each of the equations (e.g., Sales and Value Added), which incorporated the entire production function between adjacent years.

The null hypothesis for the CHOW test here states that there is no significant difference between regression functions over differing data sets. The results are significant (at the 0.05 level) between 1995 and 1996 for output measured in Value-Added terms.

The significant difference between equations could be the result of difference in number of observations from 1996 to 1997 and change in IT labor and Labor coefficients, but the analysis does add credence to the assertion that investment in information technology can lead to increased productivity over time.

## Conclusion

Using a standard Cobb-Douglas production function and firm-level data for 1995–1997, we have tested two hypotheses. Our results indicate that: (a) investment in various forms of IT have made statistically significant contributions to firm-level output measured either by gross revenue or value added and (b), there is some weak evidence on whether the contribution of IT increased over the three years studied here.

Because some other studies have also followed similar methodology, namely, firm-level data for Fortune 500 companies and a Cobb Douglas production function, our results could therefore be a source of comparison. The output/input elasticities are within a meaningful range despite differences both in some data definitions and in sample firms. The differences, in fact, strengthen the conclusion because elasticities reflect deeper tendencies and are not affected by surfacelevel differences. The estimates for the elasticities therefore are robust. When considering a comparison between the studies, our second hypothesis; H2 (investments in IT have resulted in increased productivity over time), has merit. In other words, the increases in IT productivity could span a positive function with respect to time.

One possible explanation of this increased IT productivity over time follows from a surge in capital investment in high-tech equipment beginning in 1993. Because then there has been a major improvement in the diffusion of IT so that firms have increased their expenditures in hardware, software, and telecommunications equipment. These investments have been made for the obvious objective of enhancing the efficiency of operations. The process has been accelerated by the fall in prices. Starting in 1994, advancements in computer processing and telecommunications equipment opened the door for complementary technologies such as complex software applications, along with Internet-related technology, which in turn augmented the capabilities of existing information systems resulting in still greater synergy (Greenspan 1997). The combination of these technologies has enabled corporations to bridge the gap between decision makers and their data, companies and their customers, and interfunctional areas within organizations. These reduce the uncertainty of conducting business and result in increased efficiency.

Furthermore, “state of the art” IT has enabled enterprises to store, retrieve, manipulate, and analyze data faster than any time in history. Vast information networks consisting of server mainframes, P/Cs which are utilized by an increasing number of employees and consumers, data storage, retrieval and analytical software, and the communication-enhancing systems such as LANs, WANs, e-mail, along with the Internet, facilitate retrieval, analysis and, communication of vital information to users. Business strategies such as switching to more reliable suppliers and channels of distribution (Malone 1987), identifying more appropriate target markets or implementing automated processes have been facilitated by IT. This has further helped to reduce wasteful and less productive factor inputs.

If the above analysis and explanation make economic sense, future research should entail the study of the evolving structure of the business enterprise as the information economy continues to develop. In these early stages of the information economy, enterprises are seeking to create synergy and increase productivity. Multinational corporations are trying to take advantage of specific expertise by increasing size and diversifying market participation through mergers, acquisitions, outsourcing, and partnerships. It will be interesting to know if the larger corporations operate more efficiently than their smaller counterparts.
