# An Empirical Examination of the Concern for Information Privacy Instrument

Kathy A. Stewart • Albert H. Segars

College of Business Administration, Computer Information Systems Department, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4015

The Kenan-Flagler Business School, The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, North Carolina 27599 kstewart3@gsu.edu • al.segars@unc.edu

he arrival of the “information age” holds great promise in terms of providing organizations with access to a wealth of information stores. However, the free exchange of electronic information also brings the threat of providing easy, and many times unwanted, access to personal information. Given the potential backlash of consumers, it is imperative that both researchers and practitioners understand the nature of consumers’ concern for information privacy and accurately model the construct within evolving research and business contexts. Drawing upon a sample of 355 consumers and working within the framework of confirmatory factor analysis, this study examines the factor structure of the concern for information privacy (CFIP) instrument posited by Smith et al. (1996). Consistent with prior findings, the results suggest that each dimension of this instrument is reliable and distinct. However, the results also suggest that CFIP may be more parsimoniously represented as a higher-order factor structure rather than a correlated set of first-order factors. The implication of these results is that each dimension of CFIP as well as the supra dimension derived from the associations among dimensions are important in capturing CFIP and associating the construct to other important antecedents and consequences.

## Introduction

Information privacy has been identified as one of the most important issues of contemporary management practice (Mason 1986). In today’s information-intensive environment, organizations that base strategic initiatives on the collection and use of personal information must effectively address individual privacy concerns or risk substantial consumer backlash (Culnan 1993). While it is tempting to assume that perceptions about information gathering and use are readily understood and easily measured, these attitudes and beliefs are likely to be complex in both definition and measurement. Nonetheless, as organizations continue to develop and adopt advanced information practices, the need for valid and reliable instruments to assess consumer concerns about these information practices will become increasingly important (Jarvenpaa et al. 1985, Straub 1989). In sum, a key agenda for research and practice is the development of instruments that predict and explain the information practices that are offensive to consumers.

Smith et al. (1996) developed and validated an instrument that identifies and measures the primary dimensions of individuals’ concerns about organizational information privacy practices. These authors adopted an iterative and systematic approach to scale development that includes domain specification, item specification, numerous pretests, respecification, and exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. The result is a 15-item instrument that reflects four dimensions (factors) of concern for information privacy: collection, errors, secondary use, and unauthorized access. The instrument suggests that individuals with a high concern for information privacy perceive that: (1) too much data are collected, (2) much of the data is inaccurate, (3) corporations use personal information for undisclosed purposes, and (4) corporations fail to protect access to personal information.

The four factors identified by Smith et al. (1996) provide a framework for explaining the concern for information privacy CFIP construct by identifying underlying components that permit more precision in developing and testing research hypotheses. For example, researchers can use the instrument to test explanatory theories regarding the causal links among corporate information practices, consumer perceptions of these practices, and consumers’ behavioral responses (Smith et al. 1996). While this research has provided a much-needed entry into measuring important perceptions among consumers, further validation and investigation of the construct’s structure and measurement efficacy are needed from both a theoretical and operational perspective. From an operational perspective, additional analysis of CFIP facilitates generalizability and consistency of measurement over time and context. As noted by Doll and Xia (1997), measurement models can vary across samples and research contexts. Analysis of the construct through fitting a previously validated model to an independent sample from the referent population precludes the occurrence of good model fit through capitalization of characteristics within a particular dataset (Chin and Todd 1995). Within the realm of information system research, several observers have noted that there is an alarming lack of effort in validating instruments (Klenke 1992, Doll and Xia 1997, Jarvenpaa et al. 1985, Straub 1989).

In addition to statistical validation, the theoretical and operational assumptions underlying the structure of constructs such as CFIP should be reinvestigated in light of emerging technology, practice, and research. As noted by Smith et al. (1996), many “judgement calls” are required in the development of scales within the domain of information privacy. Based on input from various sources, these authors form a theoretical model that is consistent with the views of scholars, consumers, and advocates. However, they also acknowledge that the resulting dimensionality is neither absolute nor static because perceptions of advocates, scholars, and consumers can shift over time. Culnan (1993) suggests that information privacy should be examined within varying contexts to fully understand attitudes of consumers towards business practice. Together these studies suggest that CFIP is still in its formative stages in terms of theoretical development.

Along with the issues noted above, prior work has not clearly developed the factorial nature of the CFIP construct. Recent studies have modeled complex constructs similar to CFIP as second-order factor models rather than a set of correlated first-order factors (Segars and Grover 1993). The theoretical implication of higher-order models is that each first-order factor and the implied second-order factor is important in capturing the domain of the construct. Further, the secondorder factor may be a more important mediator between a consequent and predictor variable than the first-order constructs. The operational implication of these models is that key aspects of variation among measures may be lost if the construct is modeled incompletely and then integrated as part of a network of other constructs. This loss in explained variation can lead to errors in interpretation between dependent and independent variables within a system of path models. From a practical perspective, the existence of a secondorder factor can greatly simplify the building of statistical models relating CFIP to other constructs. However, the framing of CFIP as a higher-order factor must be reconciled with the very essence of its theoretical definition. The framing of CFIP as a formative or reflective phenomenon has significant implications for researchers attempting to place the construct within the nomological network of other variables. Therefore, along with cross validation of CFIP’s first-order constructs, assessing the consistency of a second-order factor model with the theoretical description of CFIP seems justified.

This paper reports the results of a study that further develops the CFIP instrument by examining its theoretical meaning, dimensionality, reliability, and validity. Our objective is to improve the comparison, accumulation, and syntheses of findings across studies in the information privacy research stream. In the next section we develop the theoretical underpinning of the CFIP construct through definition and reconciliation of multidimensional constructs. We then describe the methodology of the study. This is followed by the results of the analysis. We conclude by discussing the implications of the study for research and practice.

## Theoretical Foundations and Alternative Models of CFIP

The specification of relationship between a construct and its underlying dimensions of measurement form the core of theoretical definition, empirical operationalization, and practical interpretation within the contexts of research and practice. Without specification of these associations, the overall construct cannot be derived from its dimensions even though the dimensions are claimed to be under an overall construct (Law et al. 1998). For example, the meaning of CFIP when it is defined as a common factor underlying its four dimensions is different from the meaning associated with CFIP when it is defined as a simple sum of its dimensions. As noted by Chin (1998), the fundamental issue is consistency in directional change among the first-order constructs. That is, “does a directional change in one construct imply similar directional shift in other constructs?” Importantly, the relations between CFIP and other constructs in its nomological network also differ when different meanings are attributed to CFIP based on its relations with its dimensions.

In developing dimensions of CFIP, Smith et al. (1996) utilized a reflective framework in associating measured items with their respective first-order constructs. In general, each dimension of CFIP is manifested in one or more of its associated indicators. For example, concern about the collection of information by organizations is reflected in four scales that are governed by the construct of “collection” (see Appendix A). Prior literature and field interviews provide the theoretical underpinning for this approach. In contrast, the nature of relationships among the four first-order factors of CFIP is not clearly defined (although a secondorder reflective model is estimated). This is largely due to the early stage of research in the area and lack of theoretical underpinning. However, Smith et al. (1996)

do note that concern for information privacy is manifested in the first-order constructs of collection, errors, secondary use, and improper access. This implies that CFIP leads to various subconcerns and that CFIP is more than an aggregate score of these dimensions. Other studies also develop a distinct dimension of concern that may potentially govern or reflect subdimensions. Culnan and Armstrong (1999) hypothesize that organizations can address privacy concerns by observing “procedural fairness.” This construct builds trust when interchangeable organization agents exercise delegated power on behalf of consumers who cannot specify or constrain their behavior. Hoffman et al. (1999) note that privacy concerns may be governed by two larger dimensions: environmental control and secondary use of information control. Environmental control is the consumer’s ability to control the actions of other people in the environment during a market transaction or commercially oriented exchange. Secondary use of information control is the consumer’s ability to control the dissemination of information related to or provided during such transactions or behaviors to those who were not present. A common theme of the constructs developed in these studies is control. Therefore, a theoretically plausible definition of CFIP is as a distinct dimension that represents a cognitive state (or perception) about corporate practice and control of that practice. For example, concern for the accuracy of information collected about an individual is also manifested in the collection of information, unauthorized access to information, and secondary use through a larger concern of control. This situation can occur when an error in credit reporting leads to difficulty in securing conventional loans, as well as phone calls and mail from nontraditional lenders or “credit repair” agencies. Spam e-mail that is (or is not) appropriately targeted to the user is also an example of consistency in movement among the first-order dimensions.

Cast in the manner of a reflective construct, CFIP is theoretically aligned with other constructs within its nomological network, such as prior experience, personality, behavioral intent, and outcomes. Smith et al. (1996) position CFIP as a consequence of prior experience and personality and as a predictor of behavioral intent and outcome. Consistent with prior work by

Culnan (1993), such a network implies that CFIP is a reflective construct that may be more accurately modeled as a second-order factor governing its first-order dimensions. Further, within this nomological network the second-order factor should act as a mediator of consequent and predictor constructs.

As an initial step in assessing the factorial nature of CFIP, we examine five models that have been proposed as plausible representations of the phenomenon. The following sections describe the theoretical and operational underpinning of the models. We then discuss the methodology and results.

First-Order Models. Model 1 hypothesizes one first-order factor, concern for information privacy, which accounts for all the common variance among the 15 items. Some studies have approached information privacy as though it were a unidimensional construct implying that one first-order factor is a plausible model of underlying data structure (Culnan 1993, Smith et al. 1996). Opinion surveys, for example, assess increasing levels of concern about information privacy with questions such as “How concerned are you about threats to your personal privacy in America today?” (Equifax 1994). If this model is accepted, then it is appropriate to view CFIP as a single dimension that governs similarities in variation among the scale items.

Model 2 hypothesizes that the 15 items form into two first-order factors. This model proposes that consumers divide their concerns into two primary areas: collection and maintenance of their information. Results of a pretest administration of the CFIP instrument among university students $( n ~ = ~ 2 5 7 ) ^ { 1 }$ provide some modest support for this model. In short, the results suggest that the items measuring three of Smith’s dimensions (errors, unauthorized access, and secondary use) do converge onto a single factor. Furthermore, the items comprising the collection subscale all load on a single factor with factor loadings ranging from 0.6244 to 0.7450. Confirmatory confirmation of this model would indicate that consumers are concerned only about two main components of personal information use by corporations: the process of data collection and the custodial care of the personal information once it is in the corporation’s possession.

Model 3 hypothesizes that three first-order factors account for the covariance among the 15 items. Smith et al. (1996) posited that information privacy concerns reflect three dimensions: information collection, information management (errors and unauthorized access), and secondary use. Here, the errors and unauthorized access items are merged to reflect consumers’ concerns about how corporations manage and protect consumers’ personal information once it is in the corporation’s possession. This dimension addresses the question of whether or not companies are taking the steps and formulating the policies to minimize inappropriate access to data as well as errors in personal data. The secondaryuse dimension reflects consumers’ concerns about corporations sharing personal information.

Model 4 hypothesizes that the 15 items form four first-order factors. In a confirmatory factor analysis, Smith et al. (1996) found substantial support for this model. This led to a recommendation of scaling the concern of information privacy construct by averaging the subscale scores to calculate an overall score. Implicit in the practice is the assumption that every item is equally important in computing each factor and each factor is equally important in computing an overall score for CFIP.

A Second-Order Factor Model. Model 5 hypothesizes that the 15 items form four first-order factors and that these four first-order factors are measured by a second-order factor CFIP. In such a model the intercorrelations among first-order factors form a system of interdependence (or covariation) that is itself important in measuring the construct. Conceptually, each factor and the second-order factor are necessary in capturing the nature of the construct domain. Therefore, concern for information privacy can be defined as more than four distinct factors. It consists of the four factors as well as the structure of interrelationships among those factors.

As noted earlier, a common theme throughout the privacy literature that might account for the interrelationships among the first-order factors deals with the issue of control. Consumers are less likely to view a given information practice as privacy invasive if they are able to maintain, even to a small degree, some measure of control over their personal information. The Direct Marketing Association, for example, has found that asking consumers for their permission to use their personal information for secondary purposes or to collect personal information greatly reduces the degree of privacy invasion experienced by the consumer. Similarly, when consumers are given access to their personal information to verify its accuracy, they feel that they have retained some measure of control over their data. Consumer control is also communicated on behalf of companies when they state on application forms that any personal information collected will not be shared with any other organization. In sum, while consumers’ privacy concerns may relate to very specific information practices such as collection, secondary use, access, and errors, the supra concern that accounts for the interdependencies among these factors may be the degree of control over their personal information that is retained by the consumer. In other words, each of the dimensions contains a concern for control that is unique to the respective dimension as well as a concern for control that is shared among other dimensions, and together across the dimensions is itself a distinguishable supradimension.

## Research Methodology

Data for model testing were obtained through a survey of 400 consumers. The use of consumers provides another context for analyzing CFIP because prior studies have used graduate business students within a university setting (Smith et al. 1996). A modified mallintercept approach was used in which individuals were approached and asked to complete the survey (Bush and Hair 1985). Four interview sites were chosen: (1) Washington, DC; (2) Atlanta, Georgia; (3) Dallas, Texas; and (4) San Diego, California. While sites were chosen to provide some element of geographic diversity, complete generalizability of the sample to the entire United States or to a global context is problematic.

Of the 400 surveys distributed, 382 were returned and 355 were deemed usable, resulting in a response rate of 80.68%. As a means of improving the generalizability of the results, quota sampling was used to ensure that the sample possessed certain demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race) in the same proportion as the United States population. Due to quota sampling, the sample corresponded well to the United States population in terms of age, race, gender, and place of residence. The sample differed slightly in terms of education and income. The results, therefore, may be biased towards more educated, affluent individuals.

Consistent with prior work (Smith et al. 1996), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is utilized to assess the efficacy of the theorized models. CFA allows researchers to specify, estimate, and respecify multiple and interrelated dependence relationships as well as unobserved constructs (Segars 1997, Segars and Grover 1993). At a very general level, CFA involves the comparison of covariance matrices in the assessment of a hypothesized model. The “observed” covariance matrix is the set of covariances between item measures that are calculated through collected data.<sup>2</sup> In this instance, the observed data for the assessment of the initial five models is a 15  15 matrix. The “implied” matrix is the set of covariances (also a 15  15 matrix) that is generated through maximum likelihood estimation as a result of a specified model. A covariance matrix is implied for each model and the closer this “implied” matrix is to the “observed” matrix, the better the model fit and the more evidence that the specified model is indicative of the collected data. Consistent with prior studies, the “fit” of the covariance matrix implied by the hypothesized model with the observed covariance matrix is assessed using multiple indices and metrics (Bentler and Bonnet 1980, Joreskog and Sorbom 1991, Marsh and Hocevar 1985). Several software tools are available to perform CFA. In this study, the data is analyzed using LISREL version 8.20 (Joreskog and Sorbom 1991).

Figure 1 illustrates the structure and estimated parameters of the four-construct, first-order factor model (Model 4). As shown, the indicator loadings of items to their respective constructs are extremely strong. The t scores obtained for the coefficients range from 16.38 to 19.05, indicating that all factor loadings are significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ and providing evidence to support the convergent validity of the items measured (Anderson and Gerbing 1988). Composite reliability, a measure of internal consistency comparable to coefficient alpha (Fornell and Larcker 1981), is far in excess of 0.70, implying acceptable levels of reliability for each of the constructs. Variance extracted, a measure of the amount of variance captured by a construct, is also strong for the four dimensions. Each scale demonstrates variance extracted estimates far in excess of the 0.50 recommended level (Fornell and Larcker 1981). In sum, each of the first-order dimensions seem to exhibit strong properties of convergent validity.

As also illustrated in Figure 1, the first-order constructs exhibit a moderate amount of correlation among themselves. To assess the degree of these associations, a formal test of discriminant validity is conducted. Discriminant validity is implied if the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ for the first model is significantly lower than the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ for the second model. This suggests that the better model will be the one in which the two constructs are viewed as distinct, yet correlated, factors (Segars 1997, Andersen and Gerbing 1988, Bagozzi et al. 1991). In all six paired comparisons, the chi square difference is highly significant, suggesting that the constructs are distinct in nature. Consistent with the findings of Smith et al. (1996), these results suggest that the item measures of collection, unauthorized access, errors, and secondary use exhibit strong properties of distinctness in content.

Figure 2 illustrates the structure and estimated parameters of the second-order factor model of CFIP (Model 5). Consistent with the first-order model, the items exhibit strong measurement properties. In addition, the paths from the second-order factor of CFIP to the first-order dimensions are strong and significant. Importantly, the second-order factor of this model explains the covariation among first-order factors in a more parsimonious way. Therefore, even when the higher-order model is able to explain the factor covariations, the goodness-of-fit of the higher-order model can never be better than the corresponding first-order model. In this sense, the first-order model provides a target or optimum fit for the higher-order model. Marsh and Hocevar (1985) suggest the efficacy of second-order models be assessed through examination of the Target (T) coefficient $\left[ \mathsf { T } = \mathsf { \chi } ^ { 2 } \right.$ (baseline model) / $\chi ^ { 2 }$ (alternative model)]. This coefficient has an upper bound of 1.0 with higher values implying that the relationship among first-order factors is sufficiently captured by the higher-order factor. The target coefficient in this instance is a very high 0.96, also supporting the efficacy of the second-order structure. Because the second-order model is more parsimonious, it provides a better fit to the data. Hence, we conclude that the higher-order concept definition of CFIP seems to be well represented by a second-order factor model (Venkatraman 1990).

Despite the growing presence of computers in our society, some individuals avoid computers due to anxiety (Heinssen et al. 1987). Computer anxiety denotes the tendency of individuals to be uneasy, apprehensive, or fearful about current or future use of computers (Parasuraman and Igbaria 1990). From a practical perspective, these results imply that a larger theme of CFIP may be reflective of the itemlevel dimensions. Theoretically, this theme may address issues of consumer control over information or procedural fairness. Therefore, successful policy regarding information privacy should not only address “what” and “how” information is collected and used; it should also consider consumers’ perceptions of control and fairness. In essence, policy and procedure should address each of the item scales as well as interactions among these scales. These interactions may create a larger issue or concern that is distinct and worthy of consideration as part of the information-gathering strategy. For example, an information-gathering or pro-filing strategy may focus explicitly on protecting existing information through explicit policy devoted to collection, access, errors, and secondary use. This focus may be effective from a simple information-gathering perspective, yet not give the firm avenues for richer forms of profiling and targeted services. As implied in the work of

## Discussion and Conclusions

The results of this study provide interesting insights into the dimensionality of the CFIP construct. Limitations of the study, however, should be noted. Confirmatory factor analysis results should always be interpreted cautiously (Chin and Todd 1995). Criteria for comparing models and assessing goodness-of-fit indices are relative and not absolute. Furthermore, the fact that a particular sample fits a model does not confirm that the model is a good representation of reality because model-data fit can be improved simply by respecifying the model. Confirmatory findings, therefore, should be viewed as scientific evidence only to the extent that they can be replicated in subsequent studies. Furthermore, the use of CFIP should be carefully matched with research context. As developed by Smith et al. (1996), CFIP represents an attitude or cognitive state of consumers towards corporate use of information. The use of CFIP as a metric of corporate practice or managers attitudes towards information use may be inappropriate.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study enhance our understanding of the nature and dimensionality of the CFIP construct. We find the concern for information privacy construct to be multidimensional and well measured by first-order constructs of collection, unauthorized access, errors, and secondary use. We also find empirical support for the theorized second-order factor of CFIP. This suggests that consumer’s information privacy concerns are more complex than previously thought. In general, consumers are concerned about corporate information practices where information is collected, used for secondary purposes, freely accessible, and inaccurate. Furthermore, the results indicate that consumers are concerned about all of these issues rather than any one dimension in particular and that the interrelationships among these factors is an important component of accurately measuring CFIP. Failing to model CFIP as a second-order factor can result in inaccurate findings by neglecting to account for the common variation explained by the interdependencies among the four firstorder factors. Importantly, modeling a set of constructs as an aggregate composite leads to different results than the modeling of constructs as reflective of a higher-order factor (Bollen and Lennox 1991). The framework of this study and associated findings provides a context for understanding the theoretical and operational perspectives of CFIP as well as other constructs that are complex in nature.

As noted by Smith et al. (1996) and Culnan (1993), privacy is likely to be a concept that evolves as computer-based information collection, storage, and retrieval become more pervasive. Recent revelations that Internet-based business such as Amazon.com, Doubleclick.com, Realnetworks.com, and Yahoo.com compile user information that is explicit (the information you voluntarily reveal when registering for service), implicit (data gathered by monitoring your navigation through sites), and derived (aggregated information about users) has alarmed many consumers and spurred lawsuits and government intervention. Such events have heightened the awareness of consumer in privacy issues, and perhaps have had an impact on the nature of CFIP. As noted earlier, a central concern that seems to underlie consumer attitudes, and is perhaps the common theme captured by the higher-order concept of CFIP, is the issue of control. Consumers desire levels of personalization and customization but also want some sense of control over how this service occurs. This information “balancing act” is difficult to achieve in practice and is also difficult to represent in empirical research contexts. These results seem to imply that this integrative “concern” by consumer is best modeled as a higher-order con struct. Although the support for such a structure may be attributable to numerous factors (sample, method, context), a major contributor to this finding may be awareness by consumers of the potential breaches in privacy and the increasing use of Internet technology to purchase goods and services. The implication for research is that CFIP is theoretically cast as a state of concern reflected by well-measured dimensions. In developing models of intent and outcomes, this concept of concern should be captured as the common variation associated with the first-order factors to avoid errors in estimation and interpretation.

Understanding consumer concerns regarding information privacy is important to practitioners and researchers. Corporations can target corporate policymaking efforts and specific information practices to address the explicit areas of consumer concerns.
