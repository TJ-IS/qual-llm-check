## Why Metrics?

Metrics, that is, measures of key attributes that yield information about a phenomenon, lie at the heart of empirical social science and economics research. They are also increasingly important to practice as organizations try to cope with hypercompetitive environments (Bogner and Barr 2000, Zohar and Morgan 1996) and reduced growth expectations. Appropriately applied, metrics are invaluable, but vary in their relevance to the researcher, the practitioner, and the policy maker. For the scholar, they provide comparability and a more assured research procedure. Good metrics earn credence and subsequently relieve researchers from repeated efforts at construct and instrument development (Boudreau et al. 2001). Metrics enable research to accumulate in a topic area as direct comparisons become possible between persons, time periods, industries, cultures, or geographic regions (Cook and Campbell 1979).

For the practicing manager, metrics are a way of learning what works and what does not, what is reinforcing and what is disconfirming feedback (Senge 1990), but in all cases metrics can lead to more effective organizations in the hands of capable managers. Measuring business performance, in fact, is the focus of the management accounting literature, which addresses the collection and analysis of company performance data (Kaplan 1989, Stuchfield and Weber 1992). For the policy maker, appropriate metrics help guide public investment and inform the regulatory process.

Clear, useful metrics that capture commercial or manufacturing processes have long enabled managers to improve strategies and operations. The formal application of scientific measurement techniques to business is less than a century old (Taylor 1911), but long before Taylor successful businesses capitalized on knowledge about holdings and trade activity. In 1760, a manager in a French pin factory is credited with studying the manufacturing process and establishing standard times for specific operations. Economic history shows that as trade and commerce expand, leading enterprises gather and use the highest quality information available to them.

Although businesses have been collecting information for deeper insights for hundreds of years, organizational research and organizational theorists have only recently turned to metrics. Taylor’s methods, time and motion studies, and nascent theories of causation that characterized the early 20th-century management literature lacked the rigor of later positivist methodologies. But since the 1940s, there has been a plethora of work on methods and measures that can only be described as a blossoming of the behavioral sciences. Groundbreaking early work in the philosophy of science and metrics was carried out by psychologists (e.g., Campbell and Stanley 1963).

Metrics for managers and the theoretical principles for their application develop in response to the business environment at the time. For example, industrialera manufacturers benefited from production efficiency metrics, while service economy businesses later in the century fostered the development of new metrics to measure service delivery satisfaction and customer retention. As the Internet grew into a customer delivery channel and a fundamental enabler of revolutionary new business strategies by the mid-1990s, managers and researchers alike began to call for new metrics and new applications of existing metrics (Hoffman and Novak 1997, Novak and Hoffman 1997). What contribution this special issue may make, then, resides less in the particular principles of measurement that have attained the imprimatur of a given era than in the evaluation and application of these metrics in this changing environment.

To what extent do the changes embodied in e-Commerce represent rapid paradigm shifts that will alter the fundamentals of doing business, that is, a “reformation?” Or will change be gradual, but permanent and significant (“a transformation”)? These questions can be addressed as we explore the elements of the phenomenon that are truly new and those that are not.

## Unique Metrics for the Internet?

What is this new business environment, and, what’s more, is it really that different from the past? When we speak of electronic commerce, we are not speaking about what is connoted simply in the composite of the terms “electronic” and “commerce.” The dot-com failures of 2000–2001 have tarnished such terms to the point where viable substitutes need to be sought out (Straub and Watson 2001). What we are talking about is the transition—rapid or gradual—from organizational systems that were heavily dependent on physical processes to those that rely on network-accessible information for fundamental business processes (Straub and Watson 2001). This transformation (or reformation, depending on how one views the depth of this change) relies on the ability of telecommunications to reduce the effects of time and space on businesses.

The essence of these changes in the way 21st-century businesses will function has been termed e-Commerce, e-Business (the IBM proprietary term), and alternatively NE or “Net”-enablement (Straub and Watson 2001).<sup>1</sup> We embrace the term “Net”-enablement for this special issue because it represents the ongoing changes in business processes brought about between organizations through the Inter-“Net” or through nonpublic “Net”-works.

We also endorse a second term that can, perhaps, serve a useful role in describing networked organizations: net-enhanced organizations. Net-enhanced organizations (the acronymn for which is also “NEOs”) are traditional, brick and mortar firms that are using networks to “enhance” their existing channels. In effect, these hybridized organizations are becoming “clicks and bricks” firms.

Terminology aside, it is clear that the burst of the dot-com bubble reflected, among other things, excessively high expectations that were virtually impossible to fulfill. While vast financial resources were channeled into Web ventures, the highly visible failures of several hundred so-called “pure plays,” including Boo, Webvan, eToys, and others, are not important in the long run other than as interesting, albeit transitory, historical footnotes.

We concur with John Chambers, Chairman and CEO of Cisco, who argues that Internet commerce represents the beginning of a second Industrial Revolution (Anonymous 2001). Fundamental changes in business activity that are on-going cannot ultimately be affected by disposable terms-of-art that are rapidly proliferated, only to be subsequently abandoned by the business press, consultants, and even some in the academic community.

One mistake many dot-coms of the late 1990’s made was equating the idea that the Internet is different with the erroneous, if not arrogant assumption that it changes everything. Yet, even if the fundamentals of what it takes to succeed in business have not altered, the Internet does have unique characteristics that must be acknowledged and addressed in any business strategy going forward (Hoffman and Novak 2001). Online brokerage firms, for instance, have different trading relationships than traditional voice-based brokers (Bakos et al. 2001).

Clearly, then, it is important to recognize that the Web enables novel forms of business that were simply not practical before, or even possible. For example, there are: (1) auctions like eBay that let geographically remote participants bid on items and participate in communities based on an affinity for categories, (2) exchanges like Covisint and ChemConnect that enlarge the market for suppliers and buyers regardless of size, (3) instant messaging that enables individuals to communicate in real time with each other, (4) peer-to-peer applications that allow individuals to share digital and other information without a centralized database, (5) personal broadcasting through Web cams, (6) personal homepages and commercial sites like photo sharing, (7) new pricing models like Priceline and Mobshop (which were largely impossible to implement before the advent of the Web), and (8) ways of sorting products and services, such as by price or size, that go well beyond display limitations in physical storefronts. Indeed, Weill and Vitale (2001) have found eight atomic business models that represent new approaches conducive to cyberspace. Even when the Internet is mainly used as a supplement to traditional offline commerce, the potential synergies between the physical and virtual channels enable heretofore impractical services (Steinfield, Bouwman, and Adelaar 2001).

Second, the unique characteristics underlying the Web may in some cases require new metrics, or at least the careful evaluation of existing ones, to facilitate the development of innovative solutions to emerging problems. With respect to banner ads on the Web, for example, the application of traditional metrics has actually hindered the development of innovative advertising approaches more appropriate for the new medium (Hoffman and Novak 2000).

The Web is unique in part because the many-tomany communication model that is underlying it reverses the traditional one-to-many broadcasting paradigm. Any user on the Internet can be an information provider, and human interaction is not just with other people but also with computers and intelligent agents. The Web also allows an unprecedented level of choice in an environment that approaches full information and shifts the balance of market power toward the end consumer (Weber 1998). Additionally, the web represents an alternative to, rather than a simulation of the “real world,” and presents individuals with opportunities where experiential and goal-directed activities are juxtaposed (Hoffman and Novak 1996). These characteristics combine to create a medium that is substantially more than the sum of its parts and, in some cases, continues to confound managers seeking ways to capitalize on its uniqueness.

It is clear that there are truly new aspects of the Web that need to be embedded in metrics, but how quickly is this change occurring? We can examine this issue by investigating what leads to success and what does not, and which metrics are useful in tapping into which part of the phenomenon.

## What Leads to Success in e-Commerce, and How Can Metrics Help?

Even in “revolutionary” times, business managers need metrics to make comparisons, benchmark performance, plan improvements, and allocate capital.

Research needs to establish metrics that reflect changes in business activities that have some degree of permanence. Nevertheless, changes in the “Net” world began with an extraordinary fireworks display of startups whose business existence took the form of pure plays on the World Wide Web. While many were oriented toward direct linkages to the consumer, very few survived the movement to profitable direct-toconsumer business and full-service provider business models (Weill and Vitale 2001). One reason for this may be that firms have not yet fully realized the need to create significant information asymmetries with their competitors (Straub and Klein 2001).

Placing success for NEOs in perspective, the dotcom build-up can be seen as just another manifestation of the “irrational exuberance” that U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman, Alan Greenspan, noted about stock valuations in 1996. But interestingly enough, the boom-bust of e-Commerce start-ups does not differ in significant ways from the launching of many other technological (r)evolutions.

When the steam-driven train was first invented, its diffusion was relatively slow and halting. Links between major British cities were developed, and the initial passengers were the elite of society. It took several decades before infrastructure and economies of scale made it possible for most levels of society to participate and derive great benefits from the technologies. Only a handful of the early railroads and their supplier companies survived (Landes 1969). Later, as the technology evolved and the infrastructure started paying off, railroads made fortunes for investors (Ambrose 2000).

The development of the automobile and commercial airline travel industries were similar in that the sense that infrastructure and widespread usage were required for firms to operate with economies of scale. Consolidation occurred as many early car manufacturers and airlines came and went. Similarly, profitability hinged on the technology being infused into the lives of people in such a way that income streams for businesses were substantial. Thus, profits in the early days for the use of the technology itself were rare.<sup>2</sup> If the fruits of the technology could easily be put aside by consumers, then the technology was more likely to be seen as a luxury, making consistent profits for its providers much less certain.

The Internet and other networked connections between organizations are an infrastructure, but the current state of the art is still more akin to a country road than to a super highway. Not only does the width of the roads (i.e., bandwidth) hobble further expansion of the medium, but the capacity and compatibility of the warehouses and the assembly factories that lie at the end points (i.e., computer clients and servers) are problematic (Rose et al. 1999). Business acumen has certainly played a role in which firms have become successful in their e-commerce pursuits, but the major problem is that the business environment remains chaotic and unsettled, with the final formula for success still elusive. Our view is that this shift to net-enabled and net-enhanced organizations will be gradual and that best practices will be emergent. Measures of this phenomenon will be forthcoming over a long period as well.

In the final analysis, it will likely be a combination of conditions that will lead to successful NEOs. Some direct-to-consumer models like Dell and value-net integrators like Cisco will continue to excel, whereas other firms will have to invest in multiple channels, some based on physical sales and distribution and others on network-coordinated sales and distribution. There is a growing literature on the staying power of traditional brick and mortar firms and how they are becoming net-enhanced by exploiting brands, existing asset bases, and other strategic resources for success in cyberspace (Otto and Chung 2000; Steinfield et al. 2001, 2001, and 1999; Weill and Vitale 2001). The best way to understand how and why this is happening is by developing techniques and methods to measure it well.

As the e-commerce valuation peak of March 2000 passes into history, we have evidence that certain business models, such as Dell’s and Cisco’s, led to enhanced profitability and growth (Weill and Vitale 2001). For other firms, formal metrics applied to business models as firms’ NE initiatives emerge will enable us to substantiate the evolving uses of the Internet that contribute to corporate bottom lines.

B2B hubs are frequently touted as the “real action” in NE (Mahadevan 2000). Is an open many-to-many website a defining condition for success, or is it ultimately business-to-consumer models that create sustainable competitive advantage? Or both? Will firms that balance investments in supplier-facing and customer-facing systems endure and prosper, or those that specialize in one or the other? Irrespective of which of these conditions and models prevails, metrics are sine qua non. Without measures of B2C and B2B activities to compare these models with each other, and with traditional physical businesses, none of these key questions can even be addressed, let alone answered.

## e-Commerce Success and Traditional Business Performance Metrics

The current special issue makes progress toward measuring NE business performance. But this effort is clearly just commencing. Traditional measures like economic-value added, return on assets, and quarterly profitability may, to some extent, be useful barometers of the health of the NE initiatives. These broad financial measures suffer as dependent variables in that they are affected by many other factors outside of NESs, including industry conditions, competition, and the health of the economy at any given point in time. Operational measures such as inventory turnover have been suggested, but often many of the benefits of NE are either intangible or not routinely tracked. New indices of contribution, productivity, relationship profits, and value creation are needed.

NEOs might benefit from having more informed customers in a variety of ways, ranging from faster, less error-prone transactions to better product matches and fewer returns. The extent to which NE contributes to supplier or customer lock-in (Shapiro and Varian 1999) could be another important metric. Likewise, click and mortar firms should be interested in the extent to which customers find it easy to seamlessly move between on- and off-line channels (Steinfield, Bouwman, and Adelaar 2001). Indeed, firms that want their traditional and new network-based channels to work together require metrics for interoperability. Such metrics could help guide process reengineering efforts for net-enhanced organizations.

The situation is even more complex when we consider that time frames for the emergence of measurable effects, especially in the first decade or so of the evolution, may be different. If, as some commentators argued, the value of NE to a business resides in the extent to which a customer becomes locked in for life, then one appropriate metric is not cross-sectional representation of initial online trust, but longer term panel measures.

If Web sites are constantly changing, then the appropriate dependent metric may be satisfaction deltas reflecting site usage over windows of time. Researchers may have to construct measures of the stability or volatility of Web sites for their independent variables. Evidence is emerging that cross-sectional metrics for the usability of Web sites are too limited in generalizability to be of any real value to researchers or practitioners, and they will usually become less accurate as competing alternatives are introduced.

## How Do the Papers in this Issue Contribute to e-Commerce Metrics?

Our macro-level speculations on e-commerce and its measurement argue that new metrics should be created to measure finely or coarsely the business activities in this emerging reality. Our final argument, and we believe the best argument that can be made, is contained within the papers being published in this special issue. The papers in this issue represent new ways of looking at NESs and NEOs that were not available in past research or practitioner instruments. And, they have been validated for further use.

The published articles in two editions of the special issue—June and September 2002—use both old and new measures to capture “Net”-enabled systems activity. Together, the two published issues represent a substantial starting point for new research programs viewing the effects of IS on NEOs.

## Article #2: Zahra and George’s “Net-Enabled Business Innovation Cycle and the Evolution of Dynamic Capabilities”

From their perspective as strategic management theorists, Zahra and George respond to Wheeler’s cycle by exploring how IT strategy may, indeed, precede corporate strategy formulation. They show ways in which the theory can be extended through a strategic entrepreseurship perspective and the theory bases that have evolved in that field. They conclude by framing the assumptions and limitations of Wheeler’s NEBIC. This extension-cum-commentary on Wheeler’s work is similar to the response format used often in the Academy of Management journals and adds value by placing Wheeler’s work in the context of contemporary strategic management thinking and the ways in which these complementary fields can contribute to each other’s knowledge base.

## Article #3: Palmer’s “Web Site Usability, Design, and Performance Metrics”

Palmer reports on Web site design and the features of a Web site that need to be measured to determine its effectiveness. The approach combines the systems usability literature with marketing concepts. These metrics simply do not exist in physical sales and distribution models, although they certainly have analogues. The author has used three methods to gather the data and the robustness of these maximally different measures in capturing the constructs as shown. ## Article #4: Agarwal and Venkatesh’s “Assessing a Firm’s Web Presence: A Heuristic Evaluation Procedure for the Measurement of Usability”

Agarwal and Venkatesh illuminate the specific strengths and weaknesses associated with Web site design. The metrics they describe differ from Palmer’s measures in that theirs result from a heuristicevaluation procedure for examining the usability of Web sites. The procedure utilizes a comprehensive set of usability guidelines developed by Microsoft. These two usability papers provide interesting companion pieces in that one depends heavily on theory and the academic literature, while the other refines a set of standards widely accepted and promulgated by an industry leader. This paper also presents a set of weights associated with each of the constructs and subconstructs. Metrics articulated by Agarwal and Venkatesh are shown in Table 3.

## Article #5: Torkzadeh and Dhillon’s “Measuring Factors that Influence the Success of Internet Commerce”

Torkzadeh and Dhillon explore measures for B2C e-commerce. Originally articulated in Keeney’s Management Science article, they posit that the “values” that consumers place on Web shopping are metrics that can be used to assess those aspects of Web shopping matter that are most important in satisfying consumers. The macro-level constructs that Keeney terms means objectives and fundamental objectives are developed to categorize these values. The authors break these macrolevel constructs into meaningful subunits that can be measured. The process used by the authors to validate their instrument was extensive. Metrics articulated by Torkzadeh and Dhillon are shown in Table 4.

Article #6: Koufaris’s “Applying the Technology Acceptance Model and Flow Theory to Online Consumer Behavior”

Koufaris combines traditional IS variables derived from the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis 1989) with variables derived from psychology and marketing to predict unplanned purchases and intention to return to an online store. His main findings are that shopping enjoyment and perceived usefulness do predict intention to return. Antecedents of enjoyment include both individual and Web site factors: product involvement, Web skills, the inclusion of value-added search mechanisms, and the degree to which the site is challenging. These are critical outcome and explanatory measures for Web-based storefronts. Metrics articulated by Koufaris are shown in Table 5.

## Conclusion

The development of metrics to capture the essence of e-commerce activities has just begun. Researchers throughout the management disciplines need to reconceptualize their theories, constructs, and measures to incorporate the uniqueness of the phenomenon into their work. Our two editions of this special issue contribute to a vibrant research stream that will lead to further insights into the nature of the systems that organizations need to become “Net”-enabled.
