# Portfolios of Control in Outsourced Software Development Projects

Vivek Choudhury • Rajiv Sabherwal

Information Systems Department, College of Business, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio 45221 CCB 206, College of Business Administration, University of Missouri, St. Louis, 8001 Natural Bridge Road,

St. Louis, Missouri 63121

vivek.choudhury@uc.edu • sabherwal@umsl.edu

his paper examines the evolution of portfolio of controls over the duration of outsourced Tinformation systems development (ISD) projects. Drawing on five cases, it concludes that many findings from research on control of internal ISD projects apply to the outsourced context as well, but with some interesting differences. The portfolios of control in outsourced projects are dominated by outcome controls, especially at the start of the project; although the precision and frequency of these controls varies across projects. Behavior controls are often added later in the project, as are controls aimed to encourage and enable vendor selfcontrol. Clan controls were used in only two of the cases—when the client and vendor had shared goals, and when frequent interactions led to shared values. In general, the outsourced projects we studied began with relatively simple controls but often required significant additional controls after experiencing performance problems. Factors influencing the choice and evolution of controls are also examined.

(Systems Development; Control; Outsourcing; Project Management; Case Studies)

## Introduction

Information system development (ISD) is a complex, intensive, and dynamic activity that requires close cooperation and coordination among diverse stakeholders, including users, information systems (IS) personnel, and senior managers (Beath 1987). Control plays an important part in ISD projects by integrating the complementary capabilities of the participants (Kirsch 1997). Most of the prior literature on control in ISD is based on internal projects, where developers and users are members of the same organization. However, companies are increasingly outsourcing all or some of their IS activities (Cross 1995, Huber 1993, Lacity and Hirschheim 1993), including IS development. According to Clark et al. (1998), “  the truly critical success factors associated with successful outsourcing are those associated with vendor governance” (p. 72; emphasis in original). An important aspect of this governance is how the client exercises control over the vendor. Outsourced ISD projects pose unique problems that make the task of controlling them particularly challenging. For instance, the client may lack the direct authority to prescribe vendor behaviors. It is more difficult to monitor vendor behavior, particularly if the vendor is remotely located because of the dispersion of people, coordination problems, and loss of communication richness (Carmel 1999). Direct observation may require extended colocation, which can be expensive. There are usually no preexisting reporting relationships or systems that serve as natural conduits for information—Thus, information systems for monitoring vendor behavior must be established from scratch. It is, therefore, important to examine control in outsourced projects.

Prior research on control of ISD projects has focused primarily on two aspects. First, it has examined the specific control modes (e.g., behavior, outcome, or clan controls) used during a project (e.g., Henderson and Lee 1992; Kirsch 1996, 1997). Second, it has explored the factors (e.g., project size, outcome measurability, behavior observability) influencing the choice of control modes (Beath 1987; Kirsch 1996, 1997). This paper aims to examine, and extend where appropriate, the key conclusions from this stream of research within the specific context of outsourced ISD projects.

The paper addresses three research questions. First, following prior research on control in ISD (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al. 2002) we expect that, even in an outsourced context, firms will use a portfolio of controls. Thus, our first research question is: What mechanisms constitute the portfolios of control in outsourced ISD projects? Second, this paper extends the findings of prior research by examining the dynamics of control, i.e., how do portfolios of controls change during the course of an outsourced ISD project? Kirsch (1997) suggests this as an important direction for research:

  the results are suggestive of changing control modes over the life of a project    . However, there is little substantive knowledge about how and why control modes change over time (p. 237).

Finally, the paper attempts to provide an initial answer to the question: What factors influence the initial composition, and the subsequent evolution, of the portfolio of mechanisms used to control an outsourced ISD project? Are these factors consistent with the influencing factors identified as affecting the choice of control mechanisms in internal ISD projects?

A qualitative approach using five detailed cases was employed to examine these research questions. The next section positions our work in the context of prior literature. We then describe the research methods, present our findings, and conclude by examining the paper’s implications.

## Theoretical Development

This paper views control broadly, in a behavioral sense (Jaworski 1988; Kirsch 1996, 1997), as attempting to ensure individuals act in a manner that is consistent with achieving desired objectives. This view of control draws upon agency and organization theories in a way consistent with prior studies in organization design (e.g., Eisenhardt 1985), IS (Henderson and Lee 1992; Kirsch 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al. 2002) and marketing (Jaworski 1988, Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989).

A control situation typically involves an individual controller evaluating and influencing an individual controllee. For example, Kirsch (1996, 1997) viewed the user manager and the ISD manager as the controllers and the IS project manager as the controllee. This distinction sometimes becomes fuzzy— For instance, in a strategic alliance the firms may exercise mutual control over each other, and in clan control each group member effectively functions as both controller and controllee. However, for the specific context of this study, the distinction between controller and controllee is largely valid.

There are, however, two differences between this study of outsourced ISD and prior studies on the control of internal IS projects. First, in the case of outsourced projects, the controllers and controllees are members of different organizations. Second, the controller and the controllee may not be single individuals but teams of individuals representing their respective organizations. Thus, in this study, the “controller” refers to the individual(s) in the client organization responsible for designing and implementing controls, while “controllee” refers to the individual(s) in the vendor organization responsible for executing the project. Although the overall context is a contract between two firms, the focus is still on individual(s) evaluating and influencing the actions of other individual(s). For convenience in presentation, in the remainder of the paper, “client” refers to the controller and “vendor” refers to the controllee (recognizing that the terms really refer to individuals in the two organizations).

## Control Modes and Mechanisms

The behavioral view of control implies that the controller uses certain devices, or control mechanisms, to promote desired behavior by the controllee (Kirsch 1997). These control mechanisms help implement control modes, which may broadly be divided into formal controls, i.e., modes that rely on mechanisms that influence the controllee’s behavior through performance evaluation and rewards, and informal controls, i.e., modes that utilize social or people strategies to reduce goal differences between controller and controllee (Eisenhardt 1985; Kirsch 1996, 1997). Some researchers (e.g., Merchant 1988) view formal and informal controls not as a dichotomy, but as opposite ends of a continuum. Both formal and informal controls are exercised through mechanisms used to influence controllee behavior. Examples include target implementation dates (formal control) and socialization (informal control—Kirsch 1997).

Formal Controls. Two types of formal controls have been commonly considered in prior literature (e.g., Ouchi 1979, Eisenhardt 1985), including the literature on the control of IS projects (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al. 2002)—behavior control and outcome control.

In outcome control, the controller focuses on the outputs (both final and interim) of the project without regard to the process by which these outputs are achieved. Thus, the controller explicitly states desired outcomes or goals and rewards the controllee for meeting those goals (Kirsch 1997). This is achieved through the use of mechanisms that specify desired outcomes (e.g., functional specifications and target implementation date) and mechanisms that help measure the controllee’s performance with respect to the specified outcomes (e.g., software testing by client personnel). We expect this definition of outcome control to apply to outsourced ISD projects as well.

In behavior control, the controller seeks to influence the process, or the means to goal achievement, by explicitly prescribing specific rules and procedures, observing the controllee’s behaviors, and rewarding the controllee based on the extent to which it follows stated procedures (Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989, Kirsch 1996). Behavior control is implemented through mechanisms that specify appropriate behaviors (e.g., development methodology), or those that allow the controller to evaluate behavior (Kirsch 1997). The latter includes various information-gathering mechanisms, such as direct observation (e.g., placing client personnel on vendor premises) and other information systems (e.g., weekly progress reports, periodic meetings, or conference calls) (Eisenhardt 1985).

Informal Controls. Informal controls are also of two types: clan control and self-control. Clan control is implemented through mechanisms that minimize the differences between controller’s and controllee’s preferences (Eisenhardt 1985) by “promulgating common values, beliefs, and philosophy within a clan, which is defined as a group of individuals who are dependent on one another and who share a set of common goals” (Kirsch 1996, p. 217), or by identifying and reinforcing acceptable behaviors through shared experiences, rituals, and ceremonies (Kirsch 1996, Ouchi 1980).

In outsourced ISD projects, the client-vendor relationship is traditionally presumed to be adversarial (Lacity and Hirschheim 1993, Williamson 1985). Lacity (2002) remarks: “For all the sourcing models, contracts are inherently adversarial in that every dollar out of the customer’s pocket is a dollar in the supplier’s pocket” (p. 31). We interpret the term clan control to refer to a situation in which the traditional relationship is replaced by a scenario where the two organizations perceive themselves as having a common, shared goal. Illustrative mechanisms to implement clan control include structuring the relationship so that it is strategic to both parties, and socialization of client and vendor executives through regular joint meetings. However, clan control may be difficult to achieve in outsourced ISD projects unless they are part of a longterm alliance (Das and Teng 1998, 2001; Carmel 1999).

Like clan control, self-control also relies on the controllee engaging in behavior consistent with the best interests of the controller without formal controls. However, there is an important distinction:

Unlike clan control, in which control stems from sharing norms and values within a group, self-control is a function of individual objectives and standards   and intrinsic motivation   (Kirsch 1996, p. 218).

In self-control, the controllee determines both the goals and the actions through which they should be achieved (Henderson and Lee 1992), as in selfregulated teams (Cummings 1978, Manz and Sims 1980). Mechanisms supporting self-control are primarily implemented by the controllee. Such mechanisms may include the controllee identifying and establishing standards for its own behavior, or establishing a timetable for project milestones and monitoring progress against these milestones. However, as several authors (e.g., Von Glinow 1983, Kirsch 1996) have noted, the controller can also encourage or enable the controllee to exercise self-control.

  controllers, who do not directly exercise self-control over others, nevertheless can encourage others to exercise self-control    . For example, controllers can train controllees in appropriate techniques (Brief and Aldag 1981) or ensure that tasks are clearly defined for discernable boundaries (Kirsch and Cummings 1996). Controllers can also institute performance evaluation schemes that reward autonomy and selfmanagement (Kirsch et al. 2002, p. 486).

Mechanisms to implement self-control in the outsourced context are initiated and implemented internally by the vendor (the controllee). For instance, members of the vendor team may determine the specific process through which the system is to be developed, or a specific timeline for system delivery, and then monitor their compliance with the selfprescribed behaviors and/or outcomes. There are, of course, limits on the extent to which the vendor can determine its goals for the system—It is hard to imagine a situation where the vendor completely determines the system’s functional requirements.

In this study, we do not observe self-controls implemented internally by the vendor, partly because we expect these controls to resemble those used for internal projects. Instead, our interest is in the control mechanisms a client uses to enhance the probability of a successful outsourced ISD project. This includes mechanisms a client can use to assist and promote the exercise of self-control by the vendor. The client may use mechanisms to encourage the vendor to exercise better self-control (e.g., by conveying to the vendor that it values the ability to create and manage to a planned project schedule), and enhance the vendor’s ability to exercise better internal control (e.g., by helping to redesign the vendor’s internal software testing procedures). Note that although the client helps the vendor design processes, these are not examples of behavior control. The client neither dictates the use of the processes, nor monitors the vendor’s adherence to them. This is akin to the client training the vendor in certain techniques, which has earlier been classified as self-control (Brief and Aldag 1981).

The Role of Rewards in Control. All forms of controls are associated with explicit or implicit rewards (Eisenhardt 1985, Kirsch et al. 2002). In outcome controls and behavior controls, the controller rewards the controllee for meeting goals or following prescribed behaviors, respectively (Kirsch 1997). In self-control the individual, independent of formal organizational mechanisms or group norms, institutes the rewards (Kirsch 1996). Finally, in clan control rewards and sanctions depend on whether the individuals act according to group values, norms, and objectives (Kirsch et al. 2002). However, rewards may be implicit in clan control. According to Kirsch et al. (2002), in clan control, “Unlike behavior and outcome control situations, there is no need for explicit incentives to align the goals of controllers and controllees because of the existence of shared goals” (p. 486).

Prior literature indicates a divergence of views regarding the nature and role of rewards in control (Coff 1999, Das and Teng 1998, Eisenhardt 1985, Kirsch et al. 2002, Ouchi 1979). According to Eisenhardt, one difference between organizational literature on control and agency theory is “  agency’s emphasis on rewards. In the organizational literature, control is a process of measurement and evaluation. Reward is implicit. In agency theory, the contracting emphasis makes rewards explicit” (p. 138). In this respect, our investigation of control mechanisms was closer to the organizational view, concentrating on measurement and evaluation but not rewards. We did not directly ask interviewees about rewards associated with controls. Although some examples did emerge during the interviews, we add the caveat that this approach may have caused us to miss some rewards.

Portfolios of Control Modes and Mechanisms. Discussion to this point has examined the four control modes separately. However, controllers often use the four modes in combination, creating a portfolio of controls (Kim 1984, Jaworski 1988, Jaworski et al. 1993, Kirsch 1997). For instance, agency theorists consider alternative control modes as mutual substitutes (Eisenhardt 1985). Jaworski et al. (1993) argue that control is achieved through a combination of primary and secondary controls. Kirsch (1997) noted that a portfolio of control modes is used in internal ISD projects, although with a greater reliance on formal rather than informal mechanisms.

In no instance did a controller in this study eliminate formal controls.   the controllers were not comfortable relying solely on informal modes of

Within a portfolio of controls, each control mode can itself be implemented through multiple control mechanisms (Kirsch 1997). (See Figure 1.) For example, behavior control may be achieved through some mechanisms that specify desired behavior and others that monitor the controllee’s behavior. Further, the same general mechanism can support more than one control mode. The same project plan might also be a mechanism of outcome control by delineating final or interim targets for the project. Thus, it is possible for a single project plan to support both behavior and outcome control, as Kirsch (1997) found in her study. A contract between the client and the vendor can facilitate outcome control by specifying outcomes and associated rewards, but it can also facilitate clan control if it is structured to enhance shared goals between the two organizations (Das and Teng 1998). A monthly progress review meeting might provide insights into the actions taken by system developers (behavior control) as well as the progress they have made relative to the project schedule (outcome control). Conference calls at specified intervals may allow the controller to assess tasks that the controllee has been performing (behavior control), but shared values and beliefs may also result over time from the interpersonal interaction that takes place in these conference calls (clan control) (Kirsch 1997). ## Factors Influencing Choice of Control Modes

There is considerable prior research on the factors affecting control, although not in the specific context of outsourced ISD projects. Prior studies have focused on retail salespeople (Eisenhardt 1985), marketing executives (Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989), and the control of internal IS projects (Beath 1987; Kirsch 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al. 2002). The influencing factors identified in these studies can be classified into three broad categories, as suggested by Kirsch (1997).

(1) Task Characteristics. The effects of several task characteristics on control have been examined. These include behavior observability, i.e., ability to gather information about controllee behavior, outcome measurability, i.e., ability to specify and track desired outcomes, and project size. Prior research generally suggests that high behavior observability facilitates behavior control (Kirsch 1996, 1997) although Kirsch et al.

(2002) recently concluded that when behavior observability is high, behavior control is used if the controller is knowledgeable, but clan control is likely if the controller has low knowledge. Outcome measurability has consistently been found to facilitate outcome control (Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989; Kirsch 1996, 1997; Kirsch et al. 2002), but the findings are mixed with respect to whether it facilitates (Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989, Kirsch et al. 2002) or inhibits (Kirsch 1996) self-control. Informal controls (clan and/or self) are often used when behavior observability and outcome measurability are both low (Aubert et al. 1996, Kirsch 1996). Finally, project size also affects the choice of control modes. Kirsch (1997) found greater use of self-control in smaller projects. In general, larger projects are controlled through more formal control mechanisms (Jaworski 1988).

(2) Project-Related Knowledge of the Participants. Project-related knowledge of the controller and the controllee also affect the choice of control modes. A knowledgeable controller is likely to be more confident, and more inclined to specify the exact process the controllee should follow. Thus, a controller’s projectrelated knowledge facilitates behavior control (Eisenhardt 1985, Jaworski and Maclnnis 1989, Kirsch 1996, Kirsch et al. 2002). On the other hand, a more knowledgeable controllee would make the controller feel more confident in using outcome control or self-control (Kirsch 1996).

(3) Role Expectations. Role expectations are individuals’ perceptions about their own and others’ roles. For example, Kirsch (1997) reported that a user manager exercised behavior control because he believed it was necessary that he, and not IS, control the project. Role expectations might include norms about collegiality (which may enable clan control) or independence (which may promote self-control). In the specific context of outsourced ISD, role expectations would reflect the beliefs client and vendor personnel have about the distribution of responsibilities.

Finally, the use of a control mode depends at least partially on the availability of mechanisms supporting the other control modes (Kirsch 1997, Merchant 1988). For example, the availability of clan controls may reduce incentives to use more formal controls.

  the presence of informal controls should be considered like other environmental factors that managers must consider when they design (formal) control systems (Merchant 1988, p. 42).

## Control of Outsourced Projects

There has been considerable research on IS outsourcing, on such issues as political and cultural implications of global outsourcing (Nicholson and Sahay 2001), transaction-cost view of outsourcing (Aubert et al. 1996), and the role of trust in outsourcing (Sabherwal 1999). While little attention has been given to the control of outsourced ISD projects (Lacity and Willcocks 2001), some a priori expectations can be formed by drawing on research on control of internal ISD projects and control in strategic alliances (Das and Teng 1998, 2001). Based on the factors discussed above, we expect that behavior control and clan control are likely to be used less extensively in outsourced ISD projects.

We believe that clan control will be more difficult to implement in an outsourced context.<sup>1</sup> The process of extended socialization typically used to create shared beliefs is hard to achieve even in a colocated group within a single firm. For instance, Kirsch (1997) found clan control to be used in only two of her four case sites. Socialization, shared experiences, rituals promoting shared beliefs, and common goals are more difficult to achieve between the members of a client firm and a vendor firm, particularly if the vendor is remotely located (Lacity and Willcocks 2001). Carmel (1999) has also suggested that global software teams often suffer from a lack of “teamness.”

Our expectation about the limited use of behavior controls is based on a number of factors. First, as discussed above, a high level of confidence in the controllee’s competence and knowledge of the project promotes the use of outcome or self-controls. In an outsourced project, the vendor is often selected after careful screening, which might make the client feel confident about the vendor’s abilities and knowledge and reduce the incentive to implement behavior control. Second, effective behavior control entails the use of mechanisms to monitor the controllee’s behaviors, either directly or through the use of information systems. In an outsourced ISD project, direct observation of the actions of a vendor is harder than in internal ISD projects, especially if the vendor is remotely located (Nicholson and Sahay 2001). It may require the client to position one or more individuals at the vendor (Heeks et al. 2001, Lacity and Willcocks 2001). While feasible, this may be perceived by the client as expensive and obviating some of the economic benefits of outsourcing. Similarly, the design and use of effective, cross-organizational information systems that reliably inform the client about vendor behavior are likely to require greater investment of managers time and/or technological resources in an outsourced context than in internal ISD projects. Celly and Frazier (1996) have made similar comments in the context of channel relationships, noting that “Between independent channel organizations, formal authority relationships are not nearly as strong, making monitoring and evaluating distributor behaviors relatively difficult” (p. 200). Thus, we expect less use of behavior control in outsourced projects.

There is one consideration that suggests a different conclusion. Kirsch (1997) noted that a controller with greater knowledge of appropriate behaviors may be better able, and more inclined, to implement behavior control. In an outsourced project, the controller is usually a group of IS employees at the client (Lacity and Willcocks 2001), who should be highly knowledgeable about the ISD process. For example, Feeny and Willcocks (1998) describe a need for the client to retain IS people to facilitate and monitor the contract with the vendor. This would normally imply greater inclination to use behavior controls. We posit, however, that the above factors will overcome this consideration.

Therefore, we posit that the portfolio of control modes used by client personnel in an outsourced project will be dominated by outcome controls, supplemented primarily by client efforts to enhance vendor self-controls. This is consistent with the finding by

Kirsch (1997) that formal controls dominate and with Feeny and Willcocks (1998), who concluded that the formal contract is sometimes followed so rigorously that the implicit, a priori understanding regarding the expected outcomes are ignored.

## Dynamics of Control

Although there is little research on the evolution of control mechanisms in IS projects, there has been some valuable research on the evolution of interorganizational relationships (Arino and de la Torre 1998, Doz 1996, Ring and Van de Ven 1994). Ring and Van de Ven proposed that an interorganizational relationship evolves through sequences of negotiation, commitment, and execution. Doz found successful alliances to undergo cycles of learning, re-evaluation, and readjustment. As the two sides learn from their interactions, they reassess the alliance and make adjustments. This research provides an initial expectation for the evolution of control mechanisms. As clients and vendors learn from their interactions during the course of a project, their perceptions of the factors affecting the choice of control modes and mechanisms may also change, which in turn would motivate them to change the portfolio of control modes and mechanisms.

To explore the dynamics of control, we use the lens of episodes and encounters (Newman and Robey 1992). Processes can be viewed in terms of stable periods, called episodes, and transitional events between episodes, called encounters. Encounters can change the nature of the process from one episode to the next. In this paper, episodes are periods during which control mechanisms remain stable, while encounters are “punctuations” (Newman and Robey 1992) or “critical turning points” (Heiskanen et al. 1996), which produce significant changes in the way the project is controlled.

  encounters are defined as relatively brief events that punctuate a process and offer opportunities for an established equilibrium to change.   Episodes, by contrast, are longer time periods wherein the pattern set within an earlier encounter is played out (Newman and Robey 1992, p. 255).

Examples of encounters in the context of user-analyst relationships in ISD include selection of field sites, users’ sign-off on information requirements, appeal to authority, and proposal to develop the system jointly (Newman and Robey 1992). In the context of outsourced ISD, selection of the vendor, completion of requirements specifications, and the completed system being handed over to the client are illustrative encounters (Heiskanen et al. 1996).

Heiskanen et al. (1996) examined the dynamics of control in outsourced ISD projects in terms of episodes and encounters. They concluded that outsourced ISD projects exhibit mutual organizational learning as the client and the vendor adjust and negotiate over time. Moreover, they observed that early in the project, efficiency benefits from outsourcing are considerable, but later, as the project moves to use and maintenance, the “costs” of managing the external vendor might become too high. However, Heiskanen et al. did not examine the specific choice, and evolution over time, of the control modes and mechanisms discussed here.

A simple model of the likely evolution of controls may be constructed by drawing on prior literature on how portfolios of control are constructed. According to Kirsch (1997), controllers usually begin building a portfolio of controls by first considering the availability of existing control mechanisms for formal control modes. To the extent that the existing mechanisms are considered appropriate—based on the task characteristics, project-related knowledge, and role expectations—they are included in the portfolio of controls. If the existing mechanisms are inappropriate or inadequate, additional controls are added. These additional controls derive from “the definition, if necessary, of new mechanisms with which to implement formal control; and the addition of mechanisms with which to implement informal control” (Kirsch 1997, p. 234). Analogously, we might expect that at the beginning of an outsourced ISD project, clients will develop and implement a set of control mechanisms based on their assessment of the influencing factors discussed earlier. As argued above, we expect these initial portfolios to show greater reliance on outcome controls and client’s efforts to enhance vendor self-controls. Following each encounter, clients would assess the adequacy of these initial portfolios of control, and whether their initial perceptions of the influencing factors were accurate or needed to be revisited. Where the initial portfolios are perceived to be inadequate or inappropriate for revised perceptions of the influencing factors, additional control mechanisms would be incorporated to complement or substitute these mechanisms. ## Research Methods

Given the lack of prior research on the use of controls in remotely outsourced ISD projects, and our interest in studying the use of such controls within their organizational contexts, a qualitative approach was considered appropriate. To identify a variety of control mechanisms, and to achieve some understanding of their evolution over time, it was also important to examine them in a number of projects. Thus, a series of five case studies were conducted, with a detailed set of semi-structured interviews in each case, as described in greater detail below.

## Data Collection

The project started with the study of one case in detail. To select the case site, IS executives at six companies were interviewed. An IS project with three attributes was sought: (a) outsourced development, (b) recent completion or in progress, and (c) top management’s willingness to allow a detailed case study. C0, a Fortune 50 diversified service company,<sup>2</sup> satisfied these conditions. The senior IS executive who allowed the case study was first interviewed and asked to describe the ISD process and identify the participants. These individuals, and participants identified later, were interviewed. The informants were asked to describe the project, including the way in which it was managed. Client interviewees were also asked questions along the lines of “How did you track the progress of the project? How did you determine whether or not the vendor was performing well?” The interviews were done over a nine-month period when the system was being rolled out and the project was vivid in the interviewees’ minds. These factors, combined with the complexity of the system being developed, allowed us to collect an extensive amount of data at this case site, which significantly enhanced our understanding of the control of outsourced IS projects.

Following the first case study, we sought to further investigate the control of outsourced IS projects in additional cases. Senior IS executives at eight more organizations were contacted, with the goal of identifying IS projects with the three attributes stated above. Five of the organizations had projects satisfying these criteria. However, one case could not be completed due to the turnover of key executives. Thus, four projects (P1 to P4) were examined in the second phase. Based on the insights gained in the first case (P0), we were able to develop a focused interview protocol, enabling more efficient data collection in these cases. Because our focus is on the client perspective of control, all five projects were studied mainly through personal interviews with key participants at each client. Table 1 provides information on the interviews, including the interviewees’ titles.

At the beginning of each interview, the informant was asked to evaluate, on a five-point scale, the nature of the project in terms of complexity, innovativeness, and size. In each case, more than half of the interviewees exactly agreed on each characteristic, and no two interviewees differed in their assessment by more than one point on the five-point scale. To help the interviewees recall and organize their thoughts, we tried to lead them through each phase of the project. Informants were asked questions related to control mechanisms used during each stage, after providing them a brief explanation of what we meant by “control.” They were encouraged to illustrate general observations with specific comments and examples. They were frequently asked additional follow-up questions. Clarifications for conflicting answers were sought either during the same interview or in later interviews. All the interviews were tape-recorded, with notes taken when necessary, and then transcribed. Interviews were supplemented with organizational charts, annual reports, and internal documentation.

## Data Analysis

The interviews produced over 200 pages of transcripts and over 40 pages of field notes. We pursued two goals in analyzing this vast amount of data. We tried to use the rich insights available in each case. For any case, these insights can best be obtained thorough immersion in the transcripts for that case. In addition, we tried to compare across cases, which requires a more macro-view of each case. To simultaneously achieve these goals, we conducted the analysis in a systematic and comprehensive, but not rigid, fashion. Three broad steps were followed.

Step 1—Identifying Control Mechanisms. Both authors independently read the interview transcripts to identify the mechanisms enabling each mode of control. While reading the transcripts, comments were highlighted, which helped segment the data into relevant and meaningful pieces of text (Tesch 1990). Some of these pieces of text described a control mechanism in an interviewee’s words, while others represented the interviewee’s perceptions of the reasons a particular mechanism was used. Analytical comments, or “memos,” were also written on the margins adjacent to each piece of text. In these comments, each author noted the control mode(s) supported by each mechanism, and the reasons the mechanism was considered as supporting this control mode (or these control modes). In making these associations between control mechanisms and the four control modes, we adapted the criteria used by Kirsch (1997) to the context of outsourced projects. In addition, we were careful to be consistent with the theoretical conceptualizations of the various control modes presented earlier.

To identify the use of outcome control, we looked for the following kinds of mechanisms instituted by the client:

• mechanisms to explicitly specify desired outcomes that were assessed later; and

• mechanisms, including information systems, to evaluate the quality and timing of outputs delivered by the vendor.

The exercise of behavior control was established by identifying the following mechanisms:

• mechanisms by which the client explicitly specified rules, procedures, or processes for the vendor (the controllee) to follow;

• mechanisms to facilitate direct observation of the vendor’s behavior; and

• information systems designed to help the client monitor vendor behavior.

The exercise of clan control was assessed in terms of the following mechanisms:

• mechanisms to promote shared goals among client and vendor; and

• mechanisms to promote and assess adherence to shared beliefs and values.

In examining self-control, our focus is on the mechanisms clients use to promote vendors’ self-control.

Therefore, we looked for the following client-initiated mechanisms:

• mechanisms to encourage or motivate the vendor to exercise greater self-control; and

• mechanisms to help enhance the vendor’s ability to exercise control internally.

As discussed earlier, some control mechanisms (e.g., meetings and conference calls) could facilitate more than one control mode. Therefore, these mechanisms were classified as supporting multiple forms of control. This approach is consistent with Kirsch (1997, p. 228, Table 6). A control mechanism was coded as supporting multiple control modes when, based on the authors’ independent assessments of the transcripts: (a) One (or both) author(s) considered it as supporting multiple control modes or (b) the two authors identified the mechanism as supporting different modes and later agreed in their discussions that the mechanism supported both control modes.

Step 2—Identifying Influencing Factors and Encounters. We again read all the transcripts, focusing on comments related to the timing of the mechanisms used and factors affecting the choice of mechanisms. We looked for concerns expressed about too much or too little control, as well as comments about specific forms of control that were unavailable or that worked particularly well.

To identify encounters, we looked for significant events and interactions between a client and vendor. This was based on Newman and Robey (1992):

Encounters, as social phenomenon, require the two parties to interact, although this interaction does not need to be face to face. Encounters can occur in formal meetings, or they can be evidenced by written memoranda, or any other type of specific interaction (p. 255).

However, not all interactions between a client and a vendor are encounters. The identification of encounters in a sequence of events is usually based on the perceived importance of the events, with the most critical ones being called encounters. Newman and Robey (1992, p. 257) note:

While events have been designated as encounters and episodes, there is a degree of simplification and interpretation. For example, during periods we called episodes, there were undoubtedly contacts between analysts and users. However, those contacts labeled encounters were judged to be more critical to the history of the project.

Thus, encounters are critical events during the process that have the potential to significantly change the nature of the subsequent activities (Newman and Robey 1992, Heiskanen et al. 1996); although, in prior research, not all encounters actually resulted in a change. In this study, we began by identifying all potentially significant events and interactions between client and vendor, such as the delivery of a significant piece of software by the vendor, or an interaction between client and vendor to discuss redefining the functional specifications or project timelines. However, because our primary focus is on control mechanisms and five case studies are examined—as compared to two each by Newman and Robey (1992) and Heiskanen et al. (1996)—the desire for simplicity and focus led us to present and discuss in the paper only those encounters that produced a change in control mechanisms.

Step 3—Drawing Conclusions. We constructed a one-page summary of the key aspects of each case (including characteristics of the project, the client and the vendor, and the control mechanisms and their influencing factors) and a brief (four to five single-spaced pages) case description. These documents were later used to draw conclusions about the evolution of portfolios of control and the factors affecting them. Each case description included some quotes from the interviews.

The analytical process was necessarily somewhat interpretive. However, we tried to minimize the subjectivity by moving back and forth between the transcripts, case descriptions, and case summaries during the analysis. This was done using the systematic process, which benefited from several analytical devices described above—highlighting to segment data into pieces of text, memos identifying the nature of each piece of text, case summaries, and the links between case summaries and memos. Together, these devices enabled us to decontextualize the interviewee comments and then recontextualize them (Tesch 1990). That is, they helped take the interviewee comments out of their original context (i.e., an interview) and present them in a different context (i.e., the control mechanisms and their evolution over time). This allowed us to surface mechanisms and relationships that may have been missed from simply reading the interview transcripts. Moreover, the independent reading of all the transcripts by both authors, followed by the discussions between them, lowered the likelihood of missing a control mechanism or an encounter. Overall, this analytical process enabled us to keep our conclusions rooted in the participants’ perceptions, and also to simultaneously benefit from the “big picture” view provided by the case summaries and the details embedded in the transcripts.

## Results

We begin first with a brief description of the five cases. The initial portfolios of controls, the encounters and resulting portfolios of control, and the influencing factors, are examined later.

Project P0 involved the development of an interorganizational system, linking C0’s district offices to personal computers at its dealers. Considering the complex nature of this system and the fact that another large system was then being developed internally, the decision was made to outsource. Vendor V0 was selected due largely to its experience with developing systems using similar technology.

Initially, C0 specified the outputs needed from the system, but, driven by extreme time pressure and confidence in V0’s “ability to perform what we wanted,” did not ask V0 to produce a detailed internal design document. C0 specified timelines for software delivery and implemented weekly monitoring against them. In addition, C0’s own IS department would test all the software delivered by V0.

It soon became evident, however, that the system timelines were unattainable and C0 made some modifications to the requirements. However, the critical event was when V0 started delivering the software, and C0’s IS group discovered several problems. This raised concerns at C0, leading C0 to increase both the types and intensity of controls. Executives at C0 felt that some of the software was not even tested before being delivered. Therefore, C0 worked with V0 to redesign its testing and quality assurance processes. C0 executives believed that V0’s project manager was a junior executive, lacking project management skills. Therefore, C0 encouraged V0 to hire a new senior executive to manage the project and actively assisted in the process. Realizing the problems caused by imprecision of the initial requirements definitions, C0 much more precisely specified modifications. C0 asked V0 to move its programmers to C0’s head-office. However, the programmers were much less effective away from their normal workplace and went back after three weeks. Instead, C0 periodically sent its own people to V0’s facilities. It also started closely monitoring V0 through daily conference calls, which continued for almost two years. C0 later implemented the system at dealers nationwide over a one and a half year period. Eventually, the system was widely acknowledged as a big success.

Project P1 was large, involving significant enhancements to a customer service system at C1, a Fortune 500 financial services company. C1 had internally done detailed requirements analysis before contacting V1. Thus, the initial contract included detailed requirements specifications, right down to the subcomponent level, and almost down to the coding level. C1 also prepared a detailed project plan with milestones down to the subcomponent level and monitored progress through conference calls and e-mails. The conference calls were more frequent initially but occured only once or twice each week later. C1 personnel tested all the software delivered by V1, and required it to place a liaison at C1. C1 conducted walk-throughs on programs that were still under development at V1. Finally, at the start of the project, C1 sent a senior manager to assist V1 with the selection of personnel who were going to work on the system by reviewing the resumes of V1 personnel. C1 was satisfied with the quality and timing of the deliverables produced by V1 and did not impose additional controls later in the project.

Project P2 involved the development of an executive information system at C2, a Fortune 500 company and one of the five largest software development firms in the United States. The cost of the project included \$15,000, a license fee per user, and “future considerations.” The vendor, V2, which is based in Colombia, developed the user interface, while C2 worked on providing the necessary data feeds. The primary motivation to outsource the project was the lack of internal manpower. V2 was selected due to its expertise in graphic user interfaces, and because it was recommended by C2’s Colombian office.

V2 regarded the project as strategically important as it would be able to mention C2 as a reference and have access to its latest technologies. C2, in turn, felt the resulting system would be a showcase for its technology. Recognizing this mutually strategic relationship, C2 and V2 began the project without a formal contract and with only the broad outlines of the system specified.

C2’s project manager worked with V2’s on-site representatives during requirements analysis, following which V2 personnel went back to Colombia and developed the software. C2 indicated to V2 that new versions of the software should be downloaded to C2 every fortnight and that C2 personnel would test the received software. Once the system was developed, V2 personnel returned to C2 and installed it. During the testing by C2, a developer from V2 was at C2. C2 was satisfied with the effort by V2 and did not feel the need to add or significantly change its control portfolio during the project.

Project P3 involved the development of a legislature tracking system for C3, a large county in the Southeast United States. C3 needed to implement the system faster than they could build it in-house and so it decided to buy the package from V3, which was the only vendor with a package in the arena. V3 was then asked to customize the package to meet C3’s needs. Initially, the controllers were not members of C3’s IS department, but the users themselves. C3 divided the project into five phases and created a timeline for each phase; although the initial time estimates included in the contract were largely proposed by V3. No specific recourse was specified for missing the timelines, so that they seemed to be more in the nature of guidelines. Functional requirements were only broadly specified. A specific system-performance requirement that C3 did impose was in terms of the system’s speed.

Soon after the analysis, V3 concluded that it had underestimated the time requirements for the subsequent phases. At this point, C3’s senior management, concerned by the lack of satisfactory progress, decided to involve its IS department in the project. Thus, the “controller” changed from C3’s users to members of C3’s IS group. The IS group decided to impose a number of additional controls on the project. First, while V3 personnel were developing the software off-site, the IS team at C3 did a fresh detailed requirements analysis with significant revisions. Second, they required the vendor to send a new version of the software each week, although as indicated by one of C3’s senior analysts, this did not always happen. C3’s IS group also started more stringent testing on the software delivered by V3. In addition, right before the first implementation of the system, C3 insisted that the vendor team come to the client site and work with C3’s staff. Finally, C3 started making considerable use of conference calls (two or three per week) with the vendor to monitor progress.

Project P4 involved the development of a \$0.5 million executive information system for C4, one of the largest food products firms in the United States. The project was based on a fixed-price contract with V4. The choice to outsource was based on a combination of an “extremely compressed time frame” and lack of internal people to develop the system within this time frame. V4 was selected for its “special skills and experience in developing graphical information systems.” C4 initially gave V4 a small, relatively loosely worded contract to develop the pilot for the larger system, both to test the vendor’s capability and also to market the system internally within C4. V4 successfully developed the pilot and was rewarded with the larger contract. At this time, C4 hired a Spanishspeaking project manager, and left the project management almost entirely to him.

The larger contract, while somewhat more finely worded, was still rather loose. It included a formal specification of the required system functionality, although some aspects were specified at a broad level. A formal project plan with timelines was created and progress relative to it was monitored weekly by C4’s project manager, and monthly by C4’s IS director, who reviewed progress before paying the vendor’s monthly bill. In addition, the IS VP monitored the project status on an ad hoc basis. C4’s IS team developed detailed plans and checklists to test all the software before distributing it to users. C4 also mandated that for every new release V4 send its own personnel to C4 to install the software. Finally, C4 required that at the beginning of the project the vendor team visit it to understand its culture.

As V4 started delivering the software, almost none of it passed C4’s testing, making C4’s project manager concerned about the quality of software testing at V4. He suspected that V4 may be implicitly relying on C4’s extensive testing to locate defects in the software. To address this problem, C4’s IS director called V4’s president. C4 then helped V4 to design a better software-testing procedure and strongly encouraged V4 to use it before delivering software to C4.

## Initial Portfolios of Controls

Outcome Control. Consistent with prior research on internal projects, firms controlled the outsourced projects with a portfolio of controls (Table 2). As expected, outcome controls dominated the initial controls, especially in four cases (P0, P1, P3, P4). Two types of mechanisms enabled outcome control. The first focused on specification of the desired outputs. One such mechanism, used by all clients, was to identify the functionality needed, but the precision with which the requirements were stated varied considerably. C1 specified the functionality in much greater detail (almost down to the code level) than C0, C2, and C4, which only broadly defined the project scope, but left it to the vendor to determine the more detailed design. Thus, at C4:

There were some important features that were explicitly defined in the contract and there were other areas that were generally required   but we were not exactly sure how it would work or look.

Another form of outcome control involved the scheduling of outputs to be delivered by the vendor. Mechanisms to do this, seen in all the cases, included project plans and timelines specifying interim and final milestones. However, these mechanisms also varied considerably in their detail. For example, C1 prepared timelines and project plans with due dates right down to each submodule, partly facilitated by the fact that they had specified their requirements with greater precision. In contrast, C3 let the vendor define the time estimates for each project phase and also did not make these time estimates enforceable. This was a source of some regret to C3 later; according to one C3 executive, “one of the things that was missing was there was no recourse for the county to fall back should the vendor miss the deadline.” C3 also required the vendor to deliver a new version of the software every week though this did not always happen, suggesting lax enforcement.

The second type of outcome control mechanism focused on the evaluation of outputs delivered by the vendor. Here, we observed two examples. The first involved the client’s validation of the quality of outputs delivered by the vendor. Specifically, the client’s IS department tested all the software delivered by the vendor in all five cases. Behavior Control. There were a limited number of behavior controls implemented at the beginning of projects. Two kinds of behavior control mechanisms were identified. The first, involving a client’s attempt to specify desired vendor behavior, was observed in only one case. C1 prescribed a specific development methodology in its project plan. The second kind of behavior control mechanism aimed to facilitate monitoring of vendor behavior, through either direct observation or appropriately designed information systems, such as periodic reports (P4) and conference calls (P1). One mechanism used to increase direct observability of vendor behavior, which was also observed by Kirsch (1997), was the use of walkthroughs for programs still under development (P1). Another observation mechanism used was colocation. Specifically, C4 built the following requirement for vendor presence on client premises into the initial contract.

Senior System Analyst: The agreement was that every time they had a deliverable, that project leader or one of their people would come up here and install it.

Self-Control. As stated above, our focus here was on mechanisms used by the client to assist the vendor’s self-control efforts. We observed one example of this at the start of a project. Recognizing that the quality of self-control is at least partially dependent on the quality of the people involved in the project, C1 sent a senior manager to help V1 with the process of examining the resumes of the vendor’s programmers who would work on the project.

Clan Control. Only one project (P2) seemed to rely largely on clan control from the beginning. C2 and V2 structured their contract so that each would be able to use the resulting system to showcase their respective technologies. This changed the typical buyer-vendor relationship into a clan-like situation where both parties strongly believed in, and strived to attain, a common goal of making the system work. This allowed C2 to leave many details unspecified, confident that V2 would adapt to later changes. This is typical in clan-control situations where specific goals are usually not known at the start of an activity, but rather evolve over time (Ouchi 1979, Kirsch 1997).

Regional Manager (C2): No, there was no contract   it is a system that would be developed in a very flexible way   we would be able to change some features and add others   the vendor also expected that to happen and they reacted positively in that way, they were flexible so that worked out good.

C4 also tried to introduce clan control by requiring that at the beginning of P4 the vendor team visit it to understand its culture and norms. However, we have no evidence to suggest that this had a significant effect on the remainder of the project.

## Encounters and Resulting Portfolios

We observed a significant change in the portfolio of controls in three of the five cases. In each of these cases, the change resulted from the client’s negative evaluation of vendor performance. In two cases (P0, P4), the encounter that led to this evaluation was the first receipt of software from the vendor. In P0, the first delivery of software by V0 was behind schedule and also revealed problems with the quality of the software. In P4, the first software delivered by V4 failed to pass the testing done by C4. In the third case (P3), the encounter was a perception of inadequate progress by the vendor, which led to a change in the controller. Earlier in the project, the client’s IS group had not been involved. Midway through the project, the IS group was assigned the responsibility of managing the project.

In the other two cases (P1, P2), there was no encounter producing any significant change in the control portfolios (Table 2).<sup>3</sup> C2 felt comfortable relying primarily on clan control and was satisfied with the outputs produced by V2. C1, which had imposed fairly stringent initial controls, was also satisfied with V1’s progress, both with respect to the quality and the timing of software delivered by V1, and did not feel the need to significantly modify the portfolio of controls. In fact, the conference calls used by C1 were more frequent initially but only once or twice each week later, apparently due to the smooth progress. However, C1’s managers felt V1 had done an inadequate job of implementing self-control in terms of assigning project personnel, and had assigned several inexperienced individuals. C1 therefore reinforced to V1 the importance of better self-control in this arena.

In the three cases where the clients perceived performance problems by the vendors, they imposed several additional controls following the “encounters.” At C3, there was a change in the controller, with the IS group being assigned the task of managing the project, and it imposed more detailed outcome controls. It appointed its own team to conduct an independent requirements analysis, which led to renegotiation on deliverables and deadlines. C3’s IS staff started testing the performance of the system more stringently. Some behavior controls were also added, primarily to increase observability of vendor behavior. Frustrated with the time it took to obtain a response from V3, C3’s IS group insisted that V3 bring its team to C3’s site and work with its staff.

Manager (C3): We knew that unless they were here working with us, we wouldn’t be able to accomplish it. We put our staff working on it with them.

In P0, the problems with the first delivery of software by V0 led to several additional controls.

IS Project Manager (C0): It was at that point we realized we were in trouble and we started getting into our daily meetings and tight control over everything that was happening and working with them, very closely monitoring them.

First, realizing that the software was inadequately tested, executives at C0 added mechanisms to enhance self-control exercised by the vendor. They worked with V0 to redesign its testing and quality assurance processes.

We have been working with them to improve their quality assurance    . We have shown them how to do testing   they are getting a little better.

Recognizing that two reasons for the inadequate selfcontrol by V0 were poor project leadership and lack of top-management involvement, C0 helped V0 to hire a new project manager and encouraged greater involvement and oversight by V0’s top managers, including its CEO and IS department head.

IS Project Manager (C0):   the head of the IS department who wasn’t involved as he should have been.   the original project manager was moved out and another person was brought in, who was a more mature project manager. He was able to pull together the departments at V0.

C0’s executives also introduced behavior control and asked V0 to move its programmers to C0’s headoffice, to increase observability of vendor behavior. When V0’s programmers went back after three weeks, C0 sought to achieve this goal by sending its own people and project managers to V0.

It is so difficult to ascertain exactly where programmers are in the development if you are not there and you have to be watching them   my people spent more and more time up there working hand in hand with the programmers.

C0 also sought to monitor vendor behavior (behavior control) more closely through information systems— daily conference calls and a monthly meeting attended by virtually every participant from both sides, including top management at C0 and programmers and analysts from V0. Interestingly, an unanticipated outcome of these frequent interactions was a strong sense of shared values and goals, and shared responsibility for project success. Thus, what began as a mechanism for behavior control evolved over time into a form of clan control:

V0’s Project Manager: Everybody wanted it to work and wanted it be successful   people cared and continue to care about this project    .

C0’s IS Project Leader: Everyone was pushing real hard to make it work. We all had to make it work, we had skin in the game. We made it successful.

In P4, as an initial outcome control, C4 had developed exhaustive test plans for its own testing of the vendor-developed software. The elaborateness of this testing plan produced an interesting effect. The vendor came to rely almost completely on C4 to do the detailed testing and became slack in its own testing. Consequently, none of the software would pass the test the first time around. To overcome this problem, C4’s IS director called V4’s president to encourage better self-control (especially in terms of testing)

and helped V4 develop more stringent internal testing procedures. Thus, excellent outcome control by the client led to a decline in vendor self-control—in response, the client encouraged and assisted the vendor to exercise greater self-control. C4’s IS director indicated that he would be more formal with IS vendors in the future, both in initially specifying project scope and in monitoring progress:

When we outsource   there has to be a tremendous amount of importance placed on communications   and keeping that up to find out what is going on   that kind of tracks what is happening, what’s been decided, what’s been promised   there definitely needs to be a lot of diligence    .

## Influences on the Initial Portfolio

The driving forces behind the initial portfolio of controls constructed by the clients, especially for C0, C3, and C4, seemed to be the careful screening process each went through in selecting the vendor, the vendor’s reputation, and the fact that all but one of them had no prior experience with the vendor.

These factors seemed to create among the clients a role expectation that the vendors should be able to exercise considerable self-control. Consequently, several clients (especially C0, C3, and C4) began the project only with some broad outcome controls and extensive reliance on self-controls. The only client that had prior experience with the vendor was C1—a different division of C1 had used the vendor previously. This division had shaped C1’s expectation that relying on extensive self-control was not optimal. As a result, C1 implemented an extensive set of controls at the start of the project. An interviewee at C1 remarked:

We had discussions with another area   their comments were that we had to make sure that we laid all the specifications out as clearly as possible which led us to doing the detail design document   and this also led to the quality checks that we were doing by doing some code walk-through    .

The vendor selection process also created in the clients a high level of confidence in the project-related knowledge and skills of the vendor and the vendor’s project leader. Thus, consistent with prior research, the clients’ (controllers’) perception of a knowledgeable vendor (controllee) led to a heavy reliance on outcome and self-controls.

Among the task characteristics identified in prior research and discussed above, a common perception expressed by all the clients was that outsourced projects involve low behavior observability, in particular the ability to monitor behavior through direct observation, especially when the vendor is remotely located. The following quotes are illustrative:

Project Manager (C0): Our biggest problems came with making sure that we were monitoring the real progress    . It is so difficult even with a project like that to ascertain exactly where programmers are in the development if you are not there and you are not watching them.

Regional Manager (C2): When you contract out, you don’t always know how the time is being invested    .

Prior research suggests that perceptions of low behavior observability would be associated with a low level of behavior control by clients. This was true in the cases studied, particularly early in the project.

However, two other factors that prior research has identified as significant influences on the choice of controls did not have much effect on the initial portfolios put together by the clients. The first is project size (generally included as a relevant task characteristic), with greater use of formal controls predicted in larger projects. We found only partial support for this. In P2, the system was small, simple, and not mission critical, and C2 did not impose an elaborate set of formal controls on the project. In fact, subsequent to P2, C2 outsourced a new project to V2. In this project, which was larger and much more complex, C2 was considerably more formal in specifying requirements and controls.<sup>4</sup> In both P3 and P5, the systems were large and critical to the future of the client. However in each case, our original expectation that this would lead to greater use of formal controls by the client did not find support. Instead, in each case, the time pressure to get the system completed by a certain date resulted in a lax statement of functional specifications, which later caused significant problems, including rework and delay in the completion of the system. These results suggest that, in the choice of controls for outsourced projects, project size may be less influential than some of the other influencing factors.

Prior research has also found the controller’s project-related knowledge to facilitate behavior control. We had posited that this would not be true for outsourced projects. Our findings supported this expectation. The controllers in most of our cases were IS personnel—highly knowledgeable about the project and ISD in general. Yet, we saw limited use of behavior control, at least initially. During the initial stages of most projects, the effect of controllers’ project-related knowledge seems to have been overshadowed by the combination of role expectations (based on confidence in the vendors’ abilities) and, in some cases, time pressure. An exception to this was P3, as we discuss below.

## Encounters and Changes in Influencing Factors

While the initial influencing factors were based largely on the vendor-selection process, as we expected and as indicated by prior research on interorganizational alliances, the subsequent evolution of these factors was shaped by the clients’ experiences during the projects. Specifically, in the three cases with a significant change in controls, the clients observed significant performance problems, which, in turn, caused them to reassess their perceptions of the influencing factors.

One change was that clients revised their role expectations. Evidence of quality problems and indications of inadequate testing challenged the clients’ a priori beliefs about the vendors’ ability to exercise selfcontrol. Consequently, some of the clients responded by implementing additional formal control mechanisms and initiating efforts to assist the vendors in exercising better self-control, for instance, by helping them design better internal testing procedures.

At C3, role expectations may also have played a part in another manner. When the controller was changed from the user group to the IS group, the latter may have perceived that in its new role, it was expected to impose stringent controls on the vendor (much more so than the prior controller, the user group). It is possible that this may have partly driven their decisions to implement such controls.

The early performance problems also reduced clients’ confidence in the vendors’ project-related knowledge and skills, particularly with respect to the vendors’ ability to conduct requirements analysis. While the vendors were all experienced software developers, none of them had any knowledge (before the contract) of the client’s operations (except V1, which had been used by a different division of C1). Consequently, the vendors possessed the technical knowledge needed to develop the software, but not the firm- and context-specific knowledge needed for effective requirements analysis. Initially, our clients failed to make this distinction and asked the vendor to do the detailed design without introducing adequate controls. This led to many of the performance problems identified earlier.

IS Group Director (Development) (C0): The fact that they really didn’t have any insight into our business turned out to be a much larger problem than what we expected   things that were so obvious to us were not to them    . There were a hundred items that collectively added up to a lot of delay and a lot of rework.

With experience, however, the clients became aware that they should have been more precise, and more involved, in the initial determination and specification of the requirements. For instance, at C3, the new controller essentially redid the specifications. Similarly, an executive at C0 remarked:

One of our blunders was in the design phase   we didn’t detail enough so when (V0) eventually got the bid it wasn’t crystal clear what we wanted. We found ourselves a year later asking what we meant about things    . So, my advice to anybody doing this would be to be detailed in your requirements.

In fact, after C0 realized the problems with the lack of precision in the initial requirements definition, it changed its practice to be much more precise in putting together “specifications for (V0) saying this is exactly what we want done.”

The performance problems also made the clients realize the need to invest significant effort in overcoming the low behavior observability. In the later stages of some projects, we saw clients devising ways to increase their ability to monitor vendor behavior, both through appropriately designed information systems and mechanisms intended to facilitate direct observation, either by requiring that vendor staff work on client premises or by sending client personnel to the vendor’s location.

Finally, in one case (P3), the controller’s project-related knowledge and skills became a significant issue. A perceived lack of progress led to a change in controller from the client’s users to a senior executive at the client’s IS department. This may have been due to the perception that stricter controls were needed and that the client’s users lacked the technical and projectrelated knowledge and ability to implement such controls. In fact, the new controller was able and willing to implement stricter controls, including helping V3 redesign its self-controls, as discussed above.

## Discussion

This paper sought to examine and extend findings from research on the control of internal ISD projects to outsourced projects. The results show that many findings from the control of internal projects also apply to outsourced projects. For instance, as with internal ISD projects, outsourced projects are controlled not with a single control but with a portfolio of controls (Kirsch 1996, 1997). But the results also reveal some interesting differences and the need to adapt conceptualizations of some control modes to the context of outsourcing, where the controller and the controllee are in different firms.

## Portfolios of Control

Outcome control in outsourced projects resembles that in internal ISD projects. In both cases, two types of mechanisms are used—those that specify desired outputs and those that monitor progress against the specifications. However, our data indicates the importance of considering not just the mechanisms of outcome control, but also the precision with which the desired outputs (functional specifications and/or timelines) are specified, and the rigor with which the controls are enforced. In several instances, we observed outcome control mechanisms that were imprecise (e.g., in P0) or inadequately enforced (e.g., in P3) and, hence, ineffective.

Behavior control has traditionally been defined as including the specification of desired controllee behavior and the monitoring of controllee behavior. In the outsourced projects we studied, however, mechanisms specifying desired behavior were used in just one case (P1). This raises the question: Can monitoring behavior that is not explicitly prescribed be considered as behavior control? We suggest the answer should be in the affirmative. The rationale for prescribing specific behaviors is knowledge-based; the controller must believe that it knows better than the controllee the optimal process to follow in the specific context of the project. In addition, specifying desired behavior is suggested only when there exists a well defined and validated<sup>5</sup> transformation process, e.g., in facilities management. The rationale for monitoring controllee behavior, in contrast, is based on agency theory. Thus, a controller may not feel the need to prescribe behavior, either because it does not believe that its knowledge is superior to the controllee’s, there is an implicit agreement on desired behavior, or there is no validated transformation process. However, it may want to monitor the controllee’s behavior to increase the probability that it acts in the controller’s best interests.

This seems consistent with our observations. As we argued earlier, the controllers (usually IS professionals) had carefully selected vendors they considered competent in systems development and who, therefore, did not need to be told the best way to develop systems. Or they may have felt that there was an implicit common understanding among them of the appropriate behaviors. Alternatively, the controllers may have felt that while there are many well-defined systems methodologies, there is no one methodology that has been validated as always being optimal. Thus, they did not feel the need to dictate or prescribe specific behaviors. Yet, they felt that monitoring behavior would motivate the vendors to act in their best interests. We believe this idea— exercising behavior control solely through monitoring without prescribing specific behaviors—deserves further investigation in other contexts.

Another dimension, common to both outcome and behavior controls in prior literature but not seen in this study, is the association of specific rewards with controls. Kirsch, for instance, observed in the context of behavior controls that it is not merely the act of monitoring, but the associated rewards that define control. However, we did not generally observe clients directly linking explicit rewards or sanctions to specific controls. One exception was in P4, where C4 paid V4’s monthly bill only after verifying adequate progress. Also in P4, V4 was rewarded with the larger contract only after successfully developing the pilot. The rewards were often implicit or long term (for instance, a good reputation, or the prospect of repeat business). As a result, sometimes the controls were not effective—a prominent example was in P3 where the client was frustrated with the lack of recourse when the vendor missed established timelines. One reason we did not observe many instances of explicit rewards tied to controls may simply be that we did not specifically ask about rewards during the interviews. However, another reason may be that clients often lack direct authority over vendor personnel and may find it hard to enforce specific rewards. Or, clients may perceive, perhaps incorrectly in some cases, that implicit reward structures are adequate.

Self-control has previously been conceptualized at the level of individuals within the same organization. In this study, our focus was on situations where the controller and controllee are individuals in different organizations, i.e., individuals at the client and vendor in an outsourced ISD project. Although we did not directly study self-control by the vendor, we found that, just as in internal ISD projects (Kirsch et al. 2002), there are several mechanisms the controller may use to encourage and enable better self-control by the controllee. For example, the client can help the vendor design a better testing procedure or encourage greater involvement in and oversight of the project by the vendor’s senior management. In fact, this seemed to be a common response by the clients when faced with performance problems.

Finally, we adapted the traditional definition of clan control to the context of outsourced projects, proposing that clan control seems to be operating when the client and vendor share a common goal (based, for instance, on the nature of the contract) or a sense of shared values, beliefs, and goals (possibly achieved through frequent interactions). In general, as compared to internal projects, clan control is likely to be more difficult in outsourced projects, partly due to the difficulty of creating a shared goal and shared beliefs (Lacity 2002). In fact, of the two cases involving clan control, one (P2) had the somewhat fortuitous situation of the client and vendor both being interested in using the system to showcase their respective technologies. In the other case, P0, the frequent interactions that ultimately led to shared beliefs and goals were initially conceived by C0’s IS personnel to support behavior control, but evolved over time, almost accidentally, to facilitate clan control as well.

## Influencing Factors

The cases also supported some prior findings from internal projects on the factors affecting control in outsourced projects. First, there seemed to be a general perception among clients that monitoring vendor behavior is tougher in outsourced projects. This was at least partly responsible for limited use of behavior controls, particularly in the early stages of projects. In several projects, when clients subsequently felt the need for greater behavior control, they expended considerable effort to increase their ability to monitor vendor behavior, either through direct observation (usually through colocation) or through information systems (e.g., conference calls and periodic progress reports).

Consistent with prior research, client perceptions of high controllee knowledge, and the role expectation that competent vendors should be able to manage their own processes, led to a greater reliance on outcome and self-controls, especially early in the projects. However, our cases did point to the importance of distinguishing between a vendor’s general knowledge of software development and its knowledge of a specific client’s business. In at least two cases (P0 and P3), the vendor did not have a good understanding of the client’s business but was nevertheless asked to design the system with minimal involvement and control by the client’s IS department. In each case, this decision was driven by the vendor’s expertise in software development, especially for the kind of system to be developed. In both cases, however, the vendors were unable to do an effective job of design, which later caused multiple problems. Both clients acknowledged that they should have been more involved in system design and more precise in specifying functional requirements to the vendors. However, our data does not allow us to assess whether more stringent controls during the initial stages would have produced better design, or if the client’s IS department should perform the design itself, especially when the vendor is new to the client. This issue needs to be empirically tested in other situations.

Our results only partially support the influence of project size on the choice of controls and do not support prior findings that knowledgeable controllers would exert greater behavior control (although the experience at project P3 does demonstrate that if tight behavior controls are necessary, project control should lie with a knowledgeable controller—in this case, the client’s IS department and not the users). Even though the controllers were knowledgeable IS personnel at most of the clients, they did not implement elaborate behavior controls, or tight outcome controls, at the start of the projects.

Our results suggest that there may be a hierarchy of influences in choosing a set of controls. In the outsourcing context, the three most important influencing factors at the start of the project seemed to be the client’s perception of the controllee’s knowledge, the consequent role expectations, and perceptions of difficulty in monitoring vendor behavior. These influencing factors seemed to have overcome the potential influence of the controller’s knowledge and project size. Later in the project, the most important influencing factor would appear to have been vendor performance in the early part of the project. We believe that this subject too deserves further investigation.

## Dynamics of Control

Our examination of the dynamics of control goes beyond previous studies, which have typically taken a static snapshot of the control of ISD projects. Our results suggest a remarkably consistent message, which conforms to the simple model presented in the theory development section for the evolution of controls. Clients, in general, started with an initial portfolio of controls based on the three influencing factors, considered in hierarchical order of importance as discussed above. In all cases where the initial controls were lax (except for the unusual situation of clan control with P2), we saw that there were initial performance problems, which led to the imposition of additional controls by the clients (both formal and informal). It is interesting to note that the client that instituted the tightest controls at the start (C1), with very detailed functional specifications and timelines, had the least number of problems in terms of needing to change control strategies midway through the project. However, we need to be careful in inferring that it is always best to create detailed, up-front specifications. It is possible that for highly uncertain or unstructured tasks the specifications need to be allowed to evolve over the course of the project. This issue deserves further investigation.

Our conclusions regarding the dynamics of control parallel Kirsch’s (1997) discussion of the way in which portfolios of control are constructed. Kirsch noted that controllers examine the adequacy and appropriateness of existing control mechanisms, based on their assessment of the influencing factors, and add or change controls as necessary. Our results add a longitudinal dimension to the construction of control portfolios. Thus, in our study, controllers constructed the initial portfolios of control based on their initial assessments of the influencing factors. One difference between our findings and that of Kirsch may be that we probably saw a greater reliance on informal (especially self) controls in the initial portfolios—This may, of course, be explained by the clients’ perceptions of the influencing factors. Over time, as they gained experience with the vendors, clients were sometimes forced to modify their perceptions of the influencing factors and incorporate additional or modified controls later in the projects.

## Implications and Limitations

The paper has significant implications for practice. Most importantly, it clearly points to the need for client managers to pay adequate attention to the control of outsourced IS projects, right from the start. Numerous informants acknowledged that it is a mistake to rely extensively on vendor self-control. Instead, client managers should guard against underestimating the agency problem even if they feel confident of the vendor’s abilities. It is important to be precise in specifying functional requirements, to monitor progress closely through timelines and progress reports, and to impose a requirement to deliver frequent and regular software updates. As one client remarked, it is not optimal to let the vendors go for months between deliverables. C3’s IS manager expressed regret that the contract was not more stringent, with no penalty clauses for the vendor’s failure to meet deadlines. The project manager at C4, when asked what he may do differently next time, expressed the need for more precise, upfront specification of functional requirements. In addition, clients should take more explicit actions to encourage and enable vendor self-control early in the project by examining, and possibly helping to redesign, internal vendor processes.

To implement behavior controls, client managers may want to consider, early in the project, establishing appropriate mechanisms to observe vendor behavior, such as periodic meetings and conference calls. When it is appropriate and efficient, colocation may be used to facilitate direct observation of vendor behavior. If possible, clients can pursue clan control by structuring contracts to create shared goals, or by encouraging frequent interaction to promote shared beliefs and values.

The above implications for practice must be viewed in light of some limitations of the paper. First, consistent with its theory-building orientation, the paper is based on five case studies, but this prevented us from statistically testing expected relationships between control modes and their influencing factors. The cases also varied considerably in terms of the size and nature of the systems. This makes it difficult to compare across them, although it also means that the findings are based on a diverse set of projects. The paper’s insights into the portfolios, influencing factors, and dynamics of control need to be investigated further through additional case studies, or through questionnaires completed at multiple points during projects.

Second, four of the cases were studied retrospectively, shortly after the systems had been implemented, although one case (P0) was studied while the system was being developed. We tried to address the potential limitation of relying on retrospective interviews through the use of multiple interviewees. More studies of projects still under development would add to our understanding.

Third, this paper’s focus was primarily on the client perspective—Thus, we have limited understanding of the self-control mechanisms used internally by vendors. Kirsch (1996, 1997) argued that control of internal IS projects is exerted by both users and IS managers. Similarly, the ultimate success of an outsourced project will depend on controls implemented by both the client and the vendor. This paper provided insights into the client’s perspective of controls. In a subsequent study, it would be interesting to study internal controls by the vendor, as well as the interaction between the vendor’s internal controls and the controls implemented by the client.

Finally, as mentioned earlier, this study did not explicitly focus on rewards. Consequently, no direct questions were asked about rewards during the interviews and consequently some explicit or implicit rewards may have been missed. An important issue for future research would be to examine the role of rewards and sanctions in the effectiveness of controls.

In conclusion, two other opportunities for future research emerge from this study. The paper has identified some differences between control of outsourced and internal ISD projects. Case studies of internal and outsourced projects involving the same client would help to further understand differences in the way these two types of projects are controlled. Finally, this paper has focused on one kind of IS outsourcing, i.e., outsourced ISD projects, but further research is needed to examine control in other IS outsourcing situations (e.g., outsourcing of IS infrastructure management).
