## Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to consider a possible limitation in the development of the consensus on appropriation (COA) scale, published in a recent issue of Information Systems Research (Salisbury et al. 2002). We believe that in the development of the COA scale a possible internal-structure limitation was not fully addressed during the principal components analysis. This paper will explain these concerns and provide possible explanations for the results. We would like to emphasize that our comments are not intended to disparage the scale’s usefulness, but rather to encourage further investigation to better understand and measure COA. In addition, we hope the issues addressed in this research note will benefit future research endeavors in developing measurement scales.

## Consensus on Appropriation Scale

Salisbury et al. (2002) developed an instrument to measure COA, which involves agreement in a group as to the use of advanced information technologies. In developing the instrument they followed a rigorous process, including a definition of the construct based on prior research (Poole and DeSanctis 1990, 1992) and face validation of the chosen questions by both experts in the field and laypeople. The items were analyzed with two separate sets of data. The first used the initial COA items to conduct a principal component analysis (PCA) with a varimax rotation. The second used the revised COA items to perform a singlefactor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and a structural equation model (SEM). The final five-item scale showed strong reliability, as well as convergent and discriminant validity. However, we see an improvement opportunity in the internal-structure analysis.

With a carefully defined construct and strong face validity, there should be reasonable assurance that the measurement instrument will load as a unidimensional measure in a PCA. In the case of Salisbury et al. (2002), no reliability estimates were reported for the initial 10 items, and the initial principal components analysis found two factors explaining a considerable amount of variability (eigenvalues of 3.99 and 1.50, respectively—see Table 1).

Looking at the results of the PCA (see Table 1), COAs 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, and 10 all load highly on Factor 1; COAs 3, 4, and 9 all load highly on Factor 2; and COA 7 loads highly on neither. From examination of the items in Table 2, it is apparent that the items loading on Factor 1 focus on group consensus, while the items loading on Factor 2 focus on group nonconsensus. However, there was little explanation or discussion about what may have been the cause of the second factor; in addition, the second factor was not fully addressed in the second data set because of the application of a single-factor CFA. We believe the presence of the second factor warrants further investigation and may indicate that the construct can be more fully measured.

It is conceivable that COA and nonconsensus on appropriation are separate but linked dimensions, rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. This is not unprecedented when dealing with broad constructs. For example, in some cases negatively worded trust items have loaded separately onto a distrust factor (Wrightsman 1991). This has led some researchers to conceptualize trust and distrust as two separate but linked dimensions (for more on trust and distrust see Lewicki et al. 1998). If this is the case, the construct of COA may require the combination of two scales (consensus and nonconsensus) to be fully measured.

A second explanation for the results could be a response bias. The existence of a response bias would suggest that participants respond on the basis of something other than the construct of interest. One type of response bias that is seemingly consistent with the PCA results is a bias due to framing effects (Tversky and Kahneman 1981). The framing effects literature suggests that people may respond to questions with equivalent information differently because of the way the question is worded (Kuhberger 1998, Levin et al. 1998). Kuhberger (1995) delineated what is commonly called a “framing effect” into a problem domain and a problem frame. The problem domain is the outcome of interest, in this case agreement or disagreement. The problem frame is the overall meaning of the statement or question; it can be positive or negative. A positive frame indicates that the overall meaning of the item refers to group consensus, while a negative frame indicates group nonconsensus. Table 2 delineates the problem domains and problem frames for each item on the COA scale.

A similar pattern can be seen in Table 2 when comparing COA 3 and 9<sup>2</sup> versus COA 6. Lastly, COA 7 presents ambiguity in the problem domain. One might interpret “debate” as a form of agreement (e.g., constructive debate—a healthy exchange of ideas) or a form of disagreement (e.g., hostile debate—mean spirited confrontations). Depending on how the domain is interpreted, the frame may be seen as positive or negative. Therefore, it is understandable that COA 7 loaded highly on neither factor during the initial PCA (see Table 1).<sup>3</sup>

The problem domains and frames indicate that the initial loadings of the COA items may be the result of a response bias. The positively framed items— COAs 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, and 10—all loaded highly on the consensus factor, while the negatively framed items— COA items 3, 4, and 9—all loaded highly on the nonconsensus factor, and COA 7 loaded highly on neither. A response bias would suggest that while the final scale displayed convergent and discriminant validity, it may be possible to more clearly tap into the construct of COA.

## Recommendations

We would like to offer some recommendations for the continued analysis of the COA scale and construct. Our first recommendation is to perform a principal components analysis on the second wave of data; the items in the second wave had word revisions and this could have changed the factor structure of the scale. If this PCA confirms the initial twofactor results, we recommend retesting the nomological validity with the inclusion of a nonconsensus factor, measured with COAs 3, 4, and 9. If the nonconsensus factor displays convergent and discriminant validity and improves the fit indices, then multiple dimensions of COA are suggested. To broaden the analysis of COA, a third wave of data could be collected on the 10 revised items. The instructions would require subjects to justify their responses. The framing literature consistently suggests that response justification reduces or eliminates any framing bias (Davis and Bobko 1986, Fagley and Miller 1987, Miller and Fagley 1991). Finally, to broaden the analysis to item selection, a card-sorting technique similar to Moore and Benbasat (1991) would allow experts and laypeople to categorize and label the COA items as they see fit.<sup>4</sup> This could forewarn the researcher of possible factor structure discrepancies.

## Conclusion

The purpose of this paper was to consider a possible limitation in the development of the COA scale. We would like to commend Salisbury et al. (2002) for the obvious rigor and detail they demonstrated during the development of the COA scale. Considering the strong reliability and convergent and discriminant validity, we do not suggest that the COA scale be discarded.

Rather, weencourage further research investigating the properties ofthe construct and the scale. We also hope that the issues we presented will assist others in our field who undertake the development of measurement instruments.
