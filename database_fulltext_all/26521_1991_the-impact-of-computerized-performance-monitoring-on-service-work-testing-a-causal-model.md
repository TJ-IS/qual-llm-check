# The Impact of Computerized Performance Monitoring on Service Work: Testing a Causal Model

Rebecca A. Grant

College of Business Administration

University of Cincinnati

Cincinnati, Ohio 45221-0130

Chris A. Higgins

School of Business Administration

University of Western Ontario

London, Ontario, Canada N6A 3K7

This research examined the impact of Computerized Performance Monitoring and Control Systems (CPMCSs) on service workers and their perceptions of work. Drawing on a thermostat model of control systems, the work built a causal model of CPMCS impact. The model demonstrated how four monitor design dimensions (tasks measured, frequency of measurement, object of monitoring, and recipient of the monitor data) affected the importance employees placed on production and service. Other constructs in the model included employees' acceptance of quantitative measures, computer appropriateness, computer accuracy, and employer's production and service messages. Using a holdback sample, an initial and revised model were tested on responses from 1,498 workers in 51 Canadian service sector organizations. Both versions of the model exhibited good explanatory power. The research led to three important conclusions. First, monitoring may not increase production. Even if it does, it need not reduce the importance of service. Second, studying monitors as multidimensional systems demonstrates that various monitor features can be altered to change the impact. Third, the credibility of the computer is a factor in the monitor's impact.

## 1. Introduction

The on-going computerization of the office is changing the nature of jobs and the . way they are measured. As employees perform more tasks on computers, managers are becoming increasingly interested in the opportunity to capture performance data on these computer-mediated tasks. The software that captures such data is commonly known as a Computerized Performance Monitoring and Control System (CPMCS). Such systems affect a sizable and growing segment of the workforce. For example, the U.S. government estimated that computer monitoring affects from 25% to 35% of all clerical workers in the U.S. (U.S. Congress, OTA 1987). Furthermore, as many as 13-15% of managers and professional may be monitored by computer (Nussbaum 1984).

The use of computerized monitoring systems is surrounded by controversy. Proponents point out that CPMCSs increase measurement accuracy and consistency (e.g. Olson and Lucas 1982, Smith et al. 1986) and improve overall productivity (Wright 1982, Young 1980). Opponents argue that productivity improvements come at the expense of customer service, teamwork and quality of work life (e.g., Gregory and Nussbaum 1982, Sheridan 1980, Zuboff 1982).

Research to date has produced rich but conflicting descriptions of monitoring effects. Many of the most prominent studies must be considered advocacy research: they were conducted by groups with a prior stand on the impact of monitoring (e.g., Nussbaum 1984, Garson 1988, Danaan 1990). In addition, few works offer theoretical justifications to reconcile contradictions or strengthen support for one side of the debate over the other. This is because most studies have concentrated on observing impact, rather than explaining it. They have not developed theory or empirically tested hypotheses. In contrast, the focus of our research was to develop a theory of CPMCS impact. Specifically, we studied its effect on attitudes toward performance in the service sector.

Service jobs have two aspects. The first is the production of quantifiable, tangible output such as paid claims, treated patients, dollar sales. The second involves intangible elements, such as customer service, creativity, cooperation, and teamwork. Service workers must often reconcile conflicting demands for performance in both areas. Monitoring may increase an individual's emphasıs on work quantity and improve overall production. But if employees focus on quantity at the expense of quality (as seen in Walton and Vittori 1983), the organization may be at risk. For example, Irving et al. (1986) reported that some monitored insurance claims' processors worked with the attitude “when in doubt, pay out." The cost of such dysfunctional behavior could more than offset gains in production arising from monitoring.

The belief that monitors increase work quantity and decrease quality is widely held. However, it has never been empirically tested. Nor have researchers tried to explain why monitors would have such effects—or how to avoid them. This paper describes our research to build and test a model of C PMCS impact. The study focused on two questions:

(1) how do the design and use of CPMCSs affect the importance that service workers attach to production in overall job performance?; and

(2) how do the design and use of CPMCSs affect the importance that service workers attach to customer service and other qualitative elements in overall job performance?

These questions treat the impact on production separate from the impact on qualitative aspects of work. This reflects the view that service sector jobs are not zero sum: an increase in production does not have to be accompanied by a decrease in service. Monitoring could have positive or negative effects on either or both of the work roles. The questions also focus on how monitors are built and used. Rather than simply comparing monitored and unmonitored workers, this research considers the impact of four dimensions of monitor design.

The research described here used sophisticated techniques to test and refine a model of CPMCS impact. Our discussion begins with the development of our research model and the constructs in it. We then describe the process of model testing.

Finally, we discuss the major implications of our findings and areas for ongoing research.

## 2. Model Development

## 2.1. Theoretical Frameworks

There is a wide body of research that looks at many aspects of computer monitoring. For example, several authors consider the power relationship between management and labor and the effects of monitoring on that relationship (Garson 1988, Nussbaum 1984, Shaiken 1984). Other research examines the issues of privacy and ethical use of monitoring systems in the workplace (U.S. Congress, OTA 1987; Danaan 1990, Marx and Sherizen 1987, Westin et al. 1985). A third group focuses on changes in motivation, satisfaction and the quality of worklife as a consequence of monitoring (Olson and Lucas 1982, Irving et al. 1986, Smith et al. 1986, Walton and Vittori 1983). Finally, there are studies which examine the impact of monitoring on the work itself and its control (Attewell 1987, Eisenman 1986, Grant et al. 1988, Griffith 1988, Kraut et al. 1989, Tamuz 1987).

Each of these research streams focuses on a different set of independent and dependent variables. For example, Nussbaum (1984) used psychosocial and socioeconomic stressors as independent variables, and physical health as the dependent variable. Eisenman (1986) used capacity to monitor as the independent variable and supervisory style as the dependent variable. Westin et al. (1985) used labor relations history as the independent variable and monitoring style as the dependent variable.

Because of the variety of issues and variables, many different theoretical frameworks have been proposed to guide CPMCS research. Attewell (1987) identified five such paradigms appropriate to the study of adoption and work pace impacts. These were corporate culture, neo-Marxist, product life-cycle, contingency and industrial sociology perspectives. Others are also possible. Tamuz (1987) used theories of salience to predict CPMCS impact on performance self-reports, while Griffith (1988) built on the foundations of social distance theory to study supervisory relationships.

Each theoretical framework brings with it a perspective on the function of computerized performance monitoring, as well as a set of questions or relationships which it can study. For example, neo-Marxist paradigms address questions of power, work pace and relationships between management and staff; social distance theories clarify the role of monitors in mediating the relationship between supervisors and employees.

Our own research concentrates on issues of CPMCSs as control systems and their impact on attitudes toward work. Thus, we turned to the control literature as a theoretical foundation. Static models of control (Landy and Farr 1983, Woodward 1970, Slocum and Sims 1980) provide useful taxonomies to characterize and compare control systems designs, but do not support causal hypotheses about control processes. Dynamic theories, on the other hand, demonstrate how the process of control leads to performance and attitudes. Taylor et al. (1984) proposed a model incorporating a comparison process, while Ilgen et al. (1979) described control system impact in terms of the many factors which influence the way feedback is interpreted. A third dynamic model, that of a control system as a thermostat (Lawler and Rhode 1976), focuses on the components of control system design and their impact on outcomes—i.e., employee performance. This focus directly addresses the research questions which ask how monitor design and use affect the perceived importance of elements of performance. Thus, we chose the thermostat model to guide our research development.

## 2.2. The Research Model and Hypotheses

In a thermostat, the sensor' (a control device or evaluation system) measures the performance level. A discriminator' (such as the supervisor) compares the measurement to a standard. The results of this comparison then go to an effector'(the employee). The effector initiates activities which produce measurable performance.

Each relationship in the thermostat model has been subjected to extensive research. Combining our review of this literature, summarized below, with prior CPMCS research led us to develop the causal model shown in Figure 1. Table 1 defines each construct in the model.

A causal model is a network of relationships among constructs. Each direct path, A → B, is a hypothesis, in which a change in A is believed to cause a change in B. The hypotheses implicit in our model are listed in Box 1.

Our model says that the credibility of monitors and the four dimensions of their design have two major effects. First, system design and credibility affect the acceptance of quantitative work measurement. Second, they influence the degree to which employees believe employers rely on quantitative measures. These factors then affect the employee's interpretation of how management values customer service and production. They also influence the employee's owı attitudes about the importance of production and customer service.

## 2.2.1. Independent Constructs

Design Dimensions. A critical feature of this research is its view of CPMCSs as multidimensional systems. Virtually all prior research has studied monitors in terms of one dimension. Participants were “monitored” or “unmonitored" (Irving et al. 1986, Nussbaum 1984, Walton and Vittori 1983) or subject to "high," “moderate," or “low" levels of monitoring (Eisenman 1986, Griffith 1988). Researchers did not always make it clear what factors they used to classify participants, nor were those factors defined consistently across the studies.

There are precedents for the concept of multidimensional monitors and the way an information system's design affects the use ofits data. The “Minnesota Experiments" (Dickson et al. 1977) focused on three dimensions of the design: format, time and decision aids. Firth (1980) suggested level of aggregation, presentation style, and data collection period or frequency. Zmud (1978) said that acceptance of information depended on four factors: (1) quality or appropriateness; (2) relevancy (accuracy, factual quality, completeness, timeliness); (3) presentation quality; and (4) quality of meaning. Lawler (1976) concluded that the design of a control system would affect its impact. He concentrated on the dimensions of measurement completeness, accuracy, and appropriateness.

We integrated these perspectives into four dimensions of monitor design which we said would help explain CPMCS impact. The dimensions were:

(1)“Tasks Quantitatively Measured" (QTASK) (how completely does the monitor cover the full range of tasks involved in doing a job?);

(2) “Frequency of Quantitative Measures" (QFREQ) (how often are data reported—immediately or periodically?);

(3) “Recipient of Quantitative Measures" (QRECIP) (how wide an audience receives the data—the employee, supervisor, or anyone with an interest in performance?); and

(4)“Object of Quantitative Measures" (QOBJ) (how specific is the object of monitoring—is it the individual, the group, or both?)

These four dimensions express pervasiveness of control. First, CPMCSs can monitor virtually any computer-mediated activity. They may count keystrokes, tasks, completed work, or be an integral part of a pacing system which directs work to employees. The more tasks the system measures or controls, the more pervasive it is. Second, systems may give immediate reports of' performance, or merely report on historical activity at regular, fixed intervals. The more immediate the monitor data, the more pervasive the system. Third, CPMCSs can be designed to provide feedback only to the employee, to the immediate supervisor, or to anyone in the workplace. The broader the audience for the data, the more pervasive the monitoring system Finally, the most pervasive monitor designs measure individuals and groups; a less pervasive system would only consider group performance.

This pervasiveness’ concept allows one to state causal hypotheses. One can hypothesize that more or less of an independent variable (i.e., one of the design dimensions) will lead to more or less of a particular dependent variable. Such causal relationships can then be tested.

Computer Perceptions. The credibility of the evaluator is an important factor in reactions to human supervision (Ilgen et al. 1979, Taylor et al. 1984). We hypothesized that the credibility of the computer would be important in monitored environments. We expressed this influence in terms of two constructs: the perceived accuracy of computers (“Computer Accuracy" or COMPACC) and the perceived appropriateness of computers as measurement devices (“Computer Appropriateness" or COMPAPP).

Nature of Work. Personal attitudes toward the importance of production and service should be influenced by the employee's view of the job being monitored (Ilgen et al. 1979). If the job is quantitative in nature, the employee should consider production elements to have major importance. Also, the more quantitative the job seems to be, the less importance the employee will attach to the nonquantitative roles of the job (Ilgen et al. 1979). Thus, another independent construct was “Quantitative Nature of Work" (JOBOTY).

Oualitative Evaluation System. The qualitative evaluation system is also a factor in an employee's performance assessment. It uses supervisor, co-worker, and customer appraisals, as well as other data, to evaluate work. While the qualitative system could be described in terms of the four design dimensions and the credibility ofinformation sources, this research concentrated on the impact of CPMCSs (quantitative systems). Thus, we did not model or study the dimensions of the qualitative system. We did, however, include two perceptions of the qualitat ve system as independent constructs in this work. They were the employee's perception of the employer's “Reliance on Qualitative Measures" (RELQUAL) and the employee's “Acceptance of Qualitative Measures" (ACCQUAL).

## 2.2.2. Intervening Constructs

Acceptance of Quantitative Measures. Our model proposed that the four dimensions of monitor design would affect the acceptance of quantitative measures of evaluation. First, we expected that measuring more tasks (QTASK) would increase the acceptance of quantitative measures of evaluation, as the employee would perceive this as one of management's priorities (Irving et al. 1986, Long 1987, Tamuz 1987, Walton and Vittori 1983). For the same reason, we expected more frequent data capture and reporting (QFREQ) to increase the acceptance of quantitative measures (Ilgen et al. 1979, Lawler and Rhode 1976, Taylor et al. 1984).

Increasing the audience (QRECIP) for the data increases the number of discriminators evaluating performance. This reduces the relative importance of self-discrimination (Lawler and Rhode 1976). It also makes deviations from the group norm more public and increases the opportunity for comparisons between co-workers. System designs which induce competition and consequently strain relationships should reduce acceptance (Walton and Vittori 1983).

Finally, acceptance of performance measures increases when the data clearly reflect performance the individual can control (Lawler 1976). Specifically, measuring the performance of the individual employee, separate from that of the group (QOBJ) increases the ownership’ of the performance data and thus its acceptability (Ilgen et al. 1979, Lawler and Rhode 1976, Sheridan 1980). These concepts led to the paths represented by hypotheses H1, H2, H3 and H4

The literature suggested that beliefs about computers should not directly affect the acceptance of quantitative measures. But for monitored employees, the credibility of the computer in measuring performance should influence acceptance of the data (Long 1987, Smith et al. 1986, Tamuz 1987). This means monitored employees must perceive that the computer is (1) an accurate device (COMPACC) and (2) an appropriate tool to measure their performance (COMPAPP). These relationships are described in hypotheses H5 and H6.

The nature of the job is hypothesized to play a role in the acceptance of quantitative measures. The more quantitative the employee considers the job to be, the more appropriate quantitative measurement would seem (Ilgen et al. 1979, Laudon 1974, Zuboff 1982). This suggested hypothesis H7.

Reliance on Measures. The degree to which an employee believes the employer relies on the measures in the evaluation system is also important. We expected the design dimensions to affect perceived reliance on quantitative measures (RELOTY) in four ways. First, measuring more tasks suggests that the measures are more comprehensive. Employees should then believe that the employer relies more on these data than if the measures were less comprehensive (Higgins et al. 1987, Whisler and Shultz 1960). Second, more frequent data collection and reporting should also increase the extent to which employees believe supervisors rely on the data (Ilgen et al. 1979). Third, increasing the audience for the data beyond the employee or immediate supervisor suggests that other parties have a need to know.' This should lead employees to believe that this broader audience also relies on these data in some way (Lawler and Rhode 1976). Fourth, collecting both individual and group data should make employees believe it is more important in their evaluation, since the employer is gathering more specific, individual information (Lawler 1976). Hypotheses H8, H9, H10 and H11 reflect these relationships.

Employer's Production and Service Message. Another key factor in the model is how much employers value production (PRODMSG) and service (SERVMSG) (Cammann and Nadler 1977, Lawler 1976, Walton and Vittori 1983). That is, does the system tell employees that employers value production, service, or both? The message depends upon the employee's belief that the measurement system reflects the performance criteria of the employer (Ilgen et al. 1979, Taylor et al. 1984). This, in turn, involves accepting quantitative measurements and believing that the employer relies on them (Cammann and Nadler 1977, Kerr 1975, Ilgen et al. 1979). Quantitative measures should influence the employee's perception that production elements are important (PRODMSG) and qualitative measures should influence the perceived importance of service (SERVMSG).

The argument has been made that what isn't measured or used is also important (Kerr 1975, Irving et al. 1986, Cammann and Nadler 1977). Relying heavily on quantitative measures, at the perceived expense of qualitative measures, should reduce the perceived importance of service. On the other hand, the belief that employers rely on qualitative measures should increase the perceived importance of service. Hence hypotheses H12, H13, H14, H15 and H16.

2.2.3. Dependent Constructs. Lawler and Rhode (1976) described “activity," Ilgen et al. (1979) referred to “intention to respond," and Cammann and Nadler (1977) discussed “energy directed toward area," as results of control systems. Each of these concepts entails attitudes toward the value of activities and the intention to respond to control system feedback.

Our research questions focused on the importance the employee gave production and service. Thus, our two dependent variables were “Personal Importance of Production" (PERSPROD) and “Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSERV). Personal importance of production represents the tangible, production role while personal importance of service incorporates the intangible, service role.

Taylor et al. (1984) asserted that quantitative measures and feedback influence an employee's performance standards. Feedback can prompt employees to change standards, if they consider the data accurate, complete, and appropriate. These are the dimensions of acceptance (Ilgen et al. 1979). Thus, increasing acceptance of quantitative data should increase the importance of the dimensions they measure. The same argument can be made for qualitative measures, giving rise to hypotheses H17 and H18.

Attitudes about production and service also arise from job perceptions (Ilgen et al. 1979). Specifically, if the job seems quantitative in nature, employees will consider production elements very important. Customer service and other qualitative elements of the job should also seem less important. Employees will use the feedback provided by the job itself and their view of it to infer importance (Ilgen et al. 1979). This led to hypotheses H19 and H20.

The value the employer attaches to job dimensions or activities is a key factor in employee standards (Cammann and Nadler 1977, Lawler 1976, Walton and Vittori 1983). If an employer seems to value production highly, the employee is more likely to consider it important. Similarly, the more employers seem to value service, the more highly employees will value it.

An employee who increases the value of production does not necessarily decrease the importance of service by an equal amount. For example, an employee may attach a certain level of importance to service. This level could remain constant, even if the perceived importance of production were to increase. Nonetheless, most arguments against monitoring contend that it increases the importance of production so much that it reduces the importance of service (Zuboff 1982, Whisler 1970, Long 1987). A similar argument would reason that the perceived increase in the importance of service to the employer will decrease the personal importance of production. Based on this material, we proposed hypotheses H21, H22, H23, and H24.

## 2.3. Case Study

A case study involving monitored and unmonitored insurance claims processors in a single organization demonstrated support for the major components of the research model (Grant et al. 1988). In that study, we interviewed and administered surveys to 81 employees and their 13 supervisors. The results gave us the confidence to proceed with a national survey of service sector workers in jobs where monitoring was commonplace. It also suggested areas to improve the survey instrument. Finally, it demonstrated significant differences in the attitudes of monitored and unmonitored employees and suggested that the proposed model could explain some of these differences.

## 3. Methods

Subsequent to the case study and theory development, we carried out a national survey of service workers to test our research model. This section describes the survey process, which was based on a modified version of the Total Design Method (Dillman 1978).

## 3.1. Research Instrument

The survey consisted of eight sections. It combined scales adapted from the literature with items developed specifically for this research. Appendix A discusses the quality of these scales. Table A-1 in the appendix presents the reflective items used to capture constructs and the reliability of the scales they comprise.

Parts of Porter and Lawler's (1968) scale of distributive justice were used to measure personal importance of production (PERSPROD), personal importance of service (PERSSERV), employer production message (PRODMSG) and employer service message (SERVMSG). We elaborated on Irving et al.'s (1986) questionnaire of attitudes toward supervision and feedback to measure acceptance of quantitative measures (ACCQTY). A subsection of the Job Diagnostic Survey (Hackman and Oldham 1975) measured the quantitative nature of work. All other constructs used scales created specifically for this research. Nine questions provided demographic and work experience data.

Each participating employee received a sealed, individually addressed envelope marked Personal and Confidential.' The envelope contained a prenumbered survey, a business reply envelope, and a personalized cover letter explaining the research. Respondents returned the completed survey via regular mail. Respondents were guaranteed confidentiality of their responses, so no employer saw completed questionnaires.

## 3.2. Respondents

The research focused on individuals doing computer-mediated work—employees whose work was or could be monitored by computer. We did this to ensure that we looked only at roles that were comparable in terms of monitoring potential, regardless of the company.

We used judgment sampling to select survey participants. As with any nonrandom selection method, this technique raises concerns about the study's external validity (Cook and Campbell 1979). However, research designs often involve trade-offs between concerns of internal and external validity, as steps to improve one can reduce the other. This research required such a trade-off: monitoring is not so widespread that random sampling could guarantee adequate variance in the model constructs. Thus, we decided to concentrate first on internal validity—that is, to focus on testing theoretical relationships before exploring their generalizability. We therefore deliberately chose firms and industries likely to provide a variety of control system designs and service roles.

We first selected 250 Canadian firms from service industries listed in Dun and Bradstreet's 1987 Canadian Key Business Directory. Letters were sent to the chief executive officers of these firms. Organizations willing to participate provided a list of employees who met the following criteria: (1) they were nonsupervisory personnel; (2) they performed at least some computer-mediated work; and (3) they had direct (telephone or face-to-face) contact with customers. Fifty-one firms in 14 different industries provided the names of 2,692 employees who met these criteria. Fourteen hundred and ninety-eight of these workers provided usable surveys for a net response rate of 55.7%.

The respondents represented a diverse group in terms of age, work experience, unionization, and types of monitoring experienced (Table 2). The jobs samples included six of seven categories currently subject to electronic work monitoring as identified by the OTA (U.S. Congress, OTA 1987). The seventh, data entry clerks, did not meet our requirement of direct customer contact. Two-thirds of the respondents were less than 35 years old. As would be expected in nonsupervisory (and primarily office) jobs, over 85% of the respondents were female. More than 85% of the respondents spent at least 25% of their work day on a computer. Nearly 35% of the respondents were subjected to one or more types of computerized performance measurement, the most frequent being systems that counted completed transactions.

## 3.3. Data Analysis Methods

Partial Least Squares (PLS), as implemented in Lohmoller's (1988) computer programs, was the primary data analysis technique uscd in this research. PLS is a secondgeneration, multivariate technique used to estimate the parameters of causal models with latent variables (or constructs) (Wold 1982).'

The large sample obtained in the survey allowed us to use a holdback technique to test and refine our model. Surveys with even numbers provided the input to test the initial model. Odd-numbered surveys were withheld until the original model was revised and then used to test the revised model. There were no statistically significant differences between the odd and even subsets on any demographic item.

## 4. Results and Discussion

## 4.1. Results of Testing the Original Research Model

Convergent validity of constructs was adequate, as shown in Appendix A, Table A-1. All but two of the individual survey items had item reliabilities (i.e., squared factor loadings) well above 0.50. The internal consistency or Cronbach's alpha of all scales exceeded 0.70.

Discriminant validity was generally good, with two exceptions (Appendix A, Table A-2). First, there was weak discriminant validity between “Object of Quantitative Measures" (QOBJ) and “Recipient of Quantitative Measures" (QRECIP). Second, discriminant validity between “Personal Importance of Production" (PERSPROD) and “Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSERV) was also weak. Since the model did not propose a causal relationship in either case, the weak discriminant validity did not reduce the power of the model. However, we addressed these problems in our revision.

The path coefficients estimated by the PLS program (Figure 2) were tested for statistical significance using jackknifing (Fornell and Barclay 1986) with 30 subsamples.² The tests revealed that 19 of the 24 coefficients were statistically significant, in the direction hypothesized. Three of the nonsignificant coefficients (the paths representing H1, H23 and H24) were opposite in sign to the original hypotheses. In fact, they would be significant if the hypotheses were stated in the opposite direction. The other two nonsignificant coefficients were associated with the paths from “Computer Accuracy" and “Computer Appropriateness" to “Acceptance of Quantitative Measures" (hypotheses H5 and H6 respectively).

The analysis using the first subsample confirmed the majority of hypotheses in the original research model. There were three important exceptions: (1) H1, represented by the path from “Tasks Quantitatively Measured" (QTASK) to “Acceptance of Quantitative Measures" (ACCQTY); (2) H23, from “Employer's Production Message" (PRODMSG) to “Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSER V); and (3) H24, from “Employer's Service Message" (SERVMSCi) to “Personal Importance of Production" (PERSPROD). These exceptions strongly contradicted expectations based on the literature. They also suggested that the hypothesized relationships should be reexamined.

## 4.2. Revising the Research Model

PLS and jackknifing provided statistical evidence of relationships between pairs of constructs in the research model. With more than 650 surveys per subsample, very small path coefficients were statistically significant. But statistically significant is not necessarily meaningful. For example, the path from “Quantitative Nature of Work" (JOBQTY) to “Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSER V) (H20) was significant, but added nothing to predicting or explaining Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSERV). Thus we had to consider how meaningful a path was when looking at revisions.

In general, one should not delete paths from a model solely on the basis of data. But this model represented an initial test of theory, so improvements were appropriate. We first dropped three paths with small coefficients. These were: H14, represented by the path from “Reliance on Quantitative Measures" (RELQTY) to “Employer Service Message" (SERVMSG); H17, from “Acceptance of Quantitative Measures" (ACCQTY) to “Personal Importance of Production" (PERSPROD); and H20, from “Quantitative Nature of Work" (JOBQTY) to "Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSERV).

Next, we reviewed paths with unexpected signs. The survey results contradicted popular claims that monitoring motivated employees to undervalue customer service. This common wisdom held that monitoring would increase the perception that employers valued production and, in turn, reduce the importance of service. We showed this as H23 in our model. Similarly, collecting qualitative information about service should have ultimately reduced the perceived importance of production (H24). Instead, our respondents demonstrated that acceptable monitor data had a positive effect on the importance of both production and service.

How might this happen? Our case study interviews and comments written on the survey suggest two explanations. First, the employer's message may trigger a compensating reaction. For example, if the employer stresses production, the employee will attend to production. But he or she may also pay more attention to service to ensure customer satisfaction despite management's apparent indifference to it. Employees whose managers stress service may compensate in production in the same way. Another explanation is that management attention to either aspect of service work prompts employees to pay more attention to the whole job. Research by Tamuz (1987), published after this study began, supported such a conclusion. In either case, the data from the survey, our earlier case study and Tamuz's work made a powerful case for reversing the signs of H23 and H24. We made these changes in the revised model.

Another contradiction appeared in the results for H1. Increasing the range of tasks in the quantitative system decreased acceptance of quantitative measures. This contradicted conclusions drawn from the control literature. Irving et al. (1986) and Walton and Vittori (1983) suggested that the process of monitoring led to dissatisfaction with the control system, but they concluded that incomplete measurement or inappropriate standards caused the problems. Eisenman (1986), however, noted that,

. . . a key factor in the development of the current concerns of VDT workers may well be that their threshold for optimum supervisory structuring has been exceeded.

Our results may reflect such an effect. Among our respondents, the most common use of a CPMCS was to count completed transactions. Increasing pervasiveness (i.e. QTASK) by extending monitoring to activities with less direct quantitative content, such as task assignment and work process, could make quantitative measurement seem less appropriate. This would show up in lower acceptance. Furthermore, human supervisors can recognize mitigating factors when measuring. CPMCSs are less able to do so (Grant et al. 1988). As pervasiveness increases, the monitor's inflexibility may reduce the perceived accuracy and completeness of information. Again, this argued for reversing the sign of H1 in the revised model.

The next issue was the high correlation between “Object of Quantitative Measures" (QOBJ) and “Recipient of Quantitative Measures" (QRECIP). Asher (1983) suggests that one alternative in dealing with such highly correlated constructs is to merge them. We replaced the two constructs with a merged construct called “Object and Recipient of Quantitative Measures" (QCOMB). The literature indicated that object and recipient would have opposite effects on the degree to which employees would accept quantitative measures (Lawler 1976, Lawler and Rhode 1976, Walton and Vittori 1983). This meant that we could not predict the effect of the merged construct on acceptance (ACCQTY). However, the literature did predict that both of the original dimensions would increase the perception that employers rely on quantitative measures. Thus, we replaced hypotheses 10 and 11 with:

H10a. The more specific the object of measurement and the more public its audience, the more employees will believe employers rely on the quantitative data.

Finally, the path from “Computer Accuracy" (COMPACC) to “Acceptance of Quantitative Measures"(ACCQTY) (H5) had a negative coefficient. This implied that monitored employees who considered computers less accurate would be more accepting of CPMCS measures—an unlikely situation. Instead, the negative coefficient seemed to indicate that computer accuracy was measured poorly. COMPACC was also highly correlated with computer appropriateness, thereby distorting the effect of appropriateness on acceptance (H6). Even reducing COMPACC to a single measure did not correct these problems, so we had to drop it from the revised model.

In summary, two constructs were merged into one, one construct was eliminated, three hypotheses were reversed, and eight paths were dropped. These revisions produced the model shown in Figure 3.

## 4.5. Discussion

Paths in a complex causal model should not be analyzed individually, for two reasons. First, each construct takes its meaning from the entire network of relationships. All measurement variables and latent constructs in causal chains are interconnected. These interconnections produce interdependent meanings. Second, when exogenous constructs are highly correlated, the magnitude of the path coefficients from these constructs may be distorted by multicollinearity.

In addition, path coefficients cannot be taken as absolute measures of the degree to which modifying one construct would change another. This is always the case when examining variables in a multiple regression. One cannot conclude that a one-unit change in “Employer's Production Message" (PRODMSG) would, all things being equal, increase “Personal Importance of Service" (PERSSER V) by 0.37 units. These constructs are perceptions, captured by many measures: a one-unit change or a 0.37- unit change has no meaning in such a construct. In addition, “all things being equal" is impossible in situations of multicollinearity. The relationship among such constructs guarantees that one construct cannot remain fixed while the other varies.

Nonetheless, significant paths support the existence of the causal relationships represented, even if they cannot be converted into a metric predicting degree of change.

Perhaps the most important outcome of this research is its strong and consistent evidence that monitoring need not reduce attention to customer service. The literature (Cammann and Nadler 1977, Lawler 1976, Walton and Vittori 1983) asserted that a strong employer performance message would decrease the importance employees gave service. Such propositions form the crux of arguments that monitoring is counterproductive in the service sector.

Our results refuted these propositions. Both models demonstrated positive paths from production and service messages to personal importance of service. Our initial case study suggested that there was a positive, rather than a negative, relationship. At that point, the evidence was not strong enough to justify contradicting the literature. Testing the original research model with 650 surveys, however, provided strong evidence that a positive relationship might be correct.

The strongest influence on employees' attitudes came from the messages they received from employers about the importance of their two roles. But rather than undermining them, strong production messages seemed to have positive effects on attitudes about customer service. Acceptable quantitative measures played a major role in shaping the perception that employers valued production. Thus, a well-designed monitor could increase the importance employees attach to production and service.

By the same token, a poorly designed monitor may not improve attention to production. It could even undermine both production and service. Designs which improved acceptance of quantitative measures strengthened the employer production message. Employees drew fewer inferences from measures they considered unacceptable. Data that seemed inappropriate, incomplete or inaccurate failed to demonstrate management concern for production.

Acceptable measures had strong direct and indirect effects on employee attitudes about the importance of their work roles. In fact, as Tables 4 and 5 show, the indirect effects of acceptance (ACCQTY) on production and service importance (PERS-PROD and PERSSERV) are stronger than the direct effects. Furthermore, acceptance had much more impact on the dependent variables than did employer reliance on monitor data (RELQTY).

Many companies try to impart a strong production message by demonstrating how extensively management uses quantitative data about performance. This research indicates that greater impact comes from ensuring acceptable measurement of performance. In fact, the very designs that demonstratec strong reliance—such as increasing task or recipient pervasiveness—reduced acceptance. Because acceptance has a stronger effect on messages, increasing reliance at the expense of acceptance would ultimately reduce the strength of the production message. Thus, rather than using the monitor as a visible hammer, companies would be better advised to construct the system with an eye toward the acceptability of its design.

The literature (Cammann and Nadler 1977, Irving et al. 1986, Kerr 1975) suggested that relying heavily on quantitative information would reduce the perceived importance of qualitative performance (H14). This research indicated the opposite effect, although the path coefficient was so small (0.01) as to be meaningless. This small coefficient was surprising, given the strong support in the literature for a significant effect. The discrepancy between the literature and our results may reflect an important difference in the way importance was defined. This study examined the absolute, rather than relative, importance of each role, while studies cited in the literature rarely distinguished between the two.

The perceived nature of the work (JOBQTY) was another strong determinant of a monitor's effect. Not surprisingly, the more routine and outcome-oriented employees considered their jobs, the more acceptable they found quantitative measures of their performance. Employees who saw their jobs as largely qualitative were less accepting of monitor measures. It is important to note that all of our respondents had direct customer service responsibilities, yet they dffered in the degree to which they saw their jobs as routine or qualitative. Thus, it is simplistic to say that customer service employees will automatically consider monitoring inappropriate, although much of the advocacy research on CPMCSs implies such a view (e.g., Danaan 1990, Garson 1988, Nussbaum 1984).

Finally, the multidimensional view of monitoring used here revealed rich information often unavailable from studies that only compare monitored and unmonitored employees. Rather than having a uniform positive or negative effect on attitudes, the dimensions differed in the direction and strengtn of their impact. Increasing frequency and more directly attributing performance to its source had positive effects on acceptance, while increasing task and recipient pervasiveness reduced acceptance. At the same time, increasing task and recipient pervasiveness increased the perception that employers relied heavily on monitor data. These results support the contention that different monitors will have different effects and could explain some contradictory findings reported in research that did not include monitor design as variables for study.

## 5. Conclusions

This research led to three important conclusions. First, monitoring need not reduce service quality, even if it does increase production. Second, looking at monitors as multidimensional systems reveals that various design features can be altered to change the impact. Third, the credibility of the computer is a factor in the monitor's impact.

## 5.1. Effects on Production and Service

Popular opinion holds that monitoring reduces the importance employees attach to service. Our results demonstrated that this is not always true. Monitors may increase or reduce service importance. Employees form complex attitudes about the absolute importance of various job dimensions, and many other factors influence these attitudes.

Furthermore, monitoring does not automatically increase the importance employees attach to production. Companies which expect monitors to motivate increased production may be disappointed. It is the acceptance of, and perceived reliance on, monitor measures that increases the importance of production roles. Monitors that produce more accurate, complete and appropriate measures of performance can be effective in increasing production importance. Designs that reduce acceptance may actually reduce production importance, even if management seems to rely on the data.

## 5.2. Multidimensional Monitors

The practice of treating CPMCSs as unidimensional systems has prevailed in research and the popular press. This appears to be a serious mistake. Our study described monitors in terms of multiple dimensions and showed that this dimensionality can help explain apparently conflicting effects of CPMCS use.

The survey results supported the concept of monitors as multidimensional systems. The study demonstrated different effects of task pervasiveness and frequency; it suggested that recipient and object would also have direct effects. Design dimensions affect both perceived reliance on quantitative measures and the acceptance of those measures. Acceptance has the stronger effect on perceived production messages, suggesting that management efforts to motivate higher production should concentrate on designing more acceptable monitor systems.

Approaches which categorize monitors only as low level,' 'moderate,’ and high level’ should specify the dimensions on which the design is low, moderate or high. They should also differentiate the systems being studied along more than one dimension. The four specific dimensions used in this study (task, frequency, recipient, and object) proved to be a useful way of discriminating among monitor designs. Message content would be a valuable fifth dimension.

## 5.3. Computer Appropriateness

Our results indicated that the device used to collect performance data makes a difference. Managers who automate established quantitative evaluation systems may be surprised to discover resistance among formerly satisfied employees. General control system models which do not differentiate between human and automated measurement may have limited applicability in monitoring environments. Within the limitations discussed below, the credibility of the computer affected acceptance of CPMCS measures. It may also affect other reactions to monitoring

## 5.4. Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it used theory grounded in existing work in MIS and reference disciplines to advance the study of computerized monitoring. Second, it provided models which can be used in ongoing research to develop a cumulative research tradition (Keen 1985). Third, the data analysis used a large base of data and sophisticated techniques to test the research hypotheses. Finally, the research design and execution tested a causal model, rather than simply correlational hypotheses.

This work is not without its limitations. Some constructs in the model demonstrated multicollinearity. This may suggest that one or more causal factors are missing from the model. A more likely explanation for the multicollinearity is that managers tend to choose from a limited set of alternative designs when implementing a CPMCS. Those who choose to monitor a full range of tasks often do so frequently and measure individual and group performance. Those who simply count transactions, however, may also be most apt to monitor only individuals and collect only periodic summaries of output. In these cases, there would be empirical correlations among dimensions even though the theory would be correct in depicting them as independent constructs.

A second limitation is the inability to analyze bi-directional relationships with PLS. The feedback literature suggests that performance priorities influence acceptance of control system data and thus should be modeled as reciprocal paths. For example, the path from personal attitudes to acceptance and from perceived messages to reliance could have been posed as bi-directional. Such relationships remain to be tested.

Third, all of our respondents were Canadian. Cultural factors may limit our ability to generalize our findings to other countries. The study should be replicated in other cultures to determine the generality of the model

Finally, this study did not adequately measure computer accuracy. That construct may or may not affect acceptance, as well as other constructs. The scales for this construct should be improved and the issue studied in greater detail to determine if it explains variance in outcomes.

## 5.5. Topics for Ongoing Research

Any research of broad and unexplored issues is bound to raise as many questions as it answers. This is certainly true of our study. A number of issues appear promising for future study.

This study demonstrated that the qualitative system, which includes the supervisor's use of monitor data, plays an important role in determining CPMCS impact. Yet Eisenman (1986) pointed out that the supervisors in her study did not fully use the data or the facilities available. This raises many questions about the impact of monitoring on supervisory styles and the qualitative evaluation systems.

The boundaries of the model should be expanded to study the impact of monitors on other constructs. Job perceptions, for example, may differ on the basis of monitor designs. The same may be true for reliance and acceptance of qualitative measures. This study did not examine these relationships. Future work should do so before concluding that monitoring only affects reliance and acceptance of quantitative messages. Opportunities also remain to study how employees form their perceptions of the monitor design dimensions.

This research used the perceptions of employees in 51 firms. This suggests that some of the unexplained variance in acceptance, reliance and production/service importance may be due to firm-level effects. For example, corporate culture or labor relations may influence perceptions and attitudes. We did not specifically collect data about such variables and so could not test for their effects in this study. However, the potential effect of such variables should be considered in future research.

Many important questions remain unanswered. The causal model and survey produced a framework to explore other relationships. They also suggested areas to expand and improve that framework. Continued research in these areas will greatly enhance our understanding and effective use of computerized performance monitoring and control systems.\*

## Appendix A: Measures of Reliability and Validity

The first step in a PLS analysis is to evaluate the research model in terms of its convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity is the degree to which two or more attempts to measure the same construct agree (Cook and Campbell 1979). In PLS, three elements can be used to determine the convergent validity of measured constructs in a single instrument (Fornell and Larcker 1981): (1) the reliability of each item in a scale (the squared loading); (2) the composite reliability (or internal consistency) of each scale; and (3) the average variance extracted by each construct (average item reliability).

Generally speaking, individual item reliabilities should exceed 0.50. This implies that the item has more explanatory power than error. Composite reliabilities, which are analogous to Cronbach's alpha, should exceed 0,70 (Nunnally 1978), Average variance extracted should also exceed 0.50
