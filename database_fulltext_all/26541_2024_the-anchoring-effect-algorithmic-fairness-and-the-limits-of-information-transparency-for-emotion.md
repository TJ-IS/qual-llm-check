# The Anchoring Effect, Algorithmic Fairness, and the Limits of Information Transparency for Emotion Artificial Intelligence

Lauren Rhue<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. Emotion artificial intelligence (AI) or emotion recognition AI may systematically vary in its recognition of facial expressions and emotions across demographic groups, creating inconsistencies and disparities in its scoring. This paper explores the extent to which individuals can compensate for these disparities and inconsistencies in emotion AI consid ering two opposing factors; although humans evolved to recognize emotions, particularly happiness, they are also subject to cognitive biases, such as the anchoring effect. To help understand these dynamics, this study tasks three commercially available emotion AIs and a group of human labelers to identify emotions from faces in two image data sets. The scores generated by emotion AI and human labelers are examined for inference inconsistencies (i.e., misalignment between facial expression and emotion label). The human labelers are also provided with the emotion AI’s scores and with measures of its scoring fairness (or lack thereof). We observe that even when human labelers are operating in this context of information transparency, they may still rely on the emotion AI’s scores, perpetuating its inconsistencies. Several findings emerge from this study. First, the anchoring effect appears to be moderated by the type of inference inconsistency and is weaker for easier emotion recognition tasks. Second, when human labelers are provided with information transparency regarding the emotion AI’s fairness, the effect is not uniform across emotions. Also, there is no evidence that information transparency leads to the selective anchoring necessary to offset emotion AI disparities; in fact, some evidence suggests that information transparency increases human inference inconsistencies. Lastly, the different models of emotion AI are highly inconsistent in their scores, raising doubts about emotion AI more generally. Collectively, these findings provide evidence of the potential limitations of addressing algorithmic bias through individual decisions, even when those individuals are supported with information transparency.

## 1. Introduction

Emotion recognition artificial intelligence (AI; emotion AI) is a growing subset of facial analysis used to automatically recognize emotions. It is already used to screen job applicants and determine threats (Thomas 2018, Harwell 2019, Telford 2019); is expanding into mental health apps, call centers, and advertising feedback (Somers 2019); and is being incorporated into virtual meeting software to measure participants’ engagement (O’Leary 2022).

Despite its benefits, emotion AI may exhibit disparities in the quality of its recognition of facial expressions and emotions based on the subject’s demographic characteristics (Xu et al. 2020), suggesting algorithmic bias. Organizations may attempt to counteract such algorithmic bias in multiple, complementary ways; they may arm their end users to counter bias through individual choices, adopt audited or fairness-aware algorithms, and/or exert public pressure on algorithm developers to debias their algorithms (Raji and Buolamwini 2019). The first of these tools, end user oversight, is a valuable aid for emotion AI because humans have evolved to recognize emotions. However, researchers in behavioral economics and applied psychology note that because of the anchoring effect, any numeric input that a human evaluator sees may influence their judgment (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Adomavicius et al. 2013). Organizations that rely on end users to counteract algorithmic bias may find that these end users have their own cognitive biases and are, therefore, influenced by emotion AI outputs, so disclosing information about AI fairness measures may not yield higherquality individual choices. Thus, emotion AI is a good context for studying the interplay of algorithmic fairness, information transparency, and the anchoring effect.

This research examines whether individuals can counteract algorithmic inconsistencies and disparities in emotion AI and yield consistent scores. To this end, we conduct a study on two facial image data sets: one data set of National Basketball Association (NBA) players and another from the Balanced Faces in the Wild (BFW) data set, which was designed for image recognition (Robinson et al. 2020). The emotions in those faces are scored using three commercially available emotion AIs that may exhibit racial disparities and inference inconsistencies (i.e., misalignment of the facial expression and the emotion score). Then, we conduct an experiment in which human labelers provided with varying information about the emotion AI, including the scores it generated and its known level of fairness or bias, score the emotions in those same faces.

This study yields several interesting findings. As expected, there is consistent evidence of the anchoring effect and some evidence of a selective anchoring effect. First, the anchoring effect appears to be moderated by the type of inference inconsistencies in the emotion AI scores such that the effect appears weaker for the easier task of happiness recognition. Second, the effect of information transparency about algorithmic fairness is not uniform across emotions. This result may occur because humans are better at recognizing happiness and rely less on the emotion AI as an anchor. Lastly, there is no evidence that information transparency leads to the selective anchoring needed to offset the emotion AI disparities, and there is limited evidence that information transparency can increase human inference inconsistencies. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that even under conditions of information transparency with regard to algorithmic fairness, individual users may have limited ability to address algorithmic bias through their decisions.

This study makes multiple contributions to the information systems literature. It finds evidence that the anchoring effect is stronger in response to harder tasks (which is also when human oversight is particularly important to remedy AI-based inference inconsistencies), suggesting that cognitive biases could limit the effectiveness of this type of human oversight. It also finds that information transparency is more influential in harder tasks, but it affects the labelers’ scores across all faces. Overall, these findings indicate that information transparency regarding algorithmic fairness is not enough to cause individuals to adjust their behavior, and separately, it may also yield lowerquality decisions. Because of the extreme variation in scores, this study raises doubts about the real-world performance and implementation of emotion AI.

## 2. Theoretical Background

This research is related to four research streams: (1) emotion AI, (2) algorithmic bias, (3) information transparency, and (4) the anchoring effect. We briefly summarize those streams.

## 2.1. Emotion A

Emotion AI, a subset of facial recognition and analysis technology, was developed to automatically identify emotions from facial expressions (Benitez-Quiroz et al. 2016, Dehghan et al. 2017). Emotion AI models work by identifying patterns of facial expressions based on human-labeled training data, often using probabilistic predictive models such as neural networks (Cohen et al. 2000). Facial analysis models employ landmark extraction and identify facial attributes, such as the chin, eyes, nose, and mouth, from photos of individuals (Xu et al. 2016). In emotion AI tasks, sets of facial images are labeled with the associated emotions by human labelers. Given enough examples of a particular facial expression with its associated emotion label, an emotion AI model will learn the factors in the facial expression that predict a specific emotion (Cohen et al. 2000). This paper builds on the emotion AI literature by investigating potential disparities in emotion AI based on demographic characteristics, exploring questions of fairness in emotion AI and the potential use of information transparency as a means of mitigating fairness concerns.

## 2.2. Algorithmic Bias and Fairness

Algorithmic bias occurs when algorithmic failures or inconsistencies are correlated with demographic characteristics. Facial recognition and analysis algorithms, including emotion AI, have shown evidence of algorith mic bias (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018, Xu et al. 2020), such as providing less accurate predictions for darkerskinned faces. Algorithmic bias can have many causes (e.g., human bias in creating the data labels or overrepresentation of one population in the training set) (Torralba and Efros 2011). A multidisciplinary approach has emerged that combines statistical techniques and organizational frameworks to combat algorithmic bias and enhance nondiscriminatory treatment (Hardt et al. 2016, Corbett-Davies et al. 2017, Kleinberg 2018, Kleinberg et al. 2018, Raji and Buolamwini 2019, Turner Lee et al. 2019).

Broadly, nondiscriminatory treatment is understood as fairness. Measures of algorithmic fairness quantify variations in how algorithms handle different groups and/or individuals in different groups; they can thus serve as mechanisms for diagnosing the presence of algorithmic bias. One common measure of algorithmic fairness is demographic parity (DP) (i.e., the difference in the expected value of an outcome variable conditional on demographic characteristics alone).<sup>1</sup> DP is a commonly used fairness measure because it is easy to calculate and straightforward to explain. However, it is not always appropriate (Hardt et al. 2016, Awwad et al. 2020, Mehrabi et al. 2022, Teodorescu et al. 2022). This can be the case when demographic groups have meaningful underlying variations, so the outcome variable may display significant group-level differences and still adhere to definitions of fairness (Kleinberg 2018). Plus, DP may fail to appropriately consider individuals on the decision boundary (Awwad et al. 2020), or it may harm the protected group over a longer time horizon (Fu et al. 2022). Despite these concerns, DP is often used because it aligns with human perceptions of fairness (Srivastava et al. 2019).

As organizations continue to adopt artificial intelligence into their processes and products, procedures to enhance algorithmic fairness are an important consideration in information systems development (Teodorescu et al. 2021). Most statistical techniques to increase algorithmic fairness rely on access to the underlying models; however, commercially available facial recognition systems (such as IBM Watson, Microsoft Cognitive Services, Face++, and Amazon Rekognition) provide client access to pretrained probabilistic models without providing access to the underlying models. Without this access, client organizations cannot see these models’ methods (chapter 1 in O’Neil 2016), precluding an analysis of procedural fairness (Morse et al. 2022). Given this limitation and the growing concern about unfair algorithmic behavior (Barocas and Selbst 2016, Buolamwini and Gebru 2018), clients may want to audit these proprietary models for fairness in the outcomes instead of fairness in the procedures.

## 2.3. Information Transparency Regarding Algorithmic Fairness

Understanding fairness in the outcomes for any specific context requires information transparency regarding the underlying algorithm. Such information transparency involves “the presence of information on an algorithm’s structure, procedure, decision rationale, or performance” (Lehmann et al. 2022, p. 3421). Yet, this information transparency, such as details about the training data and classifiers, is often unavailable for commercially available pretrained models (Barocas and Selbst 2016). Instead, such models often undergo outcome-based audits, such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology Facial Recognition Vendor Test, which reports a model’s false-positive rate, falsenegative rate, and any disparities along demographic characteristics (Grother et al. 2019).

Information transparency around racial disparities in AI outputs can spur designers to change their training data and/or models to encourage algorithmic fairness (Buolamwini and Gebru 2018, Raji and Buolamwini 2019) and galvanize organizations to regularly audit their algorithmic tools for bias (Turner Lee et al. 2019). In general, information transparency may reduce the negative externalities from artificial intelligence (Luca et al. 2016, Rhue and Washington 2020); however, it can also exacerbate disparities by delaying bias to a later point in the process (Mejia and Parker 2021). This paper contributes to the algorithmic fairness and information transparency literatures by studying how information transparency regarding algorithmic fairness affects individual choices.

## 2.4. Cognitive Bias and the Anchoring Effect

The effectiveness of information transparency on algorithmic fairness depends on the degree to which humans are susceptible to cognitive biases, such as the anchoring effect (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). Emotion AI scores are expected to influence human labelers because any information presented before a judgment is likely to act as an anchor for that judgment (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), even if these anchors are random and uninformative (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Ariely et al. 2003). In the seminal study of the anchoring effect, subjects were asked to spin a wheel to obtain a number; then, they were asked to estimate the number of countries in the United Nations (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). The researchers found that even though subjects know that the number is randomly generated, they still insufficiently adjust from that anchor point (Tversky and Kah neman 1974), and this basic anchoring effect has been replicated (Brewer and Chapman 2002).

Research on the anchoring effect lies within multiple streams (Adomavicius et al. 2013). One stream discusses the underlying psychological mechanisms (Adomavicius et al. 2013), with the original explanation being insufficient adjustment from the anchor (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). Other theories include numeric priming, where the presence of a number can “prime” the respondent, or scale distortion, which is when people use the anchor to inform the scale for their judgments (Brewer and Chapman 2002, Mochon and Frederick 2013). A related stream of literature examines factors that moderate the anchoring effect, such as familiarity with the decision and trust in the anchor’s source (van Exel et al. 2006). This paper expands on the anchoring effect literature by examining how the anchoring effect interacts with the algorithmic fairness literature.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

## 3.1. The Anchoring Effect and Inference Inconsistencies

One lens through which to analyze the quality of an AI’s emotion scoring is inference inconsistency. In this context, inference inconsistency describes the misa lignment between a facial expression and an emotion. For instance, a smile is a nearly universal indicator of happiness in pictures (Kotchemidova 2005), but a human or an emotion AI may mislabel a smiling face as angry. Inference inconsistencies can occur in two directions: a false-negative inference inconsistency (FNII) or a false-positive inference inconsistency (FPII). FNII denotes a situation in which the model or labeler mislabels a smiling face as nonhappy (i.e., a smile is present, but the emotion is not happy). Conversely, FPII denotes the situation in which a nonsmiling face is mislabeled as happy (e.g., no smile is present, but the emotion is happy). We consider these inference inconsistencies as examples of algorithmic failures on the part of an emotion AI and examples of poor quality on the part of a human labeler.

We would expect humans, when presented with inference inconsistencies generated by AI, to provide higher scores in the appropriate emotion because humans generally excel at emotion recognition. Although humans should produce scores that offset inference inconsistencies in both positive and negative directions, their emotion recognition ability is not uniform for all emotions. Specifically, humans more easily recognize happy emotions in facial expressions (Johnston et al. 2001, Leppa¨nen and Hietanen 2004, Ba¨nziger et al. 2009, Vaish et al. 2009) and are better at recognizing happy faces (Kirita and Endo 1995). If humans’ emotion recognition abilities were truly consistent, we would expect them to label faces with FNII as happier and faces with FPII as less happy. However, given that people are better at recognizing happiness, human labelers may be more successful at compensating for FNII than FPII; therefore, we expect to see a weaker anchoring effect on happy emotions and a stronger anchoring effect other emotions.

Hypothesis 1a. The anchoring effect is weaker in the case of inference inconsistencies for easier tasks, such as mislabeled smiling faces (FNII).

Hypothesis 1b. The anchoring effect is stronger in the case of inference inconsistencies for harder tasks, such as mislabeled nonsmiling faces (FPII).

## 3.2. Information Transparency About Algorithmic Fairness

The anchor effect may be moderated by the anchor’s source, and the framing of information can influence how consumers incorporate that information into their judgments (Adjerid et al. 2018, Bassellier and Ramaprasad 2023). This means that higher trust in an anchor’s source is more likely to yield judgments consistent with the anchoring effect (van Exel et al. 2006, Furnham and Boo 2011). Humans tend to trust technology and artificial intelligence (Eslami et al. 2018) in a phenomenon known as algorithmic appreciation (Logg et al. 2019); specifically, studies have found that people trust algorithmic advice at least as much as human advice (Gunaratne et al. 2018, Bogert et al. 2021). Thus, in the context of emotion AI and from the perspective of a human observer, information transparency about fairness measures could highlight the fact that the anchors (emotion AI scores) may be inaccurate, undermine confidence in the anchors, and weaken the anchoring effect (van Exel et al. 2006, Furnham and Boo 2011). However, in uncertain circumstances, human labelers’ cognitive biases have a stronger effect, so they are more likely to rely on anchors (van Exel et al. 2006, Furnham and Boo 2011). Labelers are particularly likely to rely on anchors fo more difficult tasks, for unfamiliar situations, and/or when the labeling task involves marginalized people (Nowicki et al. 1998, van Exel et al. 2006). Therefore, we expect that information transparency will lower the anchor effect for the easier task (scoring happiness) and strengthen it for the harder task (scoring anger). We hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2a. Information transparency about AI fairness will shape labelers’ scores less in the easier task.

Hypothesis 2b. Information transparency about AI fairness will shape labelers’ scores more in the harder task.

## 3.3. Information Transparency and the Anchoring Effect

Information transparency regarding AI fairness could encourage labelers to reflect on algorithmic fairness, reconsider their proposed scores, and make choices intended to offset any disparities. Human emotion recognition varies by racial groups (Kilbride and Yarczower 1983); people are less accurate at recognizing emotions from marginalized groups, such as African Americans (Nowicki et al. 1998), and may be more likely to assign anger to their facial expressions (Halberstadt et al. 2022). Given the cultural association of ange with marginalized groups, such as African Americans, this study focuses on happiness, the most easily recognized emotion, and anger, the emotion most likely to interact with racial characteristics. We expect that infor mation transparency would cause the anchoring effect to be moderated by the face’s demographic characteristics, creating a selective anchoring effect.

Ultimately, information transparency could also increase human inference inconsistencies. This is because emotions, demographic characteristics of the face, and information transparency may all shape the labeler’s confidence in their task. People tasked with emotion recognition for unfamiliar or traditionally marginalized groups may feel more uncertainty (Kilbride and Yarczower 1983), which would strengthen the anchoring effect (van Exel et al. 2006, Furnham and Boo 2011); however, information transparency regarding algorithmic fairness may undermine trust and weaken the anchoring effect. Thus, information transparency may complicate the labeler’s assessment of the emotion AI’s trustworthiness, yielding a higher chance of human inference inconsistencies. We expect the following.

Hypothesis 3a. The effect of information transparency about AI fairness measures on labelers’ scores is moderated by demographic characteristics.

Hypothesis 3b. Information transparency about AI fairness measures leads to inconsistency in labelers’ scores.

## 4. Data

This section discusses this study’s tools, variable construction, and data collection procedures.

## 4.1. Tools and Variable Definitions

This analysis used three different and common emotion AI tools: Face++,<sup>2</sup> Microsoft Azure Face Application Programming Interface (API),<sup>3</sup> and Amazon Rekognition.<sup>4</sup>

4.1.1. Face<sup>11</sup>. Face++, a Megvii product, was launched in Beijing in 2012 as one of the world’s largest facial recognition tools.<sup>5</sup> Face++ analyzes faces for emotion, smile, gender, image quality, and race (before October 2019). For emotion, each face is assigned a value for each of seven emotions: anger, disgust, fear, happiness, neutral, sadness, and surprise. Each face is then labeled based on the emotion with the highest numeric amount for that face. The sum of the seven emotional scores equals 100, which in our study, was rescaled to 10. Face++ identifies the degree to which a face is smiling, and this attribute is distinct from the assessment of happiness.

4.1.2. Microsoft Azure Cognitive Services. Microsoft’s Azure Face API provides facial recognition services to numerous large companies (e.g., Uber) and to researchers (e.g., Choudhury et al. 2019). It analyzes faces based on emotion, smiling, and noise level. Each face is scored on eight emotional dimensions: anger, contempt, disgust, fear, happiness, neutral, sadness, and surprise. For each face, the scores for all the emotions sum to one. The emotion with the highest numeric amount becomes the emotion label for that face. In addition to rating emotions, Microsoft rates each face’s smile between zero and one, and the smile score is the same as that face’s happiness score. In order to keep the scores on the same scale, we rescaled the Microsoft scores so that the highest possible value for smile and happiness is 10.

4.1.3. Amazon Rekognition. Rekognition, Amazon’s image recognition service, is one of the most widely used facial recognition services. Amazon Rekognition’s face detection and analysis API scored faces on several emotions: calm, disgusted, angry, sad, happy, surprised, and confused. The emotion with the highest numeric amount is the emotion label for that face. These scores also sum to 100 for each face and were rescaled so that the highest possible value for happiness is 10. Amazon provided a binary indicator for whether the facial expression contains a smile and the confidence interval for that smile prediction.

## 4.2. Image Data Sets

To address concerns about data set composition, this paper uses two image data sets.

4.2.1. NBA Data Set. The first data set is a collection of professional images of 471 NBA players from the 2016–2017 season that were scraped from Basketball Reference.<sup>6</sup> These pictures were taken in a professional context with relatively standard poses. All players faced the camera head-on, a preferable position for accurate facial analysis. These players were relatively homogenous in their age, gender, and physicality but fell mainly into two racial categories: African American (75%) or White (25%). These pictures were scored in 2017 using all three emotion AI tools (Face++, Microsoft, and Amazon).

4.2.2. Balanced Faces in the Wild Data Set. The second data set is the BFW data set (Robinson et al. 2020). This data set was designed to create a gender and racially balanced data set for facial recognition and was based on the VGGFace2 data set (Cao et al. 2019). BFW provided 100 faces, with four races (East Asian, African American, South Asian, and White) and two genders (male and female) represented. For each face, they provided multiple images. To narrow down the data set, we selected the largest image (by size) for each face. This resulted in 100 pictures per category and a total of 800 pictures. There were abnormalities in 14 in the pictures, leaving a total of 786 pictures in the final data set. The pictures were scored in 2020 using all three emotion AI tools.

## 4.4. Inference Inconsistencies

The emotion AI models demonstrated inference inconsistency in two directions: a false negative for happiness (i.e., a smiling face and a nonhappy emotion) or a false positive for happiness (i.e., a nonsmiling face and a happy emotion). Because smiling and nonsmiling faces can both be deemed neutral, neutral emotion was never inconsistent. Figure 2 shows examples of internal inconsistencies from both the NBA and BFW data sets and for Face++ and Amazon emotion AI models. Microsoft did not have any inference inconsistencies because it reported identical scores for smiling and happy. Amazon identified all faces in the BFW data set as calm or neutral, so Amazon had no inference inconsistencies in the BFW data set.

## 5. Empirical Analysis

5.1. Experimental Design and Summary Statistics In our nested experimental design, we asked human labelers to identity emotions from the facial expressions in the two data sets. There are two experimental conditions. (1) In the anchor condition, the labeler was exposed to the scores generated by one of the emotion Ais, and (2) in the transparency condition, the labeler was exposed to the scores generated by one of the emotion AIs and also received information about the demo graphic parity of that emotion AI. Labelers in the control group were asked to rate a face without any information about emotion AI scores. In the ancho condition, labelers received an emotion AI score for a particular face from one of the three models before scoring the facial expression. The face was labeled with the statement, “An artificial intelligence (AI) scored this face as <score> out of 10,” where <score> is the emotion AI score for that face for a given model. In the screen shown to the labelers, the emotion AI models were anonymized to remove any brand influence. For the anchor plus information transparency dimension, labelers received the emotion AI score along with the average racial disparity for each emotion. The experiment differed slightly for the NBA and BFW data sets.

## 5.2. The Anchoring Effect

To learn how labelers’ scores are shaped by emotion AI scores, we compared the scores for the same subject across different anchors. We used a mixed-level model with random effects, from the lme4 R package (Bates et al. 2015), to control for differences in each subject’s facial expression. The following model specifications were used for the NBA and BFW data sets:

$$
\begin{array}{r} L a b e l e r S c o r e s _ {i j} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} A I S c o r e _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} R a c e _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {3} A I S c o r e _ {j} \times R a c e _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i} + \epsilon_ {j}, \end{array}
$$

where LabelerScores are the scores that labelers assign to the facial expression for subject i in condition j, AIScore is the emotion AI score for subject i by emotion AI model $j ,$ and Race is the racial category of subject i. For the NBA data set, Race is a binary variable denoting whether subject i is African American. For the BFW data set, Race is a set of binary variables denoting whether subject i belongs to one of the four racial groups (White, East Asian, African-American, and South Asian). Emotion AI Score × Race is the interaction between the two variables. There is a subject-level random effect $\epsilon _ { i }$ to control for differences between subjects and an emotion AI random effect $\epsilon _ { j }$ to control for systematic differences among the emotion AIs. For this analysis, we exclude the transparency conditions because information about AI fairness would create noise in the analysis.

5.2.1. NBA Data Set. Table 4 provides a baseline for the anchoring effect. As expected, these findings suggest that the labelers’ scores are a function of the emotion AI score for both happiness and anger $( \beta = 0 . 0 7$ and β � 0.11, respectively). The results also suggest some selective anchoring, as the labelers are more likely to incorporate the emotion AI scores for anger for African American subjects (β � 0.07).

## 5.3. Information Transparency and Inference Inconsistencies

We examine information transparency conditions to understand their effects on labelers’ scores.

5.3.1. Information Transparency. As discussed, we expect that when human labelers are provided with transparent information about AI fairness, it will lead to a weaker anchoring effect because the information about any disparities, or even the suggestion of disparities, will lead the labelers to reflect on their scores. $$
\begin{array}{r l} & L a b e l e r S c o r e s _ {i k} \\ & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} A I S c o r e _ {i} + \beta_ {2} T r a n s p a r e n c y _ {k} + \beta_ {3} R a c e _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} T r a n s p a r e n c y _ {k} \times R a c e _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {5} T r a n s p a r e n c y _ {k} \times A I S c o r e _ {i} + \beta_ {6} R a c e _ {i} \times A I S c o r e _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {7} T r a n s p a r e n c y _ {k} \times R a c e _ {i} \times A I S c o r e _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}, \end{array}

Transparency × Race is the interaction between the two variables. There is also a subject-leve random effect $\epsilon _ { i }$ to control for differences between subjects. ## 6. Discussion

In this paper, we find confirmation of the anchoring effect and a moderating effect of inference inconsistency, providing support for Hypotheses 1a and 1b. Information transparency regarding AI fairness is less influential when human labelers face easier emotion recognition tasks and stronger when they undertake harder emotion recognition tasks, consistent with Hypotheses 2a and 2b. This information transparency leads to spillover effects across all faces, with no differentiation based on the photo subject’s demographic group. Because Hypothesis 3a hypothesized that information transparency about AI fairness would only affect scores for certain demographics, the results of this experiment do not demonstrate support for Hypothesis 3a and in fact, raise questions about labelers’ ability to counteract AI disparities in their own scores. Plus, there is evidence that for certain faces, transparency increases the labelers’ inconsistencies, providing limited evidence for Hypothesis 3b. Table 12 summarizes the support for each of the hypotheses.

This study makes multiple contributions to the information systems literature, and it builds on the algorithmic fairness literature by exploring the limits of information transparency on individuals’ decisions. This paper suggests that even when individuals are provided with information transparency about AI fairness, it is not enough to help them overcome the anchoring effect and sufficiently adjust their behavior. However, this type of information transparency is still important; prior research has shown that information transparency about disparities can generate public pressure and/or regulations to reduce those disparities in the algorithm (Raji and Buolamwini 2019). These findings are consistent with those of other studies showing that the helpfulness of information transparency is limited in the presence of bias. We, therefore, suggest that information transparency can merely change the locus of bias (Mejia and Parker 2021) rather than reduce it. Similarly, information transparency regarding AI fairness may lead to unintended consequences and is not a replacement for computational debiasing or higher-level oversight (e.g., regulations).

This study also indicates that cognitive biases limit the potential for individuals to counteract algorithmic bias. If the anchoring effect “corrupts” people’s judgment on subjective tasks, such as consumer preferences and recommender systems (Adomavicius et al. 2013), human labelers may be so influenced by the AI output that they may not notice any bias in the AI. Labelers scores exhibit inference inconsistencies that vary based on the demographic characteristics of the face, and this pattern is consistent with the anchoring effect’s distorting influence on human discernment.

This study is also related to research on human-AI collaboration. Prior work along these lines has found that people rely more on AI advice for difficult tasks (Bogert et al. 2021). Thus, our observation that labelers scores rely more on emotion AI for harder tasks is consistent with prior research. This study also suggests that providing human labelers with information about AI fairness is beneficial for such tasks.

This study also raises questions about emotion AI. Emotions are not necessarily universal or universally recognized (Elfenbein and Ambady 2002, Barrett 2017), and studies that depend on mapping facial expressions to emotions may not consider fairness issues (Choudhury et al. 2019). This study finds that artificial intelligence is erratic in its emotion interpretation, an observation consistent with those in other fields that question the universality of emotions (Gendron et al. 2014). In addition, we see that commercially available emotion AI models produce notably inconsistent scores for the same set of facial expressions and across demographic groups. Because of the variation in human interpretation and labeling of facial expression in emotions, these disparities are likely to persist, even as AI improves. This study provides additional evidence that AI tools, even those designed for the same task, are not necessarily interchangeable (Luca et al. 2016, Obermeyer et al. 2019). As emotion AI continues to become embedded in our communication technologies, it is important to consider its potential downsides. Many possible uses for emotion AI, such as measuring classroom engagement, have significant consequences for the human subjects. Although individuals, such as classroom moderators, may attempt to intercede and encourage fairness, these individuals may still experience the anchoring effect, and thus, emotion AI scores may distort their judgment. The substantial inconsistency we observe in our experiment signals that we should reimagine the use of emotion AI in these spaces.

This study has multiple managerial implications. Organizations may adopt AI to scale their capabilities, such as searching for human talent (Tambe et al. 2019), and they may even apply AI specifically to help reduce bias (Cowgill 2017). However, extensive prior literature has found algorithmic bias to exist in contexts where it has serious consequences, such as hiring algorithms, criminal justice risk scores, facial recognition, and patient triage algorithms (Crawford 2013, Angwin et al. 2016, Barocas and Selbst 2016, Buolamwini and Gebru 2018, Obermeyer et al. 2019, Bartlett et al. 2022). In the United States, organizations are liable for any tool or policy that increases disparate outcomes along certain demographic characteristics, such as race or gender (Barocas and Selbst 2016). In this context, orga nizations that adopt third-party AI, such as emotion AI, cannot change the underlying algorithm and may wish to incorporate an interface to report aggregate algorithmic fairness measures to empower users. This paper has multiple limitations. First, we examine the anchoring effect and the effect of information transparency on AI fairness in an experimental context. This context provides a more controlled setting, but it would be interesting to examine this type of judgment in a real-world organizational setting. Second, NBA portraits are professional images with the expectations of similar facial expressions. Third, we use the emotion AI outputs rather than a random value as our anchor. Plus, this research assesses information transparency on AI fairness only in the context of emotion AI. Although it is beyond the scope for this manuscript, future research could explore if similar dynamics occur in other AI-related contexts.

To conclude, this paper underscores the limitations of information transparency about fairness measures as a decision support. In contexts in which organizations cannot change the underlying models, individuals can experience a strong anchoring effect during more difficult tasks, and even providing human users with information transparency about algorithmic fairness may still yield subpar results. Instead, we recognize that information about algorithmic fairness can be a valuable call to action.
