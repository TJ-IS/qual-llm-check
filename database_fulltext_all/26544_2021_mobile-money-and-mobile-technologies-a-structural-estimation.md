Abstract. Using a data set on mobile technologies and mobile money in the emerging markets from 2000 to 2014, we examine the demand patterns of mobile technologies and mobile money when multiple generations of mobile technologies coexist in the market and each generation of the technologies may be bundled with mobile money. Using a structural model, we estimate the own and cross price elasticities for both mobile technologies and mobile money, and the demand effects between mobile money and mobile technologies We find that the current, dominant technology (i.e., 3G) tends to have more robust demand as compared with other technologies that are either declining (i.e., 1G and 2G) or new to the market (i.e., 4G), and that customers are more likely to substitute forward toward newer technologies than backward toward older technologies when the price increases for a technology. We also find that mobile money differentiates the market and mitigates competition for those firms that offer mobile money. Theoretical and managerial impli cations are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Mobile money is a technological innovation that combines mobile technologies and financial services to provide convenient and affordable access to banking systems and to improve financial inclusion (Lal and Sachdev 2015). It “allows users to deposit money onto their telephone handsets, transfer e-money to another user with a simple text message, and withdraw cash at one of thousands of outlets throughout the country” (Mbiti and Weil 2013, p. 369). One of the first mobile money services, M-PESA, was introduced in Kenya by the mobile network Safaricom and has been “by far the most successful example of mobile money banking in Africa” (Economist 2009). Its success has motivated continued adoption of mobile money in other developing countries (Jack et al. 2013, Jack and Suri 2014, Lal and Sachdev 2015, Mbiti and Weil 2013). By the end of 2017, a total of 276 mobile money services have been offered in more than 90 countries (GSMA 2017), and there are about 2.5 million mobile service agents serving consumers worldwide, with the network growing at 25.2% annually (Suri 2017). Mobile money is mostly adopted in developing countries with 49.1% of 690 million customers in Sub-Saharan African and 34% in South Asia (GSMA 2017).

The earliest success of mobile money was largely based on rapid developments of mobile technologies that enabled customers to access basic services such as airtime top-up, cash in and cash out, and domestic person-to-person (P2P) payments.<sup>1</sup> As mobile money evolved, mobile network operators (MNOs) attempted to bundle more value-added services to differentiate themselves in the market (Hughes and Lonie 2007) Consumers are now able to use mobile money to pay their bills (such as utilities), to make person-tobusiness (P2B) payments, to receive payments (such as wages) from business, to receive government-toperson (G2P) payments, or to use insurance and credit services (Donovan 2012, Suri 2017). GCASH in Philippines, for example, developed bulk payment services that enabled business customers to make salary disbursements through mobile money accounts, which cuts GCASH administrative and operational expenses from dealing with large numbers of inperson cash payments (Lal and Sachdev 2015).

An important aspect of mobile money is that the service is enabled by and has evolved with different generations of mobile technologies, to connect to external platforms, such as banking systems and payment provider systems, and to achieve full commercial functionality.<sup>2</sup>

Mobile money services hence require different levels of technical support, which can be facilitated or constrained by the technology platform. Specifically, firstgeneration mobile technology (1G), which was introduced in early 1980s provided the most basic mobile communications by introducing voice services based on analog transmissions. The secondgeneration (2G) emerged in late 1980s and increased the voice capacity and extended mobile access to the masses by leveraging different techniques such as Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM). Third-generation (3G) technology, launched in 2000, introduced broadband mobile access with data optimization, allowing high-speed internet access and also access to high quality audio, video, and new services such as Global Roaming (Qualcomm 2014). Fourth-generation (4G) technology, which was introduced in 2010, delivers more capacity for faster and better broadband access and for additional services such as dynamic information access and wearable devices (Qualcomm 2014). From voice and text messages to web browsing, and to fully connected intelligent sensors and devices, mobile technologies play an important role in mobile money service by blurring geographical and cultural borders.<sup>3</sup> The evolution of mobile technologies highlights the consistent improvement in capacity and new functionality. For instance, the adoption of 3G technology significantly increased functionality, motivating rapid growth of mobile apps, whereas the adoption of 4G boosted data capacity for better service performance (Shrier et al. 2016).

Hence, mobile technology and mobile money represents a technology-service bundle that provides convenience and business value to customers (Jack and Suri 2014). The continued increase in mobile capacity and functionality during the evolution of mobile technologies may intensify or weaken market competition, and may complicate the impact of mobile money and service bundling on market performance of MNOs (Porter and Heppelmann 2014). This is because mobile money services require different levels of technical support, which can be facilitated, or constrained, by the technology platform on which any service is offered. As technologies advance quickly, the dynamics in continuous innovations may challenge the effectiveness of bundling strategies in offering mobile money services. For instance, certain technology platforms may be inadequate to support effective operations of certain mobile money services, and MNOs may overestimate their internal capabilities to offer such functionality.<sup>4</sup> Hence, it has been a challenge for MNOs to optimize their pricing schemes and align their service offerings under evolving technologies, as the value proposition may change for customers when new capabilities and options are available. Our research is motivated by this challenge and focuses on how the changes in mobile technology may affect the proliferation of mobile money. In particular, we address the following research questions: How do consumers choose mobile technologies when multiple generations of technologies coexist in the market? How do the provisions of mobile money and different generations of mobile technologies affect each other in demand?

A growing body of related literature has been on mobile banking (e.g., Xu et al. 2018), mobile payment (e.g., Mallat 2007, Xu et al. 2018), or mobile apps (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014) that focuses on their impact on sales, prices, and consumer surplus (Greenstein 1996, Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Goolsbee and Petrin 2004, Narayanan et al. 2005, Mbiti and Weil 2013). Recently a number of researchers have studied mobile money specifically (Jack and Suri 2014, Economides and Jeziorski 2017). Although similar, mobile money is different from mobile banking and mobile payment in important ways. Mobile banking refers to the extension of online banking to mobile devices (Lal and Sachdev 2015), and mobile payment is an addon service on mobile technology to facilitate fund transfers between individuals and/or merchants (Aron 2017). For both mobile banking and mobile payment, consumers are required to have a bank account in the back end. For mobile money, customers typically do not need bank accounts in the back end. Hence, th costs to access the service and switch between service providers are significantly lower than mobile banking and mobile payment, which has theoretical implica tions for consumer demands. Furthermore, the mobile technologies and mobile money are bundled together and affect each other in demand. The effect may come from within the different levels of technologies as the four-generation mobile technologies coexist in the market and act as substitutes. The strategy and outcome of bundling depend on marginal costs, valuation symmetry of individual products or services, and network effects such as network externality (Dewan and Freimer 2003, Prasad et al. 2010, Pang and Etzion 2012, Bhargava 2013, Etzion and Pang 2014).

We intend to narrow the literature gaps by examining the demand patterns of mobile technologies and mobile money. Using a data set on mobile money and mobile network operations in the emerging markets compiled from multiple data sources, we estimate the own- and cross-price elasticities for both mobile technologies and mobile money, and the demand effects between the mobile money and technologies. We also expand the estimations to other bundled financial services in addition to mobile money such as G2P, cross-sell, microinsurance, and international P2P (interP2P hereafter).

Our key findings are that: (1) Although the ownprice elasticities of all technologies are elastic, the own-price elasticity for 3G technology is lower than the others, suggesting that current, dominant technology tends to have more robust demand as compared with other technologies that are either declining (i.e., 1G and 2G) or new to the market (i.e., 4G). The cross-price elasticities are higher for the effect of a technology on a newer technology than on an older technology, suggesting that customers are more likely to substitute forward toward a newer technology than backward toward an older technology when the price increases for the technology. (2) The own-price elasticity with mobile money is lower, in magnitude, than that without mobile money, suggesting that mobile money differentiates the market and mitigates the competition for those MNOs that offer mobile money service. In addition, the change in price with mobile money has greater impact on the market share of the service without mobile money than the change in price without mobile money has on the market share of the service with mobile money. (3) The magnitude of the own-price elasticity without mobile money for 3G technology is the lowest as compared with those for other generations of technology, likely because 3G is the current, dominant technology. Once mobile money is included, the elasticities are at a similar level for all generations of technology.

Our paper makes several important contributions. First, financial technology has gained much attention among practitioners and researchers alike. As a form of financial technology, mobile money has been widely and increasingly used in the emerging markets but under-studied by researchers, although a body of literature has been growing on mobile banking and mobile payments. It is important to understand consumer demand patterns associated with mobile money. Among the limited number of studies on mobile money, most of them are on the user-level and country-level economic issues associated with successes and failures in mobile money adoption (e.g., Jack et al. 2013, Mbiti and Weil 2013, Lal and Sachdev 2015, Economides and Jeziorski 2017). The paucity of literature on the demand and market competition of mobile money points our contributions. Second, mobile money is bundled with mobile technologies. The coexistence of multiple generations of mobile technology provides an interesting research setting to study the substitution patterns between different generations of mobile technology and the interactions between mobile money and mobile technologies. The existing literature on estimating the demand for mobile technologies is scarce, especially when multiple generations of mobile technology coexist as substitutes. A deeper understanding of the demand patterns for mobile technologies helps advance our understanding of emerging, innovative financial services such as mobile money. Our study contributes to the literature by examining the effects of mobile money on consumer demand, conditional on the fast-evolving mobile technologies (from 1G to 4G), and by offering service bundling implications between mobile money and financial services.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a review of the background and related literature. Section 3 describes our research setting, data, and methodology. Section 4 presents our analyses, results, and robustness checks. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper with discussion on findings, contributions, and future studies.

## 2. Background and Literature

In this section, we discuss the background of mobile money and the multiple streams of literature relevant to our research. Specifically, our paper relates to the literature on mobile money, bundling of services and technologies, and demand estimation using aggregate data.

## 2.1. Mobile Money

In recent years, mobile money has become a commonly adopted financial tool in many developing economies, allowing mobile phone users to deposit, transfer, and withdraw funds without formal bank accounts (Jack and Suri 2011). As mentioned earlier, mobile money is different from mobile banking. Mobile banking is defined as using a mobile phone to access bank account, either by accessing the bank’s web page through the web browser on a mobile phone, via text messaging, or by using an app downloaded to the mobile phone (Federal Reserve Board 2013). Mobile money is a form of electronic money allowing the conduct of transactions through a mobile phone. Hence, it is typically provided by telecommunications companies, which is outside the formal banking system. For customers, mobile money is a menu item on their SIM card and allows them to engage in a variety of financial transactions such as payment on their mobile phone. Since bank accounts are not required, consumers need to go to a mobile money agent to register an account and deposit cash into the account (i.e., the mobile money). Once the mobile money is available, they can use their mobile phone to transfer that money to or receive money from anyone else in the country with a cell phone via their phone number. To cash out, they have to return to the agent who will cash the mobile money. The growth of agents is the key to the success of mobile money. Although not considered financial institutions, the agents act like financial institutions to help customers to cash in and cash out so that they can use mobile money.

The related literature in mobile money has mostly been organized into three streams: successes and failures in mobile money adoption, adoption patterns across different levels, and the impact of mobile money on individuals, local communities, and countries. In the first stream of literature, scholars suggest that the success of mobile money systems depends on many factors, with agent network being the most important one (Suri 2017). Investment in agent networks with training and monitoring is crucial to achieve scale in offering mobile money services (Aron 2017). The demand for mobile money services is also affected by agent quality and competition (Balasubramanian and Drake 2015). Aside from the agent network, scholars also find that strong branding, service options, retail infrastructure and pricing, network penetration and coverage, and regulatory environment all play an important role in successful adoption of mobile money services (Heyer and Mas 2009, Mas and Morawczynski 2009, Lal and Sachdev 2015, Economides and Jeziorski 2017). For instance, Lal and Sachdev (2015) compare five successful deployments of mobile money to five less successful ones and highlight the importance of operating with a reliable mobile network and with a trusted brand and business. The second stream of literature studies the adoption patterns of mobile money. Specifically, scholars have investigated characteristics at the household level (e.g., household demographics), firm level (e.g., business size and transaction volume), and country level (e.g., regulatory environment) (Jack and Suri 2011, Oritgão 2015, Aron 2017). The last major stream of literature examines the impact of mobile money across various levels. Mobile money services can provide great reduction in transaction costs and time, as well as improvements in convenience and security (Economides and Jeziorski 2017). Jack and Suri (2014) find that a successful launch of mobile money, such as M-PESA, can empower households to share risks, whereas Suri and Jack (2016) suggest that better access to mobile money services has resulted in increases in household consumption and savings, and thus decreases in poverty rates. Dong et al. (2018) find that offering bundled mobile money services can lead to different impacts on the various value chain members (MNOs, banks, and end users).

Although there is a growing interest in academic research to investigate mobile money, most has focused on user-level or country-level issues that affect mobile money adoption. However, as mobile money has been bundled with mobile technology (i.e., mobile money is enabled by different generations of mobile technologies), a noticeable gap in the literature exists in understanding the challenges in bundling the mobile money and mobile technology in terms of demand patterns and pricing schemes. Our study intends to narrow the literature gap.

## 2.2. Bundling of Services and Technologies

Mobile money is a big business, but it is not easily profitable. By 2011, only M-PESA had achieved operational profitability (Aron 2017). The success of mobile money depends on the value of the service offered to end-users, and the enhanced value comes from the effectiveness of service bundles. Product and service bundling creates revenue and reduces churn (Prince and Greenstein 2014), and it can also help differentiate a firm in a competitive environment (Stremersch and Tellis 2002). Service and technology bundles allow a firm to take advantage of complementarities from service and technological innovations (Milgrom and Roberts 1990). Yet the effectiveness of product and service bundles depends on many factors, one of which is value chain member coordination. For instance, when bundling involves various channel members, conflicts in the channels can weaken the incentives for bundling (Bhargava 2013). Dong et al. (2018) find that offering bundled mobile money services increases profits of banks, while it decreases profits of MNOs.

Technology development is another important factor, but has received less attention in the literature. Technologies, particularly wireless technologies, are par ticularly important in mobile money services as they can provide connectivity, protect secure data, provide reliability in service, and encourage product and service innovation for users across the globe. The popularity of mobile services is largely dependent on wireless technology systems. For example, technology companies are strengthening mobile money’s ability to operate with online payment systems. Webtribe’s Jambopay, Symbiotics’ Moca, Intrepid Data System’s iPay, and Pesapal are a few examples of payment services that allow buyers to pay for goods and services over the internet using their phones (Kendall et al. 2011). Another example is near field communications (NFC), which is a technology that allows devices to communicate through mere proximity by waving a specially equipped phone or card near a receiving device, as opposed to having to physically swipe it (Donovan 2012). NFC, enabled by 4G and potentially boosted by 5G, could serve to make transactions more efficient and secure by reducing errors, such as those that arise from mistyped numbers. In th coming years, more phones will be equipped with NFC, and for mobile money, this means that transactions can be completed by waving a phone near a receiver, as opposed to having to text value to a recipient. However, it has also been found that numbers of high-profile mobile money services were unable to scale up and meet the capacity demands of increasing numbers of consumers.<sup>5</sup> Without understanding the dynamic changes of mobile technology systems on user demand for mobile money services, one cannot fully understand the opportunities and challenges MNOs face when bundling mobile money services under different technology systems. Hence, to fill the literature gap, we examine the role of service bundling, and in particular, how mobile technology affects consumer demand for bundling.

## 2.3. Demand Estimation of Bundling: A Structural Estimation Approach

Demand estimation in differentiated markets has been studied in depth in economics, marketing, and information systems literature (e.g., Berry 1994, Berry et al. 1995, Kadiyali et al. 2001, Narayanan et al. 2005, Ghose and Han 2014), and there is a wide body of literature that documents the models of demand estimation. The aggregate demand estimation model can be estimated using market-level price and quantity data, and it handles the endogeneity of prices (Nevo 2000). The wider literature on demand estimation using this method also estimate demand for new product categories, such as mobile applications, under different industries such as automobiles, computers, books, and cable TV (Greenstein 1996, Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Goolsbee and Petrin 2004, Narayanan et al. 2005, Ghose and Han 2014). More recently, Guajardo et al. (2016) use this method to study the effects of various product and service attributes on the demand for automobiles in the U.S., and McKie et al. (2018) employ the aggregate demand estimation model to study the impact of product and seller characteristics on the demand for refurbished products in the presence of new and used products. Similar to these studies, we leverage the Berry (1994) method for demand estimation and add to the literature by estimating the impact of price and wireless technologies and bundling characteristics on demand for mobile money services, using aggregate data.

## 3. Data and Methodology 3.1. Research Setting and Data

The research setting is the emerging markets for mobile money services. MNOs are the service providers of mobile money. The markets are bounded by countries; that is, a country is a market where multiple MNOs compete in. For example, in Kenya, four MNOs compete in the country by offering mobile money services: Airtel (Bharti Airtel), Orange (Telkom Kenya), Safaricom, and yuMobile (Essar Telecom). MNOs also offer different generations of mobile technology including 1G, 2G, 3G, and 4G. The mobile money services can be used with any generation of the technology. Consumers choose the generation of mobile technology and whether to use mobile money or not. Mobile money services include basic service items such as bill pay, domestic P2P, and additional mobile financial services such as G2P, InterP2P, cross-sell, and microinsurance.

We complied a data set from multiple data sources including proprietary data provided by a market research company and public data published by the World Bank. The market research company specializes in the telecommunication industry and operates mobile money deployment trackers to monitor global mobile money services. The first data set is the information collected from the mobile money deployment trackers that contains the MNO’s names, the mobile money service’s brand names (e.g., M-PESA in Kenya), the mobile money’s launch dates, technology platforms (1G, 2G, 3G, or 4G), and additional financial services including G2P, InterP2P, cross-sell, and microinsurance. The second data set contains quarterly prices and market information of MNOs (e.g., average revenue per user (ARPU) and total connections) between 2000 and 2014. The databases include 407 MNOs from 79 countries.<sup>6</sup> We also collected gross domestic product (GDP) and population data for all countries from the World Bank database to calculate GDP per capita.

To better understand our data, we constructed several figures. Figure 1 shows the average total con nections and average ARPU per MNO in the data. Clearly, from 2000 to 2014, the mobile technology has been increasingly used and the price as measured by ARPU has continued to drop, likely due to heightened competition. Figure 2 shows the number of MNOs that offered mobile money and bundled services. Although the bundled services (G2P, InterP2P, crosssell, and microinsurance) have remained relatively stable over time, mobile money has taken off with a rapid growth rate since 2008. It can be seen that 2G is the most commonly used mobile technology, 3G and 4G are increasingly adopted, and 1G is declining. Brazi has the lowest percentage of mobile money users, whereas Tanzania has the highest percentage of mobile money users.

## 3.2. Econometric Model

We apply the Berry (1994) method for demand estimation in our paper. The method has been widely used in economics, marketing, and information systems fields (e.g., Berry 1994, Berry et al. 1995, Kadiyali et al. 2001, Narayanan et al. 2005, Ghose and Han 2014). This model allows us to estimate the impact of price of technology and mobile money on demand using aggregate data. The utility of an individual i purchasing service j is

$$
U _ {i j} = \alpha p _ {j} + \beta \mathbf {x} _ {j} ^ {\prime} + \xi_ {j} + \epsilon_ {i j},\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { j }$ is the average price (ARPU) of purchasing service $j , \pmb { x } _ { j } ^ { \prime }$ is a vector of characteristics observed by both the researchers and consumers, $\xi _ { j }$ is a vector of characteristics (i.e., service characteristics, environmental characteristics, demographic characteristics, etc.) unobserved by the researchers but observed by consumers, and $\epsilon _ { i j }$ is an error term representing consumer $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ idiosyncratic preferences for service $j .$ Then, the aggregate utility for service j is

$$
\delta_ {j} = \alpha p _ {j} + \beta \mathbf {x} _ {j} ^ {\prime} + \xi_ {j}.\tag{2}
$$

Using Berry’s (1994) inversion method, we obtain:

$$
\ln (s _ {j}) - \ln (s _ {0}) = \delta_ {j} = \alpha p _ {j} + \beta \mathbf {x} _ {j} ^ {\prime} + \xi_ {j},\tag{3}
$$

where $s _ { j }$ is the market share of service $j$ and $s _ { 0 }$ is the market share of the outside option. We compute $s _ { j }$ as the ratio of “Total Connections” for a service in a country in a quarter to the total population in that country in that quarter. For example, Indosat in Indonesia in the first quarter of 2006 had 12,955,680 total connections, and Indonesia’s population in the first quarter of 2006 was 226,902,591, thus, Indosat had a market share of 0.06 (6%). $s _ { 0 }$ is computed as the ratio of the difference between total population in a country in a quarter and the sum of Total Connections for all services in that country in that quarter to the total population in that country in that quarter. For example, the market share of the outside option $( s _ { 0 } )$ ) in Indonesia in the first quarter of 2006 was 0.78 (78%). It should be noted that the choice of the outside option will not affect our results, qualitatively, because the magnitude of the outside option (and therefore, its market share) would affect the dependent variable, in Equation (3), for all observations in a given market by the same amount quantitatively.

The nonnested model described above violates the property of independence of irrelevant alternatives in our setting. For example, customers who use 3G technology may be more likely to choose another MNO that provides 3G technology when their first

Thus, in order to estimate the consumer demands for different technologies and different services, we build a number of models in which we nest our data either by technology (e.g., 3G) or by service (e.g., mobile money).

For the technology nested model, we create five nests $( g = 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 )$ based on technology offering where g 1 contains 1G services, g 2 contains 2G services, $g = 3$ contains 3G services, $g = 4$ contains 4G services offerings, and g  0 contains the outside option. The nested logit model is derived using the Berry (1994) inversion method:

$$
\ln (s _ {j}) - \ln (s _ {0}) = \alpha p _ {j} + \beta \mathbf {x} _ {j} ^ {\prime} + \sigma \log (s _ {j | g}) + \xi_ {j},\tag{4}
$$

where σ is the substitutability factor $( 0 < \sigma < 1 )$ and $s _ { j \vert g }$ is the market share of service j in nest g.

## 3.3. Estimation Procedure

Price (ARPU) and nested market share are potentially endogenous because they may be correlated with unobserved covariates. Further, the decision of an MNO to offer services such as mobile money and other bundled services may also be potentially endogenous. The Durbin $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic of 99.749 $( p < \dot { 0 } . 0 0 1 )$ and the Wu-Hausman F statistic of 50.219 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ confirms the presence of the endogeneity, statistically. Therefore, we use instrumental variables (IVs) for these endogenous variables, which is a standard treatment (e.g., Guajardo et al. 2016, McKie et al. 2018). Specifically, we use the sum of the characteristics (including the sum of dummy variables for mobile money and all the bundled services) of other observations within a nest in a market as instrumental variables. The sum of the characteristics of other services are appropriate instrumental variables because they are excluded from the utility equation $( U _ { i j }$ or $\delta _ { j }$ does not depend on the characteristics of other observations) but correlated with prices via the markups in the first-order conditions (Berry 1994, Berry et al. 1995). A similar set of instruments have been used in prior information systems and operations management studies that use aggregate choice models (e.g., Ghose and Han 2014, Guajardo et al. 2016, McKie et al. 2018). We use two-stage least squares (2SLS) method to estimate our model (Angrist and Krueger 1994).

In the first stage of 2SLS, the null hypothesis that the excluded instruments have no explanatory power is rejected (Staiger and Stock 1997). In particular, the F-statistics for all endogenous variables are much greater than the recommended threshold value of 10, demonstrating the strength of our instrumental variables. Second, we run the test of under-identification of instruments. The null hypothesis that our instruments are under-identified is also rejected (the Anderson Canonical Correlation LM Statistic was 340.645 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) $ ). These test results demonstrate the validity of our model and the use of instrumental variables to address the endogeneity (Guajardo et al. 2016, McKie et al. 2018).

Third, we used GDP growth, instead of GDP per capita, as a control variable. As shown in Table A.12 in the appendix, the coefficients of price and nested market share remain consistent.

To account for this, we controlled for credit card diffusion in each country in our model. Although we were not able to collect the credit card diffusion data for all years in our data, we collected the data for 2011 and 2014. Therefore, we used the average credit card diffusion between 2011 and 2014 as the credit card diffusion for all quarters in our data and added it as a control variable in our estimation. The results are presented in Table A.13 in the appendix and again, the coefficients of price and nested market share remain consistent.

## 5. Concluding Remarks

Mobile money is an innovative financial technology bundled with both basic and value-added financial services and supported by multiple generations of mobile technologies. It has been successful in providing financial inclusion for the under-served populations in the developing world. As mobile technologies advance and competition increases, MNOs, however, are challenged with adapting to newer technologies and adjusting service offerings. Using a data set on mobile money services and mobile network operations in the emerging markets compiled from multiple sources, we examine the demand patterns of mobile technologies and mobile money. Using a structural model, we estimate the own- and cross-price elasticities for both mobile technology and mobile money, and the demand effects between mobile money and mobile technologies. We also extend the estimations to other financial services such as G2P, cross-sell, microinsurance, and international P2P.

We find that 3G technology has a significantly smaller own-price elasticity than other generations of mobile technology, suggesting that 3G technology is least sensitive to changes in its own price. This is likely due to the fact that 3G technology was the current technology in demand, thereby having more robust demand. Interestingly, when the price increases for 1G, 2G, and 3G technologies, customers switch to other technologies by substituting forward. That is, more customers are to purchase newer technology when the price for the technology they use increases. We also find that mobile money mitigates the competition among MNOs by making customers less sensitive to the mobile technologies. When mobile money is added to the technology-service bundle, the elasticity is greatly reduced, suggesting that consumers are less likely to abandon the service or to defect from the MNO. By adopting mobile money, MNOs can effectively differentiate the market with an innovative mobile service which reduces the intensity of price competition for these MNOs but increases the strengths against those without mobile money. Furthermore, we find that mobile money increases the market share of an MNO for all generations of mobile technology, whereas the newer technologies (3G and 4G) increase the most.

We also find that bundling mobile money with other financial services such as G2P, cross-sell, and microinsurance may negatively affect the market share. This suggests that the tradeoff between the benefits of service bundling, such as more consumer choices of services and more deeply differentiated market, and the costs may lead to a balanced bundling strategy for MNOs, which varies with the technology platform that supports the services. It is important to note, however, despite the negative effects of G2P, cross-sell, and microinsurance bundles, the positive effect of mobile money continue to dominate so that the combined effects on market share remain positive.

Our findings have important managerial implications. First, although mobile money has been purported as a technological innovation to promote financial inclusion for the under-served populations, our findings indicate that it also benefits MNOs. Hence, it is important for managers to understand the role of mobile money in both providing social good and making a profit. Second, the effect of mobile money remains positive as mobile technology evolves and mobile capacity increases. From 1G to 4G, mobile money consistently serves as a competitive advantage for MNOs. However, this does not mean that the effect stays unchanged over the generations of mobile technology Instead, when the 3G technology allows web browsing as a major upgrade of mobile functionality, MNOs with mobile money have a substantially larger set of options to differentiate from those without mobile money; and as a result, mobile money implemented with 3G and 4G leads to larger market shares than that with 1G and 2G. Third, although service bundling with mobile money has benefits for users, it comes with costs In anticipation of competitive threats, MNOs may decide to offer additional value-added services such as G2P and microinsurance, on top of the basic services such as airtime and domestic P2P. However, we find that bundling mobile money with G2P and microinsurance may trim down the benefit of mobile money

This may indicate that services without a clear value proposition for customers, for example, services that are too costly or complex to use, may cause diminishing returns for MNOs when they continue to add options. The results highlight that there may exists an optimal level of service bundling for MNOs in offering mobile money services.

Our study has a few limitations and we believe future research, addressing our limitations and exploring the following avenues, can contribute further to both theory and practice on mobile money. First, our data are for emerging markets. Hence, the findings should be generalized with caution. Nevertheless, although limited for emerging markets, our final data (used for all estimations) includes 68 countries that had a combined GDP of over 4 trillion in 2014. Second, due to data limitations, we use a binary measure for mobile money and other bundled services. It would be ideal if we could measure the usage of mobile money and other financial services.
