# Contextual Targeting in mHealth Apps: Harnessing Weather Information and Message Framing to Increase Physical Activity

Abstract. This paper addresses how real-time weather information acquired through mobile technology can be leveraged to enhance the efficacy of mobile interventions for spurring users' healthier behaviors. Through a field experiment that each participant experience different weather conditions in two different treatment periods under the gain or loss interventions, we found that the effects of gain or loss interventions across sunny and cloudy weather are not uniformly distributed. Loss intervention induces higher levels of fulfillment of exercise goals than gain intervention in sunny weather, whereas gain interventions are more effective than loss interventions in cloudy weather. We also provided empirical evidence to uncover the underlying mechanisms and rules out alternative explanations. The follow-up experiment reveals that weather-based intervention can be used repeatedly over time without losing its effectiveness. Moreover, our result suggests that the observed effect is more evident for people with a lower exercise level and living in areas of lower income. Our study provides theoretical guidance and practical implications for academics, healthcare businesses, and policymakers on the strategy of using weather-based messaging for enhancing physical activity levels.

## Introduction

In developed countries, most adults spend a large portion of their time being sedentary. Advanced technology has allowed individuals to get through their daily lives without leaving their seats. According to the World Health Organization, up to 85% of people globally lead sedentary lifestyles. This is a worrying figure as physical inactivity holds serious implications for people's health. Linked to approximately two million deaths per year, sedentary lifestyles increase all causes of mortality; double the risk of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes; and increase the risks of colon cancer, high blood pressure, osteoporosis, lipid diseases, depression and anxiety. $^{1}$ This global healthcare issue has prompted the urgent development of healthcare solutions that educate and motivate people toward exercising more.

Among the health information technology (IT) academics, a stream of healthcare literature has emerged to study the use mobile applications to increase physical activity levels. These techniques include the use of (1) visualization and self-tracking (Vorrink et al. 2016), (2) social comparison and norms (Liu et al. 2019), (3) social support (Consolvo et al. 2006), (4) performance feedback (Fanning et al. 2012), (5) monetary incentives (Acland and Levy 2015), and (6) goal-setting (Liu and Willoughby 2018). Overall, these studies have produced mixed results, with some interventions showing promise, whereas others do not work outrightly. A recent meta-analysis of published works on the topic reveals that existing app-based interventions are largely ineffective in increasing participants' average daily steps, especially over long periods of time (Romeo et al. 2019). Literature found that once the interventions were removed, the benefits of intervention disappeared with it (Patel et al. 2016). Moreover, prior research documented that mobile health apps are not as effective toward vulnerable populations such as low-income groups (Western et al. 2021). Thus, there is an ongoing call for research toward more sophisticated personalized strategies that could bring about sustained intervention effects in mobile health (mHealth) applications.

On a closer look, we find that existing interventions have failed to consider contextual factors that influence exercise decisions. Among various contextual cues, weather is a fundamental and well-documented factor that influences the decision to exercise outdoors (Wagner et al. 2019). Inclement weather affects the ability to exercise, especially for population groups with lower socioeconomic status, as these individuals may not have easy access to indoor gyms (Eisenberg and Okeke 2009). Incidentally, it is found that population groups with a lower socioeconomic status also lead a more sedentary lifestyle compared with their affluent counterparts (Shuval et al. 2017). Moreover, studies have long established that weather directly affects one's mood (Cunningham 1979). Negative mood is known to reduce the ability to perform taxing tasks such as going through physical exercise (Fishbach and Labroo 2007). Failing to consider weather-related factors in the design of app-based intervention inevitably results in suboptimal mHealth designs. Considering these aspects, the omission of weather cues in mHealth apps represents an obvious and pressing research gap within the current health IT literature.

Contextual targeting techniques have been increasingly studied and used in marketing applications over the last few years (Li et al. 2017, Ghose et al. 2019). Existing contextual targeting have relied on users' static geographic location (Andrews et al. 2016), shopping paths (Ghose et al. 2019), time of day (Danaher et al. 2015), and weather conditions (Li et al. 2017) to nudge consumers toward making purchases. Of particular interest, Li et al. (2017) show that the effectiveness of ad copies can be enhanced by leveraging the weather condition faced by the user. Despite the relative success of using weather cues to spur marketing outcomes, the integration of these weather-based designs to health IT applications has been scarce.

The integration of weather information to mHealth applications is not a straightforward task due to important practical and theoretical differences between sending a healthcare message and a marketing message. First, users bear different mindsets when processing health and marketing interventions. In particular, engaging in health-related activities (e.g., exercising) typically involves bearing an immediate cost to receive a delayed reward, for example, good health or disease prevention (Thaler and Sunstein 2009). Conversely, users gain immediate gratification from the act of shopping as the purchase of products can directly fulfill their current desires and needs. In the former case, individuals tend to devalue the future rewards of exercising in favor of immediate rewards of sedentary behaviors (Lin and Epstein 2014) due to self-control issues. These issues are more likely to arise when individuals' choices and associated consequences are separated in time (Thaler and Sunstein 2009), which are less prevalent in the marketing settings. Apart from the differences in gratification, the resources needed for each setting are distinct. For marketing applications, monetary resources are needed for making purchases, whereas health applications require the commitment of exercise time and physical efforts (e.g., physical exertion, fatigue, and even bodily aches). Thus, individuals arguably need to overcome greater barriers of committing time and effort to exercise. Consequently, the mechanism underlying consumer purchase behavior is unlikely to be similar to that of exercise decisions (Varshney 2014). Given that the mechanism in each context is plausibly different, weather-based targeting strategies that work well in marketing applications may not necessarily work well for healthcare settings.

Second, the design of any intervention strategy needs to consider its relative impact on different user subgroups. Characteristics important in defining consumer subgroups for marketing purposes may not necessarily overlap with those considered in health settings, given that the objective of each application is different. Marketing applications are geared toward profit maximization, whereas mHealth applications aim at improving personal wellness and even public health (to some extent). Given these different objectives, intervention efficacy among subpopulations with low income and with lower exercise level is an important moderating factor to consider in mHealth applications, although this factor may not be even relevant to the design of most marketing interventions.

Finally, apart from practical differences, theoretical ambiguity clouds our understanding of how weather-based targeting should be operationalized for mHealth applications. On the one hand, the mood congruity perspective contends that one's mood state facilitates the recall of affectively congruent information from memory, making this congruent information more effective in persuasion tasks (Schwarz and Clore 1983, Pham 2008). According to this perspective, people in a positive mood (i.e., when experiencing sunny weather) respond better to a gain-framed intervention, whereas individuals in a negative mood (i.e., when experiencing rainy/cloudy weather) should respond better to a loss-framed intervention (Forgas and Bower 1987, Pham 2008).

In contrast, the mood-as-resource view argues that positive mood acts as a psychological buffer to counteract the impact of negative information (Trope and Neter 1994, Reed and Aspinwall 1998). As such, the use of a loss-framed message for individuals experiencing positive mood would be more effective in nudging them toward action, as these individuals can better cope with the negative emotional impact of the loss-framed message. However, individuals with a negative mood lack the mood resources, and would hold a defensive tendency toward the loss-frame message, thereby making a gain-framed message more effective in such cases (Keller et al. 2003). Because of these two conflicting theoretical perspectives, it is unclear how mHealth apps should position their messaging strategy with respect to weather cues.

Responding to the previous research gap, we aim to study and empirically identify the optimal weather and message framing combination to solicit behavioral changes toward greater physical activity levels over time. In addition to the main question, we are also interested in assessing the efficacy of weather-based targeting on different user groups, with the aim of uncovering potential heterogeneous effects. To this end, we design and evaluate the effectiveness of mHealth messages in spurring exercise levels while considering the real-time weather information of a user. We conduct three field experiments using between- and within-subject designs. First, the between-subject experiment is used to first test whether the use of framed messages (i.e., gain or loss) is more effective than the messages without framing (i.e., neutral). After establishing the basic result, we go on to investigate the optimum message framing to use under different weather conditions using a within-subjects experiment. A third experiment is conducted to assess whether the optimum weather message intervention would sustain under repeated use. By working with users who experienced different weather conditions, the study can demonstrate the advantages of delivering dynamic and personalized interventions based on different weather experienced by users in various locations. We collaborated with an activity-tracking app provider and randomly assigned our test subjects to one of the gain and loss mHealth messages that encourages user participation in 10,000 steps challenge under different weather conditions.

Our basic result suggests that any framed messages (gain or loss) lead to better exercise outcomes compared with messages without framing. Next, within-subject experiment results support the mood-as-resource perspective. A loss-framed message is found to be more effective on sunny days in the completion of the physical activity challenge. At the same time, a gain-framed message is more effective in spurring user completion on cloudy days. Interestingly, our follow-up experiment shows that weather-based intervention remains effective when the mHealth intervention was repeated four times over a period of 55 days. Various robustness checks are performed to ascertain that the results would hold under various assumptions. Furthermore, we found that these contextual targeting effects are especially pronounced for users with instrumental motives (e.g., exercise to lose weight, stay healthy) but are not so for users with consummative motives (e.g., exercise for the mere enjoyment of the act of exercise itself). In addition, our subanalyses reveal that the messaging strategy following mood-as-resource view is more effective for users with a lower exercise level and for users coming from a lower-income area.

Our study aims to make several academic and practical contributions. First, our study is a response to the call for more sophisticated mHealth solutions to induce sustained increases in physical activity over time (Romeo et al. 2019). The domain of healthcare and well-being is an industry to which mobile-based interventions are heavily demanded, given that the market for such services has steadily increased to USD53 billion in recent years. Current mHealth designs have failed to consider weather conditions along with the optimum messaging to use, although it is apparent that the efficacy of nudging strategies is dependent on contextual factors. Moreover, the use of contextual targeting is largely restricted to marketing outcomes (Andrews et al. 2016, Li et al. 2017), to which the contextual targeting literature lacks insights on how the technique may be applied in alternative settings, such as that of the healthcare context. Our study fills the gap in the mHealth app literature by executing a randomized field study to evaluate the optimum pairing of message framing and weather condition for spurring users' activity levels. Our results point out future directions for mHealth designs that would lead to sustained physical activity levels and can be used repeatedly, using cost-efficient instruments (i.e., weather cues). Not only do our study results help academics to better understand how weather cues work in unison with messaging strategy for healthcare settings, the success in using weather for user targeting could spark off other related interventions based on other alternative contextual cues.

Second, we make a theoretical contribution to the psychology literature pertaining to mood-related effects. Although the mood-congruity perspective was found to hold in certain marketing applications (Adaval 2001, Li et al. 2017), other works have found the mood-as-resource perspective to be more applicable in other persuasion-related contexts (Trope and Neter 1994, Raghunathan and Trope 2002). To date, no study has demonstrated the conditions by which each theoretical perspective would manifest. As such, there remains a theoretical puzzle on which of these perspectives would hold in the healthcare setting. Our work contributes to this literature by reconciling both perspectives by clarifying the conditions under which each of the theoretical perspectives would manifest. Doing so would generate deeper and precise knowledge on how contextual targeting should be deployed in healthcare and nonhealthcare settings that involve user nudging.

Finally, our study makes practical contributions by demonstrating how to leverage users' real-time weather information effectively within mHealth applications. $^{2}$ This proof of concept assures mobile app companies and app developers of the viability of using weather information in improving user outcomes, which serves as an input toward their investment decisions. In addition, our work provides design guidelines on how to directly use weather information with messaging formats to spur users toward better health behaviors. Given the costs of testing and designing specialized features in app is non-trivial, the validation of the effectiveness of weather-based targeting in this work would produce cost savings to app makers and companies. Furthermore, the optimal design uncovered by our study is also found to be effective toward at-risk populations (e.g., individuals with low exercise level) and thereby is a plausible solution that does not widen the health disparity gap between the rich and poor populations.

## Background and Related Literature mHealth Nudges

A major limiting factor toward optimum decision is the limited cognitive resources and self-control issues. Should individuals be able to devote full attention in processing complete information and have unlimited cognitive and self-control abilities, they would be able to make better health-related decisions (Thaler and Sunstein 2009). A nudge, an intervention that steers people's behavior in an optimal way while maintaining their freedom of choice is one of the more effective strategies for spurring healthy behavior (Patel et al. 2018). In particular, nudges work by directing one's attention to relevant task features, behaviors to be performed, and potential outcomes, to help people to identify and apply appropriate strategies that reduce potential biases in decision making. For instance, providing a clear description of the future reward (e.g., highlighting that exercising can lead to a healthy blood sugar level) makes the long-term benefit more vivid and cognitively accessible to individuals, allowing them to place greater value in the future rewards of exercising. In addition, nudges help individuals to better control themselves by setting directions and manageable goals. Individuals can focus on required actions instead of expending efforts to establish action plans themselves (Thaler and Sunstein 2009). Nudge-based strategies can be and have been integrated within mHealth apps, including Apple health, MyFitnessPal, and Strava, to promote healthy behavioral change. Of particular importance involves understanding the optimum message to use under the different situations to enhance a nudge's effectiveness.

Globally, more than 2.5 billion mHealth apps were downloaded in 2021, representing a steady increase from 1.9 billion downloads in 2019 (Vincent 2022). In particular, activity-tracking apps have gained significant attention as they are believed to be capable of changing how people manage their physical activity and performance (Liu et al. 2019). Given high mobile penetration rates and frequent checking of mobile devices, $^{3}$ the mHealth applications are a great vehicle for driving health behavioral changes. At the same time, mobile technology enables users to track objective user outcomes (Ghose et al. 2013). Compared with traditional methods (e.g., TV, radio, or simple pedometers), mHealth applications provide more personalized information to users, which in turn promotes the self-knowledge that is needed for health behavior change (Bandura 1986). Early studies looking at interventions based on displaying user level information has seen initial success in spurring physical activity levels (Consolvo et al. 2006, Oliveira et al. 2016, Vorrink et al. 2016), although these improvements are not always sustained (Romeo et al. 2019).

Apart from user-level data, mobile applications allow for the collection of users' real-time and dynamic contextual information. Contextual information can vary widely across users and over time, and provides additional, novel information about the users. When contextual cues are used in conjunction with individual information, highly relevant and personalized messages are produced. The combination of the dual sources of information is a powerful differentiator from traditional broadcast mediums (e.g., TV or radio), as the call to action from broadcast mediums often lacks the individualized insights about its users. (Ghose et al. 2013). In addition, it is easy to scale up healthcare interventions to a wider population through mHealth apps, as app platforms can send personalized interventions through push notifications to thousands of users by simultaneously (Aral and Walker 2011). At the same time, mobile app platforms track objective user outcomes (Ghose et al. 2013), allowing for the evaluation and fine-tuning of the intervention efficacy. As such, a mHealth application is likely to be more effective than interventions conducted on traditional mediums.

## Weather as a Contextual Cue

Weather plays a central role in human life (Watson 2000) and accounts for 40% of the variability in an individual's mood (Persinger and Levesque 1983). Studies have shown that weather conditions can affect a myriad of human behaviors from shopping activities (Li et al. 2017) to making investment decisions (Hirshleifer and Shumway 2003, Goetzmann et al. 2015). Because of its impact, marketing applications have been using environment contexts such as weather to dynamically influence individuals (Marshall 2014).

Several psychological and physiological mechanisms have been suggested to explain weather's impact on mood (Persinger and Levesque 1983, Gist and Mitchell 1992). The most significant factor is the amount of sunlight. Greater exposure to sunlight causes the human body to produce more serotonin (Schwarz and Clore 1983) and $\beta$ -endorphins (Fell et al. 2014). Serotonin is a chemical that helps relay signals from one area of the brain to another and is known to play a significant role in mood balance and a general sense of well-being. Sunlight also boosts the production of $\beta$ -endorphins, chemicals involved in the "runner's high" phenomenon (Fell et al. 2014). When released into the blood, $\beta$ -endorphins induce mood enhancement and relaxation, making individuals feel more relaxed and less tense (Shah et al. 2012). More importantly, the effects of sunlight on human mood are rather immediate. Daily variation in sunlight explains a significant amount of mood change (Denissen et al. 2008). A mere 15–30 minutes of sunlight exposure (UV radiation) is sufficient to stimulate serotonin and $\beta$ -endorphin production that can bring about significant mental health benefits (Mead 2008). Thus, serotonin and $\beta$ -endorphin levels within individuals explain why people tend to be more positive and happy and less anxious and skeptical on days with sunny weather (Cunningham 1979, Howarth and Hoffman 1984).

Conversely, the cloudy or rainy weather reduces sunlight exposure, thereby interfering with the secretion of $\beta$ -endorphins and serotonin. Scholarly work has repeatedly found that the reduced level of these hormones on cloudy days with less direct sunlight leads to a negative mood. Denissen et al. (2008) find that people are more likely to feel nervous, distressed, and more tired during days with rainy weather. Similarly, Ettema et al. (2017) find that commuting on cloudy and rainy days are associated with unhappy feelings after the commute. Moreover, psychology studies suggest that negative mood experienced during gloomy weather may escalate toward mental conditions, like the seasonal affective disorder (Rosenthal et al. 1984), seasonal depression (e.g., “winter blues”; Kamstra et al. 2003), and nonseasonal depression (Goel et al. 2005). Weather-induced mood translates directly into human behavior. Prior literature found that people are less likely to make product or stock purchases (Hirshleifer and Shumway 2003, Li et al. 2017) and complain about a service (Bujisic et al. 2019) on days with low sunlight levels. Thus, from this set of literature, weather conditions not only affect the ability to exercise outdoors, but it has a generalized mood effect that can influence one’s motivation to exercise.

## Intervention Framing

Targeted messages are often sent with a certain framing. Framing strategies have been extensively studied in marketing and healthcare research to promote desired behaviors (Riet et al. 2008). Message framing can change people's acceptance of the message, especially if these messages are framed around potential losses and gains that an individual would receive. This effect is supported by the prospect theory which contends that people think in terms of expected utility relative to a reference point rather than absolute outcomes (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Within healthcare applications, message interventions can largely be framed in terms of the benefits of engaging in health behaviors (gain framing) or in terms of the costs of failing to engage in health behaviors (loss framing).

By emphasizing the potential utility one gets, different types of message framing can evoke different types of emotion, leading to different reactions. A gain framing provides emotional benefits because it evokes pleasure, enjoyment, and favorable feelings by implying potential gain, whereas a loss framing may induce negative emotional outcomes (i.e., “spoil the mood”) by alarming one to the threats, risks, and potential losses (Kahneman et al. 1982, Keller et al. 2003). Although several studies show that framed interventions, either gain or loss framed, induce a higher level of acceptance than neutral interventions (Bassett-Gunter et al. 2013), the relative effectiveness of gain and loss framing is highly nuanced and can depend on various factors, including the recipient’s mood when receiving the message.

## Theoretical Background

Psychology literature provides two contrasting theoretical perspectives of how mood might interact with message framing. These two theoretical views are guided by the mood congruity perspective (Schwarz and Clore 1983, Mayer et al. 1992) and mood-as-resource perspective (Reed and Aspinwall 1998, Raghunathan and Trope 2002). The mood-congruent perspective argues that mood serves as an informational input to people' evaluations, judgments, and behaviors (Schwarz and Clore 1983), to which mood-congruent thoughts such as those of causes, attributes, or outcomes would appear more applicable and compatible relative to mood-incongruent thoughts (Mayer et al. 1992). Thoughts associated with good mood may serve to cue other positive material in memory, which in turn affect one's ability to process positive stimulus and engage in helping behavior. This cognitive process and behavior are enacted in a loop as an innate effort to maintain and extend the state of positive mood (Isen et al. 1978). In contrast, individuals in negative moods are in a vigilant state that is psychologically more available to process negative messages (Schwarz and Clore 1983, Mayer et al. 1992). Accordingly, gain-framed messages are more effective in persuading individuals in good moods, whereas loss-framed messages are more persuasive on people with bad mood (Forgas and Bower 1987, Pham 2008). Past work (Li et al. 2017) has provided evidence of the mood congruity theory by showing that loss-framed ad copies are more effective in cloudy weather.

Conversely, the mood-as-a-resource perspective argues that positive mood serves as a psychological buffer against the affective costs of negative information, thereby enabling individuals to focus on the knowledge that is inherent in the information (Trope and Neter 1994, Raghunathan and Trope 2002). This is especially true when the negative information is diagnostic of an important aspect related to the message recipient. Negative information tends to highlight the shortcomings of one's present situation to the message recipient, wherein the processing of such information can generate guilt, anxiety, and dejection. However, acting on negative feedback can bring about improvement to one's long-term goals. Under the mood-as-a-resource view, individuals who are rich in positive mood are likely to feel less intimidated at the prospect of processing negative self-relevant information and would actively respond in favor of improving their long-term goals instead of fulfilling short-term outcomes of maintaining their positive mood (Trope and Fishbach 2000). Consequently, positive mood facilitates the search, elaboration, and revision of prior beliefs and intentions to receive negative self-relevant information. In contrast, individuals with negative mood lack the resources to cope with negative information. Specifically, negative mood decreases the ability to cope with the harmful emotional impact of negative self-relevant information. Thus, individuals with negative mood prioritizes the improvement of their mood state over the attainment of long-term benefits (Morris and Reilly 1987, Wegener and Petty 1994). In cases of negative mood, individuals would tend to superficially process negative information, whereas they are more inclined to process positive information to improve their existing mood.

Psychological literature suggests that underlying motives of the message recipient could be an important determinant on whether mood serves as an information or as a resource (Pham 1998, Yeung and Wyer 2004). Specifically, consummatory and instrumental motives are likely drivers of how mood affects decision making behaviors. Consummatory motivation underlie behaviors that are intrinsically rewarding, wherein such behaviors are performed for the pleasure and satisfaction of the behavior itself (e.g., exercising for pleasure). In contrast, instrumental motivation underlies behaviors that are not rewarding in themselves but are undertaken to achieve other goals that lie outside the activity itself (e.g., exercising to keep fit). Because consummatory-based activities are performed for the intrinsic enjoyment of the activity in itself, affective considerations are relatively important determinants of such behaviors compared with instrumental-led motives (Holbrook and Hirschman 1982). Correspondingly, individuals would often hold a representation of the activity in their minds and “see how it feels” to gauge the favorability of engaging in the activity.

The use of individual's feelings toward a task (also termed as the “how-do-I-feel-about-it?” heuristic in other works) are more prevalent for activities that are largely of a consummatory basis. This effect has been supported in a few studies. For example, when subjects are primed to hold consummatory motives in watching a movie (i.e., telling participants how enjoyable this movie will be), they are more likely to use their mood as informational input, to which their mood state was found to significantly influence their behavioral intentions toward watching a movie (Pham 1998). Similarly, in Yeung and Wyer (2004), consumers who are primed to use hedonic criteria for product evaluation tend to evaluate the product more (less) favorably when they feel happy (unhappy). Under consummatory contexts where mood is likely to be considered as an evaluative information, individuals are likely to behave in ways that are consistent with the mood congruity perspective, wherein information aligned to one's mood states would be more effective in invoking responses. This effect is further substantiated in Adaval (2001), which showed that consumers tend to remember product attributes that are congruent to their prevailing mood states for products that are mainly evaluated for their consummatory purposes.

When instrumental motive is the main factor underlying user decision, individuals are unlikely to use their mood as an evaluative input. Past studies have indeed found that mood states of individuals did not influence decisions when they are primed to perform instrumental evaluations of their decisions (Pham 1998, Adaval 2001, Yeung and Wyer 2004). Instead, mood functions as a background resource for processing self-relevant information received (Trope and Neter 1994, Trope and Pomerantz 1998). Instrumental modes of information processing often characterize scenarios that involves a change in current behavior, in exchange for a better outcome in a separate dimension (i.e., delayed gratification). Responding to health advisory is one such example, wherein the effort to reduce or halt an unhealthy behavior (e.g., smoking, recreational drug use) involves going through an unpleasant physical experience but doing so fulfils the long-term goal of improving one's future health. Studies that examined scenarios involving the receipt of health-related feedback have found that mood is used as a resource, to which negatively framed information is more likely to be processed by message recipients with a positive mood (Keller et al. 2003, Das et al. 2012). In contrast, individuals with negative mood are more inclined to process positive health-related information over negative ones (Raghunathan and Trope 2002).

Individuals may be using mHealth app for various possible reasons (Buckworth et al. 2007, Eikey and Poole 2014). On the one hand, users who intrinsically enjoy exercising may use mHealth app to enhance their exercise experience (e.g., track their performance to assess their progress, plan out exercise regimes). In such cases, these users would hold a consummatory motive toward app usage. Such users would be more likely to incorporate their mood as informational input when processing feedback information from the mHealth app, leading them making mood-congruent decisions. On the other hand, it is plausible that users use a mHealth app for the goals of staying healthy (e.g., losing weight, avoiding sedentary-related diseases) and not for the intrinsic enjoyment of exercising. In such instances, users bear instrumental motives toward the use of the app. The mood states of these users are likely to serve as a resource when these users process feedback information from the mHealth app. Given that it is unclear which user motive dominates mHealth app adoption and use, how weather-induced mood states would interact with message framing in this setting remains largely as an empirical question.

## Study Context

To test the effectiveness of different message framings across different weather conditions, we collaborated with one of the largest activity-tracking app companies in Asia to run our field experiments. The focal activity-tracking app has been downloaded more than a million times (cumulative as of June 2023) and has an average of 200,000 active users each week. The app tracks a user's physical activity using the pedometer sensor in smartphones and presents this information to users. If users do not turn the app off explicitly, the app would run in the background on the users' smartphone. This “always-on” feature allows for the tracking user's exact location in real time, which we use to obtain the weather information for each user. At the same time, nearly 90% of the users turn on location tracking and allow push notification, allowing us to deliver interventions to the majority of users successfully.

Following prior studies, we use cloud cover as a measure of weather conditions (Sanders and Brizzolara 1982, Hirshleifer and Shumway 2003, Goetzmann et al. 2015). Cloud cover is a representative index for weather conditions as it is directly related to sunlight, humidity, and precipitation (Sanders and Brizzolara 1982, Schwarz and Clore 1983, Cummings et al. 2002). Based on each user's daily location (i.e., latitude and longitude), we measure cloud cover as the percentage of cloud cover from sunrise to sunset. This cloud cover measure ranges from 0 to 10, with 10 indicating high cloud cover all day and 0 representing no cloud cover.

## Study 1

In this first study, our objective is to understand whether the use of framed messages (gain or loss) produces better exercise outcomes compared with messages without framing. We rely on a between-subject design, where we contrast treated users who either receive a gain-framed message or a loss-framed message with control users who received a neutral message (no-framing group). One of the major advantages of the between-group design is the simplicity of experiment design and its effectiveness to demonstrate the causal impact of the focal stimulus by contrasting it with a control condition. We randomly assigned each message type to 1,248 different users scattered across the country of our study. $^{4}$ Each user would only receive the message once in this experiment. To minimize the influence of extraneous shocks that happen over time, we limit this experiment to a three-day period in the spring of 2019. Push notification of the respective message was sent to users in various experiment groups at 10 a.m. on each of these three days to encourage them to participate in a challenge that required them to walk at least 10,000 steps on that day. We chose 10,000 steps because research shows that a daily 10,000-step walking exercise brings substantial health benefits for adults (Iwane et al. 2000, Le-Masurier et al. 2003).

Here, a gain-message highlights the health benefits for exercising and a loss-message highlights the potential physical ailments one would be prone to when they do not exercise. Both messages end by inviting the user to participate in a 10,000-step walking challenge. Table 1 details the messages that were sent to users under the gain and loss interventions. The neutral messages used in the control condition simply prompts recipients to walk 10,000 steps today, without highlighting any health benefits or risk associated with exercising. $^{5}$ For users who agreed to taking part in the challenge, the activity-tracking app would start counting the user's number of steps for the next 24 hours.

Within the study users, we have information on their gender, age, height, weight, and previous walking activity. This set of information is solicited through an in-app questionnaire presented to users on their first usage of the app. Age is reported as number. Gender is a dummy variable that indicates one if female and zero if male. Height is reported in centimeters, and weight is reported as kilograms. Previous walking activity is based on the number of steps walked by the user in the last seven days prior to the start of the study. To ascertain our randomization procedure is effective, we conducted a balance check by comparing the means of various covariates, including the age, percentage of female users, weight, height, and total steps walked in the previous week across the gain, loss, and neutral intervention groups. t test results are not statistically significant for all the variables (i.e., gender, age, height, weight, previous walking activity) across the experiment groups (see Table A1 in the online appendix), indicating that the study groups are well balanced.

Our main empirical analyses aim to estimate the likelihood of users have reached the target step goal, $Pr(Achievement_{i}=1)$ , a binary variable indicating whether the user has taken at least 10,000 steps. Specifically, we use the following linear probability specification to analyze the data from this field experiment:

## Study 2

Having established that framed messages are more effective in nudging users toward engaging in physical activity, we now investigate the optimum message frame to use for different weather conditions. Given that the between-subject design relies on randomization to account for extraneous effects, it may not fully account for certain location- and individual-related factors that are correlated to the weather experienced by each user, as weather conditions are not directly within the researcher's control in the randomization process. To address this issue, we relied on a within-subject experiment design wherein each participant is randomly assigned to receive a framed message intervention on separate days with different weather conditions (i.e., each user will receive the same message throughout the experiment). With repeated observations for each user, each individual serves as a control for herself, and we could account for potential individual idiosyncratic effects (i.e., differences in circumstances, personality, and historical behavior) through user-level fixed effects. At the same time, macro-level confounders such as district-level fixed effects are included along with the day-of-the-week fixed effects in our analyses. The latter is used to control potential extraneous effects happening on different days of the week (Trost et al. 2000). This set of controls directly addresses concerns on potential confounders associated with the local weather patterns.

However, the within-subject design may have a carry-over effect where the earlier exposure of the stimuli may affect the treatment outcomes of the users at a subsequent timing. To mitigate this concern, not only do we alternate the order in which the weather conditions are experienced (i.e., sunny-cloudy and cloudy-sunny), we also incorporate a cooling period in between treatments of the within-subject experiment (details described later). At the same time, it is plausible for colocated districts to share similar weather conditions. This can cause problems as there may be unobserved location-specific factors correlated with weather conditions that drive the results. To this end, we intentionally sample our participants from districts that are dispersed across the country so that study users are well spaced out and experienced good amount of weather variability across different locations. Another design consideration we did was to conduct the study with a larger number of subjects in anticipation that not all users will be able to experience different weather from the one they experience in the first condition. Doing so, we would have a reasonable number of users who experienced contrasting weather conditions at the end of the cooling period.

Specifically, our within-subject experiment was conducted during a one-month period in September 2022. We have chosen to launch our study in Fall to allow for more neutral weather conditions, so that the impact of sunny and cloudy weather can be felt more clearly. $^{6}$ In our test sites, the average temperature in fall is $12.6^{\circ}$ C, and the average cloud cover is 5.5. According to the Meteorological Administration of test sites, 50.02% of days are sunny in the year of our study. From these figures, it does not seem that the incidence of sunny days is especially rare in the experimental area, to the point where people value sunny days so much that they have an overwhelming desire to be outdoors.

The within-subject experiment was performed as follows. In the first treatment period (T1), we randomly sampled 1,000 users per day without replacement over a period of seven days, leading to a total of 7,000 sampled users. At 10:00 a.m. daily, each user's location was identified through GPS signals and was matched with the cloud cover for their district. Each test site is based off a district (covering roughly 5 km $^{2}$ ) in the experimental area. The location was algorithmically matched with the weather information pulled from the Application Programming Interface (API) provided by the Meteorological Administration. Following the standards from the Meteorological Administration of test sites, areas with cloud cover of 0–2 are coded as sunny, and those with 8–10 are coded as cloudy. To avoid ambiguous weather-related mood levels, we do not include users whose location had a cloud cover of 3–7 during the study period. As we already examined the relative effectiveness of framed (gain or loss) and no-framed intervention in Study 1, we randomly assign users who are experiencing sunny or cloudy weather to one of the framed intervention groups: gain- and loss-framed message groups.

## Results

We first look at the model-free results of within-subject experiment. Figure 2 shows the average effectiveness of the weather-based interventions under sunny and cloudy conditions. This set of results shows that the loss-framed group had a higher level of achievement (21.8%) than the gain-framed (15.1%) in sunny weather.

In contrast, model-free evidence shows that the gain-framed messages yielded a higher level of achievement (11.8%) relative to the loss-framed (5.5%) on cloudy days.

Next, we analyzed the impact of gain and loss intervention on achievement using the LPM. The regression coefficient in this experiment directly shows the difference between gain and loss intervention. Specifically, in sunny weather, $\beta_{2} + \beta_{3}$ indicates the incremental difference that loss intervention has on physical activity achievement relative to gain intervention. In cloudy weather, $\beta_{2}$ indicates the incremental difference that the loss intervention has on physical activity achievement relative to the gain one. The result is shown in Table 3, column 1. Results of using the number of steps as a dependent variable provide a qualitatively similar result to the first result (Table 3, column 2). In sunny weather ( $Sunny_{i}=1$ ), loss intervention is more effective in increasing the number of steps compared with the gain intervention ( $\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=0.482, p<0.01$ ), whereas in cloudy weather ( $Sunny_{i}=0$ ), the gain intervention leads to more steps taken compared with the loss intervention ( $\beta_{2}=-0.329, p<0.01$ ).

## Effectiveness of Intervention on Subsequent Days After Targeting

We further test whether each study intervention can lead to improvements in physical activity on days right after the main intervention (i.e., no messages were given after the main intervention). To this end, we constructed a panel data using the average daily steps each user had taken over seven days before receiving the intervention and daily steps for each of the seven days after receiving the intervention. We contrast the changes in steps of treated users with that of control users using a difference-in-difference (DID) framework. For this analysis, we considered users who received loss-framed messages as the treatment group and those who received gain-framed messages control group, respectively. We rely on using $\#Steps_{it}$ as the dependent variable. This variable was log-transformed to account for the skewness in the data. We used the following specification:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \# S t e p s _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * L o s s _ {i} + \beta_ {2} * A f t e r _ {t} + \beta_ {3} * (L o s s _ {i} * A f t e r _ {t}) \\ \quad + \vec {\tau} ^ {T} \vec {X} _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array} \tag {3}

This means gain framing in cloudy weather is effective in leading physical activity for the consecutive six days after the treatment. This result collectively suggests that the single message intervention remains effective in spurring users to increase their exercise levels for at least the next six days.

## Mechanisms and Additional Analyses Mechanisms

In this section, we conduct analyses to understand the underlying mechanisms and to assess heterogeneity effects of weather-based targeting. As theorized earlier, the user motive will likely determine the theoretical perspective (i.e., mood-congruity and mood-as-a-resource) that will be dominant. We conduct an empirical test to affirm the role of user motive in influencing the efficacy of the weather message intervention.

To do so, we collaborated with our partner company and obtained survey responses on the users' motives for installing and using focal apps. Study participants were asked to indicate their main reason for using the focal app and were asked to choose the best option out of the following five options: (1) manage health; (2) fun and enjoyment of exercising; (3) improve self-confidence; (4) none of the above; and (5) do not want to answer. Here, users who participated in the mHealth app for the goals of managing health and improving self-confidence represent users with instrumental motives toward app use. In contrast, users who sought intrinsic enjoyment of the exercise activity in itself tend to hold a consummatory motive toward health app use. To avoid ambiguous use purpose and undefined user motives, we do not include users who did not indicate their app use purpose. We split users into two different motives and replicated the main analysis for users with instrumental and consummatory motives, respectively.

The results are documented in Table 5. We first consider users with instrumental motives. In column 1 of Table 5, it is observed that the impact of loss message is more effective in increasing physical activity compared with the gain message during sunny conditions ( $\beta_{2} + \beta_{3} = 0.077, p < 0.01$ ). In contrast, in cloudy weather, the result shows that the impact of gain framing is more effective than loss framing in increasing physical activity ( $\beta_{2} = -0.056, p < 0.05$ ). These results collectively show behavioral changes among individuals with instrumental motives are consistent with the directions projected by the mood-as-resource view (i.e., sunny-loss, cloudy-gain).

In contrast, users with consummatory motives show a reverse pattern. In sunny weather (column 2 of Table 5), the gain intervention is more effective than the loss intervention ( $\beta_{2} + \beta_{3} = -0.0541$ , p < 0.01), whereas the loss intervention is more effective than the gain intervention under cloudy conditions ( $\beta_{2} = 0.0608$ , p < 0.01). These results support the notion that individuals with consummatory motives are consistent with the mood-as-information view (i.e., sunny-gain, cloudy-loss). Overall, the results indicate that how users process and react to messages is dependent on their usage motives.

## Alternative Explanation

Next, we attempt to rule out potential alternative mechanisms. It may be plausible that users are more likely to see other exercising outdoors on sunny day, which can in turn cause people to feel a loss compared with those exercising outside and would react more strongly to the loss-framed messages. To directly rule out the possibility of such a social influence effect at work, we performed a test where we accounted for the possibility of users seeing a park from their locations. In our data, we are able to tell the distance between each user and various parks that are close to the user. We then split study participants into two groups based on the median number of parks that each user can access within 500 m of their location. We chose 500 m as these parks would be visible to most people at this distance (roughly a six-minute walk $^{8}$ ). Across the experimental sites, we first map out every park's latitude and longitude. All parks are represented as polygonal forms representing a two-dimensional space instead of a single point. Using each user's latitude and longitude at the treatment time, we measure the proximity between parks and the users. Next, we count the number of parks within 500 m of the user's location.

If peer effect effects are at work, then users with high park accessibility would tend to experience a more pronounced effect from the experimental stimulus. The results of split sample analyses are shown in Table A8 in the online appendix. We find that in sunny weather, there is no statistical difference in the coefficients of interest between users with high park accessibility (column 1: $\beta_{2} + \beta_{3} = 0.074, p < 0.05$ ) and users with low park accessibility (column 2: $\beta_{2} + \beta_{3} = 0.090, p < 0.01$ ; Z-score test: -0.388 at p > 0.1). Similarly, there is no statistical difference in the coefficients of interest on a cloudy day between users with high park accessibility (column 1: $\beta_{2} = -0.0652$ ) and those with low park accessibility (column 2: $\beta_{2} = -0.0419$ ; Z-score test: -0.554 at p > 0.1). These results indicate that peer influence effects are unlikely to play a major role in influencing the study outcomes.

## Additional Analysis

Next, we conducted a series of additional analyses to examine the extent of the observed effects. First, we are keen to understand how individuals with different exercise levels would react to the weather message interventions. Relative to individuals with a high exercise level, users with a low exercise level are at greater risk of getting health conditions such as heart attack, stroke, diabetes, and hypertension in general (Booth et al. 2012). As such, it is important to understand the effectiveness of our weather-based targeting effects on this group of at-risk set of users. To assess this, we replicated the main analysis by splitting the user with high walking steps (quantile 25%) and low and medium walking steps (quantile 75%) in the past month prior to the start of the study.

Under the cloudy weather, we found that the positive impact of gain framing on physical activity achievement is more salient for users with low and medium exercise level (column 1: $\beta_{3} = -0.0574$ , p < 0.05) than users with high exercise level (column 2: $\beta_{3} = -0.0800$ , p > 0.1). These results suggest that using an appropriate message framing that matches the weather-induced mood of users can be highly effective in spurring physical exercise among users who have low and medium exercise level.

Next, we consider another important user characteristic of income level. Given that low-income users are disproportionately more likely to lead sedentary lifestyles, there exists a health disparity between users of different income strata (Eisenberg and Okeke 2009). We are interested in understanding whether our focal targeting strategy may widen or narrow the exercise disparity among the various income groups. To this end, we generate two groups of users: users who live in areas with personal income that is lower than the median value of personal income of the study sample and users who live in areas with higher personal income than the median value. ## Discussion

In this paper, we investigate whether real-time weather information can be effectively harnessed to improve the effectiveness of mHealth app messages for promoting exercise levels. Specifically, we conduct three field experiments to which we expose participants under different weather conditions (i.e., sunny and cloudy) to different message framing, to examine the optimal weather message combination for spurring individuals toward engaging in physical activity. Results from our between-subject experiment suggest that framed messages (gain or loss) are more effective in leading exercise compared with messages without framing. Next, through our within-subject experiment, we learned that the effects of gain- or loss-framed interventions in sunny and cloudy weather are not uniform. Loss-framed messages induce higher levels of achievement of exercise goals than gain-framed interventions in sunny conditions, whereas gain-framed interventions are more effective than loss-framed interventions in cloudy weather. Our additional experiment shows that weather-based intervention can be used repeatedly over time without losing its effectiveness, constituting as a long-term solution for use in practice. This result is found to be robust against a series of checks. Results also indicate that the efficacy of weather-based mobile interventions can vary according to the message recipient's motive for using the exercise app. In particular, we found that mood-as-a-resource effects tend to manifest among users with instrumental motives, and not so for users with consummative motives. Heterogenous tests revealed that the observed effect is stronger for people with a lower exercise level and for users living in areas of lower income. Our study provides several implications for academics, healthcare businesses, and policymakers on the use of weather-based messaging for enhancing physical activity levels.

Our study makes a few contributions to theory and practice. First, our study contributes to health IT literature by showcasing a mHealth solution that can be used repeatedly to increase physical activity over time. Although past works have studied how to use mobile intervention to increase physical activity, the literature continues to be relatively uninformed about whether the benefits of such interventions may be sustained over time (Romeo et al. 2019). Studies in healthcare intervention found that the removal of the intervention can cause individuals to regress back to unhealthy behaviors (Berman and Johnson 2015, Pham et al. 2016). Moreover, prior research documented that mobile health apps are not always effective toward at-risk populations such as low-income groups and inactive individuals (Choi et al. 2016, Western et al. 2021). In other words, although other works have tried to answer the same focal research question, the literature does not yet have a satisfactory answer on how to increase physical activity for a sustained period via mHealth apps.

Responding to calls for more sophisticated mHealth designs, our work addresses the long-standing gap in health IT literature by providing experimental evidence on how best to motivate users effectively. Although past mHealth works have examined various messaging strategies (Pathak et al. 2021, Sun et al. 2021), our work is among the first to jointly consider streamlined messages and contextual cues, specifically weather conditions experienced by the user. Our intervention remains effective when repeated several times over the period of 55 days. Given that exercising can be an effortful event to which individuals tend resist against (Lieberman 2015), it is crucial for every attempt in nudging users toward physical activity to consider the individual's mood so that the optimum messaging strategy is deployed. Without the consideration of mood-influencing weather elements, it is likely that the mere use of various messaging strategies may not be effective in nudging users over time, as one's mood changes from day to day. At the same time, we learn that our mHealth solution does not widen the digital divide, unlike other digital solutions (Eisenberg and Okeke 2009), serving as an additional benefit that closes the health gap between the haves and have nots.

Second, on top of speaking to the mHealth context, we also made a broad theoretical contribution by clarifying and resolving the tension between two contrasting theories (i.e., mood congruity and mood-as-resource) that were used to characterize how contextual targeting works. Our study demonstrates that user mindset needs to be considered as it plays a crucial role in determining which theoretical perspective would hold. Specifically, our study findings show that the optimum strategy to use in the healthcare contexts can be quite different from the prescribed weather-based messaging strategy used in marketing. Broadly speaking, while a congruent message strategy is optimum in spurring users to make purchases, the opposite messaging strategy tends to work better for motivating users to exercise. This inconsistency across study findings is reconciled when we see that our users with consummatory motives demonstrate an opposite reaction to those with instrumental motives. Our study indicates that mood-congruity effects manifest when consummatory motives dominate while mood-as-a-resource effects would manifest when instrumental motives dominate. Although past works have used the mood-congruity and mood-as-resource perspective to explain their study findings (Trope and Neter 1994, Adaval 2001), no study has identified and validated the conditions by which each theoretical perspective would manifest. Here, our work fills this gap in psychology literature pertaining mood-related effects by reconciling both perspectives and showing when the prediction of each perspective will hold.

Finally, our findings bear several practical implications for different groups of individuals. Having the understanding that mHealth app users are mainly driven by instrumental motives, designers of these systems would need to streamline their approach of motivating users toward achieving exercise goals. Specifically, designers need to factor in that users' mood are used as a resource when users are processing health-related messages from the app. Despite weather being an important factor that influences users' ability to exercise outdoors, we do not see many practitioners tapping this information in their app design. Other than using weather information with the right message framing, it can be worthwhile for mHealth companies to attempt alternative ways of utilizing weather information to reduce the barriers to exercise (e.g., highlighting ways of exercising indoors on days with inclement weather). By addressing the inconclusive findings on framing effects in past works, our results also apply to healthcare and wellness professionals who are seeking to better understand the heterogeneous framing effect under specific conditions (Akl et al. 2011).

Apart from healthcare practitioners, our study holds useful implications for marketing practitioners. The effectiveness of contextual targeting is highly nuanced and is highly dependent on the application under consideration. Although contextual targeting using weather information is most effective under a mood congruent message strategy for most marketing purposes (Li et al. 2017), practitioners need to be aware that such a targeting strategy can be counterproductive when used to persuade users who have goals beyond the intrinsic enjoyment of the focal activity/product. For instance, the marketers of sustainable or eco-friendly products (e.g., goods made from recycled materials, compostable products, power saving gadgets) are likely faced with the task of selling to users who exhibit instrumental motives toward the focal product(s), which require targeting techniques that factors mood-as-a-resource effects in its design.

Although our study makes a notable contribution to our understanding of mHealth intervention, our work is not without limitations. First, our experiment was based on behaviors of users in Asia, and it is unknown whether the observed effects might generalize toward users of different origins and cultures. To this point, it would be useful for future research to consider participants from different locations, particularly those of western origin. Second, the experiment was conducted on a single mHealth app, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results beyond the exercise app. It would be valuable for future research to assess whether similar effects are found in other healthcare apps (e.g., diabetes management app, dieting and weight loss apps). Finally, our study has only examined weather-induced effects during fall, while the impacts during other seasons are omitted in the attempt to reduce biased findings. Future work might find it worthwhile to assess if similar effects hold during summer or winter to develop a complete view of the mood-related impacts across different seasons. Despite these shortcomings, our study represents one of the first mHealth solutions that have successfully harness weather information along with messaging strategy to effectively motivate users toward physical activity. We hope that our work will serve as a springboard for future studies exploring other contextual cues as mobile interventions to encourage desired user behaviors.
