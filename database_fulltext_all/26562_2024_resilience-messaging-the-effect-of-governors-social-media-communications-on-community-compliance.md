# Resilience Messaging: The Effect of Governors’ Social Media Communications on Community Compliance During a Public Health Crisis

Reza Mousavi,<sup>a,</sup>\* Bin Gu<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. When managing major disasters, authorities may ask residents to comply with certain guidelines that change community members’ daily routines. In these situations, authorities often appeal to resilience, which refers to the ability to recover from challenges. In this study, we examine whether embedding resilience-related words (resilience messaging) in governors’ social media posts increases community compliance with government guidelines in the context of the COVID-19 disaster. First, we conducted a secondary data analysis using a panel data set of U.S. states. This analysis included community mobility data, governors’ tweets, official county tweets, approval ratings, new COVID-19 cases, and states’ response data for the period between February 2020 and August 2021 (81 weeks). We measure community compliance using the time residents spent at home and the time they spent at retail places, according to community mobility data. We also conducted an online controlled experiment to complement our secondary data analysis in order to identify the underlying mechanism. We show that governors’ resilience messaging increases community compliance (12.5% increase in time spent at home and 11% increase in avoiding unnecessary trips). We also find that the effect of resilience messaging on community compliance is mediated by residents’ perceptions of inspirational leadership.

## Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has shaken the world since December 2019. The United States reported its first COVID-19 patient on January 21, 2020. As of September 2022, the United States has reported more than 94.5 million (M) cases and over 1.05M deaths. The pandemic’s immense impact on the lives of billions of people around the globe has forced authorities to devise response strategies to contain the damage. Authorities in different jurisdictions have come up with a variety of response strategies, including enforcing stay-at-home orders, restricting public and private gatherings, and mandating masks and social distancing. These restrictive measures are effective only if people adhere to them. Given the widespread resistance to these measures,<sup>1</sup> it is critical for government leaders to communicate with citizens concerning compliance with government guidelines in order to minimize the impacts of COVID-19. Traditional work in the citizen-leader relationship has provided a theoretical framework for the effectiveness of leaders cues and signals in forming public opinion (Adams and Levi 1991), encouraging voluntary citizen compliance with public policies (Lenz 2013), and steering uninformed citizens toward effective policy judgment (Popkin 1994, Lupia and McCubbins 1998). This stream of research has found that leaders’ cues and signals have a modest impact on citizens’ behaviors.

Although prior research suggests a modest, nuanced impact of leaders’ cues and signals on citizens’ behaviors in “normal” times, when the stakes of following these cues and signals are relatively small, it remains an open question whether and how community leaders’ cues might matter during a pandemic, when individuals actions can directly impact their own health. Furthermore, given the widespread use of social media by community leaders and citizens, it is imperative to ask whether leaders’ cues and signals on social media could impact citizens’ behaviors, as well as how these leaders can make their social media communications more effective. This question is even more important during the COVID-19 pandemic, as recent research has shown a significant surge in social media use during this disaster. In particular, Haman (2020) found that many citizens began to follow their community leaders on social media platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic; moreover, 64.8% of United Nations member states had a leader that tweeted about the pandemic. A similar study found that Canadian authorities gained a significant number of new Twitter followers during the pandemic (Haman 2021). The study also found that new users who joined during the pandemic were mainly passive users, who followed Twitter accounts related to the news and political and governmental agencies. These findings suggest that citizens joined Twitter primarily to seek relevant and timely information regarding the pandemic. Haman (2021, p. 1) concludes that “[n]umerous information-seeking citizens joined Twitter for the purpose of obtaining information about public health matters, which in turn suggests that authorities should incorporate Twitter into their information dissemination tools, especially during emergencies, to meet the public demand for information.”

In summary, research in this area reveals the following: 1. Community leaders’ cues and signals could impact citizens’ behaviors.

2. During disasters, more information-seeking citizens start following leaders on social media platforms.

The fact that more citizens follow their leaders on social media platforms during a crisis indicates the potential impacts of leaders’ social media posts on citizens’ behaviors and actions. For instance, Wang et al. (2022) suggest that political leaders’ tweets can indirectly impact the spread of misinformation about the COVID-19 pandemic. In one study, Grossman and colleagues (2020) examined the impacts of leaders’ social media posts on citizens’ behaviors during the COVID-19 crisis. They found that governors’ recommendations (embedded in their tweets) for residents to stay at home preceded stayat-home orders and led to a significant reduction in mobility comparable to the orders themselves. In this study, we intend to extend these findings by focusing on how leaders communicate during disasters (Griffin-Padgett and Allison 2010, Williams et al. 2017). In particular, we study whether community leaders’ resilience messaging during the COVID-19 crisis increased community compliance. We measure compliance as compliance with one of the most important components of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines: staying at home and avoiding unnecessary trips outside.

We conduct two independent, yet related, studies to examine how U.S. governors’ resilience messaging affects their residents’ community compliance. In the first study, we use secondary data collected from Twitter, Google, and a variety of other sources to test whether resilience messaging is associated with community compliance. In the second study, inspired by the findings of the first, we conduct a controlled online experiment to investigate the causal relationship between governors resilience messaging and community compliance. We also examine one potential mechanism that could shed light on the relationship between governors’ resilience messaging and community compliance. Using causal mediation analysis, we find that governors whose tweets have more resilience-related content are perceived as more inspirational; being perceived as inspirational, in turn, mediates the impact of governors’ resilience messaging on community compliance to some extent.

Our study makes several theoretical contributions. First, we contribute to the disaster-management literature by demonstrating that adding resilience content increases the effectiveness of leaders’ communications with those affected by disasters. This finding extends previous studies such as that of Grossman et al. (2020), which reported a positive impact of leaders’ commu nications on community compliance during disasters. Second, we contribute to the communication theory of resilience (CTR) literature by empirically testing its propositions to assess the claims of previous qualitative studies. We also reveal that resilience communication can still be effective in brief social media messages. In this regard, our study validates and extends previous CTR studies, such as the work of Griffin-Padgett and Allison (2010) and Williams et al. (2017). Third, we contribute to the CTR and transformational leadership literatures by revealing that resilience content in leaders messages could contribute to the leaders being perceived as inspirational. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has tested such a relationship. Therefore, we add a new dimension to the transformational leadership literature by relating it to resilience communication.

In the next section, we review the relevant literature and synthesize the theoretical background to form our hypotheses. We then elaborate on the data sets and variables, as well as the methods used in each study (secondary data analysis and controlled experiment). Finally, we present the results of both studies, discuss our findings, and conclude with a discussion of their theoretical and practical implications.

## Theoretical Background Community Compliance During Disasters

The disaster-management literature has identified several factors that influence community compliance. The perceived risk of noncompliance, public understanding of the disaster, trust in authorities, and issues related to work and finances have been identified as the primary factors influencing community compliance during disasters (Riad et al. 1999, Manuell and Cukor 2011). The COVID-19 pandemic was declared by the Federal Emergency Management Agency as a major biological disaster in every state (and the District of Columbia) of the United States.<sup>2</sup> According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, avoiding unnecessary trips to outside places of residence is one of the most effective means to curb the community spread of the novel coronavirus and thus withstand the crisis (Moreland et al. 2020). Per the CDC guidelines, many U.S. states imposed strict stay-at-home and shelter-in-place orders when the virus was spreading rapidly in their communities. In addition to mandatory stay-at-home orders and lockdowns, state governors asked their communities to stay at home and avoid unnecessary trips (Grossman et al. 2020). If we view the COVID-19 pandemic as a disaster and consider state governors as authorities responding to it, then staying at home and avoiding unnecessary trips can be regarded as the desired community actions. As such, complying with government guidelines requires spending more time at home and avoiding unnecessary trips. Therefore, we intend to study the effect of governors’ social media posts, and especially the resilience messaging they contain, on community compliance. We measure community compliance as the increase in the amount of time spent at residential places and the decrease in the time spent outside for nonessential activities, such as spending time in bars and restaurants or in recreational places such as gyms and movie theaters.

This study contributes a novel perspective to the recent research on how leaders’ communication affects community outcomes. For instance, Yang et al. (2023) introduce an advanced deep-learning approach to assess the personality traits (e.g., conciseness) of community leaders (e.g., U.S. governors and world leaders) based on their Twitter posts. They demonstrate that the predicted personality traits effectively predict community outcomes during crises (e.g., the number of cases and deaths due to the COVID-19 pandemic in the leaders communities). Given that previous studies in psychology (Friborg et al. 2005, Oshio et al. 2018) indicate a significant correlation between some of these personality traits and resilience, our research complements Yang et al. (2023) by establishing a causal link between leaders’ social media content and community outcomes. Specifically, Yang et al. (2023) offer a means to measure country and state leaders’ personality traits based on their text-based communication and demonstrate that these measures can be used to predict COVID cases and deaths. We complement their study by showing that after controlling for leaders’ personality traits and other time-invariant characteristics, text-based social media communications can directly influence community compliance during the COVID crisis, and resilience messaging is especially effective at influencing community compliance. Our study also has different practical implications compared with Yang et al. (2023). Whereas Yang et al. (2023) highlight the importance of selecting leaders with the right personality traits, our study suggests that resilience communications by a leader, regardless of his personality traits, can promote intended outcomes.

## Resilience and Resilience Messaging

Resilience is defined as a “process of reintegrating from disruptions in life” (Richardson 2002, p. 309). Resilience is a two-phase process: In phase 1, one faces a difficult life experience, and in phase 2, the individual will be able to “bounce back,” or reintegrate after that experi ence (Buzzanell 2010). In this definition, resilience is regarded as the process of embracing the adverse and difficult life experience and continuing life with the new normal. Resilience does not necessarily involve resolving the issues that arise in different life experiences. Rather, it is the ability and process of continuing a normal life, even in the presence of difficult experiences. Buzzanell (2010, p. 1) argues that “rather than an individual phenomenon that someone either possesses or does not, resilience is developed, sustained, and grown through discourse, interaction, and material considerations.” A similar view has been shared by the U.S. Department of State: “Resilience is not a trait that people either have or don’t have. It involves behaviors, thoughts, and actions that can be learned and developed in everyone.”<sup>3</sup>

From this perspective, resilience can be nurtured through communication. The communication theory of resilience posits that proper communication can result in both short- and long-term resilience (Buzzanell 2010, Lucas and Buzzanell 2012). As Buzzanell (2010) argues, resilience is a relational process by which people con struct and coconstruct new stories, organizing logics, and framings that allow them to reintegrate new realities and incorporate new normalcies into their everyday lives. According to Buzzanell (2017, p. 100), several communicative processes can produce resilience: “(a) crafting normalcy, (b) foregrounding productive action while backgrounding negative feelings, (c) affirming identity anchors, (d) maintaining and using communication networks, and (e) putting alternative logics to work.”

CTR has been used as the theoretical foundation of many studies in the communications domain. For instance, Lucas and Buzzanell (2012, p. 189) show that in families dealing with financial hardships, “general talk of resiliency transmitted through the generations a duallayer of resilience.” That is, in addition to helping people get through immediate financial hardships, resilience talk can shape the “value systems and attitudes of the younger generation” Lucas and Buzzanell (2012, p. 189). In another study by Scharp and colleagues (2020) about parental alienation (due to divorce), the authors found support for the heuristic value of resilience communication. By conducting in-depth interviews with parents who faced alienation, the authors identified four communicative practices/processes for creating new normalcies: (a) crafting normalcy, (b) foregrounding productive action while backgrounding negative feelings, (c) maintaining and using communication networks, and (d) putting alternative logics to work. In another study by Venetis and colleagues (2019), the communicative processes of resilience in CTR were found to be positively associated with increased health management and coping in both cancer patients and their partners. Overall, this domain of research provides strong support for the effectiveness of resilience communication in crafting normalcy and adapting to a new normal after adverse events.

## Community Leaders’ Resilience Messaging

Although CTR proposes the influence of resilience talk on resilience building, to the best of our knowledge, the impact of leaders’ resilience talk on community compliance has not been empirically assessed. In this section, we first define resilience messaging, then review relevant studies that have examined the impacts of community leaders’ resilience talk on community compliance.

As noted earlier, resilience is the ability to change and adapt to a new normalcy after facing hardships. Given that an array of factors can influence the ability to adapt and bounce back, resilience messaging can entail a variety of psychological constructs. For instance, Kobasa (1979) associates resilience with hardiness, self-control, commitment, and receptivity to change. Others associate resilience with constructs such as the ability to develop a strategy, being action-oriented, or the ability to deal with stress, among other factors (Rutter 1985).

Several studies have examined the impacts of community leaders’ communications on community compliance. In particular, Williams et al. (2017) examined the tweets of the former mayor of Boston, Thomas Menino, following the Boston Marathon bombing. Their findings “demonstrate the utility of social media in aiding leaders [ … ] amid high uncertainty while also initiating the healing process, including fostering resilience” Williams et al. (2017, p. 385). After analyzing the mayor’s tweets, Williams and colleagues (2017) categorized them into five groups: initial reaction, assessment of the crisis, issues of blame, healing and forgiveness, and corrective action and rebuilding through a rhetorical vision. The study concludes by encouraging leaders to use both humanistic and strategic communication when their communities are in crisis. This finding is consistent with similar studies about leaders’ communications during other crises. For instance, Griffin-Padgett and Allison (2010) examined the communications of the former mayor of New York City, Rudolph Giuliani, and the former mayor of New Orleans, Ray Nagin, after the attacks on the World Trade Center and Hurricane Katrina, respectively. The authors concluded that restorative rhetoric (i.e., embracing the crisis and framing a viable basis for action) is necessary for leaders’ communications to be more effective. In their proposed framework, such rhetoric should include both strategic and human istic communication. According to Griffin-Padgett and Allison (2010, p. 384), a good example of restorative rhe toric in Giuliani’s communications was related to the notion of resilience: “The people of New York really don’t know how strong they are.” Griffin-Padgett and Allison argue that even though both mayors were facing controversy before the events, they were able to use restorative rhetoric to emerge as much-needed leaders who could embrace change, frame the crisis as a viable basis for action, and provide a vision for an uncertain future (Smircich and Morgan 1982, Griffin-Padgett and Allison 2010). In this sense, the two processes of restorative rhetoric—(1) embracing the crisis and (2) framing a viable basis for action—align well with the two phases of resilience—(1) facing the hardship and (2) changing and adapting to a new normalcy. Overall, this stream of research proposes a connection between resilience com munication and community compliance during hard ship. However, only a handful of studies have examined this relationship, and all have used a case-based qualita tive approach. Accordingly, we believe that it is impor tant to further examine the relationship between leaders resilience messaging and community compliance using an empirical approach.

## Inspirational (Transformational) Leadership, Resilience, and Community Compliance

In Burns’s (1978) seminal book titled Leadership, he lays the groundwork for decades of research in leadership styles and analyzes the styles’ pros and cons in a variety of settings. According to Burns, leaders can be either transformational or transactional. Whereas “transformational leaders uplift the morale, motivation, and morals of their followers, transactional leaders cater to their followers immediate self-interests” (Bass 1999, p. 9). Building on Burns’ initial model, Bass (1999) points out that transfor mational leaders emphasize what one can do for thei community, whereas transactional leaders emphasize what one’s community can do for them. That is, transfor mational leadership emphasizes putting the community’s interests first, whereas transactional leadership prioritizes individuals’ interests. Transformational leaders infuse energy and optimism and emphasize a shared objective. Such leaders positively transform their followers so that they take care of each other and act in the interests of the group as a whole (Bass 1999). Based on Bass’s transformational-transactional leadership framework (1985), Rafferty and Griffin (2004) outlined three key components of transformative leadership:

• Through inspiring a vision, transformational leaders become agents of change, encouraging people to work through the change process and adapt to a new normal.

• Through intellectual stimulation, transformational leaders encourage people to think about solving the most important problems.

• Through inspirational motivation, these leaders visualize an ideal future for people and display determination and self-confidence (Frese et al. 2003).

Inspirational motivation has been an integral part of transformational leadership. Indeed, Joshi et al. (2009) identify inspirational leadership as a subfactor of transformational leadership that focuses on inspirational communications. Inspirational leadership focuses on fostering change, which is the pivotal concept that connects it to resilience. As noted earlier, resilience is about embracing change and adapting. Therefore, when leaders use communication that promotes resilience (such as restorative rhetoric), they may be perceived as inspirational, as such rhetoric inspires a vision for change. As Griffin-Padgett and Allison (2010) noted, resilience communication (i.e., restorative rhetoric) helped community leaders to emerge as inspirational leaders (and heroes) during the September 11 and Katrina crises. Therefore, we expect that resilience communication by community leaders during the COVID-19 pandemic will serve to portray them as inspirational leaders.

On the other hand, researchers have studied whether transformational leadership (which fosters working for the benefit of the group instead of self-interest) is, in fact, more effective than transactional leadership during crises. In particular, several studies show that transformational leadership is positively associated with community resilience, particularly in public organizations with elected leaders (Valero et al. 2015). In one study, Antonakis et al. (2021) show that inspirational communication fosters resilience. They suggest that “charisma nurtures a collective identity which may be particularly valuable in maintaining a group’s resilience in the face of a persistent challenge” Antonakis et al. (2022, p. 23). Moreover, effective leadership is essential during crises in order to influence people, guide them toward greater performance, and successfully manage change (Suryaningtyas et al. 2019). Inspirational leaders exhibit zeal and optimism and stress dedication to a common objective. Overall, this stream of research suggests that being perceived as an inspirational leader positively impacts followers’ behaviors (Joshi et al. 2009). Joshi and colleagues (2009) show that such leaders foster trust and commitment, even among geographically dispersed teams. Drawing on this theoretical foundation, we propose that community leaders who use resilience messaging (which aligns with restorative rhetoric) during crises are perceived as more inspirational by their followers; consequently, such messaging would positively influence their communities’ compliance with the guidelines released by authorities.

## Research Hypotheses

According to the studies we reviewed previously, we establish the following:

1. Resilience is an important process that involves adapting to a new normal after facing major disruptions in life. It is especially important for disaster management, which often requires changes in the daily routines of those impacted by disasters. During the COVID-19 pandemic, citizens were asked to change their routines by spending more time at home and avoiding unnecessary trips. Therefore, resilience should facilitate this transition from the old normal to a new normal.

2. According to CTR, resilience can be nurtured in communicative processes. Therefore, general talk of resiliency should help to foster resilience.

3. During disasters, citizens look to their community leaders for guidelines and instructions, often by following their leaders on online social media platforms, such as Twitter.

4. Anecdotal evidence suggests that leaders who use resilience messaging in communications with their constituents are perceived as more inspirational. A large body of research regarding transformational leadership sug gests that being perceived as an inspirational leader, in turn, positively impacts followers’ behaviors.

Drawing on these findings from the literature, we propose several hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. Resilience messaging in governors’ COVIDrelated tweets increases the time their constituents spend in residential places (i.e., staying at home).

Hypothesis 2. Resilience messaging in governors’ COVIDrelated tweets decreases the time their constituents spend in retail places and grocery stores (i.e., avoiding unnecessary trips).

Hypothesis 3. The relationships in Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2 are mediated by constituents’ perceptions of the leaders as inspirational (Figure 1).

To test these hypotheses, we conduct two studies:

1. Secondary data analysis based on Twitter data: In this study, we use weekly panel data on U.S. states’ community mobility, governors’ tweets, states’ COVID-related restrictions, states’ economic incentives, and COVID-19 cases. We use these data to test Hypotheses 1 and 2.

2. Controlled experiment: Based on our findings in the secondary data analysis, we conduct a controlled experiment to (a) test the causal relationships in Hypotheses 1 and 2 and (b) examine the mediating role of inspirational leadership in Hypothesis 3.

In total, we collected data from the 50 U.S. states plus the District of Columbia for 81 weeks. ## Dependent Variables: Community Mobility Data

We obtained community mobility data from Google. Google’s community mobility data set “shows how visits to places, such as grocery stores and parks, are changing in each geographic region.”<sup>4</sup> According to the documentation, this data set shows how visits and lengths of stay at different places change compared with a baseline. The baseline timeframe is the five-week period from January 3, 2020, to February 6, 2020. The baseline score is set to zero. A positive score indicates more time spent at places, expressed as a percentage relative to the baseline, whereas a negative score indicates less time. The mobility scores are normalized and deseasoned. The data set includes six categories of places: (1) grocery & pharmacy, (2) parks, (3) transit stations, (4) retail & recreation (which we refer to as retail), (5) residential, and (6) workplaces.

From these six categories, we used the residential, retail, and grocery & pharmacy categories because they portray a more accurate picture of community compliance trends during the pandemic. The residential category captures the time individuals spent at home. As the guidelines instructed residents to stay at home unless it was necessary to go outside, we would expect an increase in time spent at residential places during the pandemic. The retail category captures visits to places like restaurants, cafes, shopping centers, theme parks, museums, libraries, and movie theaters. These businesses were deemed nonessential during the pandemic, and guidelines instructed residents to avoid these places to curb the spread of the virus. If governors were successful in persuading their residents to follow the guidelines, we would expect a decrease in time spent at retail places during the pandemic. Finally, the grocery & pharmacy category tracks the amount of time individuals spent at grocery stores and pharmacies. Although these businesses were deemed essential, and residents were allowed to visit these places, even at the peak of the pandemic and during lockdowns, voluntary community compliance may result in a reduction of visits to such places. Compared with visiting retail places, which were, at times, closed to curb the spread of the virus, avoiding unnecessary visits to grocery stores and pharmacies provides a more accurate picture of voluntary compliance.<sup>5</sup> Therefore, we included this category as another indicator of community compliance. Given the above considerations, we created three dependent variables—Residential, Retail, and Grocery—based on Google’s mobility data set. Residential is the percentage increase or decrease (compared with the before-COVID baseline) in time spent at residential places, Retail is the percentage increase or decrease (compared with the before-COVID baseline) in time spent at retail places, and Grocery is the percentage increase or decrease (compared with the before-COVID baseline) in time spent at grocery stores and pharmacies. Table 1 provides the summary statistics for these variables.

In addition to the community mobility data, we used additional data about community compliance collected by the DELPHI group at Carnegie Mellon University. The data set is called COVIDcast, is collected in partnership with Meta, and tracks a variety of COVIDrelated measures based on a rolling survey of over 25 million Americans.<sup>6</sup> From this data set, we identified another compliance-related indicator to complement the three indicators from Google’s community mobility data set: wearing masks (Wear\_Mask). This indicator tracks the extent to which residents wore a mask on a weekly basis. We rerun our main dynamic panel data model using Wear\_Mask as the dependent variable in Online Appendix F.

## Twitter Data

Governors’ Tweets. To collect data on governors’ tweets from Twitter, we obtained a list of governors’ official Twitter handles. Then, we made application programming interface (API) calls to Twitter to collect all the tweets posted during the study period by governors of all 50 states, plus the mayor of Washington, DC. This process resulted in 185,087 tweets posted between week 6 of 2020 and week 34 of 2021. We then used a list of keywords (Online Appendix A) to identify tweets related to the COVID-19 pandemic. This filtering resulted in 41,240 tweets related to the disaster. After collecting the tweet data, we used the spaCy Python library to tokenize and lemmatize the text and remove the stop-words. From these data, we created the following variables:

1. COVID\_Resilience: The use of natural language processing (NLP) models to gauge psychometric measures has received tremendous attention in the past decade. In particular, with recent advances in deeplearning applications in NLP (e.g., the introduction of bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) and other pretrained transformers), applying NLP models to measure traditional psychological constructs has evolved significantly (Abbasi et al. 2019, Ahmad et al. 2020, Yang et al. 2023). Similarly, our NLP approach, which is rooted in psychometric NLP, borrows from the deep-learning paradigm to quantify the resilience content in leaders’ communications. In order to identify resilience content in the governors’ tweets, we first created a data set of 2,000 randomly selected COVID-related tweets that were manually labeled by two independent evaluators using the two-item Connor-Davidson Risk Scale (CD-RISC-2) instrument. The details of the manual labeling are reported in Online Appendix B. After creating the labeled data and splitting it into training and test sets, we used three different approaches rooted in the psychometric natural language processing literature (Ahmad et al. 2020) in order to mea sure the resilience content in the tweets and evaluate the performances of these approaches. First, we designed and fine-tuned an advanced NLP model, BERT. Second, we designed a long short-term memory network (LSTM). Third, we created a dictionary of resilience-related words using a global vectors for word representation (GloVe) model that was pretrained on 27 billion tokens extracted from public tweets. The first two models (fine-tuned BERT and LSTM) predict whether a tweet contains resil ience content. Meanwhile, the resilience dictionary mea sures the prevalence of resilience-related words in each tweet. The details regarding how we measure resilience content, as well as how we evaluate the models, are provided in Online Appendix B. Among the three models, the fine-tuned BERT model had the highest value for area under the curve (AUC; 93.11%), followed by the LSTM model (88.60%) and the dictionary-based model (71.40%), as depicted in Figure 2. Because of the better predictive performance of the fine-tuned BERT model, we use it to generate the main results reported in Tables 2 and 3. Nevertheless, we also replicate ou analyses using the LSTM and dictionary-based models and report them in Online Appendix D; our results are still robust (in terms of sign and significance of the coef ficient for COVID\_Resilience) when we use the LSTM o dictionary-based models to measure resilience content in tweets. These additional results not only demonstrate the robustness of our findings to the approach used to measure resilience content, but also address an impor tant issue that arises when using a machine-learning based construct in a subsequent econometric model. According to Ahmad et al. (2020), the results of the sub sequent econometric analyses could be biased due to the bias induced by the measurement error in the classifica tion task (in our case, the fine-tuned BERT model). As a remedy, we follow the approach taken by Gao and col leagues (2022). In their work, the authors used two “different machine learning algorithms to reduce the potential impact of measurement error” (Gao et al. 2022, p. 963). In our case, the BERT model, LSTM model, and dictionary-based model all have different errors. There fore, obtaining similar results using any of them signals the robustness of our overall findings with respect to the measurement error induced by the machine-learning model.

IV\_Resilience: In addition to the internal instrumental variables (instruments based on differences of lagged terms) used in the dynamic panel data analysis, we also use a network-based external instrumental variable (traditional instrumental variable). To construct this vari able, we collected information about governors friendships on Twitter. Then, we created an instrumental variable based on the following-follower relationships. We assume that a given governor is more likely to post a resilience messaging tweet if other governor(s) that he follows post a resilience messaging tweet, whereas the other governor’s tweet has no direct impact on the residents of the focal governor’s state. Therefore, we created IV\_Resilience by calculating the sum of COVID\_Resilience for governors that the focal governor follows. This approach to creating an instrumental variable from following– follower relationships has been adopted in previous studies (Carolan 2018, Mousavi and Gu 2019). We performed a weak instrumental variable test and reported the results in Online Appendix C.

Official County Tweets. We also collect official county tweets to control for resilience messaging by local officials. To collect tweets posted by the official Twitter handle of the county (if available), we first obtained a list of U.S. county names.<sup>9</sup> We then performed a Google search for “[county name] County Twitter” for each county in the list. Finally, we checked the Twitter handles of the first few items in the research results to check whether (a) the county name matches the search query, (b) the county is located in the state we expected, and (c) the Twitter handle is a verified handle. This process resulted in 918 unique Twitter handles.

Next, we made Twitter API calls to collect the tweets posted by these accounts, which produced 521,695 tweets. We then filtered the tweets to identify COVIDrelated tweets. This resulted in 126,157 tweets. Finally, we applied our model to measure the resilience score in these tweets (County\_Resilience).

Other Officials’ Tweets. We also collected all tweets from members of Congress (senators and representatives), along with tweets posted by mayors of the largest city (by population) in each state.<sup>10</sup> This resulted in over 1.7 million tweets. After collecting the tweets, we used our dictionary of COVID-related words to identify tweets related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, we applied our resilience model to determine whether a tweet in this data set contains resilience content. To avoid the introduction of too many controls in our models, we aggregated all these scores (by taking the mean) at the state level and called the resulting variable Resilience\_- Control. This variable tracks the extent to which senators, representatives, and mayors from each state use resilience content in their COVID-related tweets.

## States’ Responses and COVID-19 Spread Data

We obtained each state’s COVID response from Oxford University’s COVID-19 Government Response Tracker GitHub page (Hale et al. 2020). This data set tracks a set of indices related to governments’ actions to curb the spread of the virus and deal with the economic consequences of the spread of COVID-19 within communities. The data set has several versions that track federal governments (United Kingdom and United States), as well as states (U.S. states). We used the following state-level policies (i.e., states’ responses):

1. Stringency Index: This index tracks the strictness of “lockdown-style” closure and containment policies that primarily restrict people’s behavior. In addition to the states’ policies, we also control for the federal government’s stringency index.

2. Economic Support Index: This index tracks the economic support offered (including income support and debt relief) by the state.

This data set also tracks the total number of cases in each state for each day. Therefore, we calculated the number of new cases per state per day by taking the difference of the number of cases on each day from the number of cases on the previous day.

## Approval Ratings

We collected governors’ approval ratings from the COVID States Project.<sup>11</sup> This data set is based on multiple waves of a 50-state survey that gauges public opinion about how well the governors handled the COVID-19 pandemic. The responses were collected between April 2020 and September 2021. Because the approval rating for the mayor of Washington D.C. is not reported in these data, we used the average approval ratings of the governors of the three neighboring states (Virginia, Maryland, and Delaware) as the approval rating for the mayor.<sup>12</sup>

## Partisan Voting Index

The Partisan Voting Index (PVI) is a measurement of how strongly a state leans toward the Democratic or Republican Party compared with the nation as a whole. The PVI is measured based on how each state voted in the previous two presidential elections (Wasserman and Flinn 2021). Per the 2021 release of this report, PVI scores range from �15 (the most Democratic-leaning state) to +26 (the most Republican-leaning state). Hawaii, Vermont, and California are the most Democratic-leaning states, whereas Wyoming, West Virginia, and North Dakota are the most Republican-leaning states. Given that the PVI score is not reported for the District of Columbia, we used the average PVI scores of the surrounding states (Virginia, Maryland, and Delaware) as the score for the District of Columbia.

## Econometric Model

We use a dynamic panel data (DPD) model (Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998) and fixed-effects model with Driscoll-Kraay standard errors (Driscoll and Kraay 1998) to analyze the extent to which resilience messaging in governors’ tweets (COVID\_Resilience) affects state residents’ Residential, Retail, and Grocery scores. Our DPD model setup is similar to the models employed by Bapna et al. (2013), Bhargava and Mishra (2014), Chung et al. (2019), and Kitchens et al. (2018). In our model, state i’s Residential/Retail/Grocery score (y) in week t depends on state i’s Residential/Retail/Grocery score in the previous week (t � 1), COVID\_Resilience (rs) in the tweets posted in week t by the governor of state i, and a list of controls. These controls include Polarity, Subjectivity, Word\_Count, Analytic, Authentic, Clout, and Tone in state i’s governor’s tweets; state i’s Stringency, Econ\_Support, and Cases; state i’s governor’s approval rating; and the average resilience score in COVID-related tweets posted by county officials (County\_Resilience) and other officials such as mayors and Members of Congress (Resilience\_Control) related to state i at week $t ( c _ { j i t } ) \colon$

In Equation (1), rs (COVID\_Resilience) is the main variable of interest. To address the potential endogeneity between Residential/ Retail/Grocery score and COVID\_Resilience, we decided to use the system generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator. This estimator uses a system of two equations— the original Equation (1) and an equation transformed by first differencing, Equation (2). It controls for endogeneity by using lagged values of the differences and levels of endogenous variables as instruments to identify parameter estimates (Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998):

$$
\Delta y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \Delta y _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \Delta r s _ {i t} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {4} \rho_ {j} \Delta c _ {j i t} + \Delta \gamma_ {t} + \Delta u _ {i t}.\tag{2}
$$

According to Aral et al. (2012), the system GMM estimation procedure both controls for endogeneity and eliminates bias from unobserved heterogeneity. In this case, first differencing of the original model, Equation (1), removes $\eta _ { i } ,$ thereby eliminating the potential bias from the observed or unobserved individual characteristics. The estimator also addresses endogeneity in the predictors by instrumenting the differences with the available lags of levels and the variables in levels with suitable lags of their own first differences (Aral et al. 2012, Tambe and Hitt 2012, Goes et al. 2014).

In addition to these internal instruments, we use an external instrumental variable as a traditional instrument in all our DPD models. As noted above, we created IV\_Resilience by calculating the average COVID\_Resilience for governors that the focal governor follows. We argue that resilience content in tweets posted by such governors would impact the focal governor’s tendency to post resilience content in his tweets. However, residents’ behaviors should not be directly impacted by resilience content in tweets posted by governors of other states.

Although the DPD model uses internal and external instruments to control for endogeneity, one concern regarding using DPD to analyze our data set is the relatively large number of periods $( T = 8 1 )$ compared with the number of panels $( n = 5 1 )$ . According to (Roodman 2009), the difference and system GMM estimators are more applicable to cases where N is large and T is small. We address this issue using two approaches:

1. We divide the time period in our study into multiple phases and rerun the models for each phase $( T \leq 1 6$ weeks). The results of this analysis are reported in Online Appendix G.

2. In addition to DPD, we use a fixed effects model with Driscoll-Kraay standard errors. According to Wooldridge (2002), the bias from using fixed effects with a lagged dependent variable is small when T is large. The error structure in Driscoll-Kraay is assumed to be heteroskedastic, autocorrelated up to some lag, and possibly correlated between the panels. Moreover, Driscoll-Kraay standard errors are robust to very general forms of cross-sectional and temporal dependence when the time dimension becomes large (Hoechle 2007). The fixed effects estimator in this approach is implemented in two steps. First, all model variables $z _ { i t } \in \{ y _ { i t } , x _ { i t } \}$ are within-transformed as follows:

$\tilde { z } _ { i t } = z _ { i t } - \overline { { z } } _ { i } + \bar { \bar { z } }$ , where ${ \overline { { z } } } _ { i } = T _ { i } ^ { - 1 } { \sum } _ { t = t _ { i 1 } } ^ { T _ { i } } z _ { i t }$ and $\bar { \bar { z } } =$ $\begin{array} { r } { \big ( \sum T _ { i } \big ) ^ { - 1 } \sum _ { i } \sum _ { t } z _ { i t } . } \end{array}$

Recognizing that the within-estimator corresponds to the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator of

$$
\tilde {y} _ {i t} = \tilde {x} _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \theta + \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i t},\tag{3}
$$

and the second step then estimates the transformed regression model in Equation (3) by pooled OLS estimation with Driscoll-Kraay standard errors.

## Controlled Experiment

Given that it is difficult to establish causal relationships between COVID\_Resilience and Residential/Retail/Grocery using the observed data, we conducted a controlled experiment to further investigate the potential causal relationships in our data. The main objective of this controlled experiment is to study whether adding resilience-related content to a typical tweet posted by a governor would impact residents’ intentions with respect to community compliance. In this section, we explain how we conducted the experiment and describe the instrument that we used to collect data from the participants.

## Experimental Setting

To be able to clearly attribute the changes in community compliance to governors’ resilience-related tweets, we conducted a controlled online experiment. The participants in the experiment were randomly assigned to a control group and a treatment group (please see Online Appendix K for the results of random assignment checks). The participants in both groups were presented with the same introductory statement about responding to the spread of the Delta variant of COVID-$1 9 . ^ { \mathrm { f 3 } }$ The statement was followed by a hypothetical tweet from their governor in both control and treatment groups. The only difference between the groups is that in the treatment group, a brief sentence (“We will survive this”) was added to the end of the tweets; this sentence contained one resilience-related word (“survive”).<sup>14</sup> This strategy of adding a brief sentence to the control to create the stimulus has been used in previous IS studies (Yin et al. 2014, 2021). Figure 5 displays what the control and treatment groups saw in our experiment.

We conducted our experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). We embedded three attention checks and one survey completion code in the survey instrument. We initially recruited 220 participants by running the survey several times to collect the responses in batches (so that the responses were not affected by specific times of day or time zones). We used the following inclusion criteria, based on Peer et al. (2014): We only included participants who resided in the United States, had completed at least 500 tasks with an approval rating of over 95%, and had never participated in our survey before (for later batches). The survey instrument was created using Qualtrics. Although Qualtrics suggested around seven minutes for completing the survey, the average completion time was about four minutes (234 seconds). One possible reason for the difference is that the Qualtrics estimate includes the first page of the survey (the Electronic Study Information Page), which includes 355 words to describe the survey and reiterates several statements required by the Institutional Review Board office of one of the authors’ institutions. Each participant was paid 70 cents, which translates into an hourly rate of \$10.50, above the average pay rate of MTurk. Of the 250 participants, 151 (80 in the control group and 71 in the treatment group) passed the attention checks and completed the survey in over 120 seconds.<sup>15</sup> We used the responses from these participants for the analysis.

After presenting the tweet, we asked the participants in both groups to answer several questions. First, we asked the participants to indicate the extent to which the governor’s tweet would lead them to support the following: (1) spending more time at home and (2) avoiding unnecessary trips. These two items align with the two dependent variables that we studied using the secondary data. Given that our dependent variables in the secondary data were also reported in percentages, we asked the participants to use a slider on a scale from 0 (disagree) to 100 (agree). After this section, we embedded two questions to check for manipulation. These two questions are the two items from the abbreviated form of the CD-RISC, which is called CD-RISC-2. The items are “Able to adapt to change” and “Tend to bounce back after illness or hardship.” Research has shown that “the 2-item scale shows good test-retest reliability, convergent validity, and divergent validity” (Vaishnavi et al. 2007, p. 1). We modified the two items to fit our narrative: “The governor’s tweet is about being able to adapt to change” and “The governor’s tweet is about the tendency to bounce back after hardship.”

## Secondary Data Analysis

Table 2 reports the main results of the DPD analysis. In models (1), (2), (4), (5), (7), and (8), we used Arellano-Bond estimators. In these models, we used both robust (models (1), (4), and (7)) and system GMM estimators (models (2), (5), and (8)). In models (3), (6), and (9), we used Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond estimators. Given that the relationship between governors’ tweets and their states’ COVID-related restrictions may be endogenous, we introduced COVID\_Resilience as an endogenous variable. We used both internal instrumental variables, as well as the external instrumental variable (IV\_Resilience), in each model.

In models (1), (2), and (3), we report the results for Residential. The coefficients for COVID\_Resilience are significant and positive when Residential is the dependent variable. This indicates that time spent at residential places increases when governors’ tweets include more resilience-related content. Among the control variables, the coefficients for County\_Resilience are significant and positive in all models. This finding provides some evidence that the resilience content in tweets posted by county officials can have impacts similar to such content in governors’ tweets. The coefficients for Stringency are positive and significant in these three models, indicating that imposing more stringent policies to restrict the spread of COVID-19 (such as lockdowns and shelterin-place orders) results in more time spent at residential places. Not surprisingly, the three coefficients for the number of new cases are also positive and significant— that is, communities spend more time in residential places when there is an increase in the number of cases in their states.

In models (4), (5), and (6), we report the results for models in which Retail is the dependent variable. The coefficients for COVID\_Resilience are significant and negative when Retail is the dependent variable. This indicates that time spent at retail places decreases when governors post COVID-related tweets with more content about resilience. Among the control variables, the coefficients for Resilience\_Control and County\_Resilience are also negative. The coefficients for the state-level stringency index (Stringency) are negative and significant in these three models, indicating that imposing more stringent policies to restrict the spread of COVID-19 (such as lockdowns and shelter-in-place orders) is associated with less time spent at retail places. Meanwhile, the coefficients for Econ\_Support are significant in two of the three models, signaling that providing more economic support might have a positive impact on time spent at retail places. The coefficients for the number of new cases are negative and significant—that is, communities spend less time at retail places when the number of cases increases in their states. Finally, the coefficients for governor’s approval rating are positive and significant in all three models, indicating that a higher approval rating is associated with more time spent at retail places.

In models (7), (8), and (9), we report the results for models in which Grocery is the dependent variable. The coefficients for COVID\_Resilience are significant and negative in these models. This indicates that people spend less time at grocery stores and pharmacies when governors post more COVID-related tweets containing resilience content. Among the control variables, the coefficients for Resilience\_Control and County\_Resilience are also negative, but they are only significant in two models. The coefficients for the state-level stringency index (Stringency) are negative and significant in all three models, indicating that imposing more stringent policies to restrict the spread of COVID-19 (such as lockdowns and shelter-in-place orders) is associated with less time spent at grocery stores and pharmacies. The coefficient for Econ\_Support is significant in one out of the three models, signaling that providing more economic support might have a positive impact on time spent at grocery stores and pharmacies. The coefficients for the number of new cases are negative and significant—that is, communities spend less time at grocery stores and pharmacies when the number of cases increases in their states. The coefficients for governor’s approval rating are positive and significant in all three models, indicating that a higher approval rating is associated with more time spent at these places.

With respect to COVID\_Resilience, our results are consistent with the results reported based on DPD.

## Additional Analyses and Robustness Checks. In this section, we examine several additional relationships:

First, we examine whether residents’ political party moderates the relationship between resilience messaging and community compliance. Given that prior research in this domain has identified the political party of the residents (or the political parties’ vote shares) as a moderator of adherence to public health guidelines related to the COVID-19 pandemic (Gollwitzer et al. 2020, Grossman et al. 2020, Agarwal et al. 2021), we test whether residents response to the resilience content in governors’ tweets is moderated by their Partisan Voting Index. We used the Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond estimator, as well as a fixed effects model with Driscoll-Kraay standard errors, for this analysis. Per the results reported in Table 4, PVI’s interaction with COVID\_Resilience was insignificant in all models. Overall, this finding is contrary to our expectations, based on previous studies in this domain. For instance, Grossman et al. (2020) found that state leaders recommendations (to stay at home) were more effective in reducing mobility in Democratic-leaning counties than in Republican-leaning counties.

The difference in our results could be attributed to the fact that we do not study the effect of stay-at-home recommendations on residents’ behaviors. Instead, we study the effect of the resilience content in the COVIDrelated tweets on residents’ behaviors. This finding may have promising implications for policy-makers in all states, regardless of the residents’ political leanings; COVID-related tweets that contain resilience content are effective in promoting community compliance in both Democratic- and Republican-leaning communities.

Second, given that the COVID-19 pandemic had longterm impacts in the United States and other countries, we divide the period of the study into multiple phases and examine the aforementioned relationships using data from each phase. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has created a timeline of the COVID-19 pandemic in the United States (https://www.cdc.gov/ museum/timeline/covid19.html). According to this timeline, the COVID-19 pandemic can be divided into seven phases: late 2019 (December through the end of 2019), early 2020 (January through April 2020), mid-2020 (May through August 2020), late 2020 (September through the end of 2020), early 2021 (January through April 2021), mid-2021 (June through September 2021), and late 2021 (October through the end of 2021). Given that our original data set was for the period between the sixth week of 2020 (February 2020) and the 34th week of 2021 (August 2021), we ran our models for each phase that overlapped with our data (early 2020, mid-2020, late 2020, early 2021, and mid-2021). The results of this analysis are reported in Online Appen dix G. According to these results, the association between resilience messaging and community compliance (as measured using the three variables Retail, Residential, and Grocery) is consistent across different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic for the most part. The coefficients for COVID\_Resilience are positive and significant when the dependent variable is Residential for all phases except phase 3. The coefficients for COVID\_Resilience are negative and significant (marginally significant for phases 2 and 3) when the dependent variable is Retail/Grocery. Overall, with the exception of phase 3 (late 2020), the results of this analysis confirm the association between resilience messaging and community compliance, con sistent with the results in Table 2.<sup>16</sup>

Third, given that Twitter popularity varies across governors, we examine whether resilience messaging by more popular governors is more effective than messaging by others—that is, whether governors’ Twitter popularity moderates/reinforces the effects of their resilience messaging on community compliance. We normalized the number of followers by dividing it by the population of each state. Then, we added this variable (which represents the popularity of the governors on Twitter) as a moderator for COVID\_Resilience. Table I1 in Online Appendix I reports the results, which confirm our expec tation about the reinforcing role of governors’ popularity on Twitter with respect to the impact of governors’ resilience messaging on community compliance.

According to Table 5, participants in the treatment group had a significantly higher score on both items (p-values are 0.010 and 0.002).

## Discussion and Conclusion

To manage major disasters such as the COVID-19 pandemic, authorities often ask residents to follow certain guidelines. Complying with these guidelines may require residents to change their daily routines. For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, residents were asked to stay at home and avoid unnecessary trips outside their residences. However, changing daily routines and adapting to a new normal is not easy. As evidenced by many articles and media reports during the pandemic, residents have had a difficult time changing their habits. Therefore, resilience, which is about the process of adapting, can be an important process that aids residents in altering their routines and complying with the guidelines released by authorities. In a sense, a higher level of resilience can help with more compliance during disasters.

The extant literature on resilience suggests that it is not a trait that someone either possesses or does not; instead, it involves behaviors, thoughts, and actions that can be learned. One way to learn the behaviors, thoughts, and actions related to resilience is through proper communication. Indeed, the communication theory of resilience proposes several communicative processes that promote the development of resilience. Research in this domain provides evidence that general talk of resiliency can result in both short- and long-term resilience development. In two independent studies, we showed that governors’ resilience messaging did, in fact, increase community compliance. In particular, the treatment group in the experiment had an increase of 12.5% in intention to spend more time at home and an increase of 11% in intention to avoid unnecessary trips. We further revealed an underlying mechanism through which resilience messaging influences community compliance: Such messaging influences residents’ perceptions of inspirational leadership, which, in turn, can influence community compliance. In particular, perceiving a governor as an inspirational leader to some extent mediates the impact of resilience messaging on community compliance.

Our study makes several theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the disaster-management literature by revealing that adding resilience content to leaders’ communications with those affected by disasters makes such communications more effective. This finding extends those of previous studies such as Grossman et al. (2020), which reported a positive impact of leaders communications on community compliance during disasters. Second, we contribute to the CTR literature by empirically testing its propositions in order to assess the claims of previous qualitative studies. We also reveal that resilience communication in brief social media messages can still be effective. In this regard, our study validates and extends previous CTR studies, such as Griffin-Padgett and Allison (2010) and Williams et al. (2017). Third, we contribute to the literatures on CTR and transformational leadership by revealing that resilience content in leaders’ messages could contribute to them being perceived as inspirational. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has tested such a relationship. Therefore, we add a new dimension to the transformational leadership literature by relating it to resilience communication.

From a practical perspective, our findings have important implications for community leaders and social media managers. During major disasters, leaders should use resilience-related communication to increase their influence when encouraging their constituents to comply with guidelines released by authorities. Although we added a brief sentence to the end of our control tweet to create the treatment tweet in our experiment, simply adding such a short sentence to any tweet does not necessarily turn it into resilience messaging. Our objective in the experiment was to make sure our control and treatment conditions were as similar as possible; as a result, they only differed with respect to the resilience content. In a real-world setting, however, leaders and communications managers should take a holistic approach to their communications with constituents. This approach should be informed by the context (the cause of communication, such as making the community prepared for an upcoming natural disaster), and it should be augmented by best practices in communications. With respect to such best practices, which are the main focus of this study, Buzzanell (2017) provides an excellent set of instructions for crafting resilience-rich communications during crises.

Therefore, when addressing their constituents and stakeholders during a crisis, leaders and managers should incorporate these communicative processes into their communication strategies. Moreover, we show that advanced NLP models can be used to reliably examine the resilience content of communications, allowing leaders to examine whether their communications are rich in resilience. As we show in Online Appendix L, our Large Language Model can be used in tandem with generative AI tools such as ChatGPT to create resilience-rich content to be used in crisis communications.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations. First, we do not control for resilience content shared by all authorities at the town, city, and state levels. Second, we only analyzed communications channeled through one social media platform (Twitter). However, authorities use other social media platforms, such as Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram, as well as other media, such as TV, radio, and websites, to encourage their residents to comply with instructions. In our study, we neither controlled for the effects of these communications nor studied their impact on community compliance. We hope that future research provides more clarity about the overall impact of resilience messaging across different channels and the extent to which these channels may achieve a synergistic effect. Third, in our online experiment, we asked the participants about their intentions to comply with the guidelines, rather than tracking their actual compliance. Therefore, one limitation of this part of our study is that intentions may not perfectly reflect actual behavior. Fourth, in our online experiment, we asked the participants about the extent to which they would avoid outside trips. The wording in our experiment may have caused confusion, at least for a portion of the participants, as they may have thought that we were referring to traveling to other locations (e.g., cities or states), rather than traveling outside their homes.<sup>17</sup> Fifth, the COVID-19 pandemic was a long-running disaster with shockwaves still present at the time of this writing. Many disasters, however, span a much shorter period. Therefore, although our supplemental analysis of the first phase of the COVID-19 pandemic (from late February 2020 through the end of April 2020) revealed findings similar to our main results, the generalizability of ou study may be limited to major, long-lasting disasters, rather than less severe disasters.

One extension of this research could focus on differen leadership styles, rather than leader-follower communi cation. In our experiment, we showed that resilience messaging can influence the extent to which governors are perceived as inspirational, which, in turn, positively influences community compliance. An extension of our study could shed more light on the relationship between resilience messaging and other leadership styles. A recent qualitative study (Crayne and Medeiros 2021), based on the charismatic, ideological, and pragmatic (CIP) leadership model, finds more support for pragmatic leadership styles during crises. Through a sensemaking focused theory of leadership, the authors synthesized the actions of three leaders (Justin Trudeau as the charismatic leader, Angela Merkel as the pragmatic leader, and Jair Bolsonaro as the ideological leader), each representing one of the CIP’s leadership styles. They showed that Angela Merkel (who represents pragmatic leadership) was the most effective crisis leader of the group. Therefore, one extension of this study could focus on how a leader’s resil ience communication influences community compliance, given their leadership style. In addition, we only focused on the communications of leaders, rather than their actual actions. Therefore, future studies could examine whether resilience communication is instrumental when different types of leadership styles (e.g., charismatic and pragmatic) are used by the community leaders. Finally, another possi ble extension of this study is to investigate the role of resil ience messaging as part of a disaster-response system. In our study, we focused solely on leader-follower communi cations, measuring the impacts of community leaders resilience messaging on community compliance. How ever, social media enables regular community members to post content (and potentially influence) community lea ders or other community members. Future research could take a more holistic approach in evaluating the potential impacts of resilience messaging by studying different com munication channels. In such studies, conceptual frame works, such as the framework introduced by Fayoum et al. (2021), could be used as the conceptual model.
