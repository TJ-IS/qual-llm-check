# Second Screening—The Influence of Concurrent TV Consumption on Online Shopping Behavior

Oliver Hinz,<sup>a,</sup>\* Shawndra Hill,<sup>b</sup> Amit Sharma<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. The practice of using a “second screen” while concurrently watching television (TV) has become a widespread phenomenon. Whereas work on multitasking suggests that TV consumption may lead to lower online sales, research in the area of impulse buying suggests the opposite. In this paper, we study the impact of concurrent TV consumption on online shopping behavior and try to understand this con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst study analyzes the impact of TV consumption on online shopping behavior at the aggregate level using a panel of 100,000 U.S. customers over a period of 24 months. We address potential endogeneity problems with an instrumental variable approach. Our second study proposes a novel approach to realizing individual-level analysis over thousands of consumers and products. Examining massive amounts of data, we identify a sample of consumers who watched a certain TV show and who were simultaneously doing online shopping at Amazon.com. We then compare their shopping behavior on the day of the TV show to their shopping behavior one week prior. Both studies reveal that whereas TV consumption is correlated with a higher likelihood of online shopping in general, the causal effect of TV watching differs with respect to the complexity of the products purchased. If a TV program appeals to a large TV audience, then this results in fewer immediate sales of highcomplexity products (1% increase in TV consumption leads to 2.2% sales) and more sales of low-complexity products (1% increase in TV consumption leads to 8.8% sales).

## 1. Introduction

Studies from market research companies have established that television (TV) watchers frequently engage in second screening: the concurrent use of a mobile device, laptop, or other computer while watching TV. An estimated 180.8 million U.S. adults—70.1% of the total population—regularly used another digital device while watching TV in 2019, and this number is expected to reach 71.9% in 2020 (Droesche 2019). While most of the activities that take place on the second screens while watching TV are related to the TV’s content, about 35% of the people surveyed said that they usually do online shopping while watching TV (Nielsen 2018).

Whereas the phenomena of second screening and online shopping on second screens are indisputably widespread, academic research has been rather silent on this topic. Previous research in this area mainly focused on understanding why and how people use the second screens (see Neate et al. 2017 for a review), but studies that try to understand the economic impact of this phenomenon are scarce. Such an understanding could help marketers create more sophisti cated promotional strategies (i.e., providing suitable recommendations on the visited online shops), but, unfortunately, it is still unclear how second screening in<sup>fl</sup>uences online shopping behavior.

On the one hand, people engage in second screening to look up content, to take part in conversations with other TV watchers on social media, or to check products and do online shopping (Nielsen 2018). Logic dictates that as the number of people shopping online increases, sales of products will also increase, indicating that second screening may be favorable for online retailers. This line of argumentation is also supported by previous research that has shown a positive causal impact of mobile devices on online sales in general (Xu et al. 2016), as well as by industry reports claiming that online shopping and TV consumption often co-occur (Droesche 2019). This increase could be related to increased impulse buying, which is determined by the ease of buying and its related expenditures of a number of resources (like money, opportunity costs, and mental efforts) (Stern 1962). Second screening could decrease these (perceived) costs above and beyond the advantages that online shopping alone provides and could thereby enable consumers’ spontaneous actions and ultimately result in increased levels of impulse buying.

On the other hand, according to the concept of the attention economy and research on media multitasking, people have a <sup>fi</sup>nite amount of attention. If they are watching TV, then they may not have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient stores of attention to effectively engage in online shopping (Davenport and Beck 2001). The attention economy may thus be an important boundary condition for second screening behavior. The concept has been addressed extensively, both in the popular press and by research studies (e.g., Davenport and Beck 2001). The basis of this concept is that individuals have a <sup>fi</sup>xed amount of attention, and thus they need information to be presented in a manageable way (Goldhaber 1997, Davenport and Beck 2001). For example, because of the plethora of information that individuals receive while watching TV, this competition may mean that a TV watcher’s attention is spread thin and that one of these activities (such as online shopping) receives less attention. This may lead to fewer sales, indicating that it may be unfavorable for online retailers if the consumers watch TV. We seek to resolve this con<sup>fl</sup>ict and aim at answering the following research questions in this paper: How does simultaneous TV consumption impact online shopping behavior, and, if this is the case, does its in<sup>fl</sup>uence vary across product types?

We will argue in this paper that both con<sup>fl</sup>icting arguments can still be true if we consider the type of products on sale. We hypothesize that people have a limited attention budget, which would make it dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult to focus on shopping for complex products while watching TV. In this context, complexity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which a product is perceived as being dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand (Moore and Benbasat 1991) and which would ultimately mean that simultaneous TV consumption has a stronger negative effect on sales of complex products (e.g., pharmaceuticals or consumer electronics) than on sales of noncomplex products (e.g., fast-moving consumer goods like food and detergents).

This paper’s contribution is twofold. First, we will show that product complexity is an important moderator for online shopping on second screens and assess the magnitude of the effect. Second, this paper shows how to use newly available data to consider contextual factors that have previously been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or impossible to measure. New technologies now allow us to infer an individual’s context from new data sources. With these new types of data, we can construct novel data sets and create new operationalizations (here: product complexity) to test the connections between TV viewership and online sales, while considering the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product attributes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review the existing literature related to media multitasking and the potential effects of second screening on economic behavior. The subsequent section discusses our <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical study, which analyzes the impact of TV consumption on online shopping behavior at an aggregate level. We examine a panel of 100,000 individuals over 24 months and address potential endogeneity problems with an instrumental variable (IV) approach. This is supported and complemented by an analysis on individual-level behavior in study 2. We conclude with a discussion and summary of the theoretical contributions and managerial implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings while providing suggestions for further research.

## 2. Previous Research

In the following, we discuss prior research related to the content of this article. We <sup>fi</sup>rst survey papers that examine multitasking (and especially media multitasking) before we review the most relevant literature and highlight the research gap that our paper aims to close.

## 2.1. Media Multitasking

The growing possibilities for multitasking, especially enabled by the widespread availability of information and communication technology, have motivated researchers in various disciplines to better understand the phenomenon of multitasking.

Predominantly experimental research has shown that multitasking decreases task performance, especially when task juggling produces interference and distraction, which can ultimately result in errors, lost time, and mental stress (e.g., Ophir et al. 2009, Bowman et al. 2010). The roots of this view go back to experimental literature from the 1930s on divided attention, dual-task performance, and task switching. Nowadays, it is widely accepted that the human information processing system has a limited capacity and that sharing resources among tasks usually comes at a cost for performance and productivity (e.g., Telford 1931, Kahneman 1973; see Courage et al. 2015 for a summary).

Although there is no consensus, one general concern based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in dual-task and task-switching experiments is that multitasking is detrimental to the speed and accuracy of performance on a wide range of tasks (see Courage et al. 2015 for a review). Other researchers have expressed the concern that the technologyenabled availability of multiple sources of information may foster a strategy of continuous partial attention to these different sources without actually focusing on any one of them suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently (see Rose 2010 for a discussion on the subtle difference to multitasking). This could also potentially lead to a number of tasks that do not get <sup>fi</sup>nished at all.

An alternative view is that multitasking increases ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and productivity in the real world (in contrast to arti<sup>fi</sup>cial experiments), because it allows people to pursue ef<sup>fi</sup>cient time management strategies (Courage et al. 2015). This stream of literature argues, mainly conceptually, that certain technologies enable a greater <sup>fl</sup>exibility in task prioritization; thus, multitasking can produce adaptive strategies that enable higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies by altering the way that individuals process information so that they can “do more with less” in a day (Courage et al. 2015). However, empirical research in this area is rather scarce—a void that we hope to address with this paper.

These unclear in<sup>fl</sup>uences, coupled with the widespread prevalence of concurrent TV consumption and online shopping in everyday life, warrant some better understanding of this phenomenon and its economic impact. For this purpose, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the literature on concurrent TV viewing and its effects on other tasks before we narrow this down to economic decision making under TV exposure, that is, the domain where this paper can be situated.

## 2.2. Concurrent TV Viewing (Second Screening)

A couple of studies in different disciplines have examined the effects of watching TV while performing other tasks. In most cases, research has found that TV exposure has detrimental effects on other tasks. For example, Pool et al. (2000) reported a negative effect of concurrent TV consumption on homework performance, while Armstrong and Chung (2000) and Lee et al. (2012) described the interference effects of background TV on reading comprehension. More related research has focused on multiscreen content consumption. In a laboratory study, Brasel and Gips (2011) showed that people switched between media at an extreme rate, averaging more than four switches per minute and 120 switches over the 27.5-minute study exposure to second screening in the laboratory. Such a steady stream of interruptions can lead to limitations in which even simple tasks cannot be simultaneously performed without signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance loss (Jenkins et al. 2016).

Whereas these are interesting insights that are peripherally related to our paper, it is important to emphasize that we are not interested in an accuracy measure as a dependent variable; rather, we want to know if and how second screening in<sup>fl</sup>uences online shopping behavior. Unfortunately, very few studies have focused on economic decision making under media multitasking and, in particular, under concurrent TV exposure. Hinz et al. (2016) examined the impact of TV consumption on online auctions in Germany over a period of about <sup>fi</sup>ve years at an aggregated level. The authors found a negative causal effect of TV viewership on online auction sales, even after controlling for covariates, and attributed this <sup>fi</sup>nding to the limited cognitive resources of prospective auction participants. However, these authors focused on a very special economic activity with very speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics, which does not allow for generalizing their <sup>fi</sup>ndings to online shopping.

Liaukonyte et al. (2015) analyzed the effect of second screening in the area of TV advertising and argued that a second screen may compete for consumers’ attention but simultaneously lead to an immediate and measurable response to some commercials.

Chang (2017) showed with the help of questionnaires that media multitasking can have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the impulse to buy.

Already, Stern (1962, p. 60) pointed out that impulse buying is related to the ease of buying and that “the purchase of an item involves the expenditure of a number of resources: money, for the item itself and for any costs incurred in going to and coming from the store; time, in going to or from the place of purchase; physical effort, such as walking or driving to or from the place of purchase; and <sup>fi</sup>nally, mental effort, of scheduling the trip to the store and budgeting for the purchase.” This is certainly the case for using the smartphone while enjoying a TV show that could ultimately increase sales.

Taking these considerations together with the fact that individuals are not good at multitasking in general because of their limited cognitive resources, we can conclude that online shopping on second screens under the exposure of TV is likely to be moderated by the complexity of the decision. From this point, it is straightforward to hypothesize that concurrent TV consumption can result in higher sales of lowcomplexity, impulse products, while likely decreasing the sales of high-complexity products. We will examine these hypotheses in two empirical studies following an innovative mixed-methods approach.

## 3. Empirical Study 1: Aggregate-Level Data Analysis

We start with an analysis at the aggregate level, which involves following a panel of users for two years and relating their online shopping behavior to TV consumption. Beside revealing the plain association between online shopping behavior and TV consumption, we try to identify the causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of TV consumption on online shopping through the use of IVs.

## 3.1. Data and Descriptives

We compiled data from three sources covering a twoyear time span (2012–2013). First, we collected online transactions using Comscore data from Wharton Research Data Services. These data are generated from a panel of about 100,000 users who opted to use tracking software on their computers. Second, we acquired TV viewership data from Rentrak (now also belonging to Comscore), which reports hourly viewership data in the United States by Designated Market Areas (DMA). These Rentrak data are collected through cable TV partners that provide viewership data on many millions of TV viewers in the United States. Finally, we collected weather data to control for the potential in<sup>fl</sup>uence of precipitation and temperature, as these can easily in<sup>fl</sup>uence online sales. We also accounted for temperature as a squared term, because there could be a nonlinear relationship between temperature and people pursuing activities outside, which could ultimately impact TV consumption and online sales.

We restricted our analysis to consumers from the New York City DMA and thus used consumer sales data, TV consumption data, and weather data from this area to address the potential problem of uncontrolled heterogeneity due to location.

We aggregated the data using a two-hour base because of a time-stamp problem observed in the Comscore data—namely, a lag in the logging of data immediately before and after midnight. The number of transactions drops a few minutes before midnight and then peaks a few minutes after midnight. It appears that the transactions are queued because other processes that begin on the database server delay the immediate logging of data. The queue is then alleviated a few minutes later. To solve this problem, we aggregated on a two-hour base with a single period between 11 p.m. and 1 a.m. Thereby, we address the problem of delayed logging around midnight, since we assign all delayed and lagged transactions to the same period. This produced a data set with 8,772 observation periods (12 observations per day for two years).

A simple ordinary least squares (OLS) regression with the amount of products sold as the dependent variable yields a positive impact of TV viewership on sales (p < 0.01). However, a simple OLS confounds the focal effect of interest with the effect of being at home. If we introduce orthogonal variance by means of an IV (see Section 3.3 for exemplary IVs), then the effect of TV viewership on sales generally becomes insignificant (p > 0.1), which emphasizes that the relation between second screening and online sales is not that straightforward and warrants more detailed analyses.

As we pointed out earlier, theory suggests that products can be distinguished based on their complexity, with high-complexity products especially suffering from consumers’ limited attention budget. We classify products as high- or low-complexity if the complexity score in Lovett et al. (2014) is higher or lower than the average of all brands plus or minus one standard deviation.

According to the data provided in Lovett et al. (2014), the mean complexity score is 1.837 and the standard deviation is 0.387. Based on this information, the class of high-complexity products consists of those from the consumer electronics industry (e.g., Apple, IBM, Blackberry), the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry (e.g., Bank of America, HSBC), insurance companies (e.g., Blue Shield, Kaiser Permanente), travel-related companies (e.g., US Airways, Orbitz Travel, Priceline.com), luxury clothing brands and retailers (e.g., Armani, Cigna, Chicos), pharmaceutical companies (e.g., Merck, P<sup>fi</sup>zer), and companies in the health and <sup>fi</sup>tness industry (e.g., 24hour Fitness, Weight Watchers). Products from brands that include Ajax, Always, Colgate, Cheerios, Heinz, Hershey, Johnson & Johnson, Kellogg’s, Kraft, and Nestle comprise the class of lowcomplexity products. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation clearly re<sup>fl</sup>ects our intuition about complex and noncomplex products and provides face validity.

In line with our theoretical considerations, we focus the following discussion on the concept of complexity and therefore calculate the number of high- and lowcomplexity products per time period. In general, the majority of the products are in the medium-complexity category (share of low-/high-complexity products of total sales: mean 10.5%/5.3%, standard deviation 20.2%/15.5%). For the low- and high-complexity prod ucts, we arrive at the distribution over time depicted in Figure 1. Sales by time of day (left) peak between 7 p.m. and 11 p.m. (which is an expected result), and we observe more sales at the end of the year during the months of November and December (right).

TV viewership is also a function of time, as represented in Figure 2, which shows that TV viewership peaks between 7 p.m. and 11 p.m. and is higher on Saturdays and Sundays than on weekdays. This <sup>fi</sup>nd ing is similar to the sales results presented in Figure 1.

## 3.3. Identification and Endogeneity

In our setting, the number of TV viewers may be endogenous. Therefore, we <sup>fi</sup>rst need to consider potentially omitted variables; second, we need to determine if there may be some effect of the dependent variable (sales) on the independent variables, causing a so-called reverse causality bias.

Omitted variable bias results from correlations between the omitted cause, $X _ { t } ,$ and included variables. In our model, for example, the unobserved time spent at home, $X _ { t } ,$ is likely to bias the results in a simple

OLS regression. The condition of being at home could lead to a higher likelihood of online shopping and, at the same time, is likely to be correlated with the number of TV viewers. This may bias our inference for the effect of concurrent TV consumption on sales. Additionally, there may be some effect of sales on the number of TV viewers—for example, people may turn off the TV when they are shopping online—which may additionally bias the results.

For identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the effect of TV consumption on online shopping behavior, we employ a combination of strategies. First, we included variables that capture seasonality as well as daily, weekly, and monthly patterns.

Second, we use an IV that is orthogonal to the terms of our system to identify variation in the number of TV viewers. Our explanatory variable of interest that is potentially endogenous is the number of TV viewers. It is easy to imagine that there are factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the likelihood of simultaneously watching TV and shopping online. If we cannot control for these factors (e.g., the likelihood of being at home), then the estimates are likely to be biased.

We thus need to <sup>fi</sup>nd an IV that ful<sup>fi</sup>lls two requirements. First, the IV needs to be correlated with the endogenous explanatory variables conditional on the other covariates (i.e., something that is correlated with the number of TV viewers). This requirement can be easily tested by examining the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the IV in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of a two-stage model.

Second, the IV should not correlate with the error term in the explanatory equation, which means that the IV will not suffer from the same problem as the original predicting variable. However, it cannot be statistically tested whether this is true because the condition involves the unobservable residual, which is why theory or facts are essential to a convincing analysis.

A good IV should not be predictable, so that no actor (online consumer) driving the equation could anticipate and proactively change his or her behavior. Furthermore, in order to be truly exogenous, the IV should not be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the dependent variable (sales) or the endogenous explanatory factor (number of TV viewers). For our research question, a disaster could constitute a valid and promising IV. The occurrence of a disaster cannot be predicted and is usually covered during TV programs with special broadcasts. If these special broadcasts attract enough TV viewers, then this could serve as a shock to the system. We can then use this shock to partial out the effect of TV viewership on online sales if these sales are not directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced above and beyond the effect through the attention attracted by the TV program.

For this purpose, we use the live media coverage of the Boston Marathon bombing—a terrorist attack that occurred during the Boston Marathon on April 15, 2013, killing three people and injuring 264—to exemplify such a disaster. We use this event as the <sup>fi</sup>rst IV and a dummy variable for the eight hours of TV coverage after the explosion. As a second IV, we use eight hours on April 19, during which TV media reported on the unprecedented manhunt for the bombing suspect, with thousands of law enforcement of<sup>fi</sup>cers searching a 20-block area of Watertown, Massachusetts.

As a third IV—which is conceptually very different—we exploit a dispute between DirecTV and Viacom, which quarreled bitterly over the costs of a new contract in July 2012. This dispute led to a nine-day blackout of Nickelodeon, MTV, Comedy Central, and other cable channels in DirecTV homes (Stelter 2012). DirecTV is one of the largest American direct broadcast service providers, with more than 20 million subscribers. If certain channels are unavailable, then it seems likely that the number of TV viewers would decrease. Moreover, this shock quali<sup>fi</sup>es as an IV because we can rule out substitution effects that are likely during standard carriage disputes. Because when the blackout began on July 10, DirecTV initially pointed its subscribers to Hulu and other sites to watch Viacom programming. But Viacom responded quickly by taking its shows off of all websites (Stelter 2012). This event thus quali<sup>fi</sup>es as a shock on the supply side that should lead to a lower number of TV viewers. We can test this hypothesis in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of a two-stage least squares regression. We can also argue, from a theoretical point of view, that this supply shock is unlikely to affect shopping above and beyond the effect that altered media consumption could have on online shopping behavior and that we want to identify.

Using these three IVs has two advantages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst advantage is conceptual because the IVs are of a very different nature. Two of the three events should raise the interest in TV, whereas the third should theoretically lower the number of TV viewers. Second, we can statistically use the fact that three IVs facilitate the overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation of the model, which allows us to conduct the Sargan-Hansen test of overidentifying restrictions. If we observe a strong rejection of the null hypothesis with the Sargan-Hansen test, then this would raise doubts about the validity of the estimates. If we cannot reject the null hypothesis, then the three IVs should be quite orthogonal. These considerations lead to the two-stage model summarized by Equations (2) and (3):

## 3.4. Estimation Results

3.4.1. OLS Estimates. We <sup>fi</sup>rst report the OLS estimates for descriptive purposes and then the estimates generated by the IV regression. There is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of TV viewing on sales of low-complexity goods $( p > 0 . 1 ;$ see Table 1, model (1)). For high-complexity products, we observe a positive effect of TV viewing on sales $( p < 0 . 0 1 $ ; see Table 2, model (1)). This means that online shopping sales increase with the number of TV viewers. This effect is most likely driven by the fact that people are more likely to watch TV and shop online when they are at home. Because we drop the independent and identically distributed assumption and use HAC statistics, we applied the Kleibergen and Paap (2006) rk LM statistic. The Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald F-statistic is 51.47, which clearly indicates that our IVs are not within the set of weak IVs in terms of both relative bias to OLS and bias in second-stage signi<sup>fi</sup>cance.

For low-complexity products such as food and beverages, we observe a positive signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of simultaneous TV consumption on sales (p < 0.05). This means that concurrent TV consumption can cause an immediate positive impact on sales for this particular product type. Obviously, this is in line with the popular assumption that higher online activity through second screening comes along with higher online sales. However, this is true only for a special subset of products, where the decision does not require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive effort from the consumer. Such purchase decisions can be made easily while watching TV. The results indicate that if the number of TV viewers increases by 1%, then the number of low-complexity products sold increases on average by 8.8% (p < 0.05).

However, this observed relationship does not hold for all product types. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is evident when looking at Table 2, where the dependent variable captures the number of high-complexity products sold. For highcomplexity products, the relationship between TV viewers and sales is negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ If the TV program attracts many consumers, then the likelihood of online sales of high-complexity products decreases signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Obviously, consumers are not able to spread their attention between TV and a complex purchase decision. A 1% increase of TV viewers thus leads to a decrease of immediate sales by about 2.2% for high-complexity products.

Among the control variables, we observe that there is an optimum temperature for online sales of highcomplexity products. This relationship follows a U-shape. By setting the <sup>fi</sup>rst derivative to 0, we determine that the minimum is about 29<sup>8</sup>C, or 84<sup>8</sup>F. If the temperature is either too low or high, then people are more likely to be at home (or at least staying inside somewhere), which increases online sales. The results have face validity and ex post justify the addition of the weather controls. Online Appendix D provides further robustness checks and discusses potential confounders.

However, if we analyze data on such an aggregate level, we cannot control for the content that has been broadcast. In particular, broadcasted commercials may create a systematic bias. For example, if <sup>fi</sup>rms from the beauty and consumables industries prefer to advertise during prime time and consumer electronics <sup>fi</sup>rms prefer to advertise around midnight, and if the commercials are effective and in<sup>fl</sup>uence online behavior (which seems to be true a priori and has recently been shown by Liaukonyte et al. 2015), then this effect could bias our results. Up to this point, we can only claim that TV consumption, including potential advertising effects, has an effect on online sales that is moderated by the products’ complexity. With the next analysis on the individual level, we will exclude prod ucts that were advertised in the commercial breaks.

## 4. Empirical Study 2: Individual-Level Data Analysis

In the following, we present an analysis of individuallevel behavior based on a truly unique data set that was provided by Microsoft Research to address the previously mentioned shortcomings. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a sample of individuals who concurrently watched TV and engaged in online shopping, and we then compared those results with the same sample’s online shopping behavior on a comparable day where they did not consume TV and online shop simultaneously.

## 4.1. Data and Identification Strategy

To supplement our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we investigated the effect of product complexity using data on individuals’ online shopping activity during a TV broadcast. We compared these individuals’ typical online shopping behavior with potential changes resulting from the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of TV consumption. For individual shopping activity, we used data from the Bing Toolbar that provides anonymized browsing activity for users who have opted in to data collection. Although Microsoft offers a Bing Toolbar for mobile operating systems like Android, we must assume that our sample mainly consists of subjects who use a laptop as a second screen. We looked at their visits and purchases at Amazon.com, the leading e-commerce store in the United States. As in the previous study, we want to estimate the effect of watching TV on buying products, and how this behavior changes with the complexity of products. To account for potential bias caused by advertisement, we analyzed the effect of commercials that were broadcast during the advertising breaks of the particular shows.

Study 2 tries to contrast individual behavior on days with increased TV consumption to days with regular TV consumption, and we use the event shows of the Super Bowl (approximately 110 million viewers in the United States), the Oscars (approximately 40 million viewers), and the Grammys (approximately 30 million viewers) for this purpose. We identify people who did online shopping at Amazon during the time of the event (e.g. Super Bowl) and who did online shopping at Amazon at the same time one week before. This requires already immense amounts of data (several hundred petabytes per day of analysis). The statistical likelihood that a person watches TV on an event day is already higher than on the same weekday before without a major TV event. Based on our data for the New York area, we see, for example, a TV viewership lift of about 16% on an event day compared with a comparable nonevent day in the same month. Even if we do not <sup>fi</sup>lter the sample further, the analysis of this sample could reveal the effect of interest. However, to get a clearer picture, we now restrict this sample of people to those who have shown interest in the show and are thus likely to watch the show by analyzing their online search behavior (we will explain the rationale in detail in Section 4.3).

## 4.2. Inferring Product Visits and Purchases

The <sup>fi</sup>rst task is to identify the products browsed by each individual using data on their page visits to Amazon.com. Fortunately, Amazon uses a small number of templates to generate web URLs for product pages. These templates have the form www.amazon. com/dp/B010OAQPJA/, where the code “dp” corresponds to a product web page and the 10-character code following it serves as a product identi<sup>fi</sup>er, known as an “ASIN ID,” which uniquely identi<sup>fi</sup>es each product sold on the website. We use these templates to <sup>fi</sup>nd page visits that corresponded to viewing a product on Amazon.

Estimating purchases is more complicated. Most purchases on Amazon are completed over a secure https web protocol, and thus web page URLs do not contain information about the products purchased. However, adding a product to one’s cart is considered indicative of an actual purchase, and so we used this as a proxy for purchase behavior. That is, for each page visit generated after a user clicks on a product’s “Add to Cart” button, we identify the product visited just before this page visit and label it as a purchased product. Note that this proxy does not work for all product categories. In particular, Amazon-speci<sup>fi</sup>c products (e.g., the Kindle or gift cards) or online media products (e.g., Amazon Video) do not employ standard add-to-cart buttons, and thus we excluded these categories.

Combining these two methods, we obtained a timestamped data set of products visited and purchased by each user. For each product in the data set, we used the Amazon Product Advertising API to fetch additional information, such as the product name, manufacturer, and category.

## 4.3. TV Consumption

In principle, whereas we could examine the effect of TV consumption on Amazon activity for any TV show, we restricted our analysis to popular TV broadcasts that include sports events and award shows to identify suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large samples of TV viewers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we analyzed the 2017 and 2016 Super Bowl, the 2017 and 2016 Oscar, and the 2016 Grammy telecasts. We did not consider the 2017 Grammys because its reference day (which we use for our analysis) was the day of another popular broadcast show. For each of these events, we collected broadcast dates and times, as well as advertising information.

To connect TV viewing with shopping on Amazon, we also need to know, among the people who visited Amazon.com, who was also watching TV during the event. Without individual-level TV viewership data, however, this is not a trivial task; we therefore used a heuristic that builds upon previous research in neighboring areas. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed people who conveyed an interest in watching the event and thus would be more likely to watch the event on TV than others. We did so by using people’s searches about the event on the Bing search engine a few days before the event. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we looked for all users whose Bing searches included phrases indicating their interest in watching a TV program, such as “when is superbowl.” Nielsen states that “Audience interest . . . to a show predicts higher intent to watch,” and it is well known that online search is a good predictor for conversion on aggregate (e.g., Scholz et al. 2019) and at the individual level (Moe 2003, Bronnenberg et al. 2016). Moe (2003) shows, for example, with clickstream data that the conversion rate for people who search is 6.4%, whereas for standard visitors in their sample it is only 0.1%. Likewise, Bronnenberg et al. (2016) reveal that search is highly predictive for purchases using a unique data set at the individual level. We therefore assume that someone who searches for a TV show is more likely to watch it than the average internet population.

At the end, our sampling strategy is also potentially <sup>fi</sup>ltering out enthusiasts, because date and channel for many major events are well known, but it also helps <sup>fi</sup>lter out uninterested individuals. By this means, we end up with a sample of thousands of products and users over the <sup>fi</sup>ve TV broadcast events. Please note that we are not allowed to disclose the actual numbers due to nondisclosure agreement restrictions. Our assumption is that individuals in the resulting sample have a higher likelihood to watch TV on the event day than one week before, a probability that does not necessarily has to be zero. We use the event lift in viewership to understand the impact of concurrent TV consumption on sales.

## 4.4. Identifying the Impact of TV Viewing

To estimate the change in individuals’ online shopping behavior due to TV viewing, we compared activity on Amazon for each individual during the event to the same time a week before the event, which we call the “reference day.” The assumption is that everything else remains the same during identical hours a week before the event day, except for a higher likelihood of watching TV on the event day. Comparing the purchases made on the event day versus a week before provides us with an estimate of the effect of watching TV.

As mentioned, there are thousands of unique products sold during both the event and reference days. This leads to a sparse distribution of visits and purchases per product. Therefore, we aggregated products to their product category and treated each category as a unit of analysis.

As with all observational studies, we need to guard against confounding factors. First, our strategy for including users in the analysis based on their Bing searches may potentially introduce a selection bias. The reason is that people who search for an event online before the event are also more likely than others to browse e-commerce websites, simply because they are active online during this time. This is known as activity bias, and it is commonly reported in studies of online behavior (Lewis et al. 2011). To counter this bias, we only included users who searched for the event a few days before its date, instead of on the day of the event. Such queries still indicate users interest in the event but do not introduce bias toward users who have higher online activity on the event day. Still, there may be day-to-day differences in Amazon activity. To avoid bias due to these differences, we compared the relative fraction of purchases for each product category instead of the actual number of purchases on the event or reference day.

With this individual-level analysis, we aim to complement the aggregate-level analysis to some extent. Because this quasiexperimental design allows us to compare the same individuals who are at home and doing online shopping at two comparable points of time, we can rule out some obvious endogeneity problems that were likely to bias the simple OLS results on the aggregate level (omitted variable of being at home and potential ad effects). However, we must frankly admit that we can only use heuristics to measure TV consumption and sales, which may introduce some measurement error. For example, it is well known that consumers abandon their shopping carts at a rate between 50% and 80% (Baymard Institute 2019), but we assume that this does not systematically bias our results. We provide tests of this assumption and others in Online Appendix E.

## 4.5. Descriptive Study

We <sup>fi</sup>rst used complexity scores by Lovett et al. (2014) to analyze sales on Amazon. It is worth noting that Amazon categories (such as Fashion, Televisions, or Computer Accessories) are generally more speci<sup>fi</sup>c than Lovett et al.’s classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Moreover, there are many other categories that do not directly correspond to their classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. We thus combined categories from Amazon to match four relevant categories from Lovett et al. (2014). We analyzed the Amazon categories Beauty, Consumables (Food and Beverages), Fashion (Clothing), and Electronics (Technology and Telecom). Table 4 in Lovett et al. (2014) presents these categories with respect to their complexity. We rank Consumables and Beauty as low-complexity categories because their scores are among the lowest in the analyses by Lovett et al. (2014). The Fashion category has slightly higher than average complexity. Electronics is a good example of a high-complexity category (see Table 3 for the resulting classi<sup>fi</sup>cation).

According to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the previous section, we expect that sales in the low-complexity (high-complexity) category should increase (decrease) during TV consumption. We focus on the share or proportion of activity for the category of interest among all categories to address a potential activity bias, as mentioned earlier. We use the number of items sold to calculate the share of basket because it is less biased by different price levels. Table 4 presents the resulting share of the purchase baskets for the different categories in our study, both on the event day and reference day. Please note that we are not interested in the absolute values and do not want to compare these values between different categories. We are solely interested in the change between a day with a major TV event that individuals in our sample were interested in and a comparable day one week before.

We selected one event of each type—namely, the Grammys, Oscars, and Super Bowl—and focus on the changes between these two days within the different categories. Whereas a comparison between different categories could be biased, for example, by the different purchase frequencies across categories, the change within a category between a day before a major TV event and the event day should not be biased. We thus focus on the “Change” column in Table 4. The observations with check marks align with our expectations, whereas the “X” indicates a result that deviates from our expectations. The table illustrates, for example, that 16.7% of all sales initiated by people watching the 2016 Grammys came from the Beauty category. If we examine the purchases of the same people one week before the Grammys, then this share is 0%. The shares of Beauty products bought by people who watched the Oscars or the Super Bowl in 2017 were 4.3% and 6.5%, respectively, during the event, but 1.8% and 1.6%, respectively, in the week prior. This is a <sup>fi</sup>rst indicator that people are more likely to buy Beauty products while consuming TV on an event day than on the baseline day, which is in line with our hypothesis that TV consumption can increase sales of low-complexity goods. The same holds true for products from the Consumables category.

On the other side of the complexity spectrum, the Electronics category experienced a decreased share by

6.4, 1.3, and 4.6 percentage points when people concurrently watched TV during the 2016 Grammys, 2017 Oscars, and 2017 Super Bowl, respectively. In line with our theoretical arguments, this result indicates that people are less likely to buy highcomplexity goods while watching TV.

For the medium-complexity category, we observed one instance of an increase in the share of purchases and one instance of a decrease. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally observed an increase in Fashion sales during the Super Bowl, which could be driven by either the increased sales of sporting apparel or a special segment of consumers. This one observation is not in line with our hypotheses and clearly shows that other features beyond complexity (e.g., related to the category) are potentially relevant moderators.

Overall, this highly unique data set covering thousands of products and consumers allowed us to perform, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, a meaningful descriptive analysis of individual behavior in the context of TV consumption. The subsequent section further develops our approach and suggests a new operationalization for complexity that can be calculated based on observable behavior, which allowed us to conduct a quantitative study of individual behavior.

## 5. General Discussion

This paper is, to the best of our knowledge, the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the impact of concurrent TV consumption on online shopping behavior. In two empirical studies, which use very different data sets and methods, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that second screening has a nuanced impact on online sales. We thereby contribute to research on media multitasking and can provide some valuable managerial implications.

## 5.1. Research Contribution

This paper examines the impact of concurrent TV consumption on online shopping behavior and helps to shed light on the effect of media multitasking on economic decision making. At the same time, it contributes to discussions over whether the simultaneous use of multiple devices can lead to positive outcomes. The literature on multitasking mainly provides evidence for poorer outcomes (e.g., with respect to task performance; see, e.g., Kahneman 1973), while evidence for positive outcomes is rather scarce and often only anecdotal or conceptual. In two studies that adopted very different methodological approaches, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relation between second screening and online shopping behavior. People tend to behave differently when they share their attention between two screens compared with a situation where they pay full attention to the online shopping process. The impact is nuanced, however, and mainly moderated by the complexity of the purchased product. The impact of second screening can thus vary, and its result is driven by two different effects:

(1) Both studies in this paper provide evidence that concurrent TV consumption can lead to decreased sales of high-complexity products. This is in line with resource theory and con<sup>fi</sup>rms that individuals only have a finite amount of cognitive resources to process information. Processing information on high-complexity products is often too dif<sup>fi</sup>cult while watching TV, and thus individuals often refrain from making a decision. In the area of concurrent TV consumption, this <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms research on auction behavior under TV exposure, where concurrent TV consumption also decreased people’s participation in the rather complex process of an auction (Hinz et al. 2016).

(2) Both studies con<sup>fi</sup>rm that concurrent TV consumption leads to increased sales of low-complexity products. In general, online shopping makes the buying process easier and more spontaneous (especially when a second device is readily available), which in turn can increase impulse buying (Stern 1962). This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in line with a survey study on self-reported purchase intentions conducted by Chang (2017). Impulse products are typically less complex, leaving individuals with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources to watch TV and process information about these products at the same time.

Overall, our results indicate that media multitask ing allows consumers to <sup>fl</sup>exibly adapt their behavior in order to complete tasks more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. A Google study shows that consumers who do two things simultaneously (i.e., watch TV and do online shopping) have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger feeling of some “found time” (Google 2012). However, there are limits to this strategy, as complexity seems to be an important moderator. The rising prevalence of second screening behavior indicates that the perceived bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts by media multitasking are enduring. Individuals cherish the new possibilities, even though previous research has highlighted the dark sides of media mul titasking and multitasking in general. It is interesting to recognize that media multitasking does not constitute such a severe problem in the real world as experimental work in this area (e.g., Telford 1931, Kahneman 1973) suggests. For instance, a small loss in accuracy seems to be generally acceptable for most individuals. Interestingly, individuals consciously or subconsciously self-select tasks with a complexity that they are able (or believe themselves to be able) to handle. In the case of online shopping, individuals focus on purchasing low-complexity products, whereas for high-complexity products, they may tend to defer the decision to a later point when they have more cognitive resources available.

Our paper also introduces a new operationalization for complexity that can be used on click-stream data. The analyses revealed a high overlap with traditional self-reported measures (Lovett et al. 2014) that must usually be collected manually. Our new metric can help researchers and marketers better understand the perceived complexity of products without manual effort, and thus on a broader basis.

## 5.2. Managerial Implications

Besides information about individuals and their preferences, contextual information may prove valuable for advertising and recommending items in particular circumstances (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2015). Such contextual information can consist of data on time, location, and/or an individual’s state of mind or current activities. It can be used, for example, by a travel site recommender to suggest vacation packages based on the season. Recommenders sometimes ask individuals for input about context: for example, some music recommenders ask users to specify their current mood, which is then incorporated into the recommendation process.

Our results indicate why access to second screening information may be valuable. Our insights about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of concurrent TV consumption on shopping behavior can be used to optimize personalization by incorporating this contextual information. Concretely, our results indicate that website recommendations should consider second screening. If retailers are interested in immediate sales, then they should recommend low-complexity products and use slogans that highlight time savings to increase sales (e.g., “Use your time ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and order [enter low-complexity product here] now”). The process for completing the purchase should also be as lean and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient as possible. Amazon’s “buy-with-1-click” exempli<sup>fi</sup>es such a process.

Even if access to individual second screening information is not available due to technical dif<sup>fi</sup>culties or data protection regulations, our results could lay the foundation for more context-sensitive online shops and websites in general. Various service providers offer information about TV consumption over time on an aggregate level; managers can use these data to identify reoccurring patterns (even the reported results in this paper make some suggestions). Furthermore, it could be useful to exploit the information provided by the device that consumers use to visit the focal website. Net<sup>fl</sup>ix, for example, utilizes contextual information such as city or zip code, as well as time, to provide context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c recommendations of movies. The Net<sup>fl</sup>ix CEO Reed Hastings pointed out that they can improve the performance of their recommendation algorithms up to 3% when taking into account such contextual information as the time of the day or user’s location (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2015).

## 5.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Our work is subject to some limitations that provide avenues for future research. First, the underlying mechanism for the observations is unclear. We cannot observe or measure intentions or antecedents that lead to the second screening situation. This paper focuses on the statistical causal effect of concurrent TV consumption on online sales. The underlying mechanism, the reasons for the observed behavior, cannot be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with the transactional data at hand. There are a number of potential explanations for the observed behavior: it could be that the concurrent TV consumption alters mood states and leads to pure impulse buying. The situation could also be used to restock goods that are used very often (reminder impulse buying) or it could be—at least partially—a result of suggested impulse buying that was stimulated by, for example, commercials or something else the consumer sees on TV. Alternatively, the observations could be a result of intentions, namely, that people aim at making ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of their time and plan to do both activities at the same time. Future research should try to better understand the “why” question, which could help to understand the observed phenomenon better. Third, the increasingly prevalent practice of integrating software in apps that can detect sounds (e.g., when a phone is not being actively used and is in a pocket) is questionable from a privacy perspective. Nevertheless, personalized ads or recommendations may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for consumers if irrele vant pieces of information are <sup>fi</sup>ltered out and the remaining information is useful and unobtrusive. Therefore, there should be a vivid discussion on the opportunities and challenges that accompany the aforementioned technological advancements. Our research indicates that newly available data are valu able, but researchers, industry, consumers, and policy makers need to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to leverage these opportuni ties in a fair manner. Fourth, our research focuses on a time when linear TV played a more dominant role, just before the increasing consumption of videoon-demand TV. Although we would need different data (sources) to identify the causal effects of concur rent video-on-demand consumption on online sales, we see no reason why the mechanism behind the effect should be invalid in such a setting. However, more studies in the area of streamed video-ondemand TV seem to be warranted. Finally, watching TV and using the smartphone at the same time migh only be one instance of a broader setting where our results could hold. Potentially, there are other situations where it is convenient to use the smartphone to <sup>fi</sup>nish some tasks but where the attention is spread thin. People obviously self-select then into a task with a low perceived complexity. Future studies could try to understand this interplay better in more generalized settings. In sum, we hope that this article will stimulate future research in the area of multiscreen content consumption, as this phenomenon is so prevalent in ou daily lives. Studies like that of Brasel and Gips (2011) show that we steadily engage in media multitasking and that this can have severe effects on our cognitive processes (Jenkins et al. 2016). However, we have only just begun to understand the consequences of this IT-enabled behavior change. This paper hopes to contribute to this novel and evolving stream of research.
