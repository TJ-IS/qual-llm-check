## Introduction

Digital technologies that allow organizations to connect skilled employees regardless of location hold immense potential for enhancing collaboration and teamwork (Anuragini et al. 2023). The promise of these digital technologies to bring together employees with unique backgrounds and skills has prompted researchers and practitioners to investigate new concepts and processes that can amplify the effectiveness of these virtual teams. For example, collective-level efficacy, or the collective belief in the ability to collaborate effectively, is a crucial factor influencing traditional team performance that is currently being explored in virtual team research (e.g., Jacques et al. 2020, McLarnon and Woodley 2021, Awuor et al. 2022). However, despite their significant contributions, many of these studies implement collective-level efficacy conceptualizations proposed in the traditional team literature that do not capture the complexity of utilizing technology to overcome temporal and geographical dispersion (Hardin et al. 2024).

The literature review (summarized in Table A1 in Online Appendix A) substantiates that researchers have mostly sidestepped conceptualizing collectivelevel efficacy in virtual teams as a construct different from collective-level efficacy in nonvirtual teams. This approach blurs the distinctiveness of virtual teamwork and provides an incomplete view of how collectivelevel efficacy forms and operates in these settings. Compounding this lack of clarity, researchers have concentrated on empirically assessing collective-level efficacy’s relationships with other variables rather than on how it develops and functions in virtual teams. Whereas these empirical studies offer valuable insights, they do not provide a comprehensive conceptual explanation of how virtual team members utilize communication media to develop the shared understanding needed to form collective-level efficacy. Thus, a notable omission from the literature is a singular focus on the unique nature, evolution, and ramifications of collective-level efficacy in virtual team environments. This gap in knowledge limits the theoretical salience of collective-level efficacy in scholarly investigations of virtual collaboration and its application by organizational managers striving to enhance the performance of virtual teams. We contend that this incomplete understanding will persist unless the literature includes a specific theory on how collective-level efficacy develops and functions in virtual teams.

We address this need by integrating and extending components of collective efficacy (CE), collective cognition, and media synchronicity theories (these theories are summarized later) to craft the conceptual framework of virtual team efficacy theory (VTET). This comprehensive theoretical framework explains how virtual team efficacy (VTE), a virtual team-specific conceptualization of collective-level efficacy (Fuller et al. 2007), emerges and subsequently impacts downstream outcomes during a multifaceted technology-mediated cognitive process unique to virtual settings. VTET is a novel conceptual framework that researchers and managers can consult to better understand the nature of VTE and how it develops in virtual teams. Whereas we incorporate other theoretical views to construct our framework, VTET exhibits substantial points of departure from the existing literature, including its conceptual grounding and integration, theoretical placement of novel processes and components, parsimonious exhibition of key ideas, and scientific and practical usefulness. These departures deliver compelling contributions.

Motivation: Building upon Prior Literature Erudition, or “profound scholarly knowledge” (Rivard 2014, p. vii), requires a study to showcase what it learns from prior work to propel the study forward. Therefore, we reviewed the existing literature to establish our motivation and contributory vision and inform our theory development. As noted in our introduction, comprehensive conceptual efforts to unpack collectivelevel efficacy in technological settings are relatively rare. Most studies have focused on producing empirical insights into the relationship between collective-level efficacy and other variables, rather than developing conceptual frameworks to explain how it develops and operates in these settings. Thus, we highlight the literature by drawing upon two studies representing notable conceptual and empirical efforts that inform our investigation. The first study (Marakas et al. 1998) established a conceptual framework around an efficacy concept in a technological context. The second study (Schepers et al. 2011) developed a research model based on the VTE construct developed by Fuller et al. (2007). We explain below why we chose these studies and how we extend their findings to deliver a distinctive contribution to the literature on virtual teams and collectivelevel efficacy.

We highlight Marakas et al. (1998) because it is a rare study that conceptualizes how an efficacy construct develops and impacts outcomes in a technological setting. However, the study’s context and level of analysis differ from ours. Marakas et al. (1998) relied on self-efficacy theory and a comprehensive literature review to explain how computer self-efficacy (CSE) develops based on specific sources of efficacy information and impacts downstream outcomes through certain mediating and moderating factors. We expand their ideas by utilizing components of self-efficacy theory to explain collective-level efficacy development in virtual team contexts. We accomplish this by theorizing how virtual teams utilize communication media during a unique collective cognitive process to construct a shared understanding of the primary efficacy sources and other virtual team factors that lead to VTE. In the downstream portion of our model, we build on Marakas et al.’s (1998) suggestion that CSE influences computer performance through mediating and moderating processes to help inform our theori zation of the collective-level processes responsible for the relationship between VTE and virtual team outcomes. Among these is communication performance, an additional mediating process not previously considered by researchers investigating collective-level efficacy’s impacts on virtual team outcomes. Lastly, we theorize that these mediating influences occur during the later stages of collective cognition when virtual team decisions and actions affect outcomes.

The second study, Schepers et al. (2011), informs our work because its focus on developing a VTE research model in a virtual team setting closely aligns with our research objectives. Schepers et al. (2011) was also one of the few studies from our literature review substantiating the use of VTE as a pivotal construct. Schepers et al. (2011) contribute to the literature by establishing internal and external individual- and collective-level antecedents of VTE and its downstream impact on field service employee performance. We expand on the authors’ primary observation that VTE develops from specific antecedents to conceptually abstract a broader set of antecedents that subsumes the factors in their study and those included in other studies in our literature review. For example, Schepers et al. (2011) established supervisor encouragement as a precursor of VTE. We abstract this idea by proposing that collective efficacy theory’s concept of verbal persuasion includes supervisor and other forms of encouragement (e.g., from team members). We further build upon and extend their work by developing a conceptual framework that abstracts additional generalizable antecedents and explains how VTE develops during an intermediate collective cognitive process. Schepers et al. (2011) also identified a direct relationship between VTE and field service employee performance in the downstream portion of their model. Proposing a direct relationship between collective-level efficacy and outcomes was common in the studies included in our literature review. We expand upon these findings of a direct relationship between VTE and outcomes by explaining how several mediational processes, including communication performance, are responsible for this relationship. Whereas Schepers et al. (2011) also hypothesized that perceptions of task interdependence moderate the relationship between VTE and virtual team outcomes, their empirical results did not wholly support this expectation. We explain this finding by theorizing that collective-level VTE cannot form in situations where the independent efforts of virtual team members eliminate the need to develop a shared understanding.

To summarize, we are informed by Marakas et al. (1998), Schepers et al. (2011), and other studies included in our literature review. Our approach to building upon this prior literature is guided by Compeau et al. (2022), who articulate a process for updating concepts and theories to advance knowledge (please see Online Appendix B for a summary of this process in the current context). Following Compeau et al. (2022), we extend, abstract, complement, and differentiate our research from previous studies by synthesizing collective efficacy, collective cognition, and media synchronicity theories. The conceptual integration and expansion of these theories clarify our unique contributions.

## Theoretical Choices and Integration: VTET’s Nomological Net

Our theory integrates three conceptual scaffolds of VTE: “virtual,” “team,” and “efficacy.” Extending the components of self-efficacy theory to the collective level provides the basis for understanding the sources of collective-level “efficacy” and its impact on team outcomes through mediating processes (Bandura 1997). Collective cognition theory (Gibson 2001) provides the groundwork for understanding how interrelations among virtual “team” members lead to the development of collective cognition and the formation of VTE. Finally, media synchronicity theory (Dennis et al. 2008) underpins the nature of the technology-mediated communication that occurs during “virtual” collaborations (e.g., Windeler and Harrison 2018, Hassell and Limayem 2020, Eisenberg et al. 2021).

As these theories have inspired many studies, discussing them at length is impractical. Thus, we offer a summary discussion of each theory to pave the way for their integration into the VTET framework. The motivation for their integration is that each theory has specific strengths and limitations that allow us to position them in a complementary manner. For example, collective efficacy theory provides critical information about the primary efficacy sources and the mediating processes responsible for collective-level efficacy’s impact on downstream outcomes. Yet it does not expand on collective cognition processes or the use of communication media to develop collective-level efficacy in technology-mediated settings. Collective cognition theory focuses on the emergence of collective cognition but does not address how technology influences the development of collective-level efficacy or the primary efficacy sources and associated mediating processes proposed by collective efficacy theory. Finally, media synchronicity theory identifies the essential components of successful technology-mediated communication but does not address collective-level efficacy beliefs or collective cognition processes. However, when considered together, the conceptual integration and synthesis of these theories enable the theorization of VTET.<sup>1</sup>

## Collective Efficacy Theory

Self-efficacy theory (Bandura 1977) provides the groundwork for understanding the broad categories of primary sources that influence the development of collective-level efficacy beliefs. These beliefs develop based on enactive mastery experiences serving as indicators of capability, vicarious experiences that influence collective-level efficacy beliefs through the transmission of competencies and comparisons with other teams, verbal persuasion based on social influences that teams possess specific abilities, physiological and affective states through which teams judge their capability, strength, and vulnerability to dysfunction. Collective-level efficacy beliefs, in turn, regulate team functioning through specific mediating processes. These include cognitive processes, or the construal of anticipatory situations and visualized futures that enhance or undermine performance; motivational processes, or the conversion of collectively conceived future states into motivators that regulate team behavior; affective processes, or the nature and intensity of collective emotional experiences; and selection processes, or the types of activities and environments in which teams choose to be involved.

## Collective Cognition Theory

Collective cognition occurs because of the interrelations among team members’ activities during specific phases. The first phase is accumulation of information in which teams acquire knowledge and information. During this phase, members filter and store the information they act upon during team interactions. Teams label this information during their discussions, and these labels eventually become jargon (behaviors unique to the team) and categories (sets of information) that organize knowledge. During the interaction phase, teams establish a transactive memory system (Wegner 1987) to retrieve, exchange, and structure previously stored information. Members negotiate subjective impressions of this information during the subsequent examination phase to guide their evaluations and actions. Group members affect each other’s perceptions, judgments, and opinions during these negotiations. Finally, teams collectively integrate members’ perceptions, judgments, and opinions to generate decisions and actions in the accommodation phase (Gibson 2001).

## Media Synchronicity Theory

Media synchronicity theory (MST) proposes that communication performance relies on the fit between the information transmission and information processing needs of communication processes and the information transmission and information processing capabilities of media (Dennis et al. 2008). Convergence communication processes, such as transmitting higher-level abstractions of information and negotiating those abstractions to form mental models, benefit from synchronicity, whereas conveyance communication processes, such as the transmission of large amounts of raw information and subsequent retrospective analysis, do not. MST defines synchronicity as “a state in which individuals are working together at the same time with a common focus” and media synchronicity as “the extent to which the capabilities of communication medium enable individuals to achieve synchronicity” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 581). MST further proposes that appropriation factors, such as familiarity, training, past experience, and social norms, influence the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes. The fit between media synchronicity and communication processes influences communication performance.

## Integrating Three Theories to Explain the Development and Functioning of VTE

Integrating these three theories helps explain how VTE forms and impacts virtual team outcomes. Virtual teams appropriate media capabilities to carry out the communication processes necessary to reach a shared understanding of the information used to develop VTE. This process occurs during the accumulation of information, interaction, and examination phases of collective cognition. Once formed, VTE affects virtual team outcomes through the mediating processes proposed by collective efficacy theory and communication performance, which is affected by VTE’s influence on the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes. These respective mediating processes occur during the final accommodation of information phase of collective cognition. Combined, the conceptual connections among these theories enable the development of a novel theory explaining the unique process of VTE formation and its subsequent influence on virtual team outcomes.

## Virtual Team Efficacy Theory

Figure 1 depicts the VTET framework explaining the development of VTE and its downstream effects on virtual team outcomes. VTET integrates collective efficacy, collective cognition, and media synchronicity theories to explain how collective-level efficacy develops and operates in virtual team settings. Virtual teams appropriate communication media during the accumulation of information, interaction, and examination phases of collective cognition to gather, filter, store, and interpret information specific to virtual team environments. VTET proposes that this information includes the primary efficacy sources proposed by collective efficacy theory, as well as individual- and collective-level technology and team efficacy, team and task factors, and virtual team structure proposed in the virtual team literature. By progressing through this complex, technology-mediated collective cognition process, virtual teams develop a shared understanding of the information that leads to VTE.

VTET also extends the prior literature’s finding of a direct relationship between VTE and outcomes by emphasizing the critical mediating influences of cognitive, motivational, affective, and selection mediating processes proposed by collective efficacy theory. These vital mediating processes occur during the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition, in which virtual teams integrate members’ perceptions, judgments, and opinions to generate decisions and actions that produce outcomes. During this phase, virtual teams also reevaluate their appropriation of communication media to share and interpret the information used to develop VTE. In the accommodation of information phase, teams instead focus on matching media capabilities with the communication processes required to complete their project tasks. VTE impacts these appropriation decisions by influencing the fit between media synchronicity and specific communication processes. The resulting fit between media synchronicity and communication processes influences communication performance (Dennis et al. 2008), which impacts outcomes.

## Key Definitions and Boundary Conditions Virtual Teams

We adapt the classic definition proposed by Jarvenpaa and Leidner (1999) by defining virtual teams as temporary, geographically dispersed teams that use technology to collaborate. This definition is particularly relevant for our purposes because it can be generalized to various types of virtual teams. In recognition of studies advocating for context-specific descriptions of virtual teams, we explain later how VTET’s inclusion of structural factors complements and extends this more general definition.

## Collective-Level Efficacy

Bandura defines collective efficacy as “a group’s shared belief in its conjoint capabilities to organize and execute the course of action required to produce given levels of attainments” (Bandura 1997, p. 477). Researchers have extended the conceptualization of CE because of its relevance to multiple group and task domains. Typical examples include group (team) efficacy, which predicts specific group outcomes, and group (team) potency, which predicts group outcomes across multiple tasks. To minimize confusion regarding these conceptually related factors, we use collective-level efficacy when referring to collective-level efficacy beliefs unless we reference the context-specific construct of virtual team efficacy.

## Virtual Team Efficacy

Virtual team efficacy is a virtual team-specific conceptualization of collective-level efficacy. Recognizing the deficiency of traditional team collective-level efficacy concepts in these settings in terms of both their conceptual definition and measurement, researchers incorporated technology-mediated collaboration into their conceptual definition of VTE as “the belief in a team’s ability to use communication technology to coordinate their activities across time and space” (Fuller et al. 2007, p. 223). Consistent with this conceptualization, VTE’s operationalization includes measurement indicators that reference the use of communication technology for collaboration. This results in superior predictive strength over traditional team collective-level efficacy measures in virtual team settings (Hardin et al. 2006, Schepers et al. 2011).

## Boundary Conditions

VTET is a broadly applicable theory developed for situations in which the shared beliefs of virtual teams play a central role. Therefore, virtual teams must engage in interdependent activities to form VTE beliefs (Hardin et al. 2006). Furthermore, virtual teams must be involved in activities that provide sufficient time to develop these beliefs (Fuller et al. 2007). Finally, VTET does not predict the optimal time to assess VTE. Instead, VTET acknowledges that collective-level efficacy changes over time (Lindsley et al. 1995, McLarnon and Woodley 2021), which is consistent with the reciprocal relationships proposed by social cognitive theory (SCT). Later in the article, we expand on the implications and limitations of VTET, considering these boundary conditions.

## Virtual Team Efficacy Theory Propositions

## Primary Efficacy Sources

VTET proposes that virtual teams partially develop collective-level beliefs based on Bandura’s (1997) fou primary sources of efficacy. We briefly discuss these categories of collective-level efficacy sources as they relate to the development of VTE and then elaborate on how teams develop a shared understanding of these sources during specific collective cognition processes.

## Enactive Mastery

Enactive mastery experiences are the most effective way to build collective-level efficacy beliefs, providing authentic evidence of whether teams can muster what it takes to succeed (Bandura 1997). Performance success generally boosts VTE, whereas negative performance diminishes VTE. Enactive mastery is most effective through previous experience in similar settings, as collective-level efficacy beliefs are more predictive in context-specific domains (Gibson and Earley 2007). Thus, virtual teams gain impactful, enactive mastery experiences by completing similar projects and using similar technologies.

## Vicarious Experience

Vicarious experience, another critical source of collective-level efficacy beliefs, involves the team observing the performance of a specific activity (Bandura 1997). This typically occurs in two ways: through selfmodeling, where the virtual team observes itself performing a task, or through observational learning, where the virtual team watches other teams perform. Although it is not as influential as enactive mastery, it still plays a significant role in shaping virtual teams’ beliefs about their ability to perform a task (Li et al. 2022). Virtual teams can derive substantial benefits from observing their performance or that of successful teams in collaborative environments using similar technologies.

## Verbal Persuasion

Virtual teams typically receive verbal persuasion through performance feedback. For example, research shows that feedback positivity increases collectivelevel efficacy in virtual team settings (Kahai et al. 2012) and that supervisor and peer support for virtual team technology positively impacts VTE (Schepers et al. 2011). Both examples illustrate how verbal persuasion shapes VTE.

## Physiological and Affective States

Affective states influence collective cognitive functioning and team performance. Various emotional factors, such as psychological collectivism, which is a collective attachment to a group (Turel and Connelly 2012); collective-level social loafing, which is the collective reduction of effort (Turel and Yi 2011); and trustworthiness, which is the collective perception of the team’s abilities, benevolence, and integrity (Jacques et al. 2020), play a significant role in shaping the collective-level efficacy beliefs of virtual teams. Studies have also confirmed that physiological synchrony, the linking of the nervous systems of virtual team members, is associated with emergent states, such as collective-level efficacy (Peng 2022).

## Integrating the Primary Efficacy Sources Through Collective Cognition

Integrating collective efficacy and cognition theories, we propose that virtual teams develop a shared understanding of information associated with these four primary sources during a complex collective cognition process. During the accumulation phase, virtual team members share information about prior enactive mastery experiences, modeling training, performance feedback, and perceptions of affective states such as psychological collectivism or collective-level social loafing (Turel and Connelly 2012). Virtual team members then evaluate these inputs and discard irrelevant information while storing necessary information.

Collective cognition theory suggests that teams proceed to the interaction and examination stages if the stored information is unstructured or unclear (Gibson 2001). We theorize that because the effective exercise of collective action involves incorporating complex socially mediated paths of influence (Bandura 1997), sources of efficacy information are naturally ambigu ous in virtual team settings. As a result, the accumulation of information phase is insufficient for cognitively integrating the information required to develop a shared understanding. Thus, virtual teams must progress from the accumulation of information phase to the interaction and examination phases when considering information associated with the primary efficacy sources.

During the interaction phase, members retrieve and exchange the stored sources of efficacy information gathered during the accumulation of information phase, building a transactive memory repository of individual memories related to past enactive mastery and vicarious experiences, feedback, and emotiona states. In the examination phase, virtual teams receive and absorb this information, gaining a shared understanding through subjective impressions and collectively constructed interpretations (Gibson and Earley 2007). In this phase, virtual team members affect each other’s perceptions, judgments, and opinions (Gibson 2001). For example, virtual teams grow in their awareness of members’ emotional and psychological states, developing insights into the overall emotional atmosphere of the team (van der Ham et al. 2019), and create mental models based on perceptions of enactive mastery and vicarious experiences that influence their belief in the ability to coordinate effectively (Cavanagh et al. 2019).

In summary, when virtual teams undergo the collective cognition process, they gather information from the primary efficacy sources during the accumulation of information phase. Given the complexity of the cognitive integration of these primary sources of efficacy information, virtual teams must also progress through the interaction and examination phases. Virtual teams acquire, evaluate, and process relevant information and experiences in these stages and assess their relevance in judging their collective capabilities (Mu¨ ller et al. 2023). This allows the team to envision its abilities to achieve the level of coordination necessary to attain desired outcomes (Mu¨ ller and Antoni 2020). Whereas VTE emerges through a collective cognition process in which virtual teams consider the primary sources of efficacy, the critical consequence is that the shared understanding of this information (positive or negative) leads to (higher or lower) VTE.

Proposition 1. Developing a positive (negative) shared understanding of the primary sources of efficacy information during the collective cognition process leads to higher (lower) virtual team efficacy.

## Individual-Level and Collective-Level Efficacy

Virtual teams consider their technology and team collective-level efficacy beliefs when developing VTE (Fuller et al. 2007). We acknowledge this consideration and expand on the underlying logic by emphasizing that individuals incorporate their beliefs in their own abilities when forming collective-level efficacy perceptions (Bandura 1997). For instance, virtual team members consider beliefs about their individual abilities to utilize computer technology as they form beliefs about their team’s abilities to use computers (Hardin 2024). They also incorporate beliefs about their individual abilities to work in teams when forming perceptions about the team’s abilities to do the same (Hardin et al. 2006). Virtual teams may also collectively consider members individual efficacy beliefs if shared with the other members. Thus, virtual teams incorporate domain-specific individual-level beliefs into their collective-level efficacy perceptions through their members’ consideration of their own abilities, or collectively if members share them with the team.

## Individual-Level Technology and Team Efficacy

Team members consider their individual efficacy when developing beliefs about their team’s capabilities (Bandura 1997). It is therefore unsurprising that CSE (Marakas et al. 1998, Johnson and Marakas 2000, Hardin et al. 2014),<sup>2</sup> or “a judgment of one’s capability to use a computer” (Compeau and Higgins 1995b, p. 192), influences virtual team members’ perceptions of their teams’ abilities to use computers (Hardin 2024). Virtual teams may also consider these individuallevel beliefs collectively if members share their CSE beliefs with the team. Members also incorporate their individual beliefs about working in teams when they form perceptions of their team’s collaborative capabilities. Hardin et al. (2007, p. 6) suggest that group selfefficacy (GSE), or “the belief in an individual’s ability to work in groups,” influences collective-level team efficacy beliefs. Furthermore, Hardin et al. (2006) propose that virtual team self-efficacy (VTSE), or the “belief in an individual’s ability to use communication technology to coordinate their activities with others across time and space” (Hardin et al. 2007, p. 6), directly influences VTE. Ultimately, whether considered internally by individual members or collectively by the virtual team, individual-level efficacy beliefs impact collective-level beliefs about the virtual team’s capabilities within the same domain.

Highlighting the critical influence of self-efficacy on the formation of collective-level efficacy beliefs, Hardin et al. (2007) found that virtual team members from individualist cultures report higher self-efficacy beliefs than members from collectivist cultures. However, their findings also revealed an interesting relationship between individual- and collective-level efficacy beliefs. Although members from collectivist cultures reported significantly lower self-efficacy than their counterparts from individualistic cultures, there was no significant difference between collective-level beliefs across the two groups (Hardin et al. 2007). Consequently, selfefficacy beliefs among members of culturally diverse virtual teams may differ, whereas collective-level beliefs may be similar. Virtual team members must be mindful of these potential differences so they can work to resolve them during the collective cognition phases.

## Collective-Level Technology and Team Efficacy

Domain-specific collective-level efficacy beliefs can help to form other collective-level efficacy beliefs (Hardin 2024). Because virtual teams frequently rely on software applications to produce deliverables, their belief in their ability to perform general computer tasks, or computer CE (CCE), influences VTE. Collective-level team efficacy, such as a team’s belief in its ability to produce outcomes across multiple tasks, is also an antecedent to VTE (Fuller et al. 2007).<sup>3</sup> We acknowledge the empirical results supporting these direct relationships but expand on the underlying logic by proposing that collectivelevel efficacy measures focused on general computer beliefs, such as CCE, and multiple tasks, such as collective-level team efficacy, function as inputs to the collective cognition processes leading to VTE. Thus, instead of the direct effects of collective-level technology and team efficacy on VTE, virtual teams develop a shared understanding of these efficacy types as they progress through the collective cognition phases. This shared sense of collective-level technology and team efficacy partially shapes VTE.

## Integrating Individual- and Collective-Level Efficacy Beliefs Through Collective Cognition

During the accumulation of information phase of col lective cognition, team members gather, filter, and store knowledge and information regarding their colleagues’ technology and team-related efficacy beliefs. During this phase, virtual teams filter and store only the information acted upon during their interactions. Teams label this stored information using jargon (behaviors unique to the team) and categories (sets of information) to organize information about individual- and collective-level team and technology efficacy beliefs. Virtual teams then progress to the interaction phase, in which they develop transactive memory systems to effectively retrieve and exchange information regarding these efficacy beliefs. As they move to the examination phase, team members negotiate the subjective interpretation of conflicting information by affecting each other’s opinions on their beliefs in the virtual team’s abilities to utilize technology and work successfully in teams. For example, as team members interact, they may downplay the high individual- and collective-level beliefs expressed by overconfident members and adopt more acceptable levels described by the majority (Hardin et al. 2006). If the interpretation of stored information is inconsistent, virtual teams can reconsider it and revise their collective understanding accordingly (Gibson 2001).

Virtual teams establish a collective view of their team and technology efficacy beliefs by engaging in the collective cognition processes of accumulation, interaction, and examination. Applying the same logic as Proposition 1, we propose that developing a shared understanding during the collective cognition process leads to VTE. The effect of the emerging shared understanding on VTE development depends on whether it is positive or negative. Therefore, we propose that

Proposition 2. Developing a positive (negative) shared understanding of individual- and collective-level technology and team efficacy during the collective cognition process leads to higher (lower) virtual team efficacy.

## Team and Task Factors

Virtual team composition plays a crucial role in shaping VTE. Cultural and demographic diversity, leadership, and member expertise are examples of team composition factors that virtual teams may consider during the collective cognition process. For example, racial and ethnic diversity can affect collective-leve efficacy in virtual team settings (Sargent and Sue-Chan 2001, Gilson et al. 2015). Demographic diversity, especially in the context of younger, tech-savvy generations, is increasingly influential in virtual team settings. Millennials and subsequent generations, who have grown up with technology, are more familiar with using communication media for collaboration (Warden et al. 2020). This shift in perspective may align with the belief systems of newer generations. For instance, they may view face-to-face meetings as a waste of time because of their comfort using technology to collaborate, whereas members of older generations may consider them essential (Gilson et al. 2015). The resolution of these differences to develop a shared understanding is critical during the develop ment of VTE. Other team composition factors may also play a role in VTE development. For example, research findings establish that leadership style influences feedback positivity, which affects collectivelevel efficacy in virtual team settings (Kahai et al. 2012). Virtual teams can also vary widely in terms of team member expertise, and teams naturally consider members’ skills when developing collective-level efficacy beliefs (Bandura 1997, Gibson and Earley 2007).

Virtual teams consider the task when forming VTE. For example, completing shorter tasks requires more intensive efforts to develop accurate VTE beliefs (Fuller et al. 2007). The nature of the team members’ interactions when completing tasks also plays a role in this process. If virtual team members complete the tasks independently, the outcomes represent the summed contributions of individual efforts. In such circum stances, the emergent properties of collective-level efficacy are unlikely to materialize. The typology of tasks can also influence the development of VTE. Fuller et al. (2007) described how their study of virtual teams solving decision-making tasks consistent with the “choose” quadrant of the McGrath task circumplex (McGrath 1984) was suitable for investigating VTE because the projects involved solving problems with no correct answers. However, tasks associated with the other quadrants of the circumplex, such as idea generation, executing, and negotiating, vary along the axes of cognitive and behavioral, collaboration, coordination, and conflict resolution. These variations in task characteristics influence the coordination and cooperation of teams (McGrath 1984), which can impede the collective cognition processes by which VTE forms.

## Integrating Team and Task Factors Through Collective Cognition

Team composition and task type influence VTE development during the collective cognition processes. During the accumulation phase, teams acquire information and knowledge about these and other team and task factors. Virtual teams derive labels for the accumulated information acted on during group interactions. These labels affect perceptions and often involve jargon and categories understood only by the team (Gibson 2001). Jargon and categories convey appropriate attributes or behaviors for the virtual team, such as accommodating cultural and demographic considerations or forming expectations regarding the length, interdependence, or typology of tasks when filtering, labeling, and retaining information.

Given the complexity of developing a shared understanding of this newly formed knowledge, virtual teams must move from the accumulation of information phase to the interaction phase of collective cognition. During the interaction phase, virtual team members develop a transactive memory system to recall the retained information based in part on the collectively derived labels. In the examination phase, virtual team members develop subjective and alternative impressions of these factors to negotiate a shared perspective. For example, virtual team members consider other members’ cultural and demographic characteristics when developing their VTE beliefs (Hardin et al. 2006, 2024). Members then negotiate these subjective impressions, affecting each other’s perceptions, judgments, and opinions to reach a shared understanding. Virtual team members may enter a project based on assumptions about specific cultural and demographic characteristics that are altered through interactions with other team members. Developing a shared understanding of leadership is also critical during this phase, as it is fundamental to virtual team functioning, especially in guiding how teams handle obstacles and adapt to challenges (Gilson et al. 2015). Establishing leadership status is essential during the examination phase, as members may weigh their opinions more heavily during group discussions. Accurately identifying the expertise of individual team members is also vital for forming VTE. Discussions related to task length are needed, as shorter-duration projects require more intensive efforts to progress through the accumulation, interaction, and examination phases of collective cognition. Developing a shared understanding of the nuances related to the project task type or the need to complete multiple tasks that vary based on the components discussed in the McGrath Circumplex similarly requires a focused effort by the team.

Whereas team and task factors shape VTE during collective cognition, the arguments presented in Propositions 1 and 2 apply. VTE depends on whether the shared understanding of the information is positive or negative. Therefore, we propose that

Proposition 3. Developing a positive (negative) shared understanding of team and task factors during the collective cognition process leads to higher (lower) virtual team efficacy.

## Virtual Team Structure

The structure of virtual teams plays a significant role in how members work together to accomplish their objectives. Virtual team structure differs from the individual- and collective-level efficacy beliefs discussed earlier. The latter represents “beliefs” about technology use and team functioning, whereas the former represents actual virtual team configurations. The structure of the virtual team environment is also distinct from team composition, which includes members’ characteristics such as cultural and demographic backgrounds, and task type, which includes task length, interdependence, and typology. In other words, team composition focuses on team members’ attributes (Mathieu et al. 2014), and task type focuses on the task structure, which is independent of whether team members collaborate in virtual or nonvirtual settings. Ultimately, team composition and task type can be the same in traditional and virtual teams. Below, we utilize the concept of virtuality to define spe cific virtual team structures (Handke et al. 2020).

## Virtuality

Researchers rely on the concept of virtuality to define virtual teams more precisely (Chudoba et al. 2005, Schweitzer and Duxbury 2010, Handke et al. 2020). Because the virtuality components discussed in these studies have considerable conceptual overlap, we combined similar components into three categories: workplace variation, time and place, and technology use, as shown in Table 1.

Workplace variation includes workplace mobility: working with other departments, using mobile devices, working from home, and working while traveling; vari ety of practices: working with changing members, different methods for tracking work, and working with various technologies; and member virtuality, or the proportion of team members stationed at other locations (Chudoba et al. 2005, Lu et al. 2006, Hao et al. 2022). Time and place includes contextual dispersion: spatial and time differences and diversity of the team; time worked virtually: the portion of the task performed virtually; and distance virtuality: the geographic dispersion of the team members (Schweitzer and Duxbury 2010). Technology use includes reliance on virtual tools: how much teams rely on virtual tools to work and communicate; information value: the extent to which virtual tools enable communication and data transfer; and synchronicity: the level of asynchronous and synchronous exchange (Kirkman and Mathieu 2005).

## Integrating Virtuality Factors Through Collective Cognition

Combining virtuality components into three categories facilitates a discussion of how virtual teams develop a shared understanding of virtual team structure during the collective cognition phases. Virtual teams assemble information and knowledge about virtuality during the accumulation of information phase of the collective cognition process in which they filter and store the information they act upon during team interactions. For example, during this phase, teams gather data on the diversity of work processes, team members’ departmental affiliations, expectations about working while traveling or from home, and the devices they use to communicate. Teams also acquire information about time zone differences, the time spent working virtually, the time and effort it takes to meet in person, and the number of members working at each location. Teams also accumulate information about technological factors during this phase, for example, the need to rely on virtual tools to communicate because of the time spent working virtually, the value of technology (whether members perceive the use of a specific technology as a benefit or hindrance) (Raghuram et al. 2019), and the type of technology they will use, such as email, audio/video conferencing systems, and virtual reality platforms. Virtual teams use labels, which later become jargon, and categories to organize the virtuality information.

During the interaction phase, virtual team members develop transactive memory to retrieve and structure this previously stored information. Recalling this information is crucial for recognizing why variations occur in project deliverable due dates and times. For example, if a member communicates primarily using a mobile device, they may not have immediate access to large deliverable files stored on their personal computer. During the examination phase, virtual teams negotiate subjective interpretations of virtuality factors to foster a collective understanding of the team’s structure and how it impacts their collaborative efforts. In the previous example, virtual teams may form a shared understanding that certain members will need more time to act upon and forward project deliverables. Thus, evaluating interpretations of the virtual team structure is highly complex, especially when the working environments are varied, such as when some members are in the office, some are at home, and others are traveling. Gaining a shared understanding of subjective interpretations of which work practices to follow, which tasks to assign members based on their departmental affiliations, travel requirements, when and how they prefer to communicate, the amount of time spent working virtually, whether to meet in person, if possible, and what collaborative technologies to rely on for team success is a complex process that requires a great deal of interpretation that impacts the formation of VTE.

Applying the same logic as Propositions 1–3, we posit that the strength of VTE depends on whether the emerging shared understanding is positive or negative. Therefore, we propose that

Proposition 4. Developing a positive (negative) shared understanding of virtuality information during the collective cognition process leads to higher (lower) virtual team efficacy.

## Appropriating Communication Media

Establishing synchronicity, or a shared pattern of coordinated synchronous behavior with a common focus, leads to fewer misunderstandings when sharing infor mation. MST extends this to technology-mediated com munication by defining media synchronicity as “the extent to which the capabilities of a communication medium enable individuals to achieve synchronicity” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 581). MST proposes two primary communication processes: convergence processes that benefit from synchronicity and conveyance processes that do not. Convergence processes focus on higherlevel abstractions of information that inform existing mental models, allowing collaborators to quickly trans mit smaller amounts of information to develop a shared understanding (Dennis et al. 2008). Thus, communication channels that facilitate the rapid delivery of messages (transmission velocity) and offer diverse symbol sets and encoding options (symbol sets) can enhance media synchronicity. These capabilities facili tate discussions of preprocessed information based on an individual’s interpretation to reach a common understanding. However, conveyance processes focus on transmitting large amounts of information and subsequent retrospective analysis, reducing the need to communicate and process information simulta neously (Dennis et al. 2008). These processes require media capabilities that support multiple simultaneous transmissions (parallelism), allowing the sender to rehearse or refine their message (rehearsability) and enabling the reexamination or reprocessing of a message (reprocessability). Ultimately, MST proposes that the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes influences the development of a shared understanding.

## Communication Media and the Collective Cognition Process

This proposition unites the three theories to finalize the left-hand portion of the model. Collective efficacy theory proposes that teams develop beliefs about their ability to function effectively. Collective cognition theory suggests that teams move through several specific phases of cognition to achieve outcomes. MST proposes that matching the need for media synchronicity with conveyance and convergence communication processes leads to shared understanding. VTET integrates components from these three theories to articulate a complex, technology-mediated collective cognition process that leads to VTE. In the accumulation of information phase, virtual teams engage in conveyance communication processes that focus on transmitting large amounts of antecedent information and its subsequent retrospective analysis. Thus, teams should rely on communication media with information transmission and processing capabilities that support parallelism, rehearsability, and reprocessability during this phase. On the other hand, during the interaction and examination phases of collective cognition, virtual teams rely on convergence communication processes to develop transactive memory and negotiate shared interpretations. Thus, during these phases, virtual teams should rely on communication media with information transmission and processing capabilities that support synchronicity, such as transmission velocity and symbol sets, to develop a shared understanding.

VTET proposes that the collective cognitive process requires virtual teams to utilize communication media effectively to integrate the primary sources of efficacy information, technology, and team collective-level efficacy beliefs, and team, task, and virtuality factors to form VTE. During the accumulation of information phase, teams engage in conveyance communication processes that do not require synchronicity. During the interaction and examination phases, virtual teams engage in convergence communication processes that require media synchronicity. Effectively appropriating communication media with transmission and processing capabilities to meet these needs results in fewer misunderstandings and confusion as teams strive to build a shared understanding that leads to VTE. We noted in Propositions 1–4 that the emerging shared understanding that leads to VTE can be positive or negative. Therefore, we propose that

Proposition 5. Effective (ineffective) appropriation of communication media (matching media capabilities with communication processes) during the collective cognition process results in a positive (negative) shared understanding that leads to higher (lower) virtual team efficacy.

## Mediating Processes

Having explained how VTE forms, we next develop the downstream portion of our theoretical framework. As we explain below, VTE acts on outcomes through factors underlying collective efficacy theory’s four categories of cognitive, motivational, affective, and selection mediating processes. VTE also influences outcomes through communication performance by serving as an appropriation factor that influences the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes. Finally, we theorize that these mediating processes occur within the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition.

## Cognitive Processes

Cognitive processes represent virtual teams’ inferences about the impact of their collective actions during the accommodation of the information phase of collective cognition in which teams integrate members’ perceptions, judgments, and opinions to generate decisions and actions. Successful virtual teams focus on these tasks despite facing the challenges posed by ambiguous situations and the potential negative consequences of their actions. VTE beliefs impact how virtual teams cognitively prepare for these scenarios and thus indirectly influence outcomes through these processes. These indirect effects also occur through later-stage cognitive trust, which develops over time as virtual team members gain a collectivelevel understanding of the team’s abilities. High-VTE teams develop positive perceptions, judgments, and opinions about the reliability of their virtual team members to produce high-quality deliverables (i.e., trust) and generate decisions and actions that lead to higher outcomes. Thus, higher VTE leads to higher cognitive trust and increased virtual team effectiveness (Hardin et al. 2024).

## Motivational Processes

During the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition, virtual teams engage in mutual perspective taking, cognitive integration, decision making, and action (Gibson 2001). Collective efficacy theory suggests that during the later stages of collective cogni tion, motivational processes such as causal attributions and goal setting mediate the collective-level efficacy performance relationship (Bandura 1997). Thus, VTE partially affects decisions and actions through these mediating factors. For example, causal attribution can influence a virtual team’s attribution of success or fail ure to external factors. Virtual teams with strong VTE beliefs ascribe their success to their collective abilities and failures to their lack of effort. This attribution increases collective effort and persistence when facing challenging tasks, thereby increasing performance. Supporting this logic, Fuller et al. (2007) found that effort mediates the relationship between VTE and perceptual performance and that virtual teams with high VTE communicate more frequently, increasing objective performance.

Team-set goals are partially influenced by collectivelevel efficacy perceptions (Prussia and Kinicki 1996). These findings coincide with the proposal that goals are associated with calibrating collective-level efficacy and traditional team performance during the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition. When a collective-level belief is well calibrated, teams are positioned to deploy actions to control critical events in their environments (Gibson and Earley 2007). The findings of Hardin et al. (2013a) support this view in the context of virtual teams by establishing that goalsetting processes mediate the relationship between VTE and objective performance. Virtual teams with higher VTE set more challenging goals and are more committed to them. In turn, goal commitment is significantly related to objective performance.

## Affective Processes

Strong collective-level efficacy empowers traditional teams to overcome negative affective processes that hinder performance outcomes during the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition (Gibson and Earley 2007). Similarly, VTE beliefs impact virtual team outcomes through affective processes during this phase. As virtual teams integrate information through mutual perspective taking, they must exercise control over deleterious affective influences such as team anxiety. Teams with high VTE are better at tackling and shaping these troublesome situations to their advantage. Supporting these notions, Hardin et al. (2024) demonstrate how teams’ collective anxieties associated with perceptions of problems with collaboration inhibitors, such as time differences, geographical separation, and cultural differences, mediate the relationship between VTE and virtual team effectiveness. High VTE reduces perceptions of problems with these collaboration inhibitors, thereby improving the effectiveness of virtual teams.

## Selection Processes

Selection processes influence the types of activities and environments in which virtual teams engage. During the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition, virtual team members’ perceptions, judgments, and opinions lead to specific decisions and actions. Virtual teams avoid activities they believe exceed their capabilities but will readily engage in activities they think they can handle. Selection processes differ from cognitive, motivational, and affective processes because if teams choose not to engage in certain activities, the latter processes never come into play (Bandura 1997). Although no studies have examined the mediating effect of selection processes on the relationship between VTE and virtual team outcomes, circumstantial evidence provided by investigations of other mediating processes supports the idea that virtual teams with higher VTE are more willing to select complex projects and exhibit greater persistence during project challenges.

Based on the logic presented in this section, we propose that

Proposition 6. Virtual team efficacy positively influences outcomes through cognitive, motivational, affective, and selection processes by increasing beneficial processes and reducing detrimental processes.

## The Mediating Role of Communication Performance

Virtual teams rely heavily on communication media for collaboration. Thus, VTET argues that communication performance is vital to the relationship between VTE and team effectiveness. MST proposes that communication performance relies on the fit between communication processes and media synchronicity (Dennis et al. 2008). MST defines media synchronicity as the capacity of communication media to enable individuals to work together simultaneously with a common focus. MST describes conveyance communication processes as those that focus on transmitting large amounts of raw data for retrospective analysis. Convergence communication processes, on the other hand, focus on the discussion of preprocessed information to reach a common understanding among the communicating parties.

VTE serves as an appropriation factor that affects the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes in virtual team settings. This fit is critical to successful communication during the integration, decision-making, and action processes that occur during the accommodation of information phase of collective cognition. High-VTE teams will have already mastered the ability to successfully appropriate media capabilities to gather, filter, store, recall, interpret, and negotiate VTE, building information to develop a positive shared understanding. Consequently, these virtual teams strongly believe in their ability to collaborate using communication technology and appropriate media that best fit the communication processes required to successfully complete project deliverables during the decisionmaking and action processes of the accommodation of information phase. High-VTE teams will use communication media that best fit the processes of integrating members’ perceptions, judgments, and opinions to generate decisions and actions related to completing project deliverables. By influencing the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes, VTE indirectly influences virtual team outcomes through communication performance. Therefore, we propose that

Proposition 7. Virtual teams with higher (lower) virtual team efficacy will achieve higher (lower) communication performance and outcomes by appropriating media capabilities that do (do not) fit the communication processes.

## Limitations: Considerations Not Explicitly Included in Our Model Changes in VTE over Time

We expect that VTE developed during the initial stages of collective cognition will be relatively stable in temporary virtual teams working together to produce specific project outcomes. However, we also acknowledge that changes in VTE can occur when longstanding organizational teams encounter new efficacy sources, individual- and collective-level efficacy beliefs, and team, task, and virtuality factors. For example, virtual teams acquire new enactive mastery experiences when completing critical milestone deliverables. They may also gain new information through vicarious experiences, such as engaging in modeling training, changing affective states because of experiencing distressing situations, or verbal persuasion delivered by performance feedback. Changes in team factors, such as adding new team members with different levels of expertise and cultural and demographic backgrounds, may also cause teams to revise their VTE beliefs. Managerial requests to perform new tasks or to work independently or interdependently may also cause VTE to change. Finally, alterations in virtuality factors, such as the types of technologies that virtual teams use to overcome changes in work practices, tracking work and time differences, and geographical locations, may also cause teams to amend their VTE beliefs.

We expect these encounters with new information will affect virtual teams’ progress along the collective cognition phases, as the theory suggests that specific catalysts cause teams to move from one phase to another (Gibson 2001). Similarly, we expect that catalysts such as acquiring, filtering, storing, and negotiating a subjective understanding of new information about primary efficacy sources, technology and team efficacy, team and task factors, and virtual team structure may cause virtual teams to move among the collective cognition phases. Whether teams move back and forth from the accommodation to the accumulation of information phase or progress through all the phases may be dependent on the strength of VTE beliefs, as teams with lower VTE beliefs may have a greater need to progress through the interaction and examination phases to negotiate a collective view of new information. However, making predictions related to changes in VTE over time is beyond the scope of our conceptual study; therefore, we suggest this as a possibility for future empirical research.

## Environmental Factors

Schepers et al. (2011) establish that competitors’ use of virtual team technology is an external antecedent of VTE. We contend that such variables are better suited as context-specific constraints than essential factors influencing VTE formation. However, we acknowledge that environmental factors share a complex relationship with VTET. Collective efficacy theory is a subset of social cognitive theory, which proposes a triadic reciprocal relationship between the person, behavior, and environment (Bandura 1997). A classic illustration is television viewing, in which the personal preferences of the viewers (person) influence which programs are watched (behavior), which partially shapes televised offerings (environment) and reciprocally shapes viewer preferences (Bandura 1978). In virtual team settings, team members’ preferences (person) influence team actions (behavior), which partially shapes the offering of tools used by teams (environment), which, in turn, reciprocally influences virtual team member preferences. Thus, we expect that virtual team behaviors influence organizational and societal factors and that these factors influence teams choices and actions. For example, members’ collaboration technology preferences influence their use by the virtual team, which affects the suite of communication technologies that organizations or vendors provide. Similar relationships occur with the collaborative strategies used to complete virtual teamwork, as the prefer ences of virtual team members influence the strategy they follow, which affects the collaborative methods organizations adopt and endorse, which, in turn, influences members’ preferences.

Whereas we acknowledge the existence of these relationships, their complex reciprocal nature prevented us from explicitly specifying them within VTET’s nomological network. In addition, accounting for all the environmental factors that may influence VTE’s influence on outcomes is impractical. But more importantly, environmental factors are likely to have a more significant impact on the organizational adoption of technologies and practices than on the internal technology-mediated, collective cognition processes that lead to VTE develop ment and its impact on virtual team outcomes.

## Theoretical Novelty and Scientific and Practical Usefulness

A theoretical contribution can be gauged along two dimensions: how novel (or revelatory) the theory is and how scientifically and practically useful it is (Corley and Gioia 2011). Novelty occurs when the study develops concepts that are absent from the existing (related) literature. Specifically, when a study delivers new theoretical components such as constructs, processes, states, and relationships and unifies them under a common integrative framework that departs from the existing literature, a compelling theoretical contribu tion can be discerned (Weber 2012, Chatterjee et al. 2024). Scientific usefulness through inspiration for future research and practical implications is another hallmark of a good theory. In the following sections, we discuss how VTET addresses these dimensions.

## Theoretical Novelty

Our VTET framework extends the existing literature by synthesizing collective efficacy, collective cognition, and media synchronicity theories to propose that VTE develops through a complex technologymediated collective cognition process. VTET clarifies that leveraging multiple theories is necessary to conceptualize collective-level efficacy in virtual teams. For example, collective efficacy theory provides critical information about the categories of primary efficacy sources and the mediating processes responsible for collective-level efficacy’s impact on downstream outcomes but falls short of explaining how virtual teams develop collective cognition and utilize communication media. Collective cognition theory details the process of collective cognition but is silent about how communication media influences the development of collective-level efficacy and the associated mediating processes through which it impacts virtual team outcomes. Finally, media synchronicity theory explains components of successful technology-mediated communication but does not address how collective-level efficacy and collective cognition develop.

Thus, to understand VTE, we need a compelling synthesis of three theories: collective efficacy theory (because of the virtual team efficacy aspect) (Bandura 1997), collective cognition theory (because of the team cognition aspect) (Gibson 2001, Gibson and Earley 2007), and media synchronicity theory (because of the communication technology aspect) (Dennis et al. 2008). Integrating these theories helps articulate how VTE forms through a complex collective cognition process of accumulation, interaction, and examination, which involves gathering, filtering, labeling, storing, recalling, sharing, and interpreting critical information encountered by virtual teams. This specification of an intermediary collective cognitive process affords an additional contribution by providing a parsimonious explanation of how virtual teams incorporate information that influences the development of VTE. VTET also integrates concepts from these three reference theories to explain the downstream impact of VTE on virtual team outcomes. For example, VTET leverages concepts from collective efficacy theory to illustrate how VTE influences virtual team outcomes through cognitive, motivational, affective, and selection processes. VTET also utilizes media synchronicity theory to explain how VTE acts as an appropriation factor affecting the fit between communication processes and media synchronicity to influence communication performance and virtual team outcomes.

Finally, the sociotechnical nature of VTET provides novelty in this stream of work because of its identifiable information systems “signature” (Sarker et al. 2019, Chatterjee et al. 2021). Many virtual team studies position collective-level efficacy as a nominal or contextual factor (an observation supported by our literature review). These studies, according to the sociotechnical view forwarded by Sarker et al. (2019, p. 702), fall under a type I category (“absent presence of IT”). In contrast, our study is consistent with what Sarker et al. (2019, p. 703) refer to as type IV, which “is the type most compatible with the social and technical relationship as conceived within the sociotechnical perspective.” VTET substantiates its sociotechnical contribution by incorporating relationships among social and technical factors, such as virtual teams’ appropriation of communication media to enable collaboration across time and space.

## Scientific Usefulness and Future Research

VTET provides scientific usefulness by providing a generalizable theoretical explanation of how VTE develops and impacts virtual outcomes. Future research can leverage VTET to empirically contextualize constructs representing the generalizable categories of VTE-building information. These categories include the primary sources of efficacy, individual- and collective-level technology and team efficacy, team and task factors, and virtual team structure (virtuality).

For example, future research can investigate numerous factors as potential instantiations of the primary sources of efficacy to gauge their differential impact on VTE development during the collective cognition phases. Team anxiety, cohesion, mood, conflict, and trustworthiness are potential instantiations of affective state sources. Depending on their directional intensity, these factors can have beneficial or detrimental influences on VTE development during the collective cognitive process. Verbal persuasion captures peer encouragement and customer appreciation (Schepers et al. 2011) but can also account for leader feedback, which may have a substantial impact on the development of VTE during collective cognition. Future research can similarly investigate many other factors as potential instantiations of VTET’s primary sources of efficacy information.

VTET’s specification of individual- and collectivelevel team and technology efficacy as inputs to the collective cognitive process can also be the focus of future research. For example, contemporary virtual teams may utilize sophisticated virtual worlds (Hardin et al. 2013b) and artificial intelligence agent technologies (Dennis et al. 2023). During deliberations about their beliefs in their collective-level technology abilities, virtual teams must judge their capabilities to use these novel technologies. Virtual teams must also assess their collective-level beliefs about working together successfully when implementing innovative, collaborative approaches. Future research could investigate many other efficacy-related factors for their potential roles as inputs to the collective cognition process.

Future research could also investigate team composition factors such as diversity and generational effects. For example, younger and older virtual team members may disagree on using specific technologies and collaborative approaches. These differences must be resolved during the interaction and examination phases of collective cognition to avoid the detrimental effects that may occur as a result of subgroups (Davison et al. 2017). Recent research shows that the individual characteristics of virtual team members, such as personality and intelligence, are also essential in virtual teams (Dennis et al. 2022). Developing a shared understanding of these team composition factors is crucial during the collective cognition process that leads to VTE. Research has also established that leadership is an essential team composition variable. For example, studies have shown that team members perceive that leaders decode their messages better when they are geographically distant from the team (Gilson et al. 2015).

VTET can guide investigations of the impact of different task types on developing VTE. Task characteristics influence teams’ coordination and cooperation (McGrath 1984), which can affect the collective cognition process and, in turn, the development of VTE. Thus, it would be informative to investigate how teams develop a shared understanding when undertaking different tasks (e.g., idea generation, executing, and negotiating).

Factors underlying the categories of virtuality, including workplace variation, time and place, and technology use, also warrant empirical investigations. For example, how do virtual teams evaluate and resolve specific work practices, workplace mobility, and member virtuality to arrive at a collective perspective on their collaborative capabilities? Similar questions are relevant for virtuality factors related to the situational constraints associated with time and place (Hardin et al. 2024). Investigating the virtuality category of technology use may be particularly fruitful, as it relates to VTET’s conception of media appropriation during the respective phases of collective cognition.

VTET emphasizes virtual teams’ holistic consideration of information during the collective cognition phases. This specification allows for a parsimonious model that avoids numerous propositions describing all the potential interactions among the factors affecting VTE. However, this does not nullify the possibility of interactions among these factors or the need to investigate them empirically. Thus, VTET provides a conceptual framework for future research evaluating potential interactions among the many sources of VTE-building information that virtual teams consider.

The downstream portion of VTET also provides a generalizable theoretical perspective that provides opportunities for future research. Some virtual team studies have already begun investigating specific mediating factors influencing the relationship between VTE and its outcomes. For example, research has established that VTE affects virtual team outcomes through trust (cognitive process) and effort, communication frequency, and goal setting (motivational processes) (Fuller et al. 2007, Hardin et al. 2013a, Hardin et al. 2024). Investigating these factors contributes to the literature by promoting a generalized understanding of the underlying mediating processes leading to virtual team outcomes. VTET presents an abstraction of these mediating processes that can be instantiated according to the empirical context to further develop this generalized understanding. To illustrate how this is accomplished in practice, in a recent virtual team study incorporating VTE, the overarching abstraction is an affective mediating process instantiated into a construct capturing perceptions of problems with situational constraints such as time differences, geographical separation, and cultural differences (Hardin et al. 2024).

Whereas the few studies mentioned above have investigated the mediating processes that influence the relationship between VTE and virtual team outcomes, many others have examined only the direct effects of collective-level efficacy (Capiola et al. 2019, Jacques et al. 2020, McLarnon and Woodley 2021). VTET aims to resolve this inconsistency by emphasizing the abstract mediating categories of cognitive, motivational, affective, and selection processes that affect the relationship between VTE and virtual team outcomes. Thus, VTET can guide additional empirical instantiations of these abstract processes to investigate the downstream effects of VTE. For example, transactive memory, collective mindfulness, and problem solving all fall under cognitive mediating processes. Persistence and outcome expectancies are examples of motivational mediating processes. Collective-level anxiety, emotional conflict, cohesion, and affective trust are examples of affective mediating processes. Selection processes can include factors such as teams’ willingness to undertake challenging, high-value projects or to incorporate new technologies or collaborative methods. Over time, the results of these focused empirical studies can help refine the generalizable abstraction of mediating processes specified by VTET, providing further guidance for virtual team researchers applying VTE in their studies.

VTET’s specification of communication performance as a mediating factor influencing the relationship between VTE and outcomes can lead to additional research. For example, VTET suggests that VTE is an appropriation factor influencing the fit between media synchronicity and communication processes. MST proposes that the appropriation factors of familiarity, training, experience, and social norms also affect the need for media capabilities that support synchronicity. However, the relative impact of these appropriation factors within different virtual team structures remains an unanswered empirical question. Assessing the role of VTE as an additional appropriation factor increases the motivation for future studies designed to investi gate these critical relationships.

VTET is well suited for explaining how VTE develops in temporary, project-based virtual teams. However, the VTE of long-term organizational virtual teams can change over time as they encounter new sources of information. Theory suggests that these changes will affect virtual team movements through the collective cognition phases. For example, encountering new sources of information may result in brief returns to the accumulation of information phase, where virtual teams can gather, filter, and store the latest information they consider during the integration and examination phases. Future empirical studies should examine these potential temporal movements within VTET.

Finally, VTET guides the measurement of collectivelevel efficacy in future virtual team studies. Several recent studies have relied on collective-level efficacy measures developed for traditional team settings rather than on context-specific technology-related measures of VTE. These extant collective-level efficacy measures capture team considerations in their conceptual definitions and measurement indicators but do not expressly reference the use of communication media to collaborate. These conceptualizations miss the opportunity to capture the technology-related variance of virtual team factors (Hardin et al. 2006, Fuller et al. 2007, Schepers et al. 2011). Thus, operationalizations of VTE based on VTET provide a better assessment of collective-level efficacy in virtual teams.

## Practical Usefulness: Implications for Practice

The salience of VTET for informing practice is increasing because managers may view the collaborative technologies used by virtual teams as passive tools rather than active actors influencing the relationships between collaborative parties (Soga et al. 2021). Regrettably, many studies reinforce this viewpoint by using concepts developed for conventional teams in virtual team research. This approach conveys a passive view of technology to practitioners, minimizing how technology shapes essential aspects of virtual teamwork. VTET combats this by emphasizing the importance of technological factors affecting VTE development and its impact on virtual team outcomes.

Training facilitators can increase VTE by ensuring the delivery of enactive mastery experiences in settings that utilize technologies similar to those used in actual projects. In doing so, virtual team members gain valuable skills that increase technology-related efficacy beliefs that affect VTE. Similarly, organizational trainers can implement edited video conference meeting recordings to boost VTE through vicarious experience. Managers and virtual team members may also leverage VTET’s information about the complex collective cognition processes of accumulation of information, interaction, and examination. These processes guide the integration of primary sources of efficacy and other technology and team-related factors. Gaining a more profound understanding of these processes may help teams realize that developing VTE takes time and effort and that, in many cases, it is critical for them to progress deliberately through the phases of collective cognition.

The consideration of technological factors affecting VTE development highlights the importance of the design and development policies related to communi cation technology. From a design perspective, future studies can identify technological features that can help usability and the free exchange of ideas necessary for forming potent VTE (such as flexibility to allow for synchronous and asynchronous exchanges). These studies could answer questions such as whether technologies have accessibility issues that can impair the free exchange of shared information among team members who may be disadvantaged or whether the organization can implement communication policies that accentuate the salience of communication technol ogy (Toro et al. 2022). For example, corporate policy enforcing only the synchronous use of communication media during working hours (particularly for virtual teams collaborating across time zones) may compromise VTE development.

Managers can also increase VTE by helping members build strong individual beliefs about their ability to work in virtual team settings, as these beliefs act as positive inputs for their assumptions about their team’s collaborative capabilities. Aiding teams in developing robust collective-level technology and team efficacy may also have practical benefits, as empirical research shows that general collective-level efficacy and computer collective efficacy influence VTE (Fuller et al. 2007). Given that team composition is a factor that shapes VTE during the collective cognition process, organizations may institute policies on team composition for various task forces. Technology coaches may also help team members understand how the dimensions of virtuality impact the development of VTE beliefs and how they can effectively use technology to overcome these challenges.

The awareness that VTE shapes outcomes through mediating processes can lead to other practical implications. Organizational policies should ensure that virtual teams perform these mediating processes as expected (as an intermediate outcome of VTE). Organizational factors external to the team (e.g., narcissistic leadership, lack of procedural and distributive justice in organizational policies) might destabilize the effects of VTE on the mediating processes and, ultimately, virtual team outcomes. Finally, human resource managers can develop training initiatives to aid virtual teams in understanding how to improve communication performance by effectively matching the communication processes with media synchronicity. In virtual team settings, “managers should understand the nature of the individuals and the context in which they work, as this may suggest different requirements for media capabilities” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 595).

## Conclusion

Researchers have linked collective-level efficacy with the success of virtual teams. However, despite Fuller et al. (2007)’s introduction of VTE to the information system literature nearly 20 years ago, researchers have not proposed a comprehensive theoretical framework that explains how it develops in virtual team settings. The recent resurgence in the application of collectivelevel efficacy in virtual team research necessitates a guiding conceptual framework for theoretical advancement, appropriate measurement, and effective utilization in organizational settings. We have argued that without such a conceptual framework, research will progress in an unstructured fashion that ignores the critical role of technology in virtual team settings and falls prey to theoretical and operationalization issues, negatively impacting practical applications by organizational managers. We then addressed these potential academic and practical challenges by proposing VTET. We believe we have delivered a theory that “avoid[s] [both] ‘narrow empiricism’ as well as “being so general in … coverage that it is difficult, if not impossible, to test … empirically” (Weber 2012, p. 16). VTET can be instantiated into multiple contexts, possibly extended because of empirical needs (e.g., the addition of environmental factors), and provides enough degrees of freedom for future research on VTE to prosper. We hope our work will energize future studies and lead to a more meaningful, sociotechnical, unified body of VTE research.
