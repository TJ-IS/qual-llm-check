# Can Providing Algorithmic Performance Information Facilitate Humans’ Inventory Ordering Behaviors?

Abstract. Over recent years, companies have been increasingly adopting algorithmic decision systems (ADS) to replace humans. In this paper, we focus on how ADS facilitate human managers’ decision making rather than replacing humans altogether in the context of inventory ordering decisions, specifically on the effect of providing ADS performance information to human managers. Using a pair of field experiments, our results suggest that providing ADS performance information can improve their inventory ordering decisions. Interestingly, providing both positive and negative ADS performance information enhances human managers’ inventory ordering decisions, with the latter proving even more effective. Our analyses reveal that compliance and deliberation are two mechanisms through which ADS performance transparency influences inventory ordering decisions. Furthermore, we explore the heterogeneous effect and find that disclosing ADS performance information is more helpful for human managers overseeing products with lower sales and higher uncertainty, thus solving the pain points where human managers rely on algorithmic recommendations the most. Our results also show that ADS performance information serves as an equalizer that benefits low-performing managers more. These results demonstrate the importance of algorithm performance transparency in the human adoption of ADS and shed light on the managerial implications of using ADS within corporations.

## 1. Introduction

With the development of machine learning algorithms and the growing accessibility of large-scale data, algorithms have taken on an increasingly central role in a corporation’s decision-making process. Such algorithmic decision systems (ADS) leverage a large amount of data to derive insights that help stakeholders make decisions. In recent years, more companies have started to integrate ADS into their business processes. According to a survey conducted by McKinsey in 2020, 50% of respondents suggested that ADS had been adopted in at least one function of their organization (McKinsey 2020). Accompanying this trend is an emerging stream of academic research that examines the use of ADS in operations, such as in product removal (Kesavan and Kushwaha 2020) and business-to-business (B2B) procurement (Cui et al. 2022).

In this paper, we examine the use of ADS in a new context—inventory ordering decisions. Over recent years, many companies, including The Home Depot and Gap Inc., have started to allocate billions of dollars toward ADS to facilitate their inventory ordering decisions (Wall Street Journal 2021). However, to date, there has been an absence of academic research that has empirically evaluated the impact of ADS on such decisions. It is worth noting that the rendering of inventory ordering decisions is a highly repetitive and dataintensive task. To avoid both stock-out and overstock, managers need to make inventory ordering decisions across hundreds of products on a daily basis. Even more complicated, many products face a dynamic and complex inventory demand pattern that is very difficult for humans to understand, not to mention to predict consistently and accurately. Given that all managers are constrained by their cognitive capabilities (Donohue et al. 2020, Karlinsky-Shichor and Netzer 2024), it is also difficult for them to pay sufficient attention to each one of the products. These unique characteristics of inventory ordering, together with recent developments in deep learning, incentivize companies to leverage various machine learning algorithms in their inventory ordering decisions. Indeed, algorithms are designed specifically to leverage large-scale data to cope with intricate problems, which is also supported by various anecdotal information that further testifies to the successful implementation ADS in inventory ordering decisions (AWS 2021).

At the same time, inventory ordering is a process in which human decisions play a central role (Perera et al. 2020, Zhao et al. 2021). Although algorithms can make accurate predictions based on codified data, human managers often possess tacit knowledge that is difficult to capture in an algorithm. Such tacit knowledge may enable humans to outperform algorithms, particularly in “situated, social, and tacit practices” (Lebovitz et al. 2021, p. 1502). As such, incorporating private information from human decision makers is considered an important step toward enhancing the effectiveness of ADS (Ibrahim et al. 2021).

Given that both ADS and human managers play an important role in determining inventory orders, it is critical for companies to enhance the connection between human and algorithm and to use ADS to facilitate rather than to replace human managers’ decision making. Indeed, the effect of ADS can only be maximized when the decisions rendered by an algorithm and a human manager are effectively combined (Wilson and Daugherty 2018, AI100 2021). In this study, we are particularly interested in the impact of providing managers with algorithm performance information (i.e., performance transparence) in the context of inventory-ordering decisions. Although previous literature finds that algorithm performance information has significant impact on individual decision making (Akash et al. 2020, Burton et al. 2020, Glikson and Woolley 2020, Rai 2020, You et al. 2022), there is no field study examining whether and how performance information influences inventory ordering decision. Furthermore, we note that there are two ways to operationalize performance transparency: positive and negative performance information. Yet, prior research offers conflicting perspectives on the impact of negative performance information on inventory ordering decisions (Dietvorst et al. 2015, 2018; Castelo et al. 2019; You et al. 2022). From a practical perspective, because inventory managers often need to make decisions for hundreds of products on a daily basis, given human’s limited cognitive capability, it is imperative to understand what information should have higher priority when provided to managers.

Lastly, an emerging stream of literature highlights the importance of examining which tasks should be han dled by artificial intelligence (AI) and which ones should be handled by human, as it can further enhance human-algorithm connection (Brynjolfsson et al. 2018, Fu¨ gener et al. 2022). In the context of inventory ordering decisions, it is thus important to understand what products would benefit more from ADS performance information, which consequently facilitates human managers to optimize the use of such algorithmic infor mation and further improve their inventory ordering decisions. Given the importance of product sales level and sales uncertainty level in inventory ordering deci sions (Solyalı et al. 2016, Kesavan and Kushwaha 2020), we focus on product sales and uncertainty as modera tors. Furthermore, one core issue for the connection between human and algorithm lies in the equity on whether under-performing or top-performing managers benefit more from ADS performance information (Bryn jolfsson and Mitchell 2017, Luo et al. 2021). If top performing managers gain more from ADS performance information, then the gap between the two types of man agers would be bigger and exacerbate the workplace rift due to algorithm adoption. However, if the reverse is true, then ADS performance information serves as an equalizer that mitigates the chasm between high- and low-performing managers with important societal impli cations. In sum, our study aims to tackle the following pain points when companies optimize the combination of human expertise and algorithms in decision making. (1) Do high-sale products benefit more from ADS perfor mance information compared with low-sale products? (2) Do high-uncertainty products benefit more from ADS performance information compared with lowuncertainty products? (3) Do high-performing managers benefit more from ADS performance information com pared with under-performing ones? Addressing these questions can help companies enhance human-algorithm interactions and improve inventory ordering decisions.

In collaboration with a leading beverage corporation in China, we conducted two field experiments to address these research questions. The corporation in question sells more than 100 products with more than \$300 million in annual sales revenue. There are 49 sales units across China, and each is responsible for delivering products to hundreds of retailers in corresponding geographical areas, such as restaurants, supermarkets, and so on. We first conducted a pilot field experiment to evaluate whether implementing ADS improves managers’ inventory ordering decisions. Specifically, this company gradually rolled out an ADS across sales units within a 19-day window in 2021. The ADS leveraged advanced algorithms and a large volume of historical data to generate inventory ordering recommendations to assist managers’ final ordering decisions for every product on a daily basis.<sup>1</sup> Given that the sequence in which the sales units implemented ADS was randomly determined, this allowed us to use a difference-indifference (DiD) model to compare sales units with ADS recommendations with those that did not have ADS. This pilot study finds that the implementation of ADS generally improved the inventory ordering decisions (e.g., inventory levels).

Then, we designed the main field experiment to examine whether we could improve managers’ inventory ordering decisions by providing algorithm performance transparency information (i.e., whether implementing ADS reduced inventory cost and for which products). In this field experiment, we randomly selected sales units and provided them with information on ADS performance. We additionally distinguished the impact of providing positive versus negative performance information. We then randomly selected the products for which the algorithm performance information was provided to managers. This allows us to examine the impact of providing ADS performance information on the targeted segment (i.e. products of which ADS performance is provided to managers), as well as spillover segment (i.e., other products for which ADS performance is not provided to managers). Results suggest that the provision of both positive and negative performance information improved the inventory ordering performance for all products including the targeted and spillover segments. Our field data finding contrasts with the previous literature in which individuals are aversive toward algorithms when they are shown to make mistakes based on laboratory data (Dietvorst et al. 2015). Finally, we explore the mechanisms of our findings and provide empirical evidence suggesting that managers’ increasing deliberation (measured as the amount of time inventory managers spent with ADS each day) and compliance with ADS recommendations are important sequential mediators that help explain the effect of providing ADS performance information.

Our study makes the following contributions to the literature. This work is among the first to examine the role of ADS in inventory ordering decisions and to provide empirical evidence confirming the value of ADS in improving managers’ inventory ordering decisions. Second, we illustrate the importance of providing algorithm performance information to enhance the connection between humans and algorithms in the operations management context. Our novel findings suggest that negative performance information can also be beneficial and even more effective than positive performance information in the context of using ADS information to facilitate the tasks of human managers. Third, we demonstrate compliance and deliberation as two factors that mediate the impact of ADS performance information on inventory ordering decisions. Fourth, our study explores the heterogeneous effect across different products and finds that disclosing ADS performance information yields varying impacts depending on product sales volume and uncertainty level. These findings provide theoretical implications for whether and how inventory ordering decisions for products with different characteristics can benefit from ADS performance information.

Our research also offers managerial implications for using ADS within a business. Although ADS can indeed improve managers’ inventory ordering decisions, our research highlights the importance of the human-algorithm connection: in our context, where managers play a critical role in inventory decisions, instead of using ADS to replace human decision makers, companies should focus on leveraging the advantages of both ADS and human managers. As such, ADS should be designed to facilitate managers’ roles. More importantly, given the cognitive limitations of humans, it is important to prioritize the provided information to better facilitate their inventory ordering decisions. Our research shows that providing negative performance information to managers can be more beneficial compared with simply demonstrating the performance advantages of ADS. Meanwhile, companies should approach the use of ADS cautiously depending on the product characteristics, such as historical sales volume and sales uncertainty. Specifically, we showcase how to address company’s pain points by identifying low sales level and high uncertainty products to be the ones which would need the most help from algorithmic recommendations. Underperforming managers also benefit more from ADS, thus suggesting that ADS serves as an equalizer that mitigates the gap between high- and lowperforming managers. Overall, our findings demonstrate the importance of algorithm performance transparency in the human’s adoption and effective usage of ADS within corporations.

## 2. Literature and Hypotheses 2.1. Literature Review

With recent developments in deep-learning algorithms, there has been an emerging stream of research that examines the impact of algorithms on business performance. Because algorithms allow business to leverage large-scale data, the implementation of an algorithm often leads to a better business outcome. Phillips et al. (2015) show that algorithms can improve profits compared with salespeople in the context of auto lending. Feng and Gao (2020) use a laboratory experiment and suggest that a decision support system can alleviate newsvendors’ pull-to-center bias. Luo et al. (2021) find that an AI coach outperforms human coaches for sales agent training. Cui et al. (2022) demon strate that an AI chatbot is more beneficial when smartness is included as part of the chatbot. Sun et al. (2022) find that human operators deviate significantly from algorithm recommendations and propose an algorithm that incorporates human decisions for consideration. Perhaps most relevant to our research, Kesavan and Kushwaha (2020) find that ADS generally outperforms managers in terms of product removal decisions, with the exception of products in a growth stage of their lifecycle. Yet, their research mainly focuses on the use of algorithms to replace humans, whereas our work examines the use of algorithms to facilitate human decision making and how best to disclose ADS performance information to managers.

Our research also relates to the literature that empirically examines inventory ordering decisions. For example, Olivares and Cachon (2009) examine the impact of competition on the inventory level of General Motor’s dealerships and find that dealers increase their inventory level when facing additional competition. Cachon and Olivares (2010) find that both the number of dealerships and the manufacturer’s production flexibility have a significant impact on inventory holding. Rajagopalan (2013) finds that product variety and the number of stores are associated with a higher inventory level. In this study, we follow these empirical studies and use inventory level to approximate the performance of managers’ inventory ordering decisions. Our research is also related to the extant literature on human decision making in inventory management. The seminal paper by Schweitzer and Cachon (2000) uses an experiment to show the pull-tocenter effect and suggests two potential mechanisms for this finding: preference to reduce ex post inventory error and anchoring and insufficient adjustment bias. More recently, researchers have started to improve the design of systems to overcome behavior bias in the decisionmaking process. Feiler and Tong (2021) provide insights into how to design a system to address the overconfidence in estimating product demand. Please refer to Davis and Tong (2021) for a systematic review of behavior operations management.

Our research is particularly related to work on the impact of algorithm performance transparency on the individual use of algorithms. Previous research also focuses on how individuals react to algorithms and human predictions differently, and the results are mixed. On one side, some research demonstrates algorithm aversion in which individuals are less likely to use algorithms compared with human advice when algorithms fail to make correct predictions (Dietvorst et al. 2015). Dietvorst et al. (2018) further show that this algorithm aversion can be mitigated if individuals are allowed to make changes to the algorithm. Castelo et al. (2019) also show that individuals rely less on algorithms for subjective tasks compared with objective tasks. Please refer to Burton et al. (2020) for a systematic review of algorithm aversion. Yet, on the other side, some research has demonstrated algorithm appreciation (Logg et al. 2019). Castelo et al. (2019) suggest that providing performance information can increase the use of algorithms by individuals. Similarly, Bigman and Gray (2018) show that the provision of positive algorithm performance information facilitates the individual use of algorithms when making moral decisions. You et al. (2022) show that algorithm appreciation decreases when performance information is presented in an elaborated format.

However, despite the abovementioned literature on the impact of algorithms on individual decision making in other fields such as medical and customer representatives, the literature on the impact of ADS, particularly the impact of ADS performance information on inventory ordering decisions, remains scarce. We note that the context of inventory ordering decisions provides unique challenges and opportunities compared with other fields in the following ways. (1) Managers making inventory ordering decisions have certain private information that cannot be captured by a system, such as competitors promotion information. This contrasts with previous research, such as prior work on customer service representatives, whereby decision makers generally do not have private information compared with algorithms. (2) The inventory ordering decision is dynamic in the sense that managers not only need to optimize the inventory level in the current period but also need to consider the potential sales level in the next periods. This differs from more traditional contexts, such as medical diagnoses, in which decision makers make a one-time decision. (3) Given the large number of products and managers’ limited cognitive capability, managers often need to prioritize over a range of products with drastically different characteristics. As we demonstrated in our research, managers are likely to pay more attention to products with a higher sales level. However, in other contexts, such as healthcare (Jussupow et al. 2021, Liang and Xue 2022), researchers generally do not consider the scenario in which doctors will treat each medical case differently.

## 2.2. Hypotheses Development

2.2.1. Main Effects of ADS Performance Transparency to Human Managers. We argue that providing ADS performance information (versus not doing so) to human managers improves their inventory ordering performance (e.g., inventory ordering levels). ADS performance information assists decision makers in more accurately calibrating their inventory ordering decisions when utilizing ADS recommendations for the targeted segment (i.e., the products of which ADS performance is provided to managers) (Akash et al. 2020, Burton et al. 2020). Meanwhile, when ADS performance information is provided, managers will perceive the ADS to be more transparent and trustworthy and thus more likely to adopt ADS for inventory ordering of both target and spillover segments (Xu et al. 2014, Dietvorst et al. 2015, Yin et al. 2019, Glikson and Woolley 2020, You et al. 2022). Because we empirically support that on average the implementation of ADS significantly improved inventory ordering performance in a pilot field experiment, we hypothesize that providing ADS performance transparency information (versus not doing so) to human managers improves their inventory ordering performance of both target and spillover segments. Thus, we test the ADS performance transparency hypothesis in our main field experiment.

Hypothesis 1. Providing ADS performance transparency information (versus not doing so) to human managers improves their inventory ordering performance (e.g., inventory ordering levels) for both targeted and spillover segments.

Next, we further distinguish positive and negative performance information due to their nuanced difference. We argue that providing positive information about an algorithm’s performance can directly improve individual trust toward AI. This is intuitive, as individuals can now alleviate their uncertainty about algorithmic predictions, improve their perception of AI-driven outcomes and trust more the general efficacy of ADS both for products in both targeted and spillover segments (Xu et al. 2014, Dietvorst et al. 2015, Yin et al. 2019, You et al. 2022). In particular, for products in targeted segments, the provided performance information can further directly help managers re-evaluate their inventory ordering decisions and increase their compliance toward the effective ADS recommendations. Thus, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1a. Providing positive ADS performance transparency information (versus not doing so) to human managers improves their inventory ordering performance (e.g., inventory ordering levels) for both targeted and spillover segments.

Yet, the impact of providing negative performance information is much less clear. On one side, there is research suggests that providing negative performance information can lead to lower trust and lower compliance (Dietvorst et al. 2015, Castelo et al. 2019, Longon et al. 2019). In this case, providing negative performance information could attenuate the benefit of ADS on inventory ordering decisions: for example, if ADS improves decision making, following this stream of literature, providing negative performance information would reduce compliance and thus reduce the benefits of ADS on decision making.

On the other side, providing negative performance information may also help improve inventory ordering decisions. First, prospect theory finds that individuals pay significant attention when information emphasizes the loss versus the gain (Barberis 2013). Indeed, negative performance information provides the rate of progress relative to their goal and help individuals assess their decision performance (Locke and Latham 1990, Carver and Scheier 1998) and increase individual responsiveness (Koo and Fishbach 2008) for products in targeted and spillover segments. Resulting higher level of deliberation allows managers to better incorporate ADS recommendations into their own decision making and consequently improve their inventory ordering decision. Second, negative performance information could improve inventory ordering decision for products in targeted segment because of information effect, where managers become aware that they should not follow ADS recommendations for these products. This information effect may allow managers to further improve inventory ordering decisions for products in targeted segment. By contrast, for the spillover segment, when providing negative performance information, compared with not providing any performance information, it opens the black box of how ADS performs for inventory ordering decisions. Now that decision makers are aware of the specific scenarios where an algorithm may underperform, this realistic understanding might enhance overall trust, as managers appreciate the clear boundaries and capabilities of the system. This is consistent with previous literature showing that algorithm transparency leads to higher trust toward algorithms (Xu et al. 2014, Yin et al. 2019, Glikson and Woolley 2020, You et al. 2022). In addition, when an algorithm that is generally effective (as supported in our pilot field experiment results) acknowledges its occasional mistakes, humans are more likely to trust and engage with it. This is because humans will “project a feeling of companionship” toward it (Traeger et al. 2020, p. 6370) and fosters a sense of solidarity (Martelaro et al. 2016, Sebo et al. 2018). Such increased engagement within group can promote mutual learning and collaboration among group members (Jones and George 1998), even among human and algorithms (Martelaro et al. 2016). As a result, this increases managers’ trust of ADS of its generally high effectiveness and their compliance of ADS recommendations for the spillover segment and hence enhances inventory ordering performance. On balance, we believe that the advantages of providing negative per formance should outweigh the drawbacks.

Hypothesis 1b. Providing negative ADS performance transparency information (versus not doing so) to human managers improves their inventory ordering performanc (e.g., inventory ordering levels) for both targeted and spill over segments.

2.2.2. Compliance and Deliberation as Mediators of the Effects of ADS Performance Transparency to Human Managers. Furthermore, we note that there are two potential mechanisms through which ADS performance information influences inventory ordering decisions: compliance and deliberation (i.e., cognitive effort in accessing and assimilating ADS recommendations when making inventory ordering decisions). We first discuss compliance as a mechanism. The previous literature suggests that providing positive performance information helps to enhance the transparency of the algorithm implemented by the ADS and, consequently, leads to increasing trust of and more compliance with the algorithm’s recommendations for both targeted and spillover segments (Philips et al. 2015, Feng and Gao 2020, Glikson and Woolley 2020, You et al. 2022). As a result, as a potential mediator, higher compliance helps explain the effects of providing positive performance information on inventory ordering decisions for both targeted and spillover segments. On the other hand, in the case of providing negative performance information, compliance is reduced for the targeted segment due to information effect: When managers were informed that ADS increased inventory level for these products, they will do just the “opposite”—deviate from ADS recommendations by reducing compliance to ADS, and this reduction of compliance with the harmful ADS recommendations enhances inventory ordering performance for the targeted segment. For the spillover segment, when providing negative performance information, compared with not providing any performance information, it enhances ADS performance transparency and managers’ trust in ADS’s generally high efficacy for the rest nontargeted products as we argued previously. Thus, this increases managers’ trust and compliance toward ADS recommendations for the spillover segment and therefore enhances inventory ordering performance.

Second, deliberation can also be another mechanism through which ADS performance information influences inventory ordering decisions. ADS recommendations contain information that can be incorporated into the individual decision-making process. Providing performance information allows decision makers to better calibrate their inventory ordering decisions when using ADS recommendations (Akash et al. 2020, Burton et al. 2020). However, decision makers often incur significant cognitive cost when they integrate performance information and system advice into their own decisionmaking process (Jarvenpaa 1989, Heart et al. 2011, Xu et al. 2014, You et al. 2022). Insufficient deliberation and failure to thoroughly analyze and incorporate information from ADS can weaken the final decision-making performance, whereas a deliberate and in-depth reasoning can significantly improve decision making (Jussupow et al. 2021). To summarize, providing performance information improves the transparency of the algorithm implemented by the ADS and may encourage managers to invest more time in accessing and understanding ADS recommendations. Such deliberation in turn allows managers to better shape their inventory ordering decisions.

In addition, between the two mediators discussed above (deliberation and compliance), previous research suggests that deliberation could influence compliance to algorithms. However, the results are mixed. On one side, Cao et al. (2023) find that when provided with longer time to make decision, individuals are more likely to follow algorithm recommendations. Similarly, there is also research showing that when clinicians are under time pressure, they may struggle to trust the recommendations from a machine learning decision support tool when they make treatment decision for patients (Jacobs et al. 2021). On the other side, Rice and Keller (2009) find where humans are more likely to comply with automation tools when making decision under time pressure. Tunstall et al. (2014) further show that this is likely to be valid primarily with difficult task and reliable technology. We further note that these studies were all conducted in laboratory experimental setting, and it is not clear how deliberation will influence compliance in our field experiment setting in which managers make inventory ordering decisions. Thus, it is also important to empirically examine the sequential relationship between deliberation and compliance in our field experimental setting.

Hypothesis 2. Deliberation and compliance sequentially mediate the impact of ADS performance transparency information on inventory ordering performance (e.g., inventory ordering levels).

2.2.3. Moderated Effects of ADS Performance Transparency to Human Managers. The impact of providing performance information can also vary across products with different characteristics, that is, sales level and uncertainty, in this study. On one side, as argued above, when ADS performance information is provided, managers will perceive the ADS to be more transparent and trustworthy. Thus, they are more likely to comply with the ADS recommendations regarding inventory order quantities. One may argue that the business advantages of ADS are expected to be greater for products with higher sales. This is because ADS can effectively summarize large-scale data that is otherwise too compli cated for human managers to examine or comprehend (Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017, AI100 2021). This is also supported by the literature that finds that the benefit of using algorithm recommendations is larger with higher data availability in various contexts, such as operations (Kesavan and Kushwaha 2020) and finance (Costello et al. 2020). In the inventory ordering context, products with higher sales volume generally have a higher penetration level across retailers in the market. This allows a company to collect a larger volume of data for these popular products from retailers.

However, in the meantime, managers have limited cognitive capabilities in processing information (Simon 1978, Donohue et al. 2020), particularly given the large amount of information that they need to process on a daily basis. Given that products with higher sales level have higher impact on a manager’s compensation, their attention is inevitably biased by paying greater attention to products that generate higher sales, as such products bring greater economic utility to them, following economic assumptions (Caplin and Dean 2015, Gabaix 2019). In other words, their time and effort will be biased toward such popular products. In addition, the longer time spent learning the optimal inventory level for popular products helps managers accumulate more experience regarding the optimal inventory ordering decisions for these products with higher sales; this, in turn, improves a manager’s inventory ordering decisions for such products in the future. Yet, products with lower sales will receive minimum attention from managers because there are usually hundreds of Stock Keeping Units for which managers need to make inventory decisions. Consequently, the manager will not spend much time optimizing the inventory decisions for these products. Thus, the benefits of providing ADS performance information to human managers are lesser for popular products with a higher level of historical sales, but greater for less popular ones with a lower level of historical sales. Thus, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. The positive impact of providing (positive/ negative) ADS performance information to human managers on their inventory ordering performance is more significant for products with lower levels of historical sales.

Given the limitations of human cognition (Donohue et al. 2020), it is often observed that people fail to make consistent decisions, even under similar circumstances; these limitations are especially palpable when attempting to render optimal inventory decisions for products with significant fluctuations and uncertainties in sales (Karlinsky-Shichor and Netzer 2024). Kremer et al. (2011, p. 1827) also demonstrate that individual predictions suffer from forecast errors in which they tend to “overreact to forecast errors in relatively stable environments” and vice versa in less stable environments. In addition, the ADS is built to summarize dynamic and complex data derived from the real world (AI100). Thus, an important advantage of ADS is that it can capture complicated time series patterns that are otherwise difficult to apprehend and decipher by human decision makers. As mentioned before, the provision of ADS performance information could change managers’ deliberation and compliance with ADS. For example, the provision of ADS performance information could increase deliberation, and managers will be incentivized to spend more time considering the incorporation of ADS recommendations. As such, the increment of improvement will be more significant for products with a greater uncertainty level. We use uncertainty to denote such fluctuation in product sales, which is calculated as the standard deviation of this discrepancy divided by average sales of a product and propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4. The positive impact of providing (positive/ negative) ADS performance information to human managers on their inventory ordering performance is more significant for products with higher uncertainty of historical sales.

Finally, for managers with higher inventory ordering performance, there might be less room for improvement. Thus, the marginal benefit they gain from ADS are smaller. In contrast, underperformers have more room for improvement, making the performance information more impactful for their inventory ordering decisions. In addition, there is also research showing that high-performing individuals are more likely to be averse to algorithms (Logg et al. 2019, Luo et al. 2021). As a result, we believe providing ADS performance information should serve as an equalizer that benefi more for underperforming managers (Figure 1 sum marizes all hypotheses).

Hypothesis 5. The positive impact of providing (positive/ negative) ADS performance information to human managers is more significant for managers with lower prior performance.

## 3. Research Context and Pilot Field Experiment

As mentioned in the previous section, the role of compli ance critically depends on whether ADS improves or deteriorates inventory ordering decisions in the considered context. If ADS improves inventory ordering decisions, a higher compliance toward ADS recommendations is expected to further improve inventory ordering decisions. On the other hand, if ADS deteriorates inventory ordering decisions, a higher compliance may further decrease the effectiveness of inventory ordering decisions. As such, we conducted a pilot field experiment to evaluate the impact of implementing ADS on managers inventory ordering decisions.

We collaborated with one of the largest beverage corporations in China. This company sells 185 types of beverages, including milk, coffee, and juice, among others.<sup>2</sup> It operates 49 sales units across China where each unit oversees the sales of all types of beverages to hundreds of retailers such as supermarkets and restaurants in corresponding geographical areas. Each sales unit orders products from central production factories on a daily basis. On average, it takes two days between order placement and order delivery. Thus, an accurate inventory prediction system is crucial to optimize the inventory level. One manager is assigned to each sales unit, and they are responsible for ordering products in their respective unit. One of the challenges that the company faces is that these managers have been relying on their past human experience to make inventory ordering decisions. To improve inventory ordering decisions, this corporation replaced their original system with a new ADS system that recommended the optimal ordering amount for each product for every sales unit on a daily basis during the summer of 2021. Note that, instead of replacing the human decision maker, ADS provided recommendations that facilitated the managers’ decision making in our context.

More specifically, the implemented ADS system employs multiple advanced prediction algorithms, including ARIMA, XGBoost, and neural network models, to first predict the sales level of each type of product for each sales unit using historical data, such as historical sales data, seasonality effect, promotion information, and so on. This system then chooses the model with the best performance to calculate the predicted sales level for each type of product for each sales unit. This sales level is then used to provide the recommended inventory ordering amount to managers based on the current inventory level. After receiving the ADS recommended order amount, managers will make their final decision on the actual order amount. Two of the main drivers of managers’ compensation are product sales level and overstock level—products with a consistently high stock level in the warehouse. Because stockout will significantly reduce their product sales level and consequently reduce their compensation, managers need to make the inventory ordering decision to reduce the inventory level without triggering stock-out. This is consistent with the extremely low stock-out rate in our data set, as documented in Endnote 4. The implementation of ADS itself did not change managers’ compensation structure. It is noted that the delivery cost is generally not influenced by a small change in the amount ordered. Thus, we do not consider the impact on delivery cost in this study.

We attached a schema in Figure 2 that describes the information displayed by the ADS. Note that this schema applies to all products in our context. The second to last row presents the recommendations produced by the ADS. Then, managers can enter the actual order amount in the last row. We note that other infor mation listed in the schema, such as previous inventory and delivery historical data, was consistently available well before the implementation of the ADS. Thus, the only new additional information for managers is the inventory ordering recommendations from the ADS. Note that this ADS does not provide any algorithm performance information regarding product inventory.

We present the design and result of the pilot field experiment in Online Appendix I. Results show that implementing ADS generally improves inventory ordering decisions. Further analyses provide rich insights on human-algorithm interactions. First, although ADS gen erally improves inventory ordering decisions, there is still room to improve human-algorithm interaction in our context. Second, although the majority of products benefited from ADS, the inventory cost of the remaining products increased after the implementation of the ADS. This suggests that, for some products, managers have certain information that allows them to make better decisions than the ones generated by the ADS in certain scenarios. This is consistent with previous literature showing that humans possess an advantage in operations decision making, namely their tacit knowledge and private information that cannot be easily incorporated into algorithms (van Donselaar et al. 2010, Ibrahim et al. 2021, Sun et al. 2022). Both findings incentivize us to conduct the main field experiment to address the pain points mentioned in Introduction.

## 4. Main Field Experiment

## 4.1. Experiment Design

To address our research questions, we conducted a field experiment to examine how providing ADS performance information influences inventory ordering decisions. We distinguish between providing positive ADS performance information versus providing negative ADS performance information. First, we randomly assigned all sales units to three groups. Based on the findings of the pilot field experiment, among the 30 products that experienced the most significant inventory cost increase following the implementation of the ADS, we randomly selected 15 products. Subsequently, we communicated to the units within the first group (16 sales units) that the ADS recommendation adversely impacted the inventory decisions for these 15 products. Specifically, the information we provide to these managers can be translated as “While ADS generally helps improve inventory ordering decisions, for some products, ADS helps reduce inventory cost, whereas, for other products, implementation of ADS increases inventory cost. Below we list 15 of the products for which ADS hurts inventory decisions for most of the sales units.” In other words, our information is framed to focus on the negative ADS performance information for this group of sales units. We denote this treatment as T1. For sales units in the second group (16 sales units), among the 30 products whose inventory cost decreased the most after the implementation of the ADS, we randomly chose 15 and informed the units in this group that the ADS recommendation improved the inventory decision of these products. Specifically, the information we provide to these managers can be translated as “While ADS generally helps improve inventory ordering decisions, for some products, ADS helps reduce inventory cost, whereas, for other products, implementation of ADS increases inventory cost. Below we list 15 of the products for which ADS improves the inventory decision for most of the sales units.” Thus, this group received information that was framed to focus on the positive performance information of the ADS. We denote this treatment as T2. The remaining 17 sales units consis of our control group; they received no additional infor mation than what they already had prior to the treat ment. As such, we can evaluate how providing negative/positive information regarding the ADS performance influences inventory decisions by comparing the control group with T1, as well as by comparing the control group with T2, respectively. Note that for sales units in T1, there are 170 products for which the ADS performance information is not available. Similarly, for sales units in T2, there are also 170 products for which the cor responding ADS performance information is not avail able. Such randomization across both sales units and products allows us to separately identify the information effect in which managers adjust their inventory decisions corresponding to the algorithms’ performance information. For example, a manager knows that ADS hurts the inventory ordering decision of product A. Thus, this manager simply further reduces the ordering amount of product A in response to this performance information. We will discuss in more detail how we leverage the randomization of products and divide them into a targeted segment and a spillover segment to identify this information effect in next section. We started to provide ADS performance information on November 22, 2021. We collected data from two weeks before the treatment started as the pretreatment periods (November 8, 2021) and then two weeks after the treatment as the posttreatment periods (December 5, 2021). We also note that this ADS did no provide any feature containing algorithm performance information, nor did the original system used before the ADS implementation. Thus, the performance information we provided in the main field experiment is the first time that managers had access to such information.<sup>3</sup>

Before presenting the estimation model, we first demonstrate that the two treatment groups are truly randomly selected. We validate the balances of key variable statistics, as well as the managers’ demographic information for the week prior to the treatment using a t-test, and present the p-values in Table 1. The insignificant values show that the covariates in the data set are balanced across the control and two treatment groups. We also present the statistics of these variables in Table 2.

## 4.2. Main Model

We use just-in-time inventory level to measure the inventory ordering performance in our empirical model, denoted as $I n v O _ { i j t }$ in this paper.<sup>4</sup> We construct the just-intime inventory level by first calculating the absolute value of the difference between the available volume of product j and this product’s demand and take the log of this value as it is skewed. If the available volume is larger than the product demand, we denote it as an “in-stock” scenario; otherwise, if the demand is larger than available volume, we denote it as a “stockout” scenario. As mentioned in Endnote 4, we mainly focus on the “in-stock” scenario in this study given that stockout is rare and is not the focus of the company. Note that each sales unit corresponds exclusively to one manager; thus, the subscript i represents both the sales unit and the corresponding manager. In this field experiment, we employ an empirical model, as per below, to test our hypotheses:

$$
\begin{array}{r} I n v O _ {i j t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D I D _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} D I D _ {i t} * M _ {i j} + \beta_ {3} G _ {i} * M _ {i j} \\ + \beta_ {4} T r e a t _ {t} * M _ {i j} + \beta_ {5} M _ {i j} + U n i t _ {i} + P r o d _ {j} + T _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The focal variable is $D I D _ { i t } ,$ where $D I D _ { i t } = 1$ if sales unit i received treatment (T1 or T2) in period t. We estimate the model for the group that receives treatment T1 and T2 separately. The baseline group of the two regressions is the same control group in which the sales unit did not receive any treatment. To address Hypotheses 3 and 4 on the heterogeneous treatment effect, we include a variable that captures product characteristics, $M _ { i j } ,$ where $M _ { i j }$ can be the product sales level or the product sales uncertainty level. The focal variable addressing Hypotheses 3 and 4 is the interaction term between $M _ { i j }$ and the DiD term. If the value of $\beta _ { 2 }$ is positive, a higher sales level/uncertainty level will reduce the benefit of provid ing ADS performance information. Because there are three-way interaction terms in this equation, we also need to include the corresponding two-way interaction terms. Specifically, the DiD terms can be written as $D I D _ { i t } = \bar { G } _ { i } * T r e a t _ { t } .$ , where $G _ { i } = 1$ for sales unit i receiving treatment and zero otherwise. $T r e a t _ { t } = 1$ if period t belongs to treatment periods. Thus, $G _ { i } * M _ { i j } ,$ , and Treat ∗ $M _ { i j }$ are the other two-way interaction terms. We also include a sales unit fixed effect (Unit ), a product fixed effect $( P r o d _ { j } ) _ { A }$ , and a time fixed effect $\left( T _ { t } \right)$ in the model above. Note that when testing Hypothesis 5 on the moderating effect of managers’ prior performance, the DiD model above should be changed accordingly, as managers’ prior performance remains constant across products. We use $S a l e _ { i j } ,$ Uncertainty , and UnderPerfM to represent sales level, uncertainty level, and managers’ prior underperformance, respectively. We estimate Equation (1) using an Ordinary Least Squares with robust standard errors clustered on the sales units.

Note that, in our field experiment, we randomly selected 15 products and provided their ADS performance information for sales units in treatment group T1 and a group of different 15 products was randomly selected for sales units in treatment group T2. We denote these 30 products as the Targeted Segment. For the remaining 155 products of which the ADS performance information was not provided to any managers, they only received the spillover influence when managers were provided with the performance information for other products. Thus, this segment of products is denoted as the Spillover Segment. We then estimate Equation (2) for these two segments of products. ## 4.3. Mediating Analyses Model

In previous sections, we argue that there are two mechanisms through which performance information influences inventory ordering decisions: compliance and deliberation. To provide empirical support for these mechanisms, we conduct mediating analyses in this section. Our mediating analysis follows the steps of information systems (IS) literature (Venkatesh et al. 2016, Bai et al. 2020) and the serial multiple mediator model in Hayes (2015) using a two-step process. In the first step, we consider the two mediators, compliance and deliberation, as dependent variables (DVs) and treatment as covariates. Note that our theoretical discussion suggests that deliberation could influence compliance, thus we include deliberation as a covariate in Equation (2a). In the second step, we estimate the effect of our treatment and mediators on inventory ordering decisions. We also separately estimate the mediating effect for the treatment group with positive and negative performance information. The estimation model is specified as below.

Step 1:

(2a)

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Compliance} _ {i j t} = \beta_ {2 0} + \beta_ {2 1} D I D _ {i t} + \beta_ {2 2} \text {Deliberation} _ {i t} \\ + U n i t _ {i} + P r o d _ {j} + T _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t} \end{array}\tag{2b}
$$

Step 2:

$$
\begin{array}{c} I n v O _ {i j t} = \beta_ {3 0} + \beta_ {3 1} D I D _ {i t} + \beta_ {3 2} D e l i b e r a t i o n _ {i t} \\ + \beta_ {3 3} C o m p l i a n c e _ {i j t} + U n i t _ {i} + P r o d _ {j} + T _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t} \end{array}\tag{2c}
$$

Here $D I D _ { i t } = 1$ if sales unit i received treatment (it can be either positive or negative performance information) on day $t . C o m p l i a n c e _ { i j t }$ is calculated by first taking the log of inventory manager i’s deviation from ADS recommendations on product j on day t and then multiplying by $- 1 . ^ { 5 }$ We use Deliberation<sub>it</sub> to capture deliberation as a mediator, which is calculated as the amount of time inventory manager i spent with ADS on day t. ## 5.2. Mediating Analysis Results

In this section, we conduct a mediating analysis to empirically examine the mechanisms through which performance information influences managers’ inventory ordering decisions. Similar to our main analysis, we also separately estimate the effect of providing positive performance information from the effect of providing negative performance information in this mediating analysis and present the results for targeted and spillover segments.

In the first step, our results largely support the mediating role of deliberation. That is, providing negative performance information has a positive and significant impact on deliberation.<sup>6</sup> Interestingly, the impact of providing positive performance information on deliberation is insignificant (Table 5, column 1). Based on our theoretical discussion, this is because managers in our context pay more attention to negative ADS performance information compared with positive performance information, which incentivizes them to spend more time with ADS to consider how to incorporate ADS recommendations in their inventory ordering decisions. This may also explain why the benefit of pro viding negative performance information is higher compared with that of providing positive performance information as described in the previous section. The impact of deliberation on the inventory level is also negative and significant for both negative and positive performance information (fifth column). Thus, after controlling for a direct effect, when managers spend more time with the ADS, the inventory level becomes lower. These results suggest that providing ADS performance information, particularly negative performance information, increases inventory ordering decisions because it incentivizes managers to better assimilate the ADS recommendations.

Second, our results largely support the mediating role of compliance (or a lack of compliance). Specifically, for spillover segment, providing ADS performance information, both positive and negative, increases the compliance level (Table 5, column 2). In particular, providing positive information about an algorithm’s performance can alleviate managers’ uncertainty about algorithmic predictions and improve their perception of AI-driven outcomes and hence their compliance to ADS recommendations (Xu et al. 2014, Dietvorst et al. 2015, Yin et al. 2019, You et al. 2022), in line with Hypothesis 1a. Further, when provid ing negative performance information, compared with not providing any performance information, it increases managers’ trust of ADS and their compliance toward ADS recommendations, in line with Hypothesis 1b. Also, when managers are more compliant toward algorithm recommendations, it generally leads to improved inven tory ordering performance in the spillover segment.

For the targeted segment, when providing positive performance information, the coefficient of DiD term is positive and significant (fourth column), thus increasing compliance. Also, the more compliance with the positive ADS performance information, the better the inven tory ordering performance of the targeted segment (column 5). By contrast, when providing negative performance information, the coefficient before DiD term is negative and significant (fourth column), thus decreasing compliance. Further, the more compliance for the negative ADS performance information, the worse the inventory ordering performance of the targeted segment (column 5; compliance has a positive and significant coefficient of 0.145).

One may ask why providing a ADS recommendation with poor performance (negative information) leads to higher compliance and higher performance in regard to the inventory level for the spillover segment. This is because for the spillover segment, when providing negative performance information compared with not providing any performance information, it opens the black box of how ADS performs for inventory ordering decisions. In addition, when an algorithm that is generally effective (as supported in our pilot field experiment results) acknowledges its occasional mistakes, humans are more likely to engage with it due to improved AI-human team solidarity and companionship (Martelaro et al. 2016, Sebo et al. 2018, Traeger et al. 2020). As a result, this increases managers’ trust in ADS and their compliance toward ADS recommendations for the spillover segment and hence enhances inventory ordering performance. By contrast, for the targeted segment, when providing negative performance information, compliance is reduced due to information effect: When managers were informed that ADS increased inventory level for these products, they will do just the “opposite”— deviate from ADS recommendations by reducing inventory ordering amount. This reduction of complying with the harmful ADS recommendations enhances inventory ordering performance for the targeted segment.

Furthermore, the results in the second and fourth columns also indicate that greater deliberation correlates with higher levels of compliance. This supports the stream of literature that individuals who invest more time with ADS are more inclined to adhere to its recommendations (Jacobs et al. 2021, Cao et al. 2023). Yet, we want to caution that our results do not imply that companies should blindly increase compliance with ADS recommendations because human decision maker often have their own advantages in certain scenarios. Instead, we examine compliance as a mechanism through which performance information impacts inventory ordering performance.

## 6. Conclusion

In recent years, we have witnessed a growing number of companies that are leveraging ADS to enhance their decision-making process. However, across many contexts, humans hold a significant level of private information, and this has required companies to carefully introduce ADS recommendations to facilitate human managers’ decision-making processes rather than replacing them altogether in the decision loop. This phenomenon has required a better understanding of how to bolster the human-algorithm interaction to simultaneously harness the advantages that are specific to humans and algorithms. To address this question, we conduct field experiments to examine the impact of ADS on improving managers’ inventory ordering decisions and further demonstrate the effect of providing ADS performance information on managers’ decision making. Our results demonstrate that providing ADS performance transparency information generally improves inventory ordering decisions. We further distinguish between targeted and spillover segment, as well as providing positive versus negative performance information, This 2 × 2 framework provides the following findings: (1) benefit of providing negative performance information is higher compared with that of providing positive performance information, irrespective of targeted or spillover segment; (2) when provided with negative performance information, for products in targeted segment, the compliance level decreases, whereas the compliance level increases across other three scenarios; and (3) providing negative performance information increases deliberation while providing positive performance information has no significant impact on deliberation.

Our research makes the following contributions. This work is among the first to empirically examine the effect of providing positive/negative ADS performance information on inventory ordering decisions, a novel context that is understudied in previous literature. Although the use of algorithm in inventory ordering decision has attracted academic attention lately (Kesavan and Kushwaha 2020), the focus has been primarily on the use of algorithm to replace human inventory ordering decision, rather than using algorithms to facilitate human decision. In addition, on the impact of providing ADS performance information, previous literature paints a murky picture on the impact of negative performance information (Dietvorst et al. 2015, Castelo et al. 2019, You et al. 2022). Thus, our research contributes to the literature by showing that negative performance information improves inventor ordering decisions. Second, we are the first paper to demonstrate empirically that compliance and deliberation are two important mechanisms through which performance information influences inventory ordering decisions. We want to note that the research on deliberation as a mediator is particularly limited. We further show that, although nega tive performance information increases deliberation, the impact of positive performance information is statistically insignificant. Third, our research demonstrates that the impact of providing ADS performance information is more salient for products with lower sales levels and a higher degree of uncertainty. ADS also benefit underperforming managers more, serving as an equalizer among managers. This analysis on heterogenous treatment of providing performance information fills research gap in previous literature on inventory ordering decisions and provides important implications on how individuals use ADS performance information for inventory ordering decisions for products with different characteristics.

Our research also provides managerial implications for companies that are considering implementing or have already adopted ADS for inventory ordering deci sions. First, our results demonstrate the importance of enhancing the human-algorithm connection. Second, based on results from the 2 × 2 framework mentioned earlier, we propose following managerial implications: (1) the benefit of providing negative performance information is larger compared with that of providing positive performance information. Given managers’ limited cog nitive capability, companies should prioritize the provi sion of negative performance information over merely demonstrating the advantages of ADS, (2) in inventory ordering context, companies should not overly worry about providing negative ADS performance information, as managers can effectively leverage ADS performance information to improve inventory ordering decision for products in targeted segment, without jeopardize thei trust for ADS for spillover segments; and (3) our research identifies deliberation as an important mediator through which ADS performance information influences inventory ordering decisions. Thus, it is important for companies to provide incentives for managers to spend more time assimilating information provided by ADS, particularly in the scenario in which positive performance information is provided. Lastly, our results also address companies’ pain points on which scenarios ADS is most beneficial in augmenting human inventory decisions (i.e., lower sales/higher uncertainty products, and underperforming managers). Companies should carefully consider their product characteristics, such as sales and uncertainty levels, as well as manager performance when they decide how to incorporate the ADS recommendations into managers’ inventory ordering decisions. Thus, this research provides guidance on how companies can optimize the design of performance information.

Our research has several limitations, which open up future research opportunities. First, our research context involves inventory ordering decisions by managers with extensive knowledge and experience in making such decisions. If we were to further verify the hypotheses in this study, we would need to recruit subjects with extensive knowledge, which poses a major challenge. Thus, we leave it for future researchers to use laboratory experiments to explore any additional mechanisms underlying experts’ responses to performance information that are difficult to measure in a field experiment. Second, it would also be interesting to explore to what extent our results still hold in other context where decision makers have different knowledge level. Third, although we explore the different roles of positive and negative deviations in Online Appendix V, we believe distinguishing different types of deviations is an understudied direction, and we leave more detailed examination of this topic for future research. Fourth, although we model deliberation to influence compliance in mediating analysis, the underlying relationship between deliberation and compliance may require additional field experiment for causal claims. We also want to note that we could not identify deliberation at product level. We thus leave additional analysis on deliberation for future research. Lastly, although our research focus is on the impact of ADS performance information on inventory ordering decisions, previous literature demonstrate that the design of the information (such as the information length) has significantly impact on individual decision making (You et al. 2022). We believe it is critical for companies to optimize the feedback information content, and we leave it for future research.
