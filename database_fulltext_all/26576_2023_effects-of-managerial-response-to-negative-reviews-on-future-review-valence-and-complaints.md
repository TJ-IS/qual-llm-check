# Effects of Managerial Response to Negative Reviews on Future Review Valence and Complaints

Abstract. There is limited systematic research on managerial response strategies to online customer complaints and negative reviews. In this paper, we synthesize justice theory and service recovery literature to develop a model that explores the mechanisms through which appropriate managerial responses to customer complaints in<sup>fl</sup>uence aggregate future review valence and complaints. We test our model using data from TripAdvisor com, a leading travel review platform. Using text analysis (e.g., natural language processing and deep learning), we extract and code the variables in our model from the reviews and the managerial responses to these reviews. Key <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that responding to customer reviews—in particular, negative reviews—will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on future review valence. Moreover, responses with more rational cues than emotional cues to customer complaints about procedural unfairness will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on future review valence. However, responses with more rational cues than emotional cues to customer complaints about interactional unfairness will have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on future review valence. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that when reviews have both distributive and interactional unfairness, responses with more rational cues matter. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that both rational cues and emotional cues in responses to distributive unfairness and rational cues in responses to procedural unfairness are effective to decrease the future occurrence of similar complaints. We interpret and discuss the implication of these <sup>fi</sup>nding for theory and practice.

## Introduction

A growing number of popular review forums such as TripAdvisor not only provide customer reviews but also permit managerial responses to such reviews. Customers’ opinions and managerial responses are visible to all and hence subject to interpretation and reaction. Such online forums have grown in popularity because they often serve as an important <sup>fi</sup>rst step in customers seeking information about products and services. These forums facilitate rapid information exchange, and studies have found that negative electronic word of mouth (i.e., negative reviews) spreads more rapidly and could cause a more immediate damage to a company’s reputation than traditional word of mouth (Mattila and Mount 2003). Because negative online reviews are permanently archived and available to a larger audience (Dellarocas 2003), managers need to understand not only the effect of their responses to the dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers but also their effect on subsequent reviewers who read the management response (Harrison-Walker 2001). Thus, it is necessary for managers to design effective response strategies to mitigate the harmful effects of negative reviews in online review forums.

Whereas past research has extensively examined the of<sup>fl</sup>ine managerial response strategies to customer complaints (Resnik and Harmon 1983, Clark et al. 1992, Karatepe 2006), systematic research on managerial response strategies to online customer complaints and negative reviews is just emerging. Recent studies have explored the effects of managerial responses to online reviews on (1) overall customer attitudes (Sharpe et al. 2014, Sparks et al. 2016), (2) customer satisfaction (Gu and Ye 2014), (3) subsequent online review volume and valence (Proserpio and Zervas 2017, Wang and Chaudhry 2018, Chen et al. 2019), and (4) performance of focal and proximate businesses (Kumar et al. 2018). These studies, which are the <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward developing a deeper understanding of effective managerial response strategies, do not shed much light on what managerial responses should be for speci<sup>fi</sup>c complaints and how effective their responses could be. There is a clear need for developing theoretically anchored frameworks to dig deeper into both the nature of online customer complaints and that of the responses to derive effective strategies to deal with negative reviews. Although research in service recovery has explored this issue to some extent, this stream has used surveys and experiments to derive normative guidelines for dealing with customer complaints. Moreover, online negative reviews differ from traditional customer complaints in that both the reviews and the managerial responses are public and visible. The conversations between managers and prior customers can be observed by the subsequent customers, whereas the private customer-<sup>fi</sup>rm interactions are not observable and hence will not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the subsequent customers’ opinions. Thus, the normative guidelines targeted at private customer-<sup>fi</sup>rm interactions may not be necessarily applicable to online managerial responses to online customer complaints.

Online managerial response strategies could be quite different from of<sup>fl</sup>ine service recovery efforts in that the response is not only targeted at the complaining customer but also to signal subsequent customers in the online forum. A critical aspect of signaling is the appropriateness of the response to the complaint because the lack thereof can be picked up by the majority of the customers, and an inappropriate response could cause several backlashes. We focus on one aspect—namely, the role of rationality and emotionality in crafting appropriate managerial responses to online complaints. Whereas the effects of rationality and emotionality in advertising messages on customer opinions and attitudes (e.g., Heath and Stipp 2011) have been explored extensively, attention to these elements in managerial responses to customer complaints is missing. Arguably, managerial responses are also persuasive messages from the <sup>fi</sup>rm targeted at the customers, and both rationality and emotionality are important in these messages. Given the importance of crafting appropriate responses to customer complaints in online forums, in this paper, we focus on theorizing and testing what could be the appropriate mix of rationality and emotionality in responses to online complaints.

We synthesized justice theory and service recovery literature to develop our research model that explores the pathways through which managerial responses to complaints impact future review valence, which we conceptualize as the aggregate valence re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the reviews subsequent to a managerial response. We test our propositions using data from TripAdvisor.com, a popular travel forum. We used extensive textual analysis (e.g., natural language processing and deep learning) to extract and code the content in the reviews and in the managerial responses, from this online forum, to compile our data. In TripAdvisor, customers have to visit the website in order to post a review. On this landing page, the reviewer sees the most recent review and that review’s accompanying response, if they exist (Wang and Chaudhry 2018). Any person seeking to post a review will have to see at least one past review and the response to this review (Wang and Chaudhry 2018). This provides an empirical setting where information transmission between past reviewers and subsequent reviewers who seek to post a review is enabled. We examine how different types of managerial responses to particular types of negative reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence and the nature of complaints.

## Related Research

## Managerial Responses Influence Future Reviews

Recent studies have empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that managerial responses do have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on sub sequent online reviews. For example, Wang and Chaudhry (2018) found that managerial responses to negative (positive) reviews will positively (negatively) in<sup>fl</sup>uence subsequent reviewers’ opinions, and response tailoring will strengthen this positive or negative impact. Proserpio and Zervas (2017) found that managerial responses increase review ratings by .12 of a star and review volume by 12%, and hotels will receive fewer but longer negative reviews after responding. Chen et al. (2019) found that managerial responses will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the subsequent review volume. Although these studies have examined, in general, the effects of managerial response, they do not explore the effects of appropriate managerial response strategies on the future review valence and nature of complaints.

## Managerial Responses Influence Customer Attitude and Firm Performance

Managerial responses not only will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the future reviews but could also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the custome attitude and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Gu and Ye (2014, p. 570) found that managerial “responses increase future satisfaction of the complaining customers who receive the responses” but “decrease future satisfaction of those complaining customers who observe but not receive management responses.” Studies have found that managerial responses to negative reviews will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer inferences of trust toward the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm (Sparks et al. 2016), that the response rate to negative reviews would have a positive impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Kim et al. 2015), and that it could mitigate the adverse effects of negative reviews on <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation (Rose and Blodgett 2016).

Moreover, managerial responses can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the focal business’s performance, and its spillover effects on the nearby businesses depend on whether the focal business and the nearby businesses are in direct competition (Kumar et al. 2018).

## Devising Appropriate Managerial Response Strategies

Given the importance of managerial responses in terms of its effects on future reviews, customer attitudes, and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, there is an emerging literature examining the effective managerial response strategies. Gu and Ye (2014) suggested that managers should give priority to customers with low satisfaction because managerial responses are greatly effective among such customers but have limited effects on other customers. Sparks and Bradley (2017) developed a “Triple A” (i.e., acknowledge, account, and act) typology of managerial responses by using content analysis and found that “most responses included an acknowledgement of the dissatisfying event, an account (explanation) for its occurrence, and a reference to action taken” (p. 719). Recommendations from other studies include that “managers should include empathy or paraphrasing statements in their responses to online reviews” (Min et al. 2015, p. 223), respond in a timely manner (Sparks et al. 2016), adopt a conversational human voice instead of a professional voice, and use an apology with assurance or an apology with correction action (Rose and Blodgett 2016) in responding to online complaints. Others have suggested that managers should customize their responses to each negative review but do limited customization to positive reviews (Wang and Chaudhry 2018)—that is, “provide detailed responses to negative reviews but brief ones to positive reviews” (Chen et al. 2019, p. 1)—and that detailed responses are more likely to have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the future ratings of repeated customers, whereas the sentiment of the responses may not have such a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Sheng et al. 2019). Moreover, a strategy of responding to every review is likely to be too costly and ineffective (Yang et al. 2019).

## Our Contribution

These studies, a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in understanding effective managerial response strategies, have focused on some general characteristics of responses in terms of speed, sentiment, length, and keywords to be used in the responses. To develop a deeper understanding on the effectiveness of managerial responses, there is a need to focus on the tone in terms of rationality and emotionality of managerial responses for various types of complaints (Chen et al. 2019), because studies have found that a large proportion of customers alter their perception of the <sup>fi</sup>rm or the brand favorably after observing a carefully “crafted response to a negative review” (Liu et al. 2015, Yang et al. 2019, p. 352). In the online review setting, <sup>fi</sup>ndings that responding to the right type of review with the right style could have strong positive signaling effects to subsequent customers (Xie et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2019) underscore the importance of response appropriateness.

In this paper, we focus on the mix of rational and emotional cues in exploring the appropriateness of managerial responses to negative reviews. We argue that the nature of response should depend on the nature of complaints for it to be effective, and we adopt a theory-driven fundamental analysis of the nature of complaints and the nature of managerial responses to develop our conceptual model. We syn thesize justice theory and service recovery literature to develop our theoretical arguments and hypotheses.

## Theoretical Background

## Justice Theory and Service Recovery

Equity theory explains the consumer complaining behavior in terms of perceived justice and focuses on the perceived relationship between inputs, such as time and money, and outputs of transactions, such as products and services (Adams 1965, Goodwin and Ross 1992). However, “Advances in organizational justice research beyond equity theory suggest that individuals de<sup>fi</sup>ne fairness not only in terms of the outcomes received but also in terms of the procedures used to determine one’s outcomes, labeled procedural justice” (Skarlicki and Folger 1997, p. 435). Moreover, “a second form of procedural justice focuses on employees’ perceptions of the quality of the interper sonal treatment received during the enactment of organizational procedures, commonly labeled interactional justice” (Skarlicki and Folger 1997, p. 435). Thus, it is argued that individuals “involved in con <sup>fl</sup>icts or disputes base their perceptions of justice on several factors” (Blodgett et al. 1993, p. 404) such as the “perceived fairness of the tangible outcome (i.e. distributive justice; Homans 1961),” “the perceived fairness of the procedures used in arriving at the outcome (i.e. procedural justice; Thibaut and Walker 1975),” and “the perceived fairness of the manner in which they were treated” during the enactment of procedures “(i.e. interactional justice; Bies and Moag 1986)” (Blodgett et al. 1993, p. 404). Justice theory allows us to explain the complaining behaviors of cus tomers in terms of perceived justice, which is a vital factor that determines whether a customer is likely to complain (Cho et al. 2003) and engage in negative word-of-mouth behavior (Blodgett et al. 1993). Drawing from this theory, online customer complaints can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three types: complaints about distributive unfairness, complaints about procedural unfairness, and complaints about interactional unfairness. Existing research on service recovery has examined ways in which management can respond to customer complaints. Service recovery is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the actions a service provider takes to address customers’ complaints (Gronroos¨ 1988) or as the actions designed to solve customers’ problems, alter negative opinions of dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers, and <sup>fi</sup>nally retain these customers (Miller et al. 2000). In the online review forums, managerial response to online customer complaints is an important service recovery effort by the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In the of<sup>fl</sup>ine context, <sup>fi</sup>rms deal with customers complaints in private, and the service recovery efforts could be only perceived by the complaining customer. In the online context, it is important for the <sup>fi</sup>rms to signal the effort they make to address customers’ complaints because their service recovery effort largely re<sup>fl</sup>ected in their responses will be observed and perceived by all the potential customers.

Existing studies on management responses to complaints have focused on the mechanics of responding and on the substance of the response. Promptness, which pertains to how quickly a <sup>fi</sup>rm responds to a complaint, and effort, which pertains to the time and energy a <sup>fi</sup>rm expends in responding, depict the mechanics of managerial response (Brown and Peterson 1994, Karatepe 2006). The substance of managerial response has been characterized in terms of redress, facilitation, apology, explanation, and attentiveness (Smith et al. 1999, Karatepe and Ekiz 2004, Karatepe 2006). In this paper, because we focus on the actual content embedded in online managerial responses, we exclude from our theorizing promptness and effort, as they characterize how a <sup>fi</sup>rm responds and not the content of its response.<sup>1</sup> Compared with traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine service recovery, online forums allow for an easier dialog between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the customer and use such dialog to signal other potential customers. Thus, managerial responses could reinforce salient features of the service provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm (i.e., reinforcement), appreciate the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s customers for their loyalty and patronage (i.e., appreciation), and encourage them to communicate in the future (i.e., encouragement) (Strauss and Hill 2001). Thus, our conceptualization of managerial response encompasses both the aspects explored in the service recovery literature and those that are unique to online forums.

In general, <sup>fi</sup>rms can respond in rational and emotional ways (Ahmad et al. 2017). Response messages with rational cues present “information in an objective and straightforward manner” (Claeys and Cauberghe 2014, p. 184). Rational cues provide factual and concrete information about an adverse event and describe the steps the organization is taking to manage the situation (Moon and Rhee 2012). Response messages with emotional cues “appeal to individuals’ emotions by” “including subjective, evaluative properties” (Claeys and Cauberghe 2014, p. 184). Emotional cues focus “more on expressing the organization’s sincere sorrow, regret, and concern for those affected by” an adverse event (Moon and Rhee 2012, p. 682). Both rational and emotional cues “aim to persuade message recipients to engage in behaviors desired by the message senders” (Lim et al. 2014, p. 1372). In our online service recovery context, managerial responses with rational cues, which includes explanation, redress, facil itation, and reinforcement (Strauss and Hill 2001, Karatepe and Ekiz 2004, Karatepe 2006), provide the factual information and the steps <sup>fi</sup>rms plan to take to correct the service failure. Managerial responses with emo tional cues express the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ sincere regret and appreci ation, which includes apologizing for service shortfalls (i.e., apology) (Smith et al. 1999), appreciating custom ers for their patronage (i.e., appreciation) (Strauss and Hill 2001), being attentive to emotional cues from the customers and responding to them (i.e., attentiveness) (Parasuraman et al. 1988, Davidow 2000), and appeal ing to the customers to continue to engage with the <sup>fi</sup>rm (i.e., encouragement) (Strauss and Hill 2001). ## Conceptual Model and Hypotheses Responding to Negative Reviews with

Distributive Unfairness or Procedural Unfairness Some research has found that a rational framing of response messages is more effective because customers get informed with facts about a service failure instead of being distracted by emotional appeal (Huang 2008). Other studies have found the emotional framing of response messages to be more effective because the emotional appeal displays sympathy, is perceived as more credible (Schultz et al. 2011), and can increase a customer’s trust in the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Weiss and Cropanzano 1996). However, prior research has not examined what mix of rational and emotional cues might be more effective for different types of service failures.

Customers may complain that they were discriminated against, they were not getting what they deserve, or the service did not meet their requirements. All these instances of distributive unfairness are related to a service provider’s controllability regarding inequitable or equitable allocation of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts customers derive for their investments in a transaction. Similarly, complaints about procedural unfairness such as the “time taken to complete a procedure” not meeting expectations, a lack of “adaptability of procedures to re<sup>fl</sup>ect individual circumstances,” and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties faced by a customer in engaging in the service process (Tax et al. 1998, p. 63; see also Bitner et al. (1990) and Cho et al. (2003)) are all related to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s controllability regarding the enactment of policies and procedures during the service delivery process.

Firms strive to be rational and the “forces to be rational become more pronounced when there appears to be a loss of organizational control” (Dutton 1986, p. 509). Therefore, rational responses such as explanation are needed when there is a discrepancy between actions and expected outcomes (Dutton 1986). When facing online complaints about service failures that are perceived to be controllable by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, explanation is a way of keeping a rational appearance, which serves to assure the customer that meaningful actions are being considered (Dutton 1986). Responses with rational cues such as explanation of why the service failure occurred may “lower a complainant’s perception of the seriousness of” the distributive unfairness (Karatepe 2006, p. 74; see also Bowen et al. (2000)) and thereby restore equity for the customer (Smith et al. 1999). Customers’ perception of distributive fairness pertains to “the allocation of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in achieving equitable exchange relationships” (Smith et al. 1999, p. 358), and the customer expects that the <sup>fi</sup>rm should provide some compensation to restore its perceived fairness, especially in hotel and restaurant settings (Smith et al. 1999). Responses with rational cues such as redress, which pertains to the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts a customer receives by complaining, has been found to be effective in addressing distributive unfairness (Davidow 2003, Karatepe 2006).

Complaints about procedural unfairness pertain to the enactment of policies and procedures during the service delivery process. Response with rational cues such as facilitation is considered effective in responding to online customer complaints about procedural unfairness (Karatepe 2006) because facilitation refers to the policies and procedures that a <sup>fi</sup>rm uses to enable customers to express their problems and engage in a dialog with them (Karatepe and Ekiz 2004). Reinforcement stresses the salient features of the product or service and/or the quality of the service personnel (Strauss and Hill 2001). This allows managers to reinforce in the customers’ mind the quality and integrity of their service offerings, despite the service shortfall faced by the customers.

Responses with rational cues to complaints that contain distributive or procedural unfairness is likely to restore a complainant’s perception of fairness and increase customer satisfaction. However, most responses also contain some element of emotional appeal to customers. This would complement the rational cues in the response in signaling that the managers are not only aware of and understand the reasons for the service failure but also take responsibility for the service failure, empathize with the customers, and are sincere in their efforts to address the causes of the service failures. Although logical explanations are most needed, some emotional appeal serves to build trust in the explanations and remedial actions suggested by the managers. More emotional than rational appeal, on the other hand, is unlikely to be deemed appropriate service recovery effort for complaints about distributive or procedural unfairness. These complaints involve speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues encountered during the service delivery. A mere emotional appeal such as an apology is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient because these issues might require logical explanations as to why the service failure happened and steps taken by the <sup>fi</sup>rm to correct the problems, as well as sometimes even compensating for the loss or hardship faced by customers. Past research <sup>fi</sup>ndings that when corporate actions, or the lack thereof, impact third parties such as customers, an informational apology that focuses on clarifying the facts and delineating solutions is the most effective strategy (Lee et al. 2019), reinforces the basic premise of our arguments. Thus, responses with more rational than emotional cues would be perceived to be appropriate for complaints about distributive or procedural unfairness by those observing the information exchange in an online forum.

## Responding to Negative Reviews with Interactional Unfairness

Complaints about interactional unfairness involve (a) a lack of well-mannered and courteous behavior (politeness), (b) inadequate “positive energy put into resolving a problem” (effort), and (c) inadequate “provision of caring, individual attention” (empathy) (Park et al. 2008, p. 526; see also Folkes (1984), Parasuraman et al. (1988), Goodwin and Ross (1989), and Tax et al. (1998)). To the extent <sup>fi</sup>rms take steps to respond to complaints, customers could perceive that the service provider is enthusiastic to <sup>fi</sup>x the problems, and hence the service failures are less likely to happen in the future (Blodgett et al. 1995), thus increasing the customers’ satisfaction with service recovery efforts. Given that interactional unfairness is essentially about the mismatch between the customers’ need for emotional grati<sup>fi</sup>cation and a mechanistic service delivery process, empathetic responses are likely to be appropriate. Responses with emotional cues such as apology and attentiveness could be effective in handling online customer complaints about interactional fairness, as such a response humanizes the service system and allows for empathy in the complaint handling process.

Apology could provide psychological compensation for the injustice experienced by the customer as a result of impolite or careless service personnel (Davidow 2000), and studies have found it to be an effective service recovery strategy (Goodwin and Ross 1989, Greenberg 1990). Similarly, attentiveness can address perceived injustice arising from a lack of effort by the service personnel (Tax et al. 1998), and appreciation and encouragement can assist customers to reconsider their sense of loss, because such efforts signal the value placed by the <sup>fi</sup>rm on their customers and their views and opinions (Strauss and Hill 2001). Thus, responses with more emotional cues to complaints about interactional unfairness are more likely to be perceived as appropriate service recovery efforts.

On the other hand, responses with more rational cues such as explanation, redress, reinforcement, and facilitation provide factual information about and reasons for the service failures (e.g., because of variation in the quality of the service personnel) rather than expressing the “sorrow, regret and concern for those affected by” interactional unfairness (Moon and Rhee 2012, p. 61). Such rational cues may not satisfy complaining customers’ need for emotional grati<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., needs for respect, individual caring, and attentiveness) and restore their perceived fairness. Thus, responses with more rational cues to complaints about interactional unfairness are more likely to be perceived as inappropriate service recovery efforts.

In this paper, we argue that the appropriateness of managerial responses to customer complaints wil in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. Past research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three main reasons why managerial responses will in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. First, managerial responses “increase the cost of leaving a negative review” and decrease the occurrence of future negative reviews (Proserpio and Zervas 2017, p. 647). Proserpio and Zervas (2017) argued that subsequent reviewers would feel the need to write longer and more speci<sup>fi</sup>c reviews because they would think their reviews will be scrutinized closely by the <sup>fi</sup>rm and responded publicly. Some reviewers may not want to expend the time and effort to write longer and more detailed negative reviews and instead may choose not to write negative reviews at all (Proserpio and Zervas 2017), which in<sup>fl</sup>uences the future review valence positively. This would be especially true when such reviewers feel that the <sup>fi</sup>rm has responded appropriately to the issues raised in prior reviews. Second, subsequent reviewers may not want to repeat the same issues or raise similar complaints as those reported by past reviewers if the <sup>fi</sup>rm has acknowledged and responded to the issues successfully (Yang et al. 2019). Because reiterating the same information does not contain much information value, subsequent reviewers “would feel obliged to express their complaint differently” (Yang et al. 2019, p. 354), thus reducing their motivation to write a negative review (Yang et al. 2019), which leads to the improved future review valence. Third, managerial responses to negative reviews indicate “the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s willingness and intention to solve the problem, thus creating goodwill among other customers who might otherwise have written a negative review” (Yang et al. 2019, p. 354). This goodwill is likely to be high if the response from the <sup>fi</sup>rm is considered appropriate for the nature of complaints but could be low if otherwise. For these reasons, when an appropriate managerial response is offered in terms of the mix of rational and emotional cues, subsequent reviewer behaviors in the online review forum are likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced to affect future review valence.

In addition to these mechanisms, it is also likely that managerial responses could frame subsequent customers’ expectations about the services offered by the <sup>fi</sup>rm (Ho et al. 2017).<sup>2</sup> Such expectations could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the satisfaction of the subsequent customers, which, in turn, can have a bearing on the nature of their reviews. Whether such reviews are likely to be negative or positive will be shaped by the actual service experienced by the subsequent customer and the likely level of expectation discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation. Thus, the effect of expectation setting on future review valence is an empirical issue, the direction of which cannot be posited a priori. Thus, on the basis of the three aforementioned mechanisms, which have received empirical support in past studies, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1. Inclusion of more rational than emotional cues in managerial responses to negative reviews that contain distributive unfairness will be associated with higher levels of future review valence.

Hypothesis 2. Inclusion of more rational than emotional cues in managerial responses to negative reviews that contain procedural unfairness will be associated with highe levels of future review valence.

Hypothesis 3. Inclusion of more rational than emotional cues in managerial responses to negative reviews that contain interactional unfairness will be associated with lower levels of future review valence.

## Empirical Methods Data Collection

We collected a sample of 67,418 daily chronologically archived hotel reviews from TripAdvisor.com, a leading travel review platform. The sample consists of 161 hotels located in Boston (n 78) and Honolulu (n 83). The data are comprehensive for each location, presenting every customer review and corresponding managerial response (if available) published on TripAdvisor.com up to the time of data collection, covering reviews between March 2001 and November 2011. We did a purposive sampling and chose two cities that differed in the nature of their tourist attraction and geographic location, thereby adding diversity to the sample. In all, 11% of reviews received a managerial response, and 16% of all responses targeted negative reviews, which yielded 1,186 pairs of negative reviews and corresponding responses. We care fully scrutinized each pair and eliminated incomplete reviews and responses to derive 1,064 pairs of valid and complete reviews and responses.

Our data include characteristics speci<sup>fi</sup>c to hotel reviews (hotel name, review title, review date, review content, industry star rating, total user ratings, and average user rating), individual reviewers (reviewer ID; address; overall rating for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c hotel visit; stay period; and experience ratings for value, location, sleep quality, room quality, cleanliness, and service), and managerial responses (response date, responding manager’s ID, and response content). The richness of the data set allows for the systematic exploration of actual occurrences of manager-customer interaction in online review forums.

## Text Analysis and Data Coding

We <sup>fi</sup>rst used Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) to classify negative reviews and the corresponding managerial responses. Then we used a deep learning algorithm (Convolutional Neural Network for Sentence Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation) to classify the remaining negative reviews without responses. AMT is a crowdsourcing marketplace used to automate the implementation of microtasks that require human involvement, such as text analysis (Ghose et al. 2012). To content code our reviews and responses, we designed a survey in which AMT workers were asked to answer a series of binary yes/no questions (1/0) concerning a <sup>fi</sup>negrained characterization of both managerial responses and negative reviews. There were two sections in this AMT survey: One is used to classify the content of negative reviews by asking several questions about the characteristics of the complaint message. The other is used to identify the content of managerial responses by asking several questions about the characteristics of the response message. The corresponding questions are shown in Online Appendix 1. In order to ensure highquality responses from AMT Turkers, we followed rigorous and systematic validation procedures as recommended in the literature (Lee et al. 2018). Online Appendix 2 outlines our data collection strategy. Online Appendix 3 outlines the deep learning algorithms.

The content in managerial responses can be categorized as rational or emotional. Responses coded as “explanation,” “redress,” “facilitation,” or “reinforcement” are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as rational, and those coded as “apology,” “appreciation,” “attentiveness,” or “encouragement” are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as emotional. The content in negative reviews (i.e., customer complaints) can be categorized as complaints about distributive unfairness, procedural unfairness, and interactional unfairness. Complaints coded as “equality,” “equity,” or “need” are considered to be distributive; those coded as “speed,” “<sup>fl</sup>exibility,” or “accessibility” are considered to be procedural; and those coded as “politeness,” “effort,” or “empathy” are considered to be interactional. Table 1 outlines the coding schema and the precise de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the various dimensions used in our schema and the literature source.

We used Cronbach’s alpha, a commonly used interrater reliability measure, to measure interrater reliability for 1,064 pairs of reviews and responses (Lee et al. 2018).<sup>3</sup> The average Cronbach’s alpha for our tagged reviews and responses is 0.74 (median of 0.78), which is “above the typically acceptable threshold of 0.7” (Lee et al. 2018, p. 5114).

## Measures

Table 2 summarizes the measures for the theoretical constructs as well as the control variables. We elaborate on these measures in the following.

Future Review Valence. The dependent variable Future Review Valence is a forward-looking measure of the overall average review valence (the sum of all review rating/the count of ratings in 14 days). We calculated the average rating of reviews during the 14-day period after a focal response.

More Rational Cues. We refer to the Lee et al. (2018) paper to <sup>fi</sup>rst create two composite summary variables corresponding to rational and emotional cues in responses. Rational Cues are obtained by adding values of explanation, redress, facilitation, and reinforcement, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 and 4. Emotional Cues are obtained by adding values of apology, appreciation, attentiveness, and encouragement, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 and 4. Then, we calculated More Rational Cue as a dummy variable that is equal to 1 if rational cues are used more than emotional cues in a response and 0 otherwise.

Negative Reviews. In TripAdvisor.com, a rating of 1 corresponds to “terrible,” 2 to “poor,” 3 to “average,” 4 to “very good,” and 5 to “excellent.” Following the existing literature (Chen et al. 2019), we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed each review in our data set as “negative” or “positive” based on rating assignments of 1–2 or 3–5, respec tively. Among negative reviews, we created three composite summary variables corresponding to distributive, procedural, and interactional unfairness to characterize the negative reviews (i.e., customer complaints). The Distributive Unfairness variable is obtained by adding values of equality, equity, and need, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3. The Procedural Unfairness variable is obtained by adding values of speed, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and accessibility, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3. The Interactional Unfairness variable is obtained by adding values of politeness, effort, and empathy, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3.

Controls. Review-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors may in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. Previous Rating is the average ratings of prior reviews before and on the response date. We think the previous ratings before and on the response date will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the future review valence during the postresponse period. Previous Volume is the total number of reviews before and on the response date. The previous volume of reviews indicates the popularity or the prominence of a hotel, which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the subsequent customers’ attitude. We also controlled for Proportion of Positive Reviews before and on the response date, because we think the proportion of previous positive reviews will also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the future review valence during the postresponse period.

The main effects take the content of managerial response into consideration. In order to account for the effects of the mechanics of managerial response, we control for the timeliness of response and managerial response effort. We expect that a timely response to negative reviews will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the valence of the postresponse reviews. We coded Response Timeliness by counting the number of days between the date a review is posted and the date a response is received. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that among all negative reviews, approximately 1% received a managerial response on the same day as the initiating customer review, whereas a large majority (71%) received a managerial response within 10 days. In addition, we control for managerial efforts by using Response Length, which is calculated as the number of words in a response. We expect that the longer the response, the higher future review valence will be. Following the existing literature (Chen et al. 2019), we also controlled for other response-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors such as Number of Responses to Positive Reviews and Number of Responses to Negative Reviews before and on the response date.

We added hotel <sup>fi</sup>xed effects to control for unobserved time-invariant hotel-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. In addition, we added year-<sup>fi</sup>xed effects in the full model to rule out unobserved time-varying heterogeneity. We also controlled for the seasonality associated with each of the sample cities. Guided by travel industry sources (e.g., Frommer’s travel guides and TripAdvisor.com), we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the months, seasons, and holidays associated with increased travel to the sample cities, as such trends could potentially impact hotel review volume and review ratings. Seasonality is coded as a dummy variable that measures whether it is a peak or nonpeak season on or around the response date. We controlled for hotel reputation by using Industry Star Rating. We expect that future review valence will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a hotel’s brand image or reputation. We also controlled for the hotel quality changes between the day a review is posted and the day a response is received. The hotel quality was measured by aggregating the experience ratings (e.g., service quality, sleep quality, value, room condition, cleanliness, and location) for each hotel review observation (Ravichandran et al. 2015). Using this variable, we assessed the average hotel quality on the day a review is posted, because multiple reviews for a hotel may occur on the same day. Then we assessed the average hotel quality on the day a response is received by retrieving from hotel quality ratings on or around the response date.<sup>4</sup> Then we computed Hotel Quality Change by taking the difference in hotel quality between the review day and the response day.

Then, because the primary focus of our theorizing is on how different kinds of responses to various types of reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence, we did an individual review-response-level analysis.

## Robustness Checks

Our robustness checks examine whether the observed effects of managerial responses on future review valence hold with different time windows. To accomplish this, we tested all three models with future review valence over a 21-day period and 28-day period following a managerial response to a negative review (Tables 9 and 10). We recalculated all the dependent variables for the 21-day and 28-day postresponse periods. For the 21-day postresponse valence in Table 9 and for the 28-day response valence in Table 10, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that all the previously determined impacts retained signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the inclusion of more rational than emotional cues in managerial responses to negative reviews that contain procedural unfairness are associated with higher levels of future review valence in Model 5 (β 0.181, $p < 0 . 0 1$ in Table 9 and $\beta = 0 . 1 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 5$ in Table 10), supporting Hypothesis 2. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the inclusion of more rational than emotional cues in managerial responses to negative reviews that contain interactional unfairness are associated with lower levels of future review valence $( \beta = - 0 . 1 4 3 , p <$ 0.05 in Table 9 and $\beta = - 0 . 1 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 5$ in Table 10), which supports Hypothesis 3.

## Effect of Managerial Responses to a Different Mix of Complaints

Because negative reviews could contain all three types of unfairness, we conducted additional analysis to tease out the appropriate managerial responses for different contexts. To better understand this result, we plotted the three-way interactions based on the results in our main analysis (Figure 2). It can be seen from Figure 2 that when a review contains a high level of distributive unfairness and a low level of interactional unfairness, the inclusion of more rational cues than emotional cues in a response will trigger higher future review valence than the inclusion of more emotional cues. However, when a review contains a high level of distributive unfairness and a high level of interactional unfairness, the inclusion of more emotional cues than rational cues in a response will trigger higher future review valence than the inclusion of more rational cues. This corroborates the intuition behind our hypotheses that when customers complain about interactional unfairness, responses with more emotional cues are appropriate.

## Effect of Managerial Responses on Future Complaints

To shed more light on the nuanced effects of manage rial responses on future reviews, we examined if and how appropriate responses to different types of unfairness in reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence the likelihood of occurrence of similar complaints in future reviews.<sup>5</sup> Arguably, an effective response to speci<sup>fi</sup>c complaints could discourage subsequent customers from voicing similar issues if the overall hotel quality and service levels remain unchanged. To explore this, we used all the negative reviews in our data set for the analysis.

In our data set, we have 1,186 pairs of negative reviews and corresponding responses and 6,362 nega tive reviews without responses. We carefully scrutinized each pair and eliminated incomplete reviews and responses to derive 1,064 pairs of valid and com plete reviews and responses. We also deleted incomplete and non-English negative reviews without responses, which yielded 4,635 negative reviews without responses. We have already tagged negative reviews with responses of either 0 or 1 for equality, equity, need, speed, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, accessibility, politeness, effort, and empathy by using AMT, and we made three composite variables for distributive, procedural, and interactional unfairness by adding the values of each corresponding attribute. In order to code negative reviews without responses in their underlying dimensions, we used the AMT data as our training data set and a deep learning algorithm, Convolutional Neural Network for Sentence Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. We explain the deep learning algorithms and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation model performance in detail in Online Appendix 3.

Now we have all the negative reviews tagged as either 0 or 1 for each of the nine attributes (i.e., equality, equity, need, speed, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, accessibility, politeness, effort, and empathy) in our data set. These attributes are inclusive, which means a negative review could have all or none of them. Again, distributive unfairness is measured by adding values of equality, equity, and need, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3. Procedural unfairness is measured by adding values of speed, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and accessibility, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3. Interactional unfairness is measured by adding values of politeness, effort, and empathy, resulting in a composite variable ranging from 0 to 3. Then we used Python programming to calculate how many times distributive, procedural, and interactional unfairness components occur in negative reviews during the postresponse period. We use this count variable to measure the future occurrence of different types of complaints in reviews.

Given the large number of zeros in the occurrence of different types of complaints in future reviews (e.g., in 14-day post response period, 58.5% of future negative reviews contain zero distributive component, 79.94% of future negative reviews contain zero procedure component and 75.99% of future negative reviews contain zero interactional component), we used a zero in<sup>fl</sup>ated model as our estimation model (Greene 2012, Cabral et al. 2020). We compared the zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated negative binomial model with the zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated Poisson model. A nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant likelihood ratio test for α 0 indicates that the Poisson model is preferred to the negative binomial model.<sup>6</sup>

A zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated model assumes that a zero outcome is from two different processes.<sup>6</sup> In our example, the two processes are as follows: (1) if there are no negative reviews during the postresponse period, the only possible outcome is zero, and (2) if there are negative reviews but no distributive, procedural, or interactional components in a review, it is then a count process yielding a zero (e.g., if distributive component is 0, the count outcome for distributive unfairness is 0). The <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated Poisson model is a logit model to predict which of the two processes the zero outcomes belong to (in our example, whethe there are negative reviews during the postresponse period). The second part of a zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated model is a count model—in our example, a Poisson model—to model the count process. We used four variables— Previous Volume, Proportion of Positive Reviews, Number of Responses to Positive Reviews, and Number of Responses to Negative Reviews—to predict which of the two processes the zero outcomes are associated with. All these variables are expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the number of future negative reviews during the postresponse period. The zero-in<sup>fl</sup>ated Poisson models, used to explore how managers’ responses to customers’ complaints in<sup>fl</sup>uence the future occurrences of different types of complaints, were estimated using clustered errors, where clusters were de<sup>fi</sup>ned at the hotel level.

Overall, the results of this supplemental analysis suggest that appropriate managerial response to different types of complaints could reduce the future occurrence of such complaints in negative reviews. These results verify one of our proposed mechanisms that explain the effect of managerial responses on future review valence. Subsequent reviewers are less likely to repeat the same issues or raise similar complaints as those reported by past reviewers if the <sup>fi</sup>rm has acknowledged and responded to the issues appropriately (Yang et al. 2019).

## Discussion

We started this enquiry with the goal of understand ing managerial strategies to deal with negative reviews. We <sup>fi</sup>rst examined at the aggregate level the effects of responding to reviews, both positive and negative, on the valence of subsequent customer reviews. We found that responding to negative reviews does positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. To develop speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines on managerial response strategies, we followed the aggregate-level analysis with individual review-response-level analy sis. Through text analysis of individual reviews and responses and using statistical models that controlled for a number of relevant review, response, hotel, and temporal factors, we derived speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines for how <sup>fi</sup>rms should respond to negative reviews in online platforms.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that (1) when a negative review includes more complaints about procedural unfairness, responses with more rational cues (i.e., explanation, redress, facilitation, and reinforcement) are appropriate; (2) when a negative review includes more complaints about interactional unfairness, responses with more emotional cues (i.e., apology, appreciation, attentiveness, and encouragement) are appropriate; (3) when a negative review contains both complaints about distributive and interactional unfairness, responses with more emotional cues (i.e., apology, appreciation, attentiveness, and encouragement) are appropriate; (4) when a negative review includes complaints about distributive unfairness, responses with both rational and emotional cues are effective in reducing the future occurrence of complaints about distributive unfairness; and (5) when a negative review includes complaints about procedural unfairness, responses with rational cues are effective in reducing the future occurrence of complaints about procedural unfairness.

## Practical Implications

Recent surveys by the Pew Research Center<sup>7</sup> indicate that customers pay attention to online reviews and that such reviews are very instrumental in driving customer behaviors. This, coupled with the fact that negative reviews seem to have a stronger effect on customer behaviors (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006 and Yin et al. 2016), raises the stakes for managers to effectively respond to such reviews in order to protect their brand. However, given the exponential growth in the volume of reviews, a strategic approach that enables managers to focus their efforts in responding to negative reviews is needed. This study and its <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c and actionable guidelines for <sup>fi</sup>rms in responding to negative reviews in online forums. First, managers should respond to negative reviews in order to safeguard the brand and improve <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation, because we <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive effect of managerial response to negative reviews on future review valence. Second, managers should be aware that they can respond both rationally and emotionally to negative reviews. Although emotional responses have been the preferred mode in most <sup>fi</sup>rms, our theorizing and <sup>fi</sup>ndings clearly indicate that a response with rational cues is also particularly important in dealing with complaints. When complaints pertain primarily to the procedures in the service delivery process, such as speed and <sup>fl</sup>exibility, managers should respond with rational cues that explain the reasons for the service failure and the steps taken to address such failures and should reinforce the value of the service provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm. When customers complain only about the nature of their interactions with the hotel or also <sup>fi</sup>le grievances about the services not aligning with their needs, managers should respond with more emotional cues, such as apologizing or appreciating the customers for their patronage and being attentive to the empathy and emotiona grati<sup>fi</sup>cation needs of customers. When customers complain that they were discriminated against, they were not getting what they deserve, or the service did not meet their requirements, managers should respond with both rational cues that explain the discrepancy between actions and expected outcomes and should provide some compensation and emotiona cues that satisfy the customers’ need for emotional grati<sup>fi</sup>cation.

Such customized and calibrated responses that are appropriate for the nature of the complaint would be critical in shaping the views of other customers in the online review forum. Firms in their efforts to deal with the growing volume of reviews have increasingly automated the response process using template responses. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a more deliberate approach of carefully tailoring the responses to negative reviews is likely to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in online review forums. Firms could use a data-driven approach of extracting and classifying the nature of complaints, according to our proposed framework. Recent advances in machine learning algorithms allow for such classi<sup>fi</sup>cation with greater precision. Instead of drafting each response from scratch, managers can use machine-written skeletons in their responses to target some speci<sup>fi</sup>c reviews. Firms could then generate responses that are tailored to the nature of the complaints. Such approaches to generate tailored responses could allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to deal with the large review volumes in a more effective manner.

## Implications for Theory Development

Our study makes several theoretical contributions to the information systems and marketing literatures. The emerging literature on effective managerial response strategies to online negative word of mouth, although rich, lacks theoretically anchored frameworks that allow for systematic exploration of the mechanisms underlying the effects of managerial responses on the reviewing behaviors of observing customers. We <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap in several ways. First, by synthesizing justice theory with service recovery concepts, we provide a comprehensive framework that can serve as a starting point for future research aimed at exploring managerial responses to negative reviews. Our conceptualization of managerial response in terms of rational and emotional cues, as well as online negative reviews in terms of distributive, procedural, and interactional injustice, provides a frame work to understand the mechanisms through which managerial responses might mitigate the effects of negative reviews. Our delineation of the subdimensions of rational and emotional cues in responses provides a synthesis of the service recovery strategies discussed in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine complaint management literature with the unique features of complaint handling in online review forums. Our delineation of the subdi mensions of negative reviews by using justice theory provides a theory-driven mapping of various commonly observed elements of online customer complaints into one coherent framework and thereby advances theory.

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the appropriate mix of rationality and emotionality in responses is effective in shaping the reviewing behavior of subsequent customers sheds light on the nuanced effects of managerial intervention in online forums and thereby extends the discourse in the marketing literature. Although the effects of rationality and emotionality in advertising messages on customer opinions and attitudes (e.g., Heath and Stipp 2011) have been explored extensively, the appropriate mix of rationality and emotionality in responses to online complaints is missing in the literature. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that rationality in online responses works for online complaints about procedural unfairness, emotionality in responses works for interactional unfairness, and both rationality and emotionality in responses work for distributive unfairness. These results shed light on how to craft appropriate persuasive messages when there are different types of service failures and opens new avenues for investigating effective managerial intervention strategies in online forums.

Third, our comprehensive analysis of the effects of appropriate responses on the reviewing behavior highlight that the effects of an appropriately tailored managerial response manifests through multiple pathways. Whereas studies have focused on future review volume and valence, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the nature of complaints, in addition to future valence, could also be shaped by appropriate managerial responses. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that subsequent reviewers are less likely to repeat the same issues or raise similar complaints as those reported by past reviewers if the <sup>fi</sup>rm has acknowledged and responded to the issues appropriately (Yang et al. 2019). Although we examined these effects separately, it is possible that there could be causal pathways in that the changes in the nature of reviews lead to changes in review valence. It would be important for future studies to explore such causal pathways through controlled experiments to further our knowledge in this important domain of Information Systems research.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend the conversation in the literature that has used expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory to explain reviewing behaviors (Ho et al. 2017). These studies have found that reviews and managerial responses to reviews could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the expectation setting of customers, which, in turn, could shape their reviews. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding that an appropriate managerial response to complaints reduces the future occurrence of such complaints adds to this discourse. When subsequent customers come across recurring complaints on certain issues, they might pay more attention to those problems during their own experience. Prior managerial responses are likely to entice subsequent customers to form expectations that can fundamentally shift the way subsequent customers evaluate their own experience. Although we did not measure expectations and discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation—these occur at the level of individual customers, and we could not retrieve data about individual customers expectation and discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation—we believe that a deeper exploration of how managerial responses shape customer expectations and, consequently, their reviewing behavior would be a particularly interesting research direction to pursue in our quest to develop a deeper understanding of managerial response strategies and their effects.

Methodologically, our study offers some insights. Many online forums deal with millions of reviews, and automated approaches to content coding the reviews and responses are needed on larger data sets. The supplemental analyses demonstrate how to use AMT in combination with deep learning techniques to classify reviews and derive variables of interest. Recent developments in natural language processing (NLP) have yielded several transformer-based deep learning models such as BERT and ALBERT that are quite effective for extracting variables of interest from textual data. We believe that NLP is a particularly effective approach for future research to extract meaningful variables from voluminous unstructured text data that accrue in organizations these days.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with any study using archival data, this study has its limitations, some of which provide a direction for future research. First, this study assesses only the impact of managerial response in service industries. It is possible that systemic differences between product and service businesses might in<sup>fl</sup>uence managerial response strategies and their effectiveness. The intangible nature of the service we examined and the ease with which online customer complaints can be addressed through effective responses, creating a context where managerial responses can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the valence and content of subsequent customer reviews. However, it is unclear whether this will be the same in a product industry where complaints might require more elaborate actions or could be time lagged. Therefore, future studies could explore the effective managerial response strategies in online forums for products.

Second, the observed increase in review valence could also be due to hotel quality improvements. Although we control for the hotel quality change between the day a review is posted and the day a response is received, we were not able to assess the hotel quality change after the response date and before or on the day the subsequent reviewers stayed at the hotel because it was not feasible to determine the actual date the subsequent reviewers arrived and stayed in the hotel. Perhaps future studies should explore partnering with review platforms in order to compile such <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained data to control for dif<sup>fi</sup>cultto-observe factors that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence future review valence. Although it is impossible to determine whether any reviewer actually was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the managerial responses, our empirical setting allows for such information transmission. Nevertheless, it would be important for future research to use randomized controlled experiments to further explore and validate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported here.

Finally, this study only examined managerial response strategies to negative reviews. We observe that managers also respond to positive reviews. Future research could examine the effective response strategies for positive reviews and thereby lead to a more comprehensive understanding of appropriate response strategies in online forums.

## Conclusion

Given that theoretically anchored frameworks to classify online customer complaints and managerial responses are sparse, this paper <sup>fi</sup>lls a critical gap and provides a rich understanding of how an overt managerial presence in online review forums in<sup>fl</sup>uences future reviewing behavior. In addition, this paper provides guidance for managers to interact with their customers openly and skillfully in the review community in response to expressed dissatisfaction in order to restore their company’s reputation and brand image. We hope our study will simulate further exploration of managerial response strategies to all types of online reviews.
