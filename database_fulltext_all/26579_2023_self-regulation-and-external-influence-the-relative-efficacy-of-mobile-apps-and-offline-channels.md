# Self-Regulation and External Influence: The Relative Efficacy of Mobile Apps and Offline Channels for Personal Weight Management

Abstract. This study contributes to the information systems literature on mobile health interventions and omnichannel management by examining the relative effectiveness of mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels in facilitating personal weight management. Drawing on the social cognitive theory of self-regulation, our empirical analysis utilizes a system generalized method of moments approach applied to panel data on customers enrolled in a weight loss program that delivers services through multiple channels, including a mobile app and of<sup>fl</sup>ine of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits. Our results show that the use of the mobile app is positively associated with weight management by both free and paid users. For paid users, who have access to the mobile app and of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits, usage of both channels is associated with increased shortterm weight loss. Furthermore, the two channels function as substitutes for one another, with users able to compensate for infrequent of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits through more intense mobile app usage. In the long term, however, only mobile app usage (and not of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits) contributes to the sustainability of weight loss, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in reduced weight variability and lower overall failure rate. Qualitative evidence gleaned from interviews with actual customers substantiated the self-regulation mechanism enabled by mobile app usage. Additional empirical analyses further revealed that frequency and granularity of mobile app usage are positively associated with weight loss. We also found that individuals exposed to low performance pressure bene<sup>fi</sup>t more fully from mobile app usage. The results are robust to endogeneity concerns and alternative measures of the key variables. Overall, our analysis sheds light on the important role of a self-regulatory mobile app in a multichannel setting of personal weight management, as compared with the external in<sup>fl</sup>uence stemming from human experts in of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, with useful implications for research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

The ubiquity and ease of use of mobile technologies have paved the way for the transformation of all industries, including healthcare. Industry forecasts estimate that the global mHealth app market will grow from \$70 billion in 2020 to more than \$330 billion by 2025.<sup>1</sup> These mHealth apps allow consumers to continuously and easily manage their health conditions while encouraging healthy behaviors and disease self-management (Kumar et al. 2013, Steinbuhl et al. 2015). Within mHealth apps, one of the most demanded subcategories is <sup>fi</sup>tness, diet, and weight control (Melton 2020). Prior research provides mixed evidence on whether mobile interventions are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for weight control, and thus further research is called for to fully understand whether mobile usage improves weight control, and how mobile interventions compare with traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels with respect to initial weight loss and the sustainability of weight management over time (Laing et al. 2014).

The key to answering these questions lies in the concept and implementation of self-regulation, which forms the cognitive basis for human self-in<sup>fl</sup>uence and purposive action (Bandura 1991). Self-regulation can be considered synonymous to the familiar notion of self-control, which is understood as the combination of motivation and willpower that allows individuals to resist temptations and limit behaviors within personal standards and social sanctions. Needless to say, selfregulation plays a key role in the motivations and actions behind addiction, obesity, criminal and sexual behaviors, and the like (Baumeister et al. 2007). As we discuss in more detail below, the social cognitive theory of self-regulation posits that self-regulation consists of three cognitive subfunctions: self-monitoring, or the observation of one’s actions and outcomes; judgment, or the formation of personal standards of behavior; and self-reaction, or the self-satisfaction or self-censure arising from meeting or falling short of one’s personal standards, respectively (Bandura 1991).

The literature on chronic disease management (e.g., Clark et al. 2001), in which personal weight management is a special case, points out that self-regulation is central to the achievement of desired outcomes, but it also recognizes the role of external in<sup>fl</sup>uences, which can take the form of guidance and support from a variety of specialized personnel. In traditional weight management programs, for instance, customers routinely visit physical of<sup>fi</sup>ces to engage in self-regulation exercises and also obtain weight management guidance (i.e., external in<sup>fl</sup>uence) from coaches. During the course of of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits, customers update their weight, diet and nutrition, and exercise records; check the status of their weight loss progress; and work with coaches to come up with personalized regimens for diet and exercise.

However, many of these functionalities, especially those emphasizing self-regulation, are now offered in mobile apps; of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels are shifting their focus to the provision of personalized advice and guidance. In other words, whereas of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits offer the opportunity to obtain advice and guidance from human experts (i.e., external in<sup>fl</sup>uence), a mobile app works as a powerful tool for the achievement of self-regulation (Laing et al. 2014, Zahry et al. 2016). For example, mobile apps provide capabilities for the daily tracking of weight and physical activity, such as steps walked or calories burned. In addition, many of these apps also permit users to log their dietary intake and calories that they consume. Mobile apps also facilitate specifying daily goals for weight loss, physical activity, or calorie intake. Finally, prompts, badges, and leaderboards provided by mobile apps position individuals’ actions and progress in relation to their personal standards. Such features offered through mobile apps help individuals monitor and track their progress toward the achievement of weight management objectives (i.e., selfmonitoring), and thereby conduct interim assessments of their performance (i.e., judgment) and determine actions that need to be taken to achieve goals (i.e., selfreaction); see Zahry et al. (2016) for a further discussion of this perspective.

As illustrated in Figure 1, self-regulation, enabled by the use of a mobile app, is a primary driver of personal weight management; of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits and consultations with human experts (i.e., external in<sup>fl</sup>uence) can serve a supporting role. Such divided roles between mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels are mainly driven by thei different temporal proximity, which is the time gap between each self-regulation exercise (Bandura 1991). For example, by virtue of its ubiquity and ease of use, self-monitoring via a mobile app is more frequent and granular than data collection in an of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel that likely coincides with of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits, making the former a more effective channel for the exercise of self-regulation. Moreover, individuals’ emotional attachments to their smartphones, because of their highly personal and private nature (Melumad and Pham 2020), together with the sensitive nature of weight loss, reinforce the high temporal proximity of mobile channels. The relative roles of mobile apps and of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits in weight management will thus depend on how customers value the trade-off between self-regulation and external in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Thus, how the two channels interact with each other and jointly affect weight management outcomes is an empirical question—one that partly serves to motivate this study.

Given the serious health and economic implications of obesity on the one hand (Hammond and Levine 2010) and the increasing ubiquity of smartphones on the other,<sup>2</sup> it is not surprising that a number of studies have endeavored to examine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mobile interventions for weight control (for a literature review, see Free et al. 2013; for a meta-analysis, see Khokhar et al. 2014). Yet, an in<sup>fl</sup>uential mHealth evidence workshop at the National Institutes of Health (Kumar et al. 2013, p. 229) concluded that, despite positive evidence, the question of “whether mHeatlh leads to better health outcomes and reduced disease burden is still unknown.” Steinbuhl et al. (2015, p. 1) also echo this sentiment, stating that “we currently lack high-quality evidence that supports the adoption of many new mobile health technologies.” Even more recently, Rowland et al. (2020, p. 1) recognized the need for continued research efforts by noting that “despite growing interest from both patients and healthcare providers, there is little clinical guidance on how mobile apps should be utilized to add value to patient care.” Moreover, our understanding of the role of mHealth apps in relation to of<sup>fl</sup>ine healthcare services is particularly limited. We hope the present study will add useful insights to the body of evidence accumulated thus far on this subject.

For our analysis, we have access to a novel panel data set on actual weight control processes implemented by over 600 individuals supervised by one of the largest healthcare companies in Korea. The company offers a programmatic weight loss program through both mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, which provides us with a unique opportunity to investigate the relative roles of the two channels. At of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores, customers can consult with weight loss coaches and also receive several direct treatments, such as exercising with a personal trainer or availing themselves of electronic calorie-burning equipment. A mobile app is provided as an avenue for administering behavioral treatments and collecting daily customer records (e.g., weight, diet, and exercise). Our data set thus comprises comprehensive information on (1) usage behavior over mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels; (2) calorie intake, extent of exercise, use of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial electronic assistance, and weight; and (3) individual characteristics, such as demographic attributes, body mass index (BMI), and psychological diseases, which may explain individual heterogeneity in the weight control process. We applied a system generalized method of moments (GMM) model to this unique data set to examine the relative ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mobile app and offline channel usage in advancing weight loss.

Our results indicate that the intensity of mobile app usage is positively associated with short-term weight loss among both paid subscribers (who use both the mobile app and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels) and free users (who use only the mobile app). We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that mobile app usage is positively associated with long-term outcomes of weight management, that is, the sustainability of weight loss and the achievement of target weight. This is an important <sup>fi</sup>nding, as studies have shown that long-term weight loss is often more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to accomplish than initial success (Khokhar et al. 2014). Of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits are also positively related to short-term weight loss and serve as a substitute for mobile app usage; that is, fewer store visits can be offset by the increased use of the mobile app, and vice versa. However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that only mobile app usage (and not of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits) is associated with the improved longterm outcomes of weight management. Overall, the results suggest that mobile app usage and the selfregulation that it enables exert a relatively greater in<sup>fl</sup>uence on personal weight management compared with the external in<sup>fl</sup>uence stemming from of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits. The qualitative evidence from interviews with actual customers corroborates the self-regulation mechanism underlying mobile app usage. Moreover, additional empirical analyses provide further support for the proposed mechanism; that is, how frequently and granularly users log their daily activities using the app is important in ensuring the favorable functioning of mobile app usage in the achievement of weight loss, and individuals exposed to low performance pressure bene<sup>fi</sup>t more fully from mobile app usage.

## 2. Literature and Theory

In essence, weight control is governed by energy balance that is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as energy intake minus energy expenditure (Frayn 1996). Weight gain occurs if energy intake exceeds energy expenditure, but the persistence of this imbalance can cause a state of overweight or obesity (Spiegelman and Flier 2001). Because energy intake and expenditure take the forms of food consumption and physical activity, respectively, reducing calorie intake and exercising (or burning calories) exert first-order effects on weight control outcomes. However, maintaining healthy behaviors (e.g., reducing calorie intake and exercising) is at odds with people’s intrinsic preferences because biologically we are highly susceptible to being inactive and overeating (Forman and Butryn 2015). Accordingly, although people often <sup>fi</sup>nd it relatively easy to make a one-time change in their diet and physical activity, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in maintaining such changes leads to disappointing weight control results (Lowe 2003). In this regard, previous studies have consistently argued that successful self-regulation is key to maintaining health behaviors in the long term (Kitsantas 2000, Forman and Butryn 2015).

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the crucial concept of self-regulation, which plays a central role in determining the willpower and self-control behind effective weight management. Next, we describe a high-level survey of research that looks into the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mobile interventions for weight control. We then outline the conceptual framework for our empirical analysis.

## 2.1. Self-Regulation and Weight Contro

Self-regulation, a well-known concept in psychology and human behavior, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual’s abil ity to alter thoughts, desires, and actions for the purpose of achieving certain goals (Baumeister et al. 2007). It has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a “common core” of a broad range of problems, and models of this behavior have been applied in diverse areas, including education, drug treatment, emotional control, and task perform ance (Baumeister and Heatherton 1996, p. 1). In our context, self-regulation can be thought of as synony mous to the familiar notion of self-control, which we understand to be the combination of motivation and willpower that allows individuals to resist temptations and stay on track in working toward targeted goals. The social cognitive theory of self-regulation characterizes it in terms of three principal subfunctions: self-monitoring, judgment, and self-reaction (Bandura 1986).

The importance of self-monitoring lies in the fact that people cannot motivate themselves if they cannot <sup>fi</sup>rst discern their own actions and performance as well as the conditions under which they occur. Thus, selfmonitoring provides information essential to activate the other subfunctions of self-regulation. The judgmental subfunction refers to alignment between one’s behavior and personal standards, which are developed through a combination of personal experience and social referential comparisons (Bandura 1991). Individuals experience self-satisfaction when they meet or exceed their personal standards and self-censure when they fall short of their expectations. Finally, the selfreaction subfunction of self-regulation is the selfmotivation mechanism by which personal standards dictate courses of action. In the weight management context, examples of self-reaction include the indulgence of treating oneself to dessert when diet restrictions have been successful over a given period of time, or taking a day off from strenuous exercise when physical activity goals have been realized. Such selfreactions are central to motivation and purposeful action in order to achieve predetermined goals.

In modern weight management programs, the three subfunctions of self-regulation can be supported by a mobile app. For example, the focal app used in this study enables users to upload records, at any time, of various activities, including weight (i.e., weight measured right after waking and right before turning in for the night), diet, and exercise. In addition, the app also provides up-to-date feedback for customers on the basis of user logs, personal standards, and social benchmarks. Users can check their current status and identify the stage of weight control in which they are currently involved. On the main screen of the app, users can view their initial, current, and target weights, as well as the percentage of target weight loss that they have achieved. The app also helps users constantly monitor their daily logs for diet and exercise and identify how well they have adhered to their prescriptions. Thus, the self-regulation of weight management is directly enabled by the use of the mobile app.

Compared with of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, a mobile app’s superior ubiquity and ease of use lead to its higher temporal proximity, that is, smaller time gap between each self-relegation exercise (Bandura 1991), which is critical for successful self-regulation. However, of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits are inherently intermittent because of the higher physical, transportation, and monetary costs incurred in these channels. Moreover, the personal and private nature of smartphones renders them more than simply mobile channels, but pacifying devices for most users.<sup>3</sup> They correspondingly serve as a source of psychological comfort for owners and thereby provide relief from stress (Melumad and Pham 2020). Such emotional attachments to smartphones are more prominent in our context given the sensitive nature of weight loss, thereby further reinforcing the high temporal proximity of mobile channels. As a result, this high temporal proximity makes mobile pathways, rather than of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, pri mary avenues for self-regulation.

As shown in Figure 1, although self-regulation enabled by a mobile app is the primary determinant of the achievement of desired target weights, external in<sup>fl</sup>uence can play an important supporting function as well (Clark et al. 2001). In this respect, customers acquire more direct (i.e., without an intervening medium) help from human experts through of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels. Individual customers determine their own weight management strategies, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, what combination of mobile app usage and of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits works best for them.

## 2.2. Mobile Interventions for Weight Control

A large number of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been conducted to examine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mobile interventions in aiding personal weight management. The earliest studies predated smartphones and examined the impact of the SMS (Short Message Service) feature on phones and personal digital assistants, <sup>fi</sup>nding bene<sup>fi</sup>cial impacts of technology-based interventions (e.g., Free et al. 2013). More recent studies have looked at smartphone apps related to weight control. Carter et al. (2013) used the RCT approach to examine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of My Meal Mate, which enables self-monitoring of diet, provides feedback, and delivers weekly progress via text messaging. They found that adherence to a weight loss regimen was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher for the app-using group as compared with the control group not using the app. Rabbi et al. (2015) used an RCT to examine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of MyBehavior, which is an app designed to track physical activity and eating behavior data in conjunction with personalized suggestions. Compared with the control group, subjects in the treatment group (i.e., app users) walked signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more frequently, but the dif ference in calorie consumption was not statistically sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant. A postexperiment survey indicated that users valued the personalized suggestions provided by the app. Laing et al. (2014) examined the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the MyFitnessPal app in a clinical setting and found mixed results. The app bene<sup>fi</sup>ted users who were ready to selfmonitor calories, but did not produce positive results for most patients that did not have the same level of motivation. As a result, the difference in weight loss between groups with and without assistance in using the weight loss app was statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant.

Apart from these individual RCTs, a number of other studies conducted a survey or meta-analysis of research that performed RCTs to ascertain the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of mobile interventions. For instance, Bacigalupo et al. (2013)

conducted a survey of 174 studies that used an RCT approach to examine the impact of using mobile technology interventions for weight control. They found strong evidence of a short-term impact on weight loss and moderate evidence for medium-term effects. Khokhar et al. (2014) carried out a meta-analysis of six studies that focused on the effects of mobile device interventions and found that the use of a mobile phone was positively associated with weight loss. In addition, Mateo et al. (2015) conducted a systematic review of 12 studies and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that use of a mobile app led to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant weight loss, although its impact on physical activity was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant.

Although most of the studies reviewed above focused on mobile apps designed around self-regulation of the weight control process and offered useful insights into their impact on weight loss outcomes, our understanding of the relative roles of mobile apps and of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits in weight management is limited. Moreover, this lack of understanding is more pronounced particularly regarding the different roles of the two channels in weight management depending on how and under what conditions mobile apps are used and over what period. The present research aims to expand the literature to those areas.

## 2.3. Conceptual Framework

Our analysis is guided by social cognitive theory and the roles of self-regulation and external in<sup>fl</sup>uence in personal weight management. Figure 2 depicts the conceptual framework for our study, wherein mobile channel usage is a proxy for self-regulation, and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel usage represents the extent of external in<sup>fl</sup>uence. We examine the direct and interactive effects of mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel usage on both short-term weight loss and the sustainability of the weight loss in the long term. We control for time-varying covariates (e.g., calorie intake and exercise) as well as time-invariant individual characteristics (e.g., age, gender, height, mental diseases, and eating disorders).

## 3. Data

## 3.1. Context

We use a unique panel data set obtained from HealthCo (pseudonym), one of the largest healthcare companies in Korea, with approximately 3,000 customers per year.

HealthCo provides a systematic and integrated weight loss program to its customers through its more than 20 of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores and mobile channels. To avail themselves of the full service, customers must <sup>fi</sup>rst visit a physical store and create a contract. At the time of contract establishment, a customer decides on how many weeks (at least four weeks) she will use the service and pays the corresponding fees in one payment. After the customer signs the contract, store employees evaluate her body and mental conditions (e.g., weight, body composition, presence of mental diseases) using electronic equipment and surveys. The surveys cover eight mental diseases that are widely considered important factors in weight management: carbohydrate addiction, exercise addiction, diet addiction, anorexia, bulimia, depression, stress, and appearance complex. Each of the eight surveys contains 10 questions that are rated on a one-to-<sup>fi</sup>ve scale. Based on each user’s health conditions, HealthCo generates tailored prescriptions for diet and exercise.

HealthCo offers a consultation service at its of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores, which customers can visit up to three times a week. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, customers can receive professional feedback, advice, guidance, and emotional relief (i.e., external in<sup>fl</sup>uence) from coaches at of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores. In addition to this major consultation service, the <sup>fi</sup>rm provides two additional services at of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores that are designed to exert a <sup>fi</sup>rst-order effect on weight loss by directly increasing energy expenditure. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is a personal weight training service (PT), under which customers can exercise with a personal trainer at a gym. The second is an electronic device-aided weight loss service (ES), wherein customers can use several electronic devices designed to stimulate muscles, reduce cellulite, burn fats, and promote lymphatic circulation, thereby burning calories. Once customers visit an offline branch, they <sup>fi</sup>rst have a consultation with coaches. After <sup>fi</sup>nishing the consultation, they can decide whether to use PT or ES. After receiving these treatments, customers measure their of<sup>fi</sup>cial weight by using the same scale provided at the company’s stores. In addition to these of<sup>fl</sup>ine services, a mobile app is offered by the company.<sup>4</sup> The basic structure and functions of the app are similar to popular weight loss apps as described earlier in Section 2.1. The <sup>fi</sup>rm also provides a PC-based website with similar functionalities.

## 3.2. Variable Description

We have a number of time-varying covariates at the individual level, such as calorie intake, amount of exercise, mobile app usage, and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel usage. We also have time-invariant variables that account for individual-level demographics and health conditions. We start by operationalizing time periods, wherein period t is taken to be the time interval between visits t 1 and t to an of<sup>fl</sup>ine store. Thus, $t = 2$ represents the time frame that spans the end of the <sup>fi</sup>rst visit to the end of the second visit. Given the time period, outcome variable $W e i g h t _ { i , t }$ represents customer i’s weight measured at the tth visit to an of<sup>fl</sup>ine store, and time-varying covariates indicate each customer’s behavior between her (t 1)th and tth visits to of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores (i.e., in period t), which are normalized by the interval between the two visits.

This speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of time periods, book-ended by of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits, allows us to utilize weight outcomes measured through a consistent and standardized manner in the of<sup>fi</sup>ces rather than relying on noisy and idiosyncratic self-reported weight measurements. In this regard, note that weight measurement usually varies by more than hundreds of grams depending on diverse factors, including the speci<sup>fi</sup>c scale that is used and whether clothes are worn at the time of measurement. In consideration of the unique context where overweight and obese customers <sup>fi</sup>nd it arduous to lose even hundreds of grams in a couple of days,<sup>5</sup> such a variation would confound the tracking of weight control outcomes, leading to misleading implications. Because the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm measures users weight using identical professional scales at the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s physical stores under the same conditions (e.g., physica and physiological), we could minimize the measurement error related to weight, which is the key outcome variable in weight management, compared with self-reported weight measures commonly used in the literature. Thus, our speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of time period t and weight management outcome $W e i g h t _ { i , t }$ based on of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits provides the most reliable outcome measure.

## 4. Empirical Model

We start with an analysis of the impact of mobile and offline channel usage on weight loss, while accounting for different treatment effects. We specify a dynamic model where the weight in period t is related to the weight in period t <sub>−</sub> 1, while controlling for time-varying covariates, as well as time-invariant individual heterogeneity:

$$
W e i g h t _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta W e i g h t _ {i, t - 1} + X _ {i, t} ^ {\prime} \pmb {\gamma} + W _ {i} ^ {\prime} \pmb {\rho} + \mu_ {i} + \lambda_ {t} + u _ {i, t},\tag{3}
$$

where $X _ { i , t } ^ { \prime }$ and $\mathbf { } W _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ are as de<sup>fi</sup>ned above, $\mu _ { i }$ is a user-<sup>X W</sup>speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>xed effect, $\lambda _ { t }$ is a period-speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>xed effect, and $u _ { i , t }$ is the remainder disturbance. On the face of ${ \mathrm { i t } } ,$ the model may raise several speci<sup>fi</sup>cation issues because of the presence of lagged dependent variables, potential correlation between model variables and unobservable factors, and a potential serial correlation among the disturbances. Of particular concern is the potential endogeneity of the channel usage variables MobileUsageIntensity and OfflineUsageIntensi $\mathcal { Y } _ { i , t }$ . As we discussed earlier, the combination of the mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels used by an individual depends on her self-regulation, which in turn is captured by the unobserved individual error term $\mu _ { i } .$ . In other words, the channel usage variables are likely to be correlated with $\mu _ { i } ,$ which is the basis for the endogeneity concern. Fortunately, our estimation approach is effective at dealing with this and other speci<sup>fi</sup>cation issues, as we discuss next.

We adopt Blundell and Bond’s (1998) system GMM estimator, which utilizes lagged levels of endogenous variables as instrumental variables for the model in <sup>fi</sup>rst differences in conjunction with lagged differences of endogenous variables as instrumental variables for the model in levels. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use $\Delta X _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { \prime }$ and $\Delta W e i g h t _ { i , t - 2 } ^ { \prime }$ as instruments for the model in levels, and $\bar { X } _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { \prime } , \ . . . , X _ { i , t - k } ^ { \prime }$ and $W e i g h t _ { i , t - 2 } ^ { \prime } , \ldots , W e i g h t _ { i , t - k } ^ { \prime }$ as <sup>X X</sup>instruments for the model in <sup>fi</sup>rst differences. Note that <sup>fi</sup>rst differencing would eliminate $\mu _ { i } ,$ the component of the error term corresponding to unobserved individual heterogeneity. In addition, the observed heterogeneity variables $\mathbf { } W _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ and period t are strictly exogenous, allowing us to include them directly in the set of instruments for the model in levels. We performed a series of tests, including the Hansen test for the overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation of instruments, the differencein-Hansen test for instrument exogeneity, and autoregressive AR(1) and AR(2) tests for the autocorrelation of the residuals, and determined that our instruments satisfy the necessary conditions to adopt the system GMM estimator. For an easier interpretation of our results, we transform the dependent variable from weight into weight loss as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & W e i g h t L o s s _ {i, t} = W e i g h t _ {i, t - 1} - W e i g h t _ {i, t} \\ & \qquad = - \alpha + (1 - \beta) W e i g h t _ {i, t - 1} - X _ {i, t} ^ {\prime} \pmb {\gamma} \\ & \qquad \qquad - W _ {i} ^ {\prime} \pmb {\rho} - \mu_ {i} - \lambda_ {t} - u _ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

## 5.2. Sustainability of Weight Management

In the previous analyses, we found that mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels substitute for one another as mechanisms for effecting short-term weight loss (i.e., in each period). However, mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels have different impacts on long-term weight management, where the role of their trade-off becomes more apparent. Besides short-term weight loss, another important, longer term criterion for successful weight management is the sustainability or consistency of weight loss for the entir service period, as opposed to a potential yoyo effect where weight lost is quickly regained (Kitsantas 2000, Wing et al. 2006). With this consideration in mind, we generated three variables to measure each user’s inconsistency in weigh loss for the entire service period. First, Clumpiness measures how evenly successful periods of weight loss are dis tributed across different periods. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned a successful period of weight loss as an event and meas ured its clumpiness accordingly (see Zhang et al. 2015, equation 1). Second, PercentFailure measures how often users fail to lose weight during their contract period. It is computed as the proportion of the number of failed peri ods to the total number of periods. Third, SDWeightLos measures the variation (i.e., the standard deviation) of weight loss amount in each period. Thus, a higher value of each variable represents more inconsistent weight loss. Given that all the three variables are at the individual level (each customer has one value for each variable), all the input variables were averaged out for each user. We then regressed each of the three inconsistency variables on the averaged variables.

As can be seen in the <sup>fi</sup>rst three columns of Table 3, mobile app usage is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and negatively related to all three measures of longer-term weight loss inconsistency, whereas the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel usage are consistently insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. The results imply that whereas mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels are both effective in increasing weight loss in each short-term period, mobile channels are especially important for improving the consistency in weight loss for sustained long-term periods. This result could be attributed to the fact that mobile channels’ unique features (i.e., ubiquity, ease of use, and emotional attachments) allow users to manage their selfregulation more effectively (e.g., by conducting continuous self-monitoring, judgment, and self-reaction). However, of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels can be accessed only infrequently, which poses a challenge for consistent self-regulation, especially in between of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits. In other words, this result highlights the critical role of the higher temporal proximity of mobile apps in self-regulation compared with the external in<sup>fl</sup>uence from human experts at of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, particularly in the longer term.

Given that we focused on the comparative effects of mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels on the two different weight management outcomes (i.e., the short-term weight loss amount and longer-term weight loss consistency), we carried out further analyses to identify whether such outcomes serve as a precursor to the achievement of ultimate goals of weight management, that is, weight management success. Note that at the time of contract, users consult with a coach to determine their <sup>fi</sup>nal weight loss goals on the basis of their current weight, contract periods, individual characteristics, and social benchmarks. Accordingly, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the variable WMSuccess as a dummy variable of whether the weight measured at user i’s <sup>fi</sup>nal visit (at the end of her contract period) is smaller than the target weight determined at the time of contract. We then replicated the previous analyses using a logistic regression. As can be seen in the last column of Table 3, the probability of achieving <sup>fi</sup>nal weight loss goals (i.e., successful weight management) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positively related only to mobile app usage, and the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel usage is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. This again highlights the critical role of mobile channels partic ularly in longer-term weight management in which the disparity in temporal proximity between mobile and offline channels becomes more evident.

To summarize, the results collectively suggest that whereas mobile channels can substitute for of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels for the purpose of short-term weight loss, it is only mobile channel usage that is associated with consistency of weight loss in the longer term. This demonstrates the critical role of the mobile channel, relative to of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, especially for the sustainability of weight management.

## 5.3. Deeper Insight into the Role of Mobile App Usage

This section recounts our deep dive into the role of mobile app usage in weight loss. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate (1) the link between how the app in question is used and weight loss and (2) to whom (or under what conditions) app usage is more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. With regard to the <sup>fi</sup>rst issue, we focus on two factors, namely, how frequently and granularly users record their behaviors using the app. Mobile channels are distinguished from their traditional counterparts by their ubiquity and ease of use as well as individuals’ emotional attachments to them. The ubiquity of mobile apps enables individuals to log their daily activities at any time and place immediately after an event occurs. The ease with which these innovations are used simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the typically vexing process of noting down everyday tasks to only a few steps. Moreover, the personal and private nature of mobile devices and individuals’ emotional attachment to them, together with the sensitive nature of weight loss, would motivate users more willing to record information on weight management in an app than share this information with coaches in of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores. As a result, individuals recording behaviors using the app can be more frequent and more granular than data collection in offline channels. For example, users can frequently upload daily records of diverse activities via the focal app, including those involving weight; exercise; the consumption of meals, snacks, water, and other beverages; alcohol consumption; sleep; and pill intake. Such consistency and breadth help individuals more comprehensively understand their weight loss progress (more effective and temporally proximate self-monitoring), thereby facilitating improved judgment and self-reaction and ultimately aiding weight loss.

To provide more comprehensive insight into individual heterogeneities in the role of mobile app usage, we investigate how the different performance pressure levels encountered by individuals (Gardner 2012) <sup>fi</sup>gure into their weight loss progress. This assessment aligns with the literature on the relationship between performance pressure and self-regulation (e.g., Mitchell et al. 2019).

Previous studies illustrate that for individuals with low (high) trait resilience, such as obese people who are more likely to have lower susceptibility to stress (van der Valk et al. 2018), performance pressure tends to be regarded as a threat (challenge), thus reducing (increasing) self-generated resources associated with self-regulation (Baumeister 2002).

Against this backdrop, we concentrate on two determinants of performance pressure as follows. To begin with, we expect customers who aim to lose more weight per day and have a shorter period to achieve their weight loss goals experience a higher degree of performance pressure. Accordingly, we calculated (1) each customer’s contracted weight loss goal per day (in kilograms/day) and (2) the length of his or her contract period (in days). The results suggest that mobile app usage is more effective for customers with low performance pressure or those with modest daily goals and a long time limit within which to realize their targets. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that customers who experience low performance pressure develop and practice self-regulation more effectively by using the mobile app, which is well aligned with the theoretical expectation for individuals with low trait resilience. The additional analyses regarding app usage patterns and conditional factors lend consistent support for the selfregulation mechanism of mobile app usage.

## 5.4. Robustness Checks

Although the Blundell and Bond (1998) system GMM approach that we used is designed to effectively deal with endogeneity of all input variables, our analysis might be subject to other speci<sup>fi</sup>cation issues, which we address next. The <sup>fi</sup>rst issue has to do with the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of our time periods, which is taken to be the interval between successive of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits. One might wonder whether the variable lengths of these periods might cause a problem with estimation, even though we normalized the input variables by the of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visit frequency. As a robustness check, we consider an alternative de<sup>fi</sup>nition of time period, which has a constant interval. To this end, we replicated our main analysis by de<sup>fi</sup>ning time period t as a seven-day period.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, t 1 represents the time frame that spans the <sup>fi</sup>rst day to the seventh day of service usage, and so on. The variable $W e i g h t _ { i , t }$ is calculated as the average weight measured at of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores in each week, CalorieIntake represents the average daily calorie intake in each week, and ExerciseAmount indicates the average daily exercise time in each week. The terms MobileUsageIntensity<sub>i,t</sub> and PCUsageIntensity<sub>i,t</sub> indicate the ratios of the number of days during which mobile and PC channels, respectively, are used for user i in week t to the total number of days in week t (i.e., seven days). Variable OfflineUsageIntensity<sub>i,t</sub> represents user i’s number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits in week t. Customers who did not visit of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores in at least one period (i.e., one week) are excluded from the estimation. We found the results to be qualitatively consistent with the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Next, we looked into whether alternative measures of the input and output variables would affect the results. First, to address concerns about the truncated channel usage variables, we standardized Mobile UsageIntensity and OfflineUsageIntensity and replicated the main analyses. Second, we replicated the main analyses with weight loss replaced with percentage weight loss, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as $( W e i g h t _ { i , t - 1 } -$ $W e i g h t _ { i , t } ) / W e i g h t _ { i , t - 1 }$ . Replacing these input and output variables caused no change to the qualitative nature of the results. In addition, we considered a potential interaction effect between exercise and diet. As a part of the self-reaction function, users may reward themselves with overeating when they have surpassed their exercise goal, and vice versa. To see whether diet drives exercise in this way, or vice versa, each of these variables in period t (i.e., ExerciseAmount and CalorieIntake ) is modeled as a function of the other variable in period t, period $t -$ 1, or both. Using a <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects estimator, we found that calorie intake has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on exercise. Neither did we <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect in the other direction.

## 6. Qualitative Corroboration of the Self-Regulation Mechanism

Although the empirical analyses illustrate a positive relationship between mobile app usage and weight loss, and its self-regulation mechanism was supported by the additional analyses, they provided no direct evidence of a self-regulation mechanism at play. To address this issue, we carried out a series of semistructured interviews with actual paying customers of the focal service (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007, Greenwood et al. 2019). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we reached out to 40 former customers, half of whom were chosen by HealthCo, with the other half selected by us. In the end, 10 customers agreed to participate in interviews. All the interviewees are female and in their 30s or 40s, and this group is highly representative of the actual paying customers at HealthCo. The interviews entailed multiple open-ended questions about how the respondents use the mobile app and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels of the company, as well as their perceptions of whether and why each channel may aid weight loss.<sup>7</sup> We interviewed these customers via email, given considerable privacy concerns among them—to be expected in the sensitive weight loss context.

With respect to mobile app usage, all the interviewees stated that they basically use the app to record daily activities related to weight loss. The following are illustrative responses:

“During the service period, I recorded almost every thing using the app. I uploaded information on my morning weight, night weight, meals, snacks, water consumption, pills, exercise, and even bedtime and wake-up time.”

“I used the app every day to record my morning weight, night weight, water intake, time and amount of meals and snacks, and exercise level, etc. I did this as soon as I got up until I went to sleep.”

All the interviewees also highlighted the importance of the mobile app’s ubiquity and ease of use as a tool for frequent and granular daily recording behaviors (i.e., high temporal proximity):

“While I could write down [my daily activities related to weight loss] in a small notebook that I received from a [public] clinic, even carrying that small notebook was burdensome. Using the app provides greater accessibility since I always carry my phone.”

“The app is very easy to use. I can upload all my daily activities within a few taps.”

In connection to how app usage advances weight loss, we argue that the frequent and granular daily recording enabled by the app helps customers monitor and track their progress toward weight management objectives (i.e., self-monitoring). This self-monitoring enables individuals to self-diagnose their behavioral patterns (i.e., judgment), conduct personal experimentation, and take corrective action when called for (i.e., self-reaction) (Bandura 1991). This self-regulation mechanism underlying the positive role of mobile app usage was corroborated by the interviewees:

“As my own data [on daily weight, diet, exercise, etc.] were accumulated day by day, and thus, I could see my weight loss pace on a graph [on the main screen of the app], I could figure out by myself that I had slow weight loss progress in a period when I had low willpower, and that I had significant progress when I didn’t snack but adhere to a strict diet.”

“Frequent app usage made me pay more attention to my weight, diet, and exercise records and their relationships. I often reviewed and analyzed my own data to achieve better [weight loss] performance.”

“Previously, I usually tried to starve myself without thinking too much. Since I didn’t know exactly what made me lose weight, I couldn’t maintain weight loss and got burned out early. After using the app, I could see when and in what conditions I lost weight, which improved my willpower.”

“Because I wanted to sustain my weight loss without help from coaches after the service period, I tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd my own way of losing weight. My own data [on daily weight, diet, exercise, etc.] were helpful in this regard. When my weight loss progress was slow, I checked what I ate in recent days and what was different from when I had made signi<sup>fi</sup>cant progress. I could infer the important indicators of my weight loss progress.”

Moreover, a few of the interviewees provided narratives of how the lack of willpower to continue using the app led to short-term failures in weight loss, further corroborating the self-regulation mechanism of the positive app effect:

“The downside was that there was no one who kept watching me and making me use the app. It was easy to lie and give up using the app when I didn’t have a strong motivation, or when I am bored or tired.”

“Basically, daily logging [through the app] is a thing that should be done on my own. So it became less frequent over time, and my self-control over my diet, exercise, and sleep became loose.”

This direct narrative evidence provides additional qualitative support for the self-regulation mechanism behind the positive role of mobile app usage: (1) the ubiquity and ease of use offered by the app allow individuals to record their behaviors in a more frequent and granular manner, and (2) such app usage behaviors improve individuals’ self-regulation (i.e., self-monitoring, judgment, self-reaction), which in turn helps them reach their weight loss goals.

Given that customers use the app to develop and practice self-regulation for weight loss, the interview results also provide direct narrative evidence of the substitution between mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, all the respondents indicated that they visit of<sup>fl</sup>ine branches to consult with coaches and remotivate themselves when they encounter dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in adequately policing themselves using the app. Put differently, when customers <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (easy) to exercise suf<sup>fi</sup>cient self-regulation using the app, external in<sup>fl</sup>uences at of<sup>fl</sup>ine stores are more (less) helpful. This phenomenon explains the substitution effect between mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels:

“The app enables 24-hour self [weight] management. I can easily figure out a reason for occasional weight gains because there are exact records of what I did [on the app]. But, since [app usage is] voluntary, when I don’t have strong motivation or am feeling lazy and tired, I tend not to use it. Coaches at offline stores are helpful in this regard.”

“Self-management using the app can easily become negligent. But coaches keep me on my toes. They help me endure and get past a weight loss plateau by providing professional feedback, consolation, and motivation.”

“Basically, the app and coaches do the same job. Between of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits, I would easily be lazy and would often want to have cheat days. But I could stay the course by recording my daily activities via the app. Once such self-management using the app becomes dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, I visit a branch and consult with coaches, which makes me stay focused, even on weekends.”

Despite the positive role of physical facilities, however, most of the interviewees (eight customers) declared that the low accessibility of of<sup>fl</sup>ine branches and the resulting high transportation and time costs (i.e., low temporal proximity) constrain the effectiveness of these locations in the long term. One interviewee noted,

“But downsides are the hassle of going to stores, round-trip transportation time, and service time, which together take up to two to three hours. One more thing is that sometimes, I cannot make a reservation at a desired time.”

Taken together, the qualitative evidence from actual customers helps us ground the work in reality and provides relevant insights on how mobile apps and of<sup>fl</sup>ine branches, in concert, help individuals lose weight.

## 7. Conclusions

This study adds to the health information technology (IT) literature (Agarwal et al. 2010), and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to the emerging stream on mHeatlh applications (Rowland et al. 2020, Ghose et al. 2022), as well as to the omnichannel information systems (IS) literature (Wang et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2020). Drawing on the social cog nitive theory of self-regulation, we examined the relative roles of mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels on both shortterm and longer-term weight management on the basis of novel panel data on users enrolled in a multichannel weight loss program. In the short term, we found that mobile app usage and of<sup>fl</sup>ine store visits are both positively associated with weight loss; the two modes serve as substitutes for one another. However, the mobile app allows users to more effectively preserve sustainability and consistency in weight management over time, as compared with of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits. The results highlight the greater impact of temporal proximity of self-regulation enabled by the mobile app, as compared with external in<sup>fl</sup>uence from human experts in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel. The additional analyses corroborate the self-regulation mechanism enabled by mobile app usage. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, more frequent and granular recording behavior (i.e., higher temporal proximity), shaped by mobile channels unique ubiquity, ease of use, and emotional attachments, helps individuals exercise self-regulation and make weight loss progress. Moreover, the positive role of mobile apps is more pronounced for individuals under lower performance pressure. The proposed self-regulation mechanism is also substantiated by qualitative interviews with actual customers. The results are also generalizable to free users and robust to potential endogeneity concerns as well as variable and model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

The speci<sup>fi</sup>c contributions of this study to mHealth research and practice are fourfold. First, numerous studies have looked into the economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from mobile apps as sales mediators (e.g., Ghose et al. 2019), but little is known about how these mobile innovations promote behavioral changes and improve individuals’ ability to self-regulate their habits and health outcomes (e.g., Kyung and Kwon 2022); we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll that void. Second, although a growing stream of research has been conducted on a wide range of medical and administrative technological innovations for healthcare professionals and hospitals (e.g., Wang et al. 2020), little attention has been devoted to IT artifacts that are designed exclusively for individual patients. The current study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to empirically investigate the effects of IT artifacts (i.e., mobile apps) on the success of individual initiatives for health and wellness while considering their relationships with of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels and individual heterogeneity. Healthcare and weight management are ripe for disruption through mobile platforms, and this study provides initial insights into the prospect of such a radical transformation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we paid particular attention to whether mobile usage improves weight control (i.e., improves both short-term weight loss amount and longerterm weight loss consistency, as well as achievement of target weight), who bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., those who are not able to use of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels frequently and are under lower weight loss pressure) and how to bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from such mobile health apps (i.e., use mobile apps to record daily activities in a more frequent and granular manner), and under what circumstances the use of mobile apps becomes more critical (i.e., as weight management is prolonged).

Third, our comprehensive and novel data allowed us to include the key determinants of successful weight management with minimal measurement errors into our analysis, providing a less-biased understanding of the role of mobile apps in weight management.

Although several studies have endeavored to examine the effectiveness of mobile channels for weight loss, because of their limited capability to collect reliable data to control for important determinants of weight management (e.g., calorie intake, exercise amount, individual characteristics), they found mixed evidence, ranging from a positive (e.g., Allen et al. 2013, Partridge et al. 2015) to an insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant (e.g., Brindal et al. 2013, Laing et al. 2014) impact of mobile weight loss apps.

Last but not least, our study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate the interplay between mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels for weight management and the underlying self-regulation mechanisms based on mobile channels high temporal proximity driven by their ubiquity, ease of use, and emotional attachments, contributing to the omnichannel literature of IS (Son et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2020). Although most people who require considerable weight loss are aware of the importance of dietary control, regular exercise, and of<sup>fl</sup>ine interventions, they minimally comprehend how behavioral mobile self-regulation treatments work in tandem to determine the success of weight management efforts. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the uniqueness of our context and data set, where the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm provides both mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels for customers, enabled us to uncover the relationships between mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels with respect to their distinct roles in shortterm and longer-term weight loss management.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings lay bare ways that mobile apps can be made even more effective. App designers should devise additional features that facilitate the impro vement of the self-regulation subfunctions as well as their temporal proximity. The latter is especially important for longer-term weight management. Adding a social networking capability would also improve mobile peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence on social support and the setting of personal standards (e.g., Yan 2018, Uetake and Yang 2020)—a key aspect of self-regulation. Perhaps providers should consider gamifying the use of mobile apps (i.e., offering points, badges, and other monetary or nonmonetary rewards) to enhance the appeal that self-regulation subfunctions offer to users. These additional features would also encourage users to perform self-regulation subfunctions more frequently and granularly (i.e., higher temporal proximity), and thus improve longer-term outcomes of weight management.

In the policy arena, as comparable effects of mobile apps and drugs on health problems (e.g., insomnia and diabetes) have been recently ascertained, digital therapeutics, or “digiceuticals,” have established a reputation as the third phase of medicine without the high costs and side effects associated with traditional treatments (Farr 2017). According to our results, the mobile health app used in our study can fall under both categories of digital therapeutics: augmentation and replacement of medication (i.e., mobile app usage complements and substitutes of<sup>fl</sup>ine treatments in the long term and short term, respectively). In this regard, users should be educated about the value of mobile apps as self-regulation mechanisms for monitoring and tracking progression toward the achievement of weight management objectives. Given our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, this would be especially bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for people who cannot frequently access of<sup>fl</sup>ine-based weight loss programs and those under low weight loss pressure.

At the same time, however, the value of mobile apps should not be underestimated for individuals who have no trouble availing themselves of of<sup>fl</sup>ine interventions. Seeing as only mobile apps are positively related to longer-term outcomes of weight management (i.e., weight loss consistency and achievement of target weight), of<sup>fl</sup>ine-only weight management initiatives would be associated with high vulnerability in longterm weight loss. In addition, it is worth noting that the applicability of our results on the positive role of mobile-driven weight control goes beyond paid weight management subscribers, which constitute a small fraction of mobile app users. Our analysis of mobile ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy suggests a broader relevance that encompasses free users—a meaningful coverage if we consider the volume of individuals who favor complementary services. For instance, Noom has 50 million users, most of whom use the free tier of the popular weight loss app’s freemium services (Melton 2020). Our analysis of mobile ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy for free users suggests a broader applicability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the positive role of mobile app usage in weight control.

The additional <sup>fi</sup>ndings obtained from the interviews, together with our empirical results, also offer fruitful practical implications for weight loss program providers regarding their multichannel freemium strategies. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the results illustrate that paid of<sup>fl</sup>ine services will be more (less) effective for individuals who <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (easy) to develop and practice self-regulation using a mobile app. Thus, on the one hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms should identify and target free (mobile-only) users whose mobile usage suddenly drops and offer paid of<sup>fl</sup>ine services to them because they can bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from of<sup>fl</sup>ine services. On the other hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms should keep track and take extra care of paid customers whose mobile usage is consistently high. This is because such customers would receive minimal help from of<sup>fl</sup>ine services and thus are more likely to stop using such paid services.

This study is not without limitations. We used unique data on users’ daily records of diet and exercise, but such data were self-reported by the users and likely re<sup>fl</sup>ect subjective and inaccurate perceptions instead of actual behaviors (Fowler 1992). Nonetheless, we believe our research design suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently compensates for this limitation, as we employed both mobile and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels to collect such information (Burke et al. 2011). Another caveat is related to the composition of our sample; that is, our sample’s commitment to weight loss is likely to be higher than that of the public. In addition, a portion of our sample might terminate their membership because of concerns related to high cost or limited weight loss progress. Our anecdotal evidence suggests that an average commitment level of our sample was not too high to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly bias the results, and that truncation of the sample did not occur during the data period. However, such potential selection and truncation issues could hardly be fully addressed in our secondary data context, and thus result in an upward bias of the estimated mobile app impact. We acknowledge this issue as one of the limitations of this paper.

Despite these limitations, our hope is that this research lays a foundation for additional inquiries into the effects of mobile innovations and other digital artifacts on self regulation of positive health and wellness behaviors.
