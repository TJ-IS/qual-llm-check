# Should Doctors Open Online Consultation Services? An Empirical Investigation of Their Impact on Offline Appointments

Abstract. Online healthcare portals have become prevalent worldwide in recent years. One common form of a healthcare portal is the online consultation website, which provides a bridge between patients and doctors and reduces patients’ time and cost when seeking healthcare services. Another form is the healthcare service appointment website, which facilitates of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits for patients. Although nominally separate, the behaviors of the users (including patients and doctors) on these two types of websites could be related to each other. In particular, how does opening online consultation services impact the of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments of doctors? Although this is an important question for healthcare portals, doctors, and policy makers, it has not been rigorously examined in the literature. In this paper, we attempt to bridge this important gap by examining the overall impact of offering online consultations on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Our results show that the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments for doctors increases after opening an online consultation service. Additionally, we examine how several factors, such as recommendation heat, hospital ranking, and doctor title, moderate the impact of opening online consultation services on doctors’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Given that online consultation is a new but important way to connect patients and doctors, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide useful implications for all the stakeholders— doctors, patients, hospitals, and policy makers—regarding how to integrate online and offline channels in the healthcare context.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid development of Web 2.0 technology, online marketing channels have become a common way for <sup>fi</sup>rms to provide services to customers. It is estimated that online retail sales worldwide will increase to \$5 trillion in 2022 and \$6 trillion in 2024 (eMarketer 2021). Similarly, in the United States, the total e-commerce sales of the retail industry reached \$861.12 billion in 2020, an increase of 44.0% from the previous year (Ali 2021). According to a recent article, “e-commerce for nontraditional items (think cars and furniture) have a good chance of gaining more traction in the U.S. in a post-COVID-19 world” (Lee 2020). Online marketing channels are becoming increasingly domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c and niche. At the same time, scholars have become more interested in the relationship between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels from different perspectives (Kumar et al. 2019, Shi et al. 2019, Kumar and Qiu 2022).

Likewise, online portals play an important role in healthcare to broaden and diversify the available channels to serve patients (Liu et al. 2019, Nikou et al. 2020). For example, it is estimated that England will enable more patients to be treated remotely and electronically by 2023–2024 than right now, thereby reducing onethird of face-to-face visits (Iacobucci 2019). According to a recent survey, about 87.44% of the surveyed people worldwide use the internet to <sup>fi</sup>nd health information (Wong and Cheung 2019), and a 1% increase in free consultation services would help doctors obtain an additional 3.1% of paid consultation services and an 11.4% increase in income (Liu et al. 2018).

The development and popularization of online healthcare websites are bringing reforms and opportunities. Most of them can be categorized as one of two types based on their primary business. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type is online healthcare portals that connect patients and doctors as a bridge for online consultations. In China, Haodf.com (https://www.haodf.com/) is one of the largest healthcare websites of this type. Although Haodf.com provides many services for patients, its primary services are online consultation services (Lu and Wu 2019). The healthcare platform allows patients to ask questions about their illnesses to doctors, who can then answer them via an online channel.

The second type is online appointment booking websites for patients to make face-to-face consultation appointments in hospitals. Healthcare portals of this type are the healthcare service appointment systems that usually connect to the interfaces of hospitals (Kitsios et al. 2019), and patients can make of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments with their doctors from different hospitals. Prior literature has mainly focused on examining these two types of online healthcare websites separately (Hao 2015, Hao and Zhang 2016, Wu and Lu 2017, Zhang et al. 2018, Khurana et al. 2019, Liu et al. 2019), and there is a lack of a broader view on the relationship between the behaviors of patients and doctors on different types of healthcare websites. Different from the previous studies, our paper examines the interaction between these two types of online healthcare websites. In particular, we investigate how opening and providing online consultation services in online healthcare portals affects face-toface consultation appointments made via online healthcare booking websites.

In our paper, we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally study whether patients behavior of choosing doctors to consult of<sup>fl</sup>ine is affected by the performance of doctors’ online consultation service, as well as the moderating factors in the impacting relationship, based on the data from the healthcare website portals Haodf.com and Guahao.com. We believe this topic is of interest to hospital managers, healthcare professionals, and other stakeholders, because it not only answers whether doctors should spend their time on online consultations, but also who can bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from them and to what extent. This leads us to our <sup>fi</sup>rst broad research question: Do doctors who open online consultation services get more of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments?

The answer to this question is not clear. On the one hand, Kumar et al. (2018) show that online management responses positively impact <sup>fi</sup>rms’ performance, and Kumar et al. (2019) <sup>fi</sup>nd that a retailer’s opening of stores leads to an increase in its online purchases.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that in a similar situation with online healthcare portals, there may also be a spillover effect. According to the signaling theory (Spence 1973, Connelly et al. 2011, Khurana et al. 2019), a good doctor can credibly convey quality information about his or her medical services to patients (expertise and responsibility) by providing online consultations. On the other hand, one may argue that a cannibalization effect can exist in the healthcare setting as it occurs in other areas. For example, in the context of online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine interactions in social networks, prior literature shows that online socialization can meet users’ needs, thereby reducing the fun of of<sup>fl</sup>ine socialization (Li et al. 2017, Qiu et al. 2017). Moreover, some studies demonstrate that adding an online store to a traditional store would reduce of<sup>fl</sup>ine sales (Karray and Sigue 2018). Because of these two opposing effects, the overall effect is unclear in the healthcare domain. The answer to this question will help doctors and hospitals manage their online consultation services. In addition, after doctors open online consultation services, they can provide price information for these services.

After opening online consultation services, doctors can provide price information on different types of online consultation services. On the one hand, based on the signaling framework (Connelly et al. 2011, Khurana et al. 2019), the prices of doctors’ online consultations can be a signal of the effectiveness of doctor–patient communication: doctors who effectively communicate with patients in online consultations can charge highe prices. Therefore, higher prices of online consultation services may bring more of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. However, on the other hand, a higher online consultation price often indicates a higher of<sup>fl</sup>ine service price, and higher online consultation prices may bring fewer offline appointments because of the law of demand (Mas-Colell et al. 1995). Therefore, it remains unclear how online consultation prices impact patients’ choices, and we examine the signaling value of online consultation prices among doctors who have opened online consultation services based on the signaling theory.

Furthermore, some important doctor-level, hospitallevel, and region-level characteristics could moderate the main impact of online consultation on the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Therefore, we ask our second broad research question: How do doctor-level, hospital-level, and region-level characteristics moderate the main impact of opening online consultation services on the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments? In our context, whether doctors offer online consultations is a signal for patients of doctors’ willingness to better communicate with patients. In our moderating analysis, we dig deeper into the boundary condition of the main effect: The signaling effect of opening and providing online consultation services could be stronger/weaker under some conditions. In the signaling theory (Spence 1973,

Connelly et al. 2011), the signal strength (the informational value of a signal) depends on whether the signal enables the receiver to reliably distinguish high-ability signalers from low-ability ones. In our analysis, we examine whether the signaling effect of offering online consultations on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments is larger for doctors (i) with higher recommendation heat, (ii) with higher job titles, (iii) from higher-ranked hospitals, (iv) from key departments, and (v) from regions with higher gross domestic product (GDP) per capita. These moderators can increase or decrease the signal strength and allow us to further test the implications of the signaling theory. Moreover, doctors with different titles and from different hospitals and regions may bene<sup>fi</sup>t differently from online consultations, which has important implications for hospital managers and healthcare platform practitioners.

Our research <sup>fi</sup>lls an important research gap by investigating the interaction between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels in the healthcare setting. In particular, we examine how opening and providing online consultation services in online healthcare portals affects face-to-face medical appointments. All the main effects and moderating results can be consistently explained in our signaling framework. Our research also contributes to practice by informing policy makers and healthcare practitioners about the important role of online consultation services on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments and the heterogeneous effect across different doctors, hospitals, and regions.

## 2. Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

The healthcare industry has made great leaps in the past few decades (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2011, Chen et al.

2021a, Qiu et al. 2022); for example, information exchange in healthcare can promote the electronic transmission of healthcare information and alleviate problems caused by the fragmentation of patients’ healthcare information (Yaraghi et al. 2015, Demirezen et al. 2016, Rajapakshe et al. 2020, Janakiraman et al. 2022). Researchers are now paying increased attention to online healthcare portals because they provide a platform for both patients and doctors to exchange information. We review the literature from the following areas that are closely related to our study: (i) online doctor reviews on healthcare websites (Section 2.1), (ii) the impact of reviews on patient selection (Section 2.2), and (iii) the usage of online consultations in healthcare (Section 2.3). In Section 2.4, we develop our hypotheses by applying the signaling theory (Spence 1973, Connelly et al. 2011).

## 2.1. Online Doctor Reviews on Healthcare Websites

On the one hand, users’ online reviews are affected by various factors, for example, emotion and habits (Hong et al. 2016, Huang et al. 2016, Wang et al. 2022b). On the other hand, users’ reviews affect the behavior of others to some degree (Kwark et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2022a). Like users tend to check online reviews before they purchase on e-commerce websites, more and more patients rely on doctor reviews before making of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments with doctors. Nevertheless, Lagu et al. (2010) <sup>fi</sup>nd that most reviews appear to be positive. This may be because most patients like to give good evaluations rather than bad reviews to doctors on healthcare websites (Kadry et al. 2011). Emmert and Meier (2013) study narrative texts about online evaluations of doctors and <sup>fi</sup>nd that 80% are positive reviews in Germany, whereas only 4% and 16% are neutral and negative, respectively. This phenomenon also occurs in China. Hao (2015) investigates Chinese doctor rating websites across almost all healthcare professions and <sup>fi</sup>nds that online reviews are largely positive. Several studies focus mainly on negative comments. For example, Zhang et al. (2018) study the negative online comments, aiming to study patients’ online complaints about doctors and how to improve the doctor–patient relationship.

## 2.2. Impact of Reviews on Patient Selection

A growing number of customers are willing to trust and use online reviews before making purchase decisions (Filieri et al. 2018). Similar effects are observed in the healthcare domain (Li et al. 2019b, Lantzy and Anderson 2020). Prior studies have found a positive correlation between online ratings and doctors’ quality (Gao et al. 2012, Okike et al. 2016, Lu and Rui 2018). Burkle and Keegan (2015) show that the information on healthcare websites plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role for patients seeking doctors. Carbonell and Brand (2018) also emphasize that users’ comments and ratings are of notable value to patients’ selections.

Although the previous studies provide general insights into online consultations, the implementation of these general insights is still in its infancy. The practice calls for an engineering approach based on a moderating analysis: How can a healthcare portal optimize the impact of online consultations and provide useful services to doctors and patients? Essentially, a healthcare portal needs to focus its limited resources on incentivizing the right types of doctors (who have the potential to enjoy a stronger signaling effect) to open online consultation services. The answer to this question does not depend merely on the main impact of online consultations but also on our moderating analysis. Lu and Wu (2019) examine the impact of online physician reviews on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. The empirical design in the study by Lu and Wu (2019) focuses on the endogeneity issues of their main independent variable, online physician reviews. They do not explicitly consider the endogeneity issues of online consultations.

Instead, we focus on the endogeneity issues of online consultations using a combination of matching and regression. Establishing a credible causal impact of online consultations is important in the design of online healthcare portals.

Moreover, the study by Lu and Wu (2019) is more exploratory in nature because the sample size is relatively small, whereas we collect information from 37 hospitals with 28,466 observations. In addition, we also examine how the main effect of online consultations is moderated by recommendation heat, docto job title, hospital ranking, whether a doctor is from a national key department, and per-capita GDP of the doctor’s region. In particular, our moderating analysis informs us of which types of doctors can enjoy a stronger signaling effect from opening online consultation services. These important results cannot be inferred from the results in the prior literature, such as those from Lu and Wu (2019). Another difference between our paper and Lu and Wu’s (2019) is that we incorporate pricing dynamics.

The results in the prior literature indicate that even though people are not sure whether online reviews re<sup>fl</sup>ect the quality of the doctors, these review watchers are affected by online comments when they make their decisions. This <sup>fi</sup>nding still leaves an open question regarding what other factors affect patient choices in addition to online reviews. As such, we may consider whether online consultations (a new way of diagnosis and treatment) affect traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits, which is of great importance to healthcare managers and practitioners. Our study examines whether opening online consultation services positively affects patient selection (measured by the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments). Our research provides valuable theoretical and practical insights for the development of online healthcare portals.

## 2.3. Usage of Online Consultations in Healthcare

Online consultations are a popular method of primary healthcare that have received tremendous support from the Chinese government (Balla 2017). Online consultations may reduce healthcare costs, increase the utilization of healthcare resources, and bring economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to doctors (Li et al. 2019a). The emergence of online consultations has triggered a series of studies, including on what factors can enhance the use of e-consultations (Nijland et al. 2009), the motivation of online health information seeking (Caiata-Zufferey et al. 2010), practical applications of online consultation systems (Casey et al. 2017), and online consultation system law (Zhai 2018).

Researchers have studied the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of online consultations for patients and their impact on patients behavior. For example, Zhang et al. (2018) focus on the negative doctor reviews on Haodf.com to identify potential ways to improve patient satisfaction. Liu et al. (2019) aim to study the impact of online reviews on patients’ consultation behaviors on Haodf.com. Some scholars have found that patients use online consultations mainly because they are a convenient way to seek information (Liu et al. 2017), and patients trust the online healthcare portals (Li et al. 2018). They have also found that health-anxious people are more likely to seek online health information (Tanis et al. 2016). These studies are mainly from the patient’s point of view. From the doctor’s point of view, not only can healthcare professionals receive personal compensation through online communities, but they can also build their relationship networks by answering patients’ questions (Chang et al. 2019, Li et al. 2019a, Yang et al. 2021). As such, doctors who open online consultation services may be more likely to establish strong relationships with patients, and then patients tend to visit those doctors of<sup>fl</sup>ine.

In a similar study, Wu and Lu (2017) examine the impact of offering online consultations on a doctor’s of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits. Their study captures one snapshot of website data, and hence they obtain only the total number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments for each doctor since the opening of her or his account, which does not offer a fair comparison between, say, a doctor who has had an open account for one month and a doctor who has had an open account for one year. In contrast, we collect four different snapshots of data in four consecutive months. Therefore, we can calculate the number of monthly of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments for each doctor by using the data of the current month minus the data of the previous month. Using monthly data, we can account for different starting times of doctors’ online consultation accounts, which is not possible using the one snapshot of data in the study by Wu and Lu (2017).

Bavafa et al. (2018) examine the impact of telemedicine on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. The main contribution of our paper is to establish the impact of online consultations on of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments, which is not discussed by Bavafa et al. (2018). Our contribution is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because we cannot infer the impact of online consultations from the results from Bavafa et al. (2018): online consultations are different from telemedicine in nature. In our study, doctors decide whether to open online consultation services, whereas patients decide whether to adopt telemedicine in the study by Bavafa et al. (2018). In our study, the main purpose of online consultations is to signal doctors’ willingness to communicate with patients, hoping to attract new patients to visit their of<sup>fl</sup>ine of<sup>fi</sup>ces, whereas in the study by Bavafa et al. (2018), the main purpose of telemedicine is to provide existing patients with a new, additional communication channel.

From the prior literature, such as the work of Bavafa et al. (2018), we know online telemedicine complements of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels in healthcare, but we do not know whether online consultations complement or substitute of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels in healthcare. The reason is that (a) online consultations can substitute of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels and reduce the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments because of the cannibalization effect (patients get answers from online consultations and do not need to visit doctors off line) or (b) online consultations may increase the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments because of the signaling effect discussed in our paper (online consultations are signals of whether doctors care about patients). Both directions are theoretically plausible, which presents a viable opportunity for empirical testing.

## 2.4. Hypothesis Development

In this section, we apply the signaling theory (Spence 1973, Connelly et al. 2011, Khurana et al. 2019) to develop our hypotheses. A signal sender can credibly convey some information about itself to a signal receiver by sending signals. In our context, a good doctor can credibly convey quality information about his or her medical services to patients (on his or her expertise and responsibility) by providing online consultations. There are two preconditions for the application of signaling theory: The <sup>fi</sup>rst is information asymmetry between a signaler and receiver. In our context, doctors know more than patients. The second is the potential for divergence or con<sup>fl</sup>ict of interest between the signaler and receiver. In our context, the healthcare market is inherently <sup>fl</sup>awed because of asymmetric information (Arrow 1963). Doctors may not serve the best interest of patients and bene<sup>fi</sup>t from information asymmetry.

In the signaling theory (Spence 1973), the informational value of a signal comes from the fact that the receiver believes that the signal is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a lowability signaler to obtain (single-crossing condition). Therefore, the signal enables the receiver to reliably distinguish high-ability signalers from low-ability ones. In our context, offering online consultations is a signal that doctors are willing to spend more time communicating with patients. In other words, they care more about patients. It is also a signal of a doctor’s communication skills. If doctors are not good at communicating with patients, they may not be willing to offer online consultations. Because all users on the healthcare portal can publicly observe written online consultations, they can also feel these doctors’ willingness to help patients by reading the online interactions between patients and doctors. In addition, during the process of online consultations, patients have more information about these doctors, which reduces the information asymmetry between doctors and patients. For these reasons, patients are more willing to make of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments with these doctors.

In sum, offering online consultation services can be regarded as a signal of doctors’ willingness to better communicate with patients because these services indicates that doctors are more willing to provide a new channel to communicate with patients. It shows that these doctors care more about patients and are willing to spend additional time answering their questions. For users who directly participate in online consultations, online consultation services can enhance mutual communication and understanding between patients and doctors, which may lead patients to visit doctors’ of<sup>fi</sup>- ces of<sup>fl</sup>ine. The many users in the healthcare portal who may not consult directly with doctors online, when searching for answers to questions about their illnesses, can still look at doctors’ written online consultations, which are direct signals of doctors’ willingness to communicate with and help patients. Therefore, opening online consultation services is an effective way of reducing information asymmetry between doctors and patients. Accordingly, we propose our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 1. Doctors who open online consultation services have more offline appointments than those who do not.

After doctors begin offering online consultations, they can provide price information on different types of online consultation services they provide (text and photo consultation, phone consultation, etc.). The variation in prices for different services is large on Haodf. com. (As shown in Table 1, the mean price in our sample is around RMB 20, and the maximum is over RMB 1,000.) Among the doctors who have opened online consultation services, the prices of online consultations can be treated as an additional signal for doctors’ services based on our signaling framework (Connelly et al. 2011, Khurana et al. 2019): doctors who can effectively communicate with patients in online consultations can charge higher prices. Therefore, patients may interpret the prices of online consultations as signals for the effectiveness of doctor–patient communication in online consultations, and higher prices of online consultation services may bring more of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Accordingly, we propose our second hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 2. Doctors charging higher prices for online consultation services have more offline appointments.

Next, we dig deeper into the boundary condition of the main effect: The signaling effect of opening and providing online consultation services could be stronger/ weaker under some conditions (moderators). In the signaling theory (Spence 1973, Connelly et al. 2011), the signal strength (the informational value of a signal) depends on whether the signal enables the receiver to reliably distinguish high-ability signalers from lowability ones.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst moderator is recommendation heat. Higher recommendation heat often means a higher level of popularity or patient satisfaction (Gray et al. 2015). A doctor with a higher level of recommendation heat is more likely to be able to provide thoughtful online consultations, which are stronger signals for medical service quality. In other words, if a doctor with a higher level of recommendation heat opens and provides online consultation services, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for low-expertise doctors to mimic and send the same signal (Spence 1973). Therefore, higher recommendation heat makes the signal (opening and providing online consultation services) stronger and enables patients to better distinguish low-expertise doctors from highexpertise ones. Accordingly, we propose our third hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. The signaling effect of opening and providing online consultation services on offline appointments is larger for doctors with higher recommendation heat.

Our second moderator is doctor job title. In China, titles of doctors include chief doctor (senior professional title), deputy chief doctor (senior professional title), attending doctor (intermediate professional title), and resident doctor (junior professional title). Doctors attain a higher level of quali<sup>fi</sup>cation through rigorous training and evaluations. Hence, a higher level of job title typically indicates a more experienced doctor with higher expertise. Khurana et al. (2019) show that doctors with more experience could give more satisfactory answers to patients’ questions. A doctor with a higher level of job title is more likely to be able to provide higherquality online consultations, which are stronger signals for medical service quality. Therefore, a higher level of job title can strengthen the signaling value of opening and providing online consultation services. In other words, it is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for low-expertise doctors to mimic the online consultations of a chief doctor than to mimic those of a resident doctor. Accordingly, we propose our fourth hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4. The signaling effect of opening and providing online consultation services on offline appointments is larger for doctors with higher job titles.

Our third moderator is hospital rankings. Pope (2009) and Liu et al. (2016) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the reputations of hospitals are critical when patients choose hospitals. In our context, the reputations (rankings of hospitals) make doctors’ signals more credible and informative. A doctor from a higher-ranked hospital is more likely to be able to provide higher-quality online consultations, which are stronger signals for medical service quality. It is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for low-expertise doctors to mimic the online consultations of a doctor from a higher-ranked hospital than to mimic those of a doctor from a lowed-ranked hospital. Therefore, the signal of opening and providing online consultation services from a doctor in a higher-ranked hospital enables patients to more reliably distinguish high expertise doctors from low-expertise ones. Accordingly, we propose our <sup>fi</sup>fth hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 5. The signaling effect of opening and provid ing online consultation services on offline appointments is larger for doctors from higher-ranked hospitals.

Our fourth moderator is whether doctors are from a key department. In China, a national key department in a hospital has a team of high-quality <sup>fi</sup>xed personnel, including many excellent academic leaders, highquality research backbones, high-level technical personnel, and capable management personnel.<sup>1</sup> We are more likely to observe an ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed signal in key departments: doctors from key departments are more likely to be able to provide higher-quality online consultations, which are stronger signals for medical service quality. The reason is that doctors from key departments have an opportunity to use the latest technology and work with authoritative experts in their <sup>fi</sup>elds. Therefore, it is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for low-expertise doctors to mimic the online consultations of doctors from key departments than to mimic those of doctors from nonkey depart ments. Accordingly, we propose our sixth hypothesis.

Hypothesis 6. The signaling effect of opening and provid ing online consultation services on offline appointments is larger for doctors from key departments.

Our last moderator is whether doctors are from high-GDP regions. The regional per-capita GDP represents the development of the economy in general. In China, regions with rapid economic development usu ally invest heavily in healthcare (Kapur et al. 2006, Wang and Lee 2018). Therefore, these regions with higher per-capita GDP can attract better doctors and provide opportunities for doctors to use the latest medical technology. Hence, a doctor from a higher per-capita-GDP region is more likely to be able to provide higher-quality online consultations, which are stronger signals for medical service quality. Accordingly, we propose our seventh hypothesis.

Hypothesis 7. The signaling effect of opening and providing online consultation services on offline appointments i larger for doctors from higher-GDP regions.

Studying these moderating factors helps hospital managers, policy makers, and healthcare professionals deeply understand the impact of doctors’ involvement in online consultations on the patients’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine visits. Even though the impact of opening online consultation services could be considerable, the magnitude of the impact will vary in the different characteristics of doctors, hospitals, regions, etc. A complete understanding of how moderating factors affect the impact of opening online consultation services provides better guidance when making strategic decisions.

## 4.1. Impact of Online Consultations on the Quantity of Doctors’ Offline Appointments

Following Ayer et al. (2019), we use a multivariate regression model and control for patient, hospital, and regional characteristics to examine the relationship between the opening of online consultation services and the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Although we collected data for three months, we did not use the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model in our study. The reason is that our main independent variable, Open (whether a docto opens online consultation services), is time invariant in our sample period. If we use a <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model, all the time-invariant independent variables will cance out in the within transformation. Furthermore, we use log Appointment in the regression analysis; that is, we apply a logarithmic transformation for Appointment (Ayer et al. 2019). 4.3.2. Instrumental Variable Analysis. In our context, doctors’ online consultation decisions could be endogenously determined. We exploit some more exogenous shocks from IVs to alleviate the endogeneity concerns (Wooldridge 2010). Appropriate IVs should explain the variation in doctors’ decisions regarding opening online consultation services, but are not related to the variation in the quantity of doctors’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. Doctors who open online consultation services often like to open personal web pages and publish more articles on their websites. Therefore, we consider the opening of a personal web page and the number of a doctor’s papers displayed on Haodf.com as the IVs in our analysis.

As mentioned earlier, a good IV should satisfy two conditions (Wooldridge 2010). First, the IV should be strongly correlated with the key independent variable. Second, the IV should be exogenous in the sense that it should not directly affect the dependent variable. In our context, the key independent variable is Open (whether a doctor opens online consultation services). In terms of the <sup>fi</sup>rst condition, our two instruments (whether to open personal websites and the number of online articles) represent how active a doctor is on Haodf.com and should be correlated with the key independent variable Open. The spirit of our IVs is in line with the literature on using individuals’ other activities to instrument the focal activity (Jabr and Zheng 2014). It is possible that doctors <sup>fi</sup>rst open their personal web pages on Haodf.com to get familiar with the website (because it is costly). After they are familiar with the website environment, they decide whether to open online consultation services (because it is a major decision, and doctors need to spend signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time and effort to provide online consultation services). In terms of the second condition, our two instruments (whether to open personal websites and the number of online articles) represent how active a doctor is on Haodf.com, and they are not quality measures for doctors. Therefore, they should not directly affect the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments except through the channel of opening online consultation services. In the signaling theory (Spence 1973), the informational value of a signal comes from the fact that the receiver believes that the signal is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a low-ability signaler to send (single-crossing condition). Therefore, the signal enables the receiver to reliably distinguish high-ability signalers from low-ability ones. Unlike opening online consultation services, the two IVs (whether to open personal websites and the number of online articles) are not credible signals for doctors’ willingness to spend time communicating with patients. The reason is that the two IVs cannot enable us to distinguish doctors who are willing to spend more time communicating with patients from those who are not. Opening a personal website is easy and does not involve any future time and effort commitments. Similarly, it is easy to increase the number of low-quality online articles. In contrast, opening online consultation services means that the doctor will receive patients’ questions, which involves signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time and effort commitments. If doctors are not willing to spend more time communicating with patients, they may not be willing to open online consultation services. Therefore, our two IVs should not directly affect the dependent variable, the number of offline appointments (because they are not credible signals for patients to distinguish between different types of doctors). In fact, doctor job titles and the rankings of hospitals are more relevant signals, which have already been controlled for in our analysis. We also conduct an overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation test (Sargan–Hansen test) and <sup>fi</sup>nd that we cannot reject the null hypothesis $( p > 0 . 1 )$ , indicating that our two instrumental variables are likely to be exogenous. Moreover, we use the Hausman test to examine whether an IV is needed in our context by comparing the IV estimates with ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates. The p-value is zero, which means that an IV approach is needed in our context.

From column (2) in Table 8, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that doctors who open online consultation services enjoy a 40.13% $( = e ^ { 0 . 3 3 7 4 } - 1 )$ increase in the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments compared with doctors who do not. The p-value is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and the results based on instrumental variable analysis further verify our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 4.4. Alternative Analyses

In this section, we conduct some alternative analyses to estimate the impact of opening online consultation services by using different data and different measurement methods. First, we consider whether doctors have actually served patients after opening online consultation services. Then, we focus on the impact of hospital types. Furthermore, we explore how the number of patients that a doctor has helped affects the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments.

4.4.1. Alternative Measure of Opening Online Consultation Services. In the main model, we set $O p e n _ { i t }$ as a binary variable, not considering whether the doctor has ever served a patient. We further wonder whether the doctors have helped patients and answered questions because some doctors have never answered patients’ questions even if they have opened services. To test the robustness of our results, we use an alternative measurement of opening online consultation services. We introduce a new variable, Trueanswer , to indicate whether a doctor has indeed provided online consultations, that is, whether the doctor has answered patients’ questions after opening the online consultation service. The variable Trueanswer is one if doctor i has helped patients until time t after opening online consultation services and zero if doctor i has not helped any patient until time t after opening the online consultation services. If the value of Open (whether a doctor opens online consultation services) is zero, the value of Trueanswer will be zero, and if the value of Open is one, the value of Trueanswer will be equal to the value of Trueanswer Open. Hence, Trueanswer is equivalent to Trueanswer Open, and we can use Trueanswer to represent Trueanswer Open in the regression equation. The model is as shown in regression equation (8):

$$

4.4.2. Impact of Hospital Type on the Quantity of Doctors’ Offline Appointments. Apart from the rankings of hospitals, hospitals can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into general hospitals and specialist hospitals. General hospitals contain many specialties, whereas specialist hospitals are mainly targeted at certain types of diseases. General hospitals and specialist hospitals usually differ in hospital size and technology, and even the quality of care.

Some scholars <sup>fi</sup>nd that patients in different types of hospitals have different hospitalization and mortality rates (Yuan et al. 2000, Roberts et al. 2003, Weller et al. 2005). Roberts et al. (2003) demonstrate the link between mortality in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and hospital type. Furthermore, many studies have investigated the relationship between the type of hospitals and healthcare quality (van der Geest et al. 2015, Raol et al. 2016). They <sup>fi</sup>nd that the quality level of care varies among different types of hospitals. In our data, we can see that most top-ranked hospitals are general hospitals. Based on the results presented earlier, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that doctors from higher-ranked hospitals get more of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments by opening online consultation services than those from lower-ranked hospitals. ## 5. Discussions and Conclusions

In this paper, we investigate the impact of online consultations (from Haodf.com) on the number of of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments (from Guahao.com). Our paper <sup>fi</sup>lls the research gap regarding the impact of online healthcare portals on doctor-side of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. In particular, we look at the impact of opening online consultation services on doctors’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments and what factors moderate the impact of opening online consultation services.

## 5.1. Managerial Implications

Online healthcare websites have attracted people’s attention as platforms for information exchange between patients and between doctors and patients. Online consultation is a way for doctors to communicate with patients through healthcare websites directly. For policy makers, online consultations can make up for the de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in traditional consultations and achieve a balance of medical resources. For hospitals, online consultations can increase hospitals’ visibility, increase medical income, and coordinate resources between hospitals. Moreover, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of online consultations on healthcare websites cannot be ignored. Managers of healthcare websites aim to create value by increasing the number of participating patients and doctors. Personally, patients can save time and reduce medical costs through online consultations, and doctors can increase their own income and improve popularity through online consultations. Hence, online consultations have become an important trend that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t policy makers, hospitals, healthcare websites, doctors, and patients. Therefore, understanding the dynamics of using online healthcare platforms is an important area of research from the perspective of all the stakeholders.

First, doctors’ online consultations can be observed by all active users on Haodf.com, and many of them are looking for of<sup>fl</sup>ine doctors. High-quality online consultations show doctors’ expertise and whether they care about patients, and hence attract users on Haodf. com to make of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments with those doctors. Although users on Haodf.com cannot choose doctors online for free consultation services, they can choose doctors online for charged online consultation services. Those patients can further choose whether to consult the same doctors of<sup>fl</sup>ine (physically visit doctors’ of<sup>fi</sup>- ces). That is why opening online consultation services (sending a signal) and paying attention to moderators (sending a stronger signal) are important to doctors. Opening online consultation services can improve the interaction between doctors and patients and provide better opportunities for doctors to communicate with patients who are far away. Hence, doctors should pay attention to the new features launched by the online media to keep pace with patients. We also extend the prior signaling framework (Connelly et al. 2011, Khurana et al. 2019) and show that the prices of doctors online consultations can be a signal of the effectiveness of doctor–patient communication, and higher prices for online consultation services may bring more of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments. This result has implications for doctors pricing of online consultations: They should not consider only the classical law of demand (Mas-Colell et al. 1995), which suggests that higher prices lead to a lower level of demand. Our results indicate that in the healthcare domain, patients are more likely to perceive prices as signals of the quality of healthcare services, and doctors should incorporate this into the pricing of online consultation services.

Second, our results show that the impact of opening online consultation services on the number of appoint ments is positive. Some factors, such as the recommendation heat, ranking of the hospital, type of department, and per-capita GDP, have moderating effects on the impact relationship. Using these results, doctors can make better decisions on whether to open online consultation services or not. For doctors with higher recommendation heat, with higher job titles, from higher-ranked hospitals, from key departments, or from regions with higher per-capita GDP, providing online consultation services is an opportunity to signal their service quality and attract patients to visit of<sup>fl</sup>ine.

Third, because of the unbalanced distribution of medical resources, effective allocation of medical and health resources has always been a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and urgent prob lem in China (Qiao and Zhai 2017). The development of online healthcare portals breaks the regional boundary, and patients from all regions can choose doctors equally through online consultations. For example, policy makers may consider encouraging doctors from regions with higher per-capita GDP to open online consultation services to reduce the shortage of medical resources in lower-per-capita-GDP regions. Also, hospitals in regions with higher per-capita GDP could encourage doctors to communicate with patients via online healthcare portals and attract more patients to visit their hospitals of<sup>fl</sup>ine.

Last but not least, for healthcare portals, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of providing service features of online consultations and booking functions cannot be ignored. The key implication is that online consultations can attract more patients to visit the websites, and more online consultation brings more booking via online portals.

## 5.2. Future Research Directions

Our work can be extended in several directions. First, although appointments on Guahao.com are representative of total of<sup>fl</sup>ine appointments, we realize that patients can make appointments through other of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels. A possible future research direction is to collect data from all registered channels, such as the of<sup>fi</sup>cial websites of major hospitals, for analysis. Second, future studies could examine the impact of online consultations on doctor performance in small and lower-ranked hospitals, which may perform differently regarding the impact of online consultations. Because of resource limitations, they may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently aware of the importance of online healthcare portals and engaging with patients online. Third, fully untangling the spillover and cannibalization effects in our context requires a more granular patientlevel data set, which is not available in our study. Fourth, in our study, we use online consultation services on Haodf.com to capture online consultation services. The reason is that a majority of patients have chosen online consultations on Haodf.com. In reality, a doctor may open online consultation services in multiple healthcare portals, which can be studied in the future.
