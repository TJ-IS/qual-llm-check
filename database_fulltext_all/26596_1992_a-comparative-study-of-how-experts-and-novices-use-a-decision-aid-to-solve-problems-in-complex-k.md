This paper reports the results of a study that examined the way that professionals used a particular decision aid, spreadsheet software, to address a business-oriented task. The professionals were divided into four groups, depending on whether they were experts or novices in the functional area of business represented by the task and on whether they were experts or novices in the use of spreadsheet software. The physical interactions as well as the thought processes underlying these interactions were captured. Results of the study indicated that a lack of expertise in spreadsheet software usage inhibited the application of functional area knowledge. The behavior and outcomes of the functional area expert/spreadsheet novice group did not match those of the functional area expert/spreadsheet expert group. In fact, they paralleled more closely those of the other two groups that did not possess functional area knowledge. Results of the study also indicated that individuals need to obtain a level of expertise in using a decision aid before they are able to apply their functional area knowledge to the problem at hand.

## Introduction

ne approach for identifving the impact of decision aids is to examine the underlying decision processes used by individuals and to assess the quality of the analysis represented by these processes. Todd and Benbasat (1987) criticize the current literature for accepting the positive impact of decision aids on decision processes without empirical support. Keen (1979) and Hurt. Elam and Huber (1986) agree that the effects of design features of decision aids on the quality of decision making have been ignored in empirical research and recommend undertaking more study in this area. Thus, there is increasing interest among researchers to understand decision processes (Benbasat, Dexter and Todd 1986; Biggs, Bedard, Gaber and Linsmeier 1985; Elam and Mead 1990; Isenberg 1986; Jarvenpaa 1989; Mackay, Barr and Kletke 1992; Schweiger, Anderson and Locke 1985; Todd and Benbasat 1987).

It is no longer adequate just to demonstrate that a particular decision aid is effective. An understanding of why and how systems are effective is needed to move research on decision aids to a more advanced stage (Todd and Benbasat 1987). One way to increase this knowledge is to examine the differences and similarities between novices and experts using a decision aid to solve a problem. We studied the effects of two kinds of expertise on the use of a decision aid on a business-oriented task: (1) experience with the decision aid itselfin this case, spreadsheet software; and (2) background in a functional area—in this case, health care planning. The study was designed to obtain information on how levels of both kinds of knowledge—and their interaction—brought by an individual to the user-spreadsheet encounter affect the process and the outcome of the individual's use of the decision aid.

Little is known about the interaction of functional and decision aid knowledge. Many studies have examined cognitive processes of experts and novices in a wide variety of functional domains such as strategy formulation games (Schweiger et al 1985), physics problems (I.arkin. McDermott, Simon and Simon 1980), chess (de-Groot 1966, Chase and Simon 1973), programming languages (Moran 1981; Adelson and Soloway 1988; Anderson. Pirolli and Farrell 1988; Soloway, Adelson and Ehrlich 1988), judicial issues (Lawrence 1988), medical diagnosis (Groen and Patel 1988, Lesgold et al. 1988), interactive devices (Kieras and Bovair 1984, Young 1983) restaurant orders (Ericsson and Polson 1988), and thermodynamies (Bhaskar and Simon 1977). In general, the researchers find that experts are inclined to approach problems from the top down and are able to group problems with the same underlying structure. In contrast, novices tend to use a bottom-up procedure which lacks a comprehensive plan, and they usually group problems by surface characteristics. Novices also lack the ability to put information in chunks or to create associative patterns, whereas experts are able to do both. Through chunking, the experts can recall organized patterns more easily than scrambled patterns, whereas novices do not differ significantly in their recall of organized patterns and scrambled patterns.

A different set of studies addresses the differences between experts’ and novices’ use of computers, such as the use ofinteractive text editors (Card, Moran and Newell 1983; Mack, Lewis and Carroll 1983). A study on how people learn to use a word processor found that users who were not experienced with computers encountered serious problems when trying to accomplish straightforward tasks with these systems (Mack et al. 1983). Learning was difficult and took longer than expected. Users did not know what was relevant to understanding and solving problems. Moreover, the users' lack of knowledge made it difficult for them to interpret what was happening accurately, although they tried to generalize from what thev alreadv knew.

In our study, we investigate the simultaneous effects of different levels of user knowledge in both spreadsheet usage and a functional area (health care planning) on the user-decision aid interaction. The remainder of the paper is divided into the following sections: (1) the research basis for user-spreadsheet interaction, including a review of the literature on expert/novice research; (2) the research methodology; (3) results; and (4) discussion and conclusion of the study.

## Research Basis for User-Spreadsheet Interaction

Our primary research goal is exploratory: we want to identify that knowledge which experts (functional or decision aid) seem to have and novices have not yet acquired (Ehrlich and Soloway 1984). We also want to provide preliminary answers to two questions that have not been adequately addressed:

Do individuals need to have a given level of expertise in using the software before they can effectively apply their task domain knowledge to the problem at hand?

Do the problem-solving processes employed by experts in both spreadsheet usage and task domains differ from those of novices in both domains? Or does the decision aid impose a structure that suppresses any differences?

In order to develop and test expectations of how these interactions may vary, we will draw upon research in expert-novice problem-solving behavior in four major areas: (1) problem definition, (2) problem categorization, (3) problem understanding. and (4) problem-solving strategies.

Problem Defınition Problem definition refers to the way in which an individual represents or conceptualizes a problem and the way in which inferences can be drawn about the problem from the representation. In the initial phases of problem solving, experts seem to apply a qualitative analysis or physical intuition to the problem representation which results in the identification of important features of the problem and their relationships (Black and Sebrechts 1981; Chi, Feltovich and Glaser 1981; Hardiman, Dufresne and Mestre 1989; Hayes and Simon 1976; Hinsley, Hayes and Simon 1977; Leinhardt 1983; Newell and Simon 1972: Sımon and Simon 1978). In contrast, novices dispense with such activities and instead rush into problem solving without appreciating the interrelationship between different parts of the problem. Thus, in our study we expect to observe differences between experts and novices in both spreadsheet usage and the task domain by examining, first, the number of subjects' protocols which occurred during the formulation or conceptualization of the problem and, second, their strategies for solving it.

Problem C’ategorization Previous studies have shown that problem solvers represent problems by categories. Moreover, how a problem is characterized influences how it is solved (Hinsley, Hayes, and Simon 1977). In categorizing problems into types, novices tend to cue on “surface structure"—objects referred to in the problem, key words that have meaning in the problem domain, or a relationship between several object components (Chi et al. 1981, Schoenfeld and Herrmann 1982). In contrast, experts classify problems according to major principles or methods relevant for problem solving (Chi et al. 1981, Moran 1981, Schoenfeld and Herrmann 1982). For instance, in physics, novices tend to group problems together that share common characteristics such as “objects that rotate." Experts, on the other hand, group physics problems together according to the various laws of physics that apply, such as the “Conservation of Energy Law." These underlying principles are referred to as the “deep structure” of the problem. It appears that experts create over time a fundamental set of processes that they can use to solve different types of basic problems. Solving a problem becomes a matter of categorizing the problem into one or more basic problem types and applying the existing processes. Thus, in our study, we expect spreadsheet experts to approach the problem-solving process first by determining the groups of commands or “procedures" that are necessary to solve the problem and then executing these commands. In the task domain, we expect our novices to focus primarily on data—in particular, certain attributes such as income and population— rather than on a procedural model for completing the assigned task

Problem U'nderstanding. An individual attempts to “understand" a problem by constructing a problem representation, an initial problem state, desired goal state. and a plan for moving from the initial state to the goal state (Chi et al. 1981). The quality of these efforts is dependent on the knowledge available to the problem solver. Experts generally have access to both procedural and declarative knowledge. Novices generally have the declarative knowledge to solve a problem, but they lack abstracted solution methods (Chase and Simon 1973, deGroot 1966). Experts differ from novices in that experts often know more, and in a more elaborate way, about the subject than do novices.

The ability to "understand" a problem also depends on the way in which knowledge is organızed. Experts organize their knowledge in a manner different from that of novices. The same words may be used by both novices and experts. but for the experts those words denote a deeper. more elaborate structure. Experts in health care planning and spreadsheet usage will have developed an integrated view of the problem. We would expect experts in both domains will switch easily betu een working on the task and applying the software. We expect to see a more fragmented approach by those individuals who are either novices in one domain and experts in the other or novices in both areas.

Problem-so/ving Strategtes. Researchers note that novices and experts apply distinctive strategies in solving problems (Larkin et al. 1980, Simon and Simon 1978). For example, when solving physics problems, novices used a means-ends analysis in which they generated solutions by initially selecting an equation containing the goal and working backward toward the problem givens. Once the novices determined the equations for the solutions, they then reversed the process to work forward to solve the problem. Experts, on the other hand, solved problems using forward problem solving by beginning to work with the givens to create the equations to reach the goal. Sweller, Mawer and Ward (1983) report that after solving manv problems, novices changed from means-ends analysis to forward problem solving. We expect our health care planners/spreadsheet experts to use forward problem solving and our health care and accountant/spreadsheet novices to use means-ends analysis.

In other studies (Dee-Lucas and Larkin 1984, Mehle 1982) on problem-solving strategies and hypothesis generation, researchers found that novices and experts generated about the same number of hypotheses. However, novices formulated rules which were domain-specific and over-generalized to the point of being incorrect. All subjects expressed that thev felt their hypotheses were consistent and exhaustive. But the research demonstrated that novices, although thinking that they had been completely thorough in their attempts, were actually severely lacking in their ability to generate reasonable hypotheses and that their models of the problem were not as rich as those of the experts. We expect the novices in the task domain to overgeneralize and create more simplistic models: we also predict that the limitations of these simple models will not be overcome by expert levels of spreadsheet knowledge

Experts act on problems by using automatic processes, evidenced by the fact that they tend to verbalize less when solving problems than novices do (Sımon and Simon

1978). For spreadsheet experts, this factor suggests the use of fewer, more powerful commands. For health care planning experts, the research implies less description about how they are solving the problem. Thus, we would expect our health care planners to verbalize less about the health care planning problem and the spreadsheet novices to employ more spreadsheet commands.

## Research Methodology

## Research Design

The primary objectives of the study reported here were (1) to assess how individuals interact with a decision aid to solve a particular business problem, and (2) to learn how the knowledge that these individuals possess, in terms of the decision aid and the functional area, influences the nature of this interaction. These objectives required the selection of a problem within a particular task domain, a decision aid. and individuals with varying levels of knowledge of both the task and the decision aid.

## Task

The task domain selected for this study was health care planning. The particular problem within the task domain was to determine the site locations for six medical convenience care centers (24-hour emergency medical clinics) from 71 possible locations. Associated with each possible location were 12 demographic variables related to such items as population, income, age, education, and employment. A sample of the database is included in Appendix A. Expertise in the task domain was clearly dichotomous in this study; our experts had experience in health care planning decisions, and our novices had none

## Software

The decision aid selected was Lotus 1-2-3. Based on our analysis of the health care problem, we specified a level of Lotus 1-2-3 expertise that would be needed to solve the problem. Because the problem required the simultaneous evaluation of multiple criteria, a cursory knowledge of Lotus 1-2-3 would not be helpful. However, for the category of“expert" we did not necessarily require a subject to have high-level macro programming skills, but rather to have concrete experience in the use of Lotus 1-2-3 to be able to solve the problem easilv. Our precise operationalization of expertise in Lotus 1-2-3 is detailed in Appendix B.

## Subjects

Four distinct groups of professionals were selected for this study, and three professionals within each group participated in the study. The categories were as follows:

(1) Experts in both spreadsheet usage and in health care planning. Experienced health care consulting professionals who had used Lotus 1-2-3 extensively in their work to solve this type of problem were selected for this category

(2) Experts in spreadsheet usage but not in health care planning. Experienced accountants who had used Lotus 1-2-3 extensively in their work were selected for this category.

(3) Experts in health care planning but not in spreadsheet usage. Experienced health carc consultants who had solved this type of problem, but had little or no experience using Lotus 1-2-3, were selected for this category.

(4) Experts in neither spreadshcet usage nor in health care planning. Experienced accountants who had little or no experience using Lotus 1-2-3 were selected for this category.

The main limitation of this study was the sample size. A small number of subjects decreases the power of any statistical comparisons as well as the generalizability of the results. There were two reasons for the small size of the group. The first reason was the high cost of transcribing and coding the protocols, which required approximately 30 hours each. (The direct cost of protocol analysis has caused researehers using the method to restrict sample sizes dramatically.) The second reason was the lack of available combined expertise in health care planning and Lotus 1-2-3. Thus, a subject group of twelve, with three in each group, is consistent with related research in different fields. Bouwman (1984) compared three expert C.P.A 's with five novices. Adelson (1981) compared five expert computer programmers with five novices. Chase and Simon (1973) compared a total of three chess players of different levels with each other (Isenberg 1986)

## Procedures

Each spreadsheet novice was given a 45-minute review session in Lotus 1-2-3 prior to the experiment to ensure that he or she had the necessary spreadsheet skills to complete the assigned task. The review sessions utilized training materials provided by a commercial computer training organization. As an incentive for participating in the study, all participants were told that more advanced Lotus l-2-3 training would be available, if they desired it.

All subjects practiced “thinking aloud" while solving two problems unrelated to the actual experimental task. This particular procedure was based on those described by Ericsson and Simon (1984) and Smıth (1971). Afteı practicing “thinking aloud" with the two warm-up tasks, subjects received one sheet of paper containing the problem and a second sheet of paper with the column headings of the Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheet. Each subject was told that no more than two hours could be spent on this exercise and that the exercise would be completed when the subject had identified six zip codes for the locations of the convenience care centers Subjects were also asked to think aloud while performing the task. Each subject's responses were taperecorded, and each subject's computer keystrokes were recorded by a VHS camera.

## Protocol Analysis

Verbal protocol analysis is frequently used to study cognitıve phenomena (Ericsson and Simon 1984). Protocol analysis is the process of translating a chaotic collection of verbalizations spoken by an individual engaged in some task into more accessible representations (Bouwman 1983).

Protocol analysis has been emploved in business-related discıplines (Bettman and Park 1980, Biggs et al. 1985, Bouwman 1983, Jarvenpaa 1989. Mackav et al. 1992, Schweiger et al. 1985), and various methodological ıssues have been addressed (Ericsson and Simon 1984; Ford, Schmitt, Schechtman, Hults and Doherty 1989; Nisbett and Wilson 1977; Payne, Braunstein and Carroll 1978; Schweiger 1983; Todd and Benbasat 1987). Concurrent verbal protocols, such as those used in this study, have been found to be accurate and representative measures of cognitive processes (Ericsson and Simon 1984).

The recorded verbal responses of each subject were transcribed. and the transcripts were divided into protocol episodes following the methodology developed bv Smith (1971) and Ericsson and Simon (1984). The videotape for each subject was viewed and the Lotus 1-2-3 commands invoked, executed or abandoned by each subject were noted on the transcript. For any given protocol episode, one of the following two conditions existed: (1) the protocol episode related to an information-processing activity operation in health care planning (e.g., comparing of values of attributes in a record) or (2) the protocol episode related to a command of the spreadsheet (e.g. sorting data in the file). Coding of the protocols followed.

## Coding Scheme

Early researchers in human problem solving proposed that humans process information through stages when solving problems (Duncker 1945, Wallas 1926, Polya 1957, 1968. Newell and Simon 1972). Wallas described the four stages as (1) preparation, (2) incubation, (3) illumination, and (4) verification, whereas Polya labelled them (1) understanding the problem, (2) devising a plan, (3) carrying out the plan, and (4) looking back. Norman (1986) refined these stages for studies of user-computer interaction. Norman's theory distinguishes among different kinds of activities that appear to capture the critical aspects of accomplishing tasks with an interactive computer-based system.

The primary coding scheme used in this research for spreadsheet usage and the health care planning problem is based on Norman's (1986) theory that action consists of (1) planning (goal formation and specifying the action sequence) and (2) execution (executing and making contact with the input mechanisms of the interface). We coded planning as a single stage of formulating or modifying a plan (Stage 1). Execution in Lotus 1-2-3 we coded as two stages: first invoking (Stage 2) and then executing (Stage 3) a command. Pretesting identified a third basic component of action not included in Norman's original model—abandoning a plan. which we included as a fourth stage. Thus, the four stages of action we coded were as follows;

## Stage 1: Formulate or modify

A protocol episode was coded as Stage 1 when a subject (1) first formulated a particular plan related to either spreadsheet usage (e.g., “I'm going to sort") or the health care planning problem (e.g.. “I'm going to use population") or (2) modified a current plan in either domain (e.g., “I'll sum the column instead").

## Stage 2: Invoke or prepare to execute

Since several steps may be required to complete a spreadsheet command, a protocol episode was coded as Stage 2 when a subject invoked a spreadsheet command and did not finish the execution of the command in one protocol episode (e.g., “I am in the process of trying to sort the records using population growth"). In this example the subject started the sort function, which includes data sort. define range, definuing keys, choosing the sequence for sorting, and selecting go If the subject verbalized multiple episodes during the process of sorting, then the episodes which did not include the go of the spreadsheet commands were coded as Stage 2 or invoke We wanted to make a defnite distinction between the invoking of the spreadsheet procedure and its completion.

If the subject executed a spreadsheet command during one protocol episode, then Stage 2 did not exist for that particular protocol episode. If a subject invoked a spreadsheet command, such as data sort. but did not select go, and then canceled the command by escaping out of it, the protocol episodes were not coded for Stages 2 (invoke) and 3 (execute). Rather, they were coded for Stages 2 (invoke) and 4 (abandon), without any coding for Stage 3 because the command was not executed. The protocol episodes were coded for both Stages 2 and 3 only when the execution of the spreadsheet command extended beyond one protocol episode.

## Stage 3: Execute

A protocol episode was coded as Stage 3 when the result of applying either a spreadsheet command (e.g., “I’m sorting the records"—and the sorting is completed) or a health care planning operation (e.g., “Population is in decreasing order") was demonstrated.

## Stage 4: Abandon

A protocol episode was coded as Stage 4 when the subject abandoned the current problem-solving strategy in the spreadsheet (“I'm quitting trying to sort") or in the health care planning decision (“Forget about population").

In order to determine the operational problem-solving processes that experts and novices employed in solving the health care problem while using a spreadsheet, we assigned an operational code for spreadsheet usage commands or health care planning operations, in addition to the strategic general problem-solving stage codes. If the protocol episode was related to the spreadsheet usage, then a stage code and a spreadsheet command code were assigned. If the protocol episode was related to the health care planning task, then a stage code and a health care planning operation code were assigned. In all cases there was either a pair of codes for spreadsheet usage or a pair of commands for health care planning, or both, for each protocol episode. In the health care planning problem, the operations corresponded to different types of cognitive activities such as compare, examine, and delete. We developed the set of codes for health care planning operations by consulting those used in previous studies (Bettmann 1970; Biggs 1979; Braunstein and Coleman 1967: Clarkson 1962; Kleinmuntz 1968; Payne 1976; Vessey 1985, 1986; Vitalari 1985). We created the coding scheme for both the spreadsheet commands and the health care planning operations prior to running the experiment. Following a pretest, necessary adjustments to the coding schemes were made. (Spreadsheet commands and health care planning operations are included in Tables 1 and 2.)

The primary stage codes were adapted from Norman's (1986), while the secondary specific codes were created directly from (1) Lotus 1-2-3 commands and (2) collecting protocols related to the health care planning task from the pretest group.' While other researchers' coding schemes were considered, they were not appropriate for examining problem solving, nor were they specifie enough for the problem-solving situation in this study. Most of the protocol coding schemes were related solely to decisionmaking strategies, which are not the thrust of our study.

## Reliabılity of Coding

Two individuals were trained to code transcripts independently to verify reliability of the coding scheme. Four transcripts, one from each cell of the matrix, were randomly selected for determining the interrater reliability. The kappa coefficient (Cohen 1960) was 0.85, an acceptable level.

## Results

To compare the problem-solving approaches of our four experimental groups, we examined their verbal protocols and spreadsheet commands. In this section, we first report on health care planning operations and spreadsheet usage by analyzing the formulation/modify and execution stages, presenting the performance of each of the four groups in order. We then discuss task solutions and comparative performance of all four groups together.

## Health Care Planner/Spreadsheet Expert Group

This group was able to integrate the application of the health care planning problem with the use of the spreadsheet by manipulating multiple attributes and records simultaneously. Moreover, the group members demonstrated “deep structure" in their problem understanding by executing the two most complex health care operations: constructing a new attribute and ranking varı- ables. These processes were complex in both the task and the combination of the spreadsheet commands to produce the strategies needed to solve the problem. Accountants/Spreadsheet Expert Group

We found that these subjects, lacking constructive knowledge of how to solve the health care problem, nonetheless engaged in a great deal of superficial activity. As shown in Table 1, overall they executed far more health care planning operations than did the other groups (a total of 548 operations vs. 280, 252, and 364 for the other three groups). Lacking theoretical health care knowledge, this group specified criteria less than any other group (28 operations vs. 57, 36 and 57), but performed actions on the attributes they selected more than the others did (50 operations vs. 33, 0 and 35). They reordered the records, restructuring the visual image much more than any other group (30 operations vs. 4, 0, and 0), perhaps attempting to gain insight from rearranging the data. In addition to restructuring the database, thev concentrated on comparing attributes of records and then eliminating the records not selected. The accountant/sprcadsheet experts did not use any of the ranking and weighting strategies that the health care planners/spreadsheet experts did.

To apply their spreadsheet expertise, they kept themselves occupied by executing the same commands over and over, resulting in the greatest number of cxecutions (a total of 411 commands vs. 331, 102, and 179 for the other three groups). (See Table 2.) Their spreadsheet expertise allowed them to use a strategy of medium complexity: for example, they executed the data commands more than any other group (61 commands vs. 39, 0, and 15). They generally sorted the records according to an attribute and then used the move command to relocate records to another section of the spreadsheet. This group also used data commands to extract portions of the spreadsheet to other files and then used the f/e commands to combine the individual spreadsheets back into the main spreadsheet. They executed some formatting of the spreadshect, but not as much as the health care planners/spreadsheet experts. Like the health care planners/spreadsheet experts, they became occupied with entering labels and hashed lines to make the spreadshcet attractive. Thcse subjects tended to repeat the execution of the same commands over and over on the same data. It was as if they hoped that the data could tell them what to do to solve the problem

Finally, the simplistic models this group developed suffered from overgeneralization. as expected. For example, the group tended to assume that a high population guaranteed a successful site location—although the income could be so low that patients could not afford to use the health care center. The group exemplified using “surface structures" in the task as noted by their selection of attributes, and “deeper structures" for the spreadsheet. However, spreadsheet expertise did not help them to overcome this tendency. as they were no better than the accountants who had no spreadsheet experience.

## Accountant/Spreadsheet Novice Group

The accountant/spreadsheet novice group, which was limited in spreadsheet command usage and in health care planning knowledge, addressed the problem in the simplest fashion: mainly, they compared records and eliminated potential site locations from those being compared until they had only six site locations remaining. Because the accountant/spreadsheet novices knew neither the appropriate attributes to select nor the appropriate spreadsheet commands to use. thev employed a meansends analysis as expected. They worked backwards, trying to elimınate records one at a time. As 'Table 1 shows, this group had the greatest number of verbalızations on the attributes to employ for solving the problem of all groups (104 select attribute operations vs. 23, 20 and 67 for the other three groups). substituting the activity of planning for the execution of useful problem-solving strategies.

In attempting to utilize the spreadsheet, they performed fewer commands than the spreadsheet experts (a total of 179 commands vs. 331 and 411)—but more commands than the spreadsheet novices with health care expertise (179 commands vs. 102). (See Table 2.) Being unable to generate deeper conceptual approaches to either the functional problem or use of the spreadsheet, they kept busy by performing many simple activities. This group avoided the move command in favor of the copy command. They may have decided to use the copy' command out of fear of destroying the original database without being able to restore it to its original format.

## Task Solutions

Subjects produced a variety of answers at the end of the experiment. Table 3 shows the location solutions for each of the six health care convenience centers for each group by individual. The purpose of examining these solutions is less to identify the “best" solution than to analyze the similarity of the solutions within each group and among the groups. In practice, the quality of the solution to this standard health care training problem is dependent on the selection of appropriate site attributes, the weighting of those attributes. and the processing of the information. The relative weightings are not clear-cut, varying among expert practitioners, but general agreement exists on the selection of the attributes themselves and on the best way to process the information, given the limitations of the spreadsheet. Our health care planner/spreadsheet expert group did, in fact, closely follow the accepted procedures and select the appropriate attributes.

As expected, the solutions for the health care planner/spreadsheet expert group of subjects showed the least within-group variance and the most variance from those of other groups. The other three groups' solutions showed a high degree of similarity.

To confirm that the health care planner/spreadsheet expert subjects did indeed have answers which were similar in nature and that these answers were different from those of the other three groups, a K-means cluster analysis (Morrison 1967) was performed. We selected this procedure because the K-means cluster analysis is sensı- tive to outliers. A clear pattern exists deseribing which subjects are most like each other and most different from other cases. The two-cluster solution shows that subjects K, 1. B, and C' have similar answers, and that their answers are different from those of the other eight cases.

Going to three clusters sets subject K apart from the others. The three cluster solution indicates that subject K is now a unique cluster, with the rest of the solution identical to that of the two-cluster solutions. It shows that subject K's answers differ from those of subjects . 1, B and C', while the answers of the other eight subjects remain relatively similar. Subject K was an outlier because this individual accidentally erased the database near the end of the experimental task and, therefore, was not able to recreate the previous data for his solutions. His answers were not well thought out The clustering of the subjects' solutions supports the proposition that the health care planner/spreadsheet expert group solutions differed from those of all other groups and that the other three groups had similar solutions.

The variation between the health care planner/spreadsheet novice group and the health care planner/spreadsheet expert group is particularly enlightening in that all of the health care planners—whether versed in Lotus 1-2-3 or not—should have been able to arrive at the similar answers, given their expertise in health care planning. But functional proficiency was not enough to overcome lack of knowledge of the decision aid, rendering our health care planning experts no better off than accountants in solving a complex health care problem. ## Discussion and Conclusions

The findings of this study largely support the prior research on expert/novice problem-solving differences. Expectations as to surface vs. deep structure, forward vs. backward problem solving, level of generalization, and the value of domain expertise were largely confirmed. Only the number of verbal expressions and spreadsheet commands went against previous findings.

In regard to our first research question, we explored how different levels of software and functional expertise of individuals influenced the use of the decision aid. To address the second question, we examined the differential effects of system constraints on problem-solving processes, as discussed above.

Thus the first form of knowledge which experts seem to have and novices have not yet acquired (in terms of our exploratory goal) is the differences in the procedures used to solve multi-attribute problems. Perhaps because of the number of attributes and records in our experimental task, our novices seem to have applied certain elements of cost-benefit theory: when a problem solver is faced with a large task, costbenefit principles predict an increase in incentives for the problem solver to reduce cognitive processing by eliminating some of the alternatives. Furthermore, Shugan (1980) proposed that the cost of thinking increases as the number of comparisons increases, enhancing the need to minimize the cost. Thus, we would expect novices to reduce the number of alternatives as an immediate goal.

Our findings further suggest that we have an interaction effect of the spreadsheet with the health care planning problem. Since accountant/spreadsheet experts have higher verbalization and spreadsheet operation counts in problem solving than do the health care planners/spreadsheet experts, it is plausible that the software becomes a vehicle which enhances the trial and error capabilities of problem solving or else is used as a surrogate for lack of knowledge in the functional area. If this is the case, then we need to train individuals adequately in the functional area before expecting them to be able to solve problems competently using the decision aid.

The reverse interaction was the effect of unfamiliarity with the spreadsheet on functional area expertise. The health care planners/spreadsheet novices, who used the lowest number of spreadsheet commands, may have felt that the spreadsheet was an encumbrance to the problem-solving process. They could verbalize how to solve the problem but could not act upon their plans. In this case they could merely visually select the appropriate records without performing any of the desired manipulations on them. Since their answers to the problem were similar to those of the accountant/ spreadsheet experts, we would suggest that the software, instead of being a problemsolving enhancer, hindered it. Recent research on the cognitive fit between the task and the format presentation would also support this finding (Vessey 1991, Vessey and Galletta 1991). We cannot ignore the impact of software on the problem-solving process when only spreadsheet experts with health care planning expertise select site locations which are appropriate.

## Future Research Implications

This research has examined the effects of the user-decision aid interaction on the problem-solving processes of four groups of users confronted by a complex managerial task. The findings of this study can serve as a basis for further research in several areas.

By using a protocol analysis methodology, we derived information essential to the development of a theory of user-decision aid interaction. Based on the knowledge gained in this research, a number of propositions can be formulated and tested using traditional experimental techniques in subsequent studies. Propositions that emerge from this study include the following:

PRoposition 1.– Indtviduals who are expert in the sottware domamn will use more commands/operations in problem solving than will software novices

PRoposition 2. Individuals who are experts ın both the tash and sofiware domains will solve problems more directly and wuth less trial and error behavior than individuals who lack expertise in euther or both the task or sottware.

PRoposiTion 3. Individuals who are experts in both the task and software domains will produce solutions different from those of undıviduals who lack expertise in euther task or software or both

PRoposition 4.– Individuals who are novices in the software domamn will spend most of their time handling software issues rather than dealing with the task, regardless of their level of expertise in the task domain

More research is needed to test the robustness of the findings. This research did not include a control group. It is unknown if the problem-solving behavior of experts and novices in the task domain who did not have access to the decision aid would have been similar to or different from that of experts and novices in the task domain who did have access to the decision aid. It is also unknown w hether the problem-solving behaviors observed in this study would be different in other task domains or with other decision aids

Many researchers have emphasized the evolutionary nature of decision aid design and use. At the core of these arguments is the recognition that users undergo a significant learning experience as they work with the decision aid. However, the user's learning problem is confounded by requirements to learn about both the task and software domains. As the number of individuals who use decision aids increases via the growth in end-user computing, so does the need to train these users to use the decision aid effectively. This research has shown that differences exist between novices and experts in problem-solving behaviors and outcomes. To understand how these differences should affeet the development of effeetive training programs, we need further research. Questions that need to be addressed include the following: How does a novice become an expert in both the software and task domains? Does the path to expertise depend on where the user begins in terms of task and software knowledge? Are different educational strategies required for different user groups?

These questions are motivated by practical concerns. Decision aids are becoming standard tools for business professionals in organizations todav. The business professional is expected to be able to use the available decision-aiding technology as part of his or her professional responsibilities. The ideal user is one who is very knowledgeable of the functional domain in which the decision of interest resides and who is also very experienced with the software itself. Unfortunately. this characterization applies to only a subset of potential users. Because of promotions, transfers and career changes, business professionals constantly face new sets of problem-solving tasks in new functional domains. Because of new developments in technology, business professionals constantly must learn how to use new support tools. Therefore, in order to promote the effective use of decision aids in organizations, one must identify how the interactions of individuals with a decision aid may differ in terms of usage patterns. problems encountered, and performance attained when those individuals possess varying levels of expertise in the task and software domains.

This research focused on problem solving. As Svenson (1979) notes, “. . . it is gradually becoming clear that human decision making cannot be understood by simply studying final decisions. The perceptual, emotional and cognitive processes which ultimately lead to the choice of a decision alternative must also be studied if we want to gain an adequate understanding of human decision making" (p. 86). Complementary research that uses the same research design but adds a focus on choice should also be pursued. This research would determine whether groups with varying levels of expertise in the task and software domains employ the same or different information-processing strategies and decision rules to choose from a given set of alternatıves. Such research will add to the wealth of empirical evidence that currently exists concerning how individuals make choices in multi-attribute tasks (Payne 1976).\*
