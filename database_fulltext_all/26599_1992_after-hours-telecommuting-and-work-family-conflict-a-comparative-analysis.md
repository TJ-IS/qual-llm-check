After-hours telecommuting (AHT) is a work arrangement where job-relevant work is done at home on a computer outside of regular office hours. This study examined how after-hours telecommuting affects an individual's ability to balance work and family demands (measured as role overload, spillover of interference from work to family and spillover of interference from family to work). It also examined the impact of gender and maternal career employment on these relationships. The analysis showed that men and women who performed after-hours telecommuting worked significantly more hours per week and a greater number of hours of overtime at home than did individuals without computers at home. After controlling for total work hours of both spouses. significant gender differences and differences due to performing after-hours telecommuting were found. These differences were associated with role overload and spillover of interference from work to family.

## Introduction

Wolfe, Quinn, Snoek and Rosenthal 1964), is becoming increasing prevalent in North American society due to changing work place demographies. For most individuals, there is no longer the option of a division of paid and family labor by gender. Women are increasingly being forced to deal with job-related demands which place limits on the performance of their family role. Men are becoming more involved with their family and are experiencing a shift in their priorities away from work ( Pleck 1978, 1985; Michelson 1983).

This increase in work-family conflict is a major problem for individuals and organizations alike as high levels of work-family conflict are associated with many negative effects including absenteeism, turnover, poor morale, poorer mental and physical health, and reduced productivity (Michelson 1983). Clearly, the traditional model of separate job and family roles for men and women is inadequate (Kanter 1977).

To date, most organizations have been slow in designing jobs to accommodate the needs of employees who have competing family responsibilities (Christensen 1988). These organizations operate under what Kanter (1977) calls the myth of separate worlds, assuming that work and family are separate entities. In recent times, however, some organizations have enhanced job flexibility through flextime, job-sharing, compressed work-weeks and other nonstandard work schedules (e.g., Staines and Pleck 1986, Bohen and Viveros-Long 1981). Job flexibility is also being enhanced through computer and telecommunications technology (Becker and McClintock 1981, Olson 1983, Schiff 1983, Toffler 1980) which are allowing individuals to perform job-relevant work at home.

There are various definitions given to these flexible work arrangements being made possible by technology. Computer-based homework is a broad term referring to situations where individuals do job-relevant work at home using computing technology. Telecommuting (Nilles, Carlson, Grav and Hanneman 1976; Toffler 1980) refers to a work arrangement where individuals perform most of their work at home during regular office hours using computers and telecommunications. Computer-based homework differs from telecommuting in that it includes situations where the job has a substantial office component. Supplemental work at home (Vitalari and Venkatesh 1989), or after-hours telecommuting (AHT) as it is called in this paper, is a subset of computer-based homework. It refers to a work arrangement where job-relevant work is done at home on a computer outside of regular office hours.

Computer-based homework has been praised for expanding individual autonomy, control, flexibility, convenience and family togetherness (Leider 1988). However, discussions generally focus mainly on “what technology can do for you, not on what it can do to you" (Gutek 1983, p. 163). These discussions commonly gloss over class and gender issues and fail to assess whether working at home will have different ramifications for men and women (Leider 1988). As the climate for AHT grows increasingly favourable, more research is needed. Accordingly, the following questions are addressed in this study: (1) How does AHT affect an individual's ability to balance work and family demands? (2) What impact does gender have on the relationship between AHT and work-family conflict? and (3) What impact does wives employment status have on the relationship between AHT and work-family conflict? More specifically, this study compares the work-family conflict levels of dual-career fathers (men in a professional job with a wife also in a professional job), traditional fathers (wife at home) and dual-career mothers (women in a professional job with a husband also in a professional job) who choose the AHT work style to individuals in these three groups who, while having the opportunity to perform AHT, do not use this work arrangement

## Hypotheses

Recent literature reviews have examined work-family conflict from a number of different perspectives (Burke and Bradshaw 1981; Kabanoff 1980; Kanter 1977; Near, Rice and Hunt 1980; Staines 1980; Voydanoff 1982, 1987; Greenhaus and

Beutell 1985). The literature reviewed below focuses on threc areas which correspond to the hypotheses of this study: (1) work-family conflict and AHT, (2) the impact of gender on the relationship between work-family conflict and AHT in dual-career couples, and (3) the impact of maternal employment status on the relationship between work-family conflict and AHT.

## Work-Family Conflict and After-Hours Telecommuting

The literature suggests several mechanisms by which AHT mav reduce workfamily conflict. First, AHT increases work flexibility bv relaxing space-time work constraints. With a computer at home, an employee has more than one option with respect to where overtime work is done. This increase in work schedule flexibility could reduce work-family conflict by helping employees fulfill multiple responsibilities (Michelson 1983, Greenhaus and Parasuraman 1986, Staines and Pleck 1986). Second, AHT may reduce work-family conflict by increasing the amount of control one has over the pacing and scheduling of their work (Katz and Piotrkowskı 1983, Portner 1983). Finally, the opportunity to spend more oftheir time at home should reduce work-family conflict because it allows emplovees to spend more time with their children (Leider 1988, Antanoff 1985).

While some of the literature on the consequences of computer-based homework has stressed the opportunities the new technology will create for performing both work and family roles more fully, much of the literature contends that AHT will increase work-family conflict. To understand this, it is important to note the three different components of work-family conflict: overload; spillover of interference from the family to the work domain; and spillover of interference from the work to the family domaın (Voydanoff 1987, Pleck 1978, Wiley 1987, Grcenhaus 1989). Overload exists when work and family demands make it difficult or ımpossible to perform both roles adequately or comfortably. Interference occurs when the demands of one's job and family are contradictory (Voy danoff 1982) There are two types of work-family interterence: spillover of interference from the family to the work domain, and spillover of interference from the work to the family domain (Evans and Bartolome 1984, Greenhaus 1989. Voydanoff 1989, Wilev 1987). The direction of spillover of interference has been found to be dependent on the salience of each role to the focal person, as well as the negative sanctions associated with noncompliance with each role pressure (Greenhaus 989. Greenhaus and Beutell 1985). Wiley (1987) found that scales that measured the directionality of the conflict (i.e., work interfering with family, family interfering with work) are necessary to shed light on the relative priority of work and family roles.

The literature is fairly consistent in linking AHT with increased role overload. Vitalari and Venkatesh (1989) found that computer owners worked approximately twice as many overtime hours per week. They felt that, although computer ownership per se did not lead to AHT, it did act as an agent that promoted or reinforced AHT activities, and over time legitimized work at home. These results are consistent with those of Kraut (1987) and Olson (1985).

AHT has also been linked to increased role interference (Kraut 1989, Hall and Richter 1988, Olson 1985). The physical separation of work and family has traditionally provided a convenient cue for switching roles and keeping what are often considered conflicting roles at bay (i.e., behavior that is appropriate in the work setting is inappropriate in the home and vice versa) Losing the opportunity that location-specific roles provide for balancing competing and conflicting role expectations may generate interference rather than enhance the performance of multiple roles (Hall and Richter 1988, Salomon and Salomon 1984, Olson 1985).

The following related hypotheses are tested in this study:

H1. Dual-career fathers who perform after-hours telecommuting will experience greater work-family conflict (i.e., greater role overload, greater interference from the family to the work domain, and greater interference from the work to the family domain) than will dual-career fathers who do not use this work arrangement.

H2. Dual-career mothers who perform after-hours telecommuting will experience greater work-family conflict (i.e., greater role overload, greater interference from the family to the work domain, and greater interference from the work to the family domain) than will dual-career mothers who do not use this work arrangement.

H3. Traditional fathers who perform after-hours telecommuting will experience greater work-family conflict (i.e., greater role overload. greater interference from the family to the work domain, and greater interference from the work to the family domain) than will traditional fathers who do not use this work arrangement.

## Gender, W’ork-Family Conflict and Afier-Hours Telecommuting

Although it is commonly believed in the literature on telework that the shift to this arrangement will reduce the level of work-family conflict for dual-career mothers and enable them to obtain a better balance between the demands of the two roles (Shamir and Salomon 1985), two important theoretical arguments suggest the opposite. More specifically, Hall's (1972) theorv of sequential versus concurrent work and family demands and Karasek's work strain model indicate that dual-career women who perform AHT are more likely to experience work-family conflict than are dualcareer men who use this work arrangement.

Hall (1972) argued that men's family responsibilities are sequential to their work responsibilities while women's are simultaneous. For example, a woman may be called at work regarding a sick child (simultaneous demands) whcreas, unless the demands are urgent, the father can fulfill family role obligations after work hours (sequential). This vulnerability of the female work role to family demands is a major source of work-family role interference for women, since the sex role norm that women take responsibility for the family conflicts with the norms of the job role (Pleck 1984). It is also a major source of role overload for women who frequently add the demands of the work role with no concomitant decrease in family role responsibilities.

Karasek (1979) proposed a two-dimensional model of work strain that provides further justification for hypothesizing that women who use AHT will be more likely than men who use this work arrangement to experience work-family conflict. Karasek's model focuses on describing the objective conditions conducive to role conflict He identified two key operating forces: role demands and control. His job strain model asserts that jobs with similar demands or expectations may differ substantially in the amount of conflict generated by virtue of the degrce of individual control over the situation. Karasek (1979) envisions that a job characterized by high demands produces conflict only when accompanied by constraints placed on the worker in terms of the possible mechanisms for control.

By considering the family role as an occupation, Karasek's (1979) model can be applied in the context of this research. It can be argued that because men's work and family roles are sequential rather than simultaneous, men who perform AHT have more control over the distribution of their time than do women who use this work arrangement. In terms of Karasek's model, men can be characteriızed as having higher control than women with respect to their ability to balance work and family expectations. This, in turn, will lead to lower work-family conflict for men. The following related hvpothesis is tested in this analvsis:

H4. Dual-career mothers who perform after-hours telecommuting will experience greater work-family confliet (i.e., greater role overload. greater interference from the family to the work domain, and greater interference from the work to the family domain) than will dual-career fathers who use this work arrangement.

## Maternal Career Employment, Work-Family Conflict and After-Hours Telecommuting

A majority of the available research indicates a positive association between women's career employment and the perception of work-family conflict in their spouse (Lewis and Cooper 1988: Sekaran 1986: Pleck, Staines and Lang 1980; Pfeffer and Ross 1982). A dual-career household structure implies that both spouses wil have significant external responsibılities. This creates a more complex household schedule where time allocation decisions are difficult (Vitalari and Venkatesh 1989). With expanded work and family responsibilities for both partners the likelihood of work-family conflict increases (Greenhaus & Parasuraman 1986, Gupta and Jenkins 1985, Rosin 1989).

Karasek's (1979) model can be used to support the hy pothesis that traditional men who use AHT will be less likely than dual-career men who use this work arrangement to experience work-family conflict. The literature suggests that the higher familv demands associated with the dual-career lifestyle will reduce the amount of control dual-career men have over their family domains when working at home. Traditional men, who have wives at home to assume primary responsibility for childcare and housework, should have a greater degree of control of this domain. Karasek's (1979) job strain model would, therefore, support the following hypothesis:

H5. Dual-career fathers who perform after-hours telecommuting will experience greater work-family conflict (i.e., greater role overload, greater interference from the family to the work domain, and greater interference from the work to the family domain) than will traditional fathers who use this work arrangement.

Thus, MANCOVA's were run for each hvpothesis using the total time spent by the respondent and his/her spouse working at the office and at home as a covariate. For traditional men, the total hours worked by the respondent's spouse was zero.

## Discussion

The discussion is divided into two major parts. The first deals with the intra-group comparisons of Hypotheses 1, 2 and 3. The second part discusses the inter-group comparisons done to test Hypotheses 4 and 5.

## Intra-Group Comparisons

The findings obtained from the three intra-group comparisons done in this study were remarkably consistent. Career mothers and fathers who performed AHT experı- enced significantly more role overload and spillover of interference from work to family (and hence, greater work-family conflict) than did career mothers and fathers who did not use this work arrangement.

These results are consistent with those obtained by Vitalari and Venkatesh (1989), Kraut (1987), and Olson (1985), and support the contention that computer ownership legitimızes work at home. It also provides further evidence that the presence of a computer in the home is associated with the tendency to overwork. These data do not, however, enable one to determine the direction of this relationship. That “workaholics" may be more likely than “nonworkaholics" to adopt this work arrangement is as plausible an interpretation of the data as the conclusion that AHT leads employees to increase the amount of work they do

The increased levels of role overload experienced by individuals who performed AHT cannot be attributed to the longer hours spent working by these individuals in comparison to those who didn't perform AHT (we controlled for these variables). While men who conduct AHT reduce the amount of uime they spend with their family, the decrease in family time is significantly less than the increase in time devoted to work. Dual-career mothers who perform AHT do not decrease the amount of time they spend with their family. These data suggest that individuals who adopt AHT increase the amount of time they devote to work at the expense of personal time rather than family time. It may be that the loss of personal time increases the perception of role overload in these individuals.

While mothers and fathers who perform AHT are able to devote significantly more hours to paid work per week. thıs greater productivity appears to come at a cost: significantly higher levels of spillover of interference from the work to the family role. It has already been noted that men who select AHT devote significantly fewer hours to childcare each week than do their counterparts without computers. This perception of interference for men may, therefore, be due to the fact that increased time in work leaves less time for the family.

Women who perform AHT, on the other hand, do not decrease the amount of time they spent in childcare. It may be that, in their case, excessive involvement in the work role increases their perception that work interferes with family by arousing anxiety and guilt regarding their performance in traditional family roles. This contention is supported by previous research (1.e., Greenhaus and Beutell 1985, Gutek et al. 1988, Gupta and Jenkins 1985, Burke and McKeen 1989) and mav be equally applicable to those men in our study who are highl involved with their family.

## Inter-Group Comparisons

1. The Impact of Gender. The data indicate that the relationship between workfamily conflict and AHT may be related to gender. Dual-career women who perform AHT experience significantly more role overload than dual-career men who use this work arrangement. Statisticallv dual-career women who perform AHT have the same work demands as men who use this work arrangement (i.e.. spend the same number of hours a week in paid employment) and more family demands (i.e., more time spent in childcare). The increased levels of role overload experienced by dual-career women who perform AHT may be attributed to the fact that women seem to be able to add the role of wage carner but often have considerable difficulty dropping the roles of wife and mother (erborg 1985).

The finding that women who perform AHT are significantly less likely than any other group examined in thus analysis to have time for themselves is consistent with the above results. Women with computers at home spend more time with their children and work the same number of hours as their male counterparts. This leaves them little time for themselves

2. The Impact of Maternal Emplovment Status The data indicate that the relationship between work-family conflict and AHT is also related to maternal career employment. However, the results were opposite to those hypothesized. Traditional men who perform AHT experience significantly more spillover of interference from work to family than dual-career men who use this work arrangement. One possible explanation for this finding is that dual-career women, who have work demands of their own. may be more sympathetic to their husband's work demands than women in traditional families. Wives in traditional families who have been home with the children all day may be less understanding about work brought into the home domain and expect their spouse to assume family role responsibilities when in the family domain. Traditional men would, under such circumstances, feel more conflict about working at home rather than spending time with the family. Work done by Rossman (1983) on “computer widows seems to describe the situation that occurs when traditional men perform AHT.

It should be noted that, for all the groups examined in our study, spillover of interference from the work to the family role was greater than spillover of interference from the family to the work role. This finding is consistent with that observed by Gutek, Searle and Kelpa (1991) and supports their contention that these two types of perceived work-family conflict are separable and relatively independent of each other. There are several possible explanations for the differences in size of these two forms of work-family conflict. First, it is possible that the measure of spillover of family to work may not have captured all the demands on the individual (Gutek et al. 1991). This conclusion is consistent with the low alpha we found for the construct family to work. The second explanation rests on the fact that people spend more time in paid labor than family work. Because family work is more elastic than paid work people may limit the number of hours in the family so that they do not experience this kind of conflict (Gutek et al. 1991). Finallv, it is also possible that people who are likely to let their family take precedence over their work will be less likelv to adopt AHT.

## Limitations of the Research

This research has several limitations. First, the measures taken were all from the same questionnaire and open the results to questions of method variance. A more rigorous study would be longitudinal and involve multiple measures of data collection. A second weakness in this study is our operationalization of career-oriented. We used the simplistic assumption that somcone in a managerial or professional job would be career-oriented. Clearly, there is a psychological component to this measure.

Another limitation is the low internal consistency for the construct family to work. It is likely that our measure of the spillover of family to work did not adequately tap the construct. Clearly, more items should have been used. A fourth limitation of the study is the fact that self-report measures were used to collect all time data used in this analysis. Future research should identify objective ways of measuring the use of AHT and time spent in various activities.

A final limitation of the research is that with the present research design we cannot eliminate the possibility that a spurious correlation exists in our data. There may be an underlying construct such as perceived pressures at work that both cause people to select to perform AHT and cause greater work-family conflict. Future research in this area must be based on more comprehensive models or frameworks so that a wider variety of relationships can be considered simultaneously. While the present study represents a start in this direction, an examination of the literature suggests several variables that should be included in future work including: work conflict, family conflict, family responsibilities, amount of overtime performed, location of overtime work, mental and physical work and family demands, work involvement and family involvement. Additional studies should also examine whether there is a meaning ful conceptual difference between working at home and working at home on a computer.

## Conclusions

Individuals with computers at home work significantly more hours per week and a greater number of hours of overtime at home than do individuals without computers at home. This relationship occurs regardless of gender or wives’ employment status. It would appear that having a computer at home for AHT makes it possible for men and women to work longer hours, perhaps by increasing their ability to fit work demands in with family expectations or by legitimizing work at home.

The results of this study suggest that career-oriented parents who perform AHT do not enjoy many of the benefits of work at home touted in the popular press. AHT does not appear to be an effective way of coping with work-family conflict as the use of a computer for after-hours work at home is positively associated with spillover of interference from the work to the family domain and greater role overload. The increase in work schedule flexibility offered by a computer at home may help employees manage conflict in the work domain. perhaps by making it easier for them to perform overtime work. However. the increased work role flexibility offered by AHT is not sufficient to offset the increase in work-family conflict that is associated with the greater number of total hours worked per week, the greater amount of overtime work done in the home domain, and the conflict which may arise from a parent's guilt regarding performance of the family role.

Interference between work and family for individuals who perform AHT was found to be unidirectional. spilling over from the work domain into the family domain for all three groups included in the study. This suggests that the boundary between work and family is differentially permeable for individuals who use AHT Pleck (1977, 1984) found an asymmetrical boundary between work and family. He established that the direction of spillover of interference was dependent on gender. For women, the boundary is typically permeable in the direction of familv responsibilities that enter and affect work life. For men, the demands of the work role are allowed to intrude into the family role. Our results are interesting as they suggest that for both men and women, AHT is associated with a work-famıly boundary that is permeable in the direction of work responsibilities that enter and affect family life. It could be that women who perform AHT have embraced traditional male socio-cultural expectations (i.e., they try and manage their familes so that their family responsibilities do not interfere with their work efficiency)

Sex role expectations (Gutek et al. 1991) provide an alternative explanation for these findings. Traditional gender roles prescribe different role responsibilities for men and women: work is for men, family is for women. Research indicates that despite the many changes in gender roles in the past 30 vears, this tradition persists (Gutek et al. 1991). The women in our study spend more time in family work than the men (thereby fulfilling their family role responsibilities) while spending the same number of hours in paid employment. Women adopt AHT because they perceive that it will help them balance work and family demands. In reality, however, the additional pressures of separating work and family role demands, when work is brought into the home, seem to exacerbate the conflict experienced by women.

AHT appears to be especially harmful to dual-career women. Women who use AHT experience significantly more role overload than do all other groups considered in this study. They also report significantly more spillover of interference from work to family than do dual-career women who do not use this work arrangement. The time data offer one possible explanation for these findings. AHT is associated with increased work demands for women with no concomitant decrease in family role responsibilities. Socio-cultural expectations make it difficult for women to ignore family responsibilities, perhaps because their work and family roles are simultaneous The data suggest that these difficulties are not reduced when work is done in the home.

Our results suggest that organizations who wish to implement AHT work arrangements will realize one immediate benefit—greater productivity from their work force from the increased number of hours worked per week. This work arrangement may also help parents manage their work domain by providing them with greater control over their job and faster turnaround on their work. However, the results also indicate that such work arrangements should be implemented with care, as they may exacerbate, not relieve, work-family conflict caused by the spillover of interference from work to family and role overload.

Additional studies in this area must be undertaken, with different samples and a consideration of different consequences (other than work-family conflict), before the full impact of this work arrangement can be understood. One conclusion is clear, AHT is not the solution to the work-family dilemma for a large percentage of today's work force. Clearly, more research is needed on both individuals who adopt AHT and those who do not before valid conclusions can be drawn about the consequences of this work arrangement. Classification of people who perform AHT by the use they make of this work arrangement (1.e., light. medium and heavy users) and consideration of job and organizational differences should also be undertaken to fully understand how people use their computers at home and why they perform AHT. Future research should also be expanded to include nonprofessional employees of both genders. Finally, a better appreciation of the family consequences of this work arrangement could be gained if one examined the reactions of both the employees who perform AHT and their spouses.\*
