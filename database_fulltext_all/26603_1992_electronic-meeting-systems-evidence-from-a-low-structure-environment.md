# Electronic Meeting Systems: Evidence from a Low Structure Environment

Poppy Lauretta McLeod

School of Business Administration

University of Michigan

Jeffrey K. Liker

Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109-1234

Industrial and Operations Engineering

University of Michigan

The research literature to date on electronic meeting systems (EMS) has been dominated by studies of high structure systems. High and low structure are defined here in terms of the preponderance of influence that technology and groups exert on each other. High structure EMS attempt to directly influence group structure and processes through explicit rules and procedures embedded in the software. Low structure EMS do not explicitly build in rules or procedures that govern group interaction. Low structure EMS are hypothesized to increase task focus through the use of shared flexible software tools and shared views of joint work. Results of two experiments, that compare low structure EMS-supported groups to manually-supported groups on equality of participation, degree of task focus, task performance and member satisfaction, for two different tasks, are reported. The experiments found that this low structure EMS had no effect on participation equality or member satisfaction. Contrary to the hypotheses, the EMS was found to decrease task focus. The EMS led to marginally better task performance on a simple evaluative task, and to worse performance on a complex generative task. A revision of the study's theoretical model is developed which takes into account the nature of the task.

## Introduction

he rapidly increasing body of literature on electronic meeting systems (also commonly labeled as group decision support systems--GDSS) has established that these systems significantly affect group process and task performance. Nearly everyone who has written about these systems agrees that the structure electronic meeting systems provide for group task and communication processes is a main explanation for these effects. In fact, the literature has been dominated by studies of systems that provide structured decision-making tools (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1991, George et al. 1990, Easton et al. 1990, Steeb and Johnston 1981, Watson et al. 1988, Zigurs et al.

1988). The accumulated findings from these studies suggest that electronic meeting systems (EMS) can increase participation equality, increase task focus and increase decision quality (McLeod, in press, Lim and Benbasat 1991, Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1989).

The obiective of the current research was to explore the effects on group process and task performance of an EMS—the Capture Labthat did not provide specific structures. This was the first controlled experiment of Capture Lab effects on group process and performance. The question guiding this research was, will electronic support in the simple form of shared software and a shared workspace affect group process and task performance in ways similar to other, more structured, forms of electronic meeting support? To address this question we conducted two experiments using two different tasks in the Capture Lab—a low structure EMS.

## Low versus High Structure EMS

Much of the early theorizing about the impact of technology on organizational structure was marked by a deterministic view now referred to as the "technological imperative"(Khandwalla 1974, Fry 1982). Structure was viewed as an external, formal system of rules, regulations and constraints that determined the actions of individuals and groups. Technology presented one source of external constraints. As Markus and Robey (1988, p. 585) state, this perspective "views technology as an exogenous force which determines or strongly constrains the behavior of individuals and organizations."

In contrast, we take the perspective shared by organizational theorists such as Weick (1979), Van Maanen (1979) and Barley (1986) who reject this static deterministic view, and rather characterize social structures as “patterned action, interaction, behavior and cognition" (Barley 1986, p. 79). Thus, we see group structure as an emergent pattern created in the process of action and interaction (Giddens 1979, Poole et al. 1985, Thierauf 1989, Bostrom et al. 1990). Structure, as defined here, exists in all groups. Thus technology provides an “occasion for structuring" (Barley 1989), not a deterministic cause of structure.

The small group literature has repeatedly found that when left to their own devices, task-oriented groups often evolve structures that are suboptimal and sometimes even dysfunctional to task accomplishment (Hackman and Kaplan 1974, Hall and Watson 1970, Steiner 1972). A number of studies have shown that providing specific rules and resources for task interaction (i.e., attempting to influence group structures), can lead to improved group performance along a number of dimensions, such as information sharing (Stasser et al. 1989), task quality (Delbecq et al. 1975), conflict resolution (Hall and Watson 1970) and individual member satisfaction (Delbecq et al. 1975, Green and Taber 1980). Having a task-appropriate structure facilitates information exchange (Stasser et al. 1989, Thompson and Tuden 1964) and ensures that all group members have opportunities to participate (Green and Taber 1976). High structure EMS provide specific structuring techniques—the rules and resources to govern interaction—to help groups overcome the process losses (Steiner 1972) resulting from the suboptimal structures they typically develop on their own.

The designers of what we refer to as “high structure EMS" attempt to improve task effectiveness of groups by building into the software explicit rules and resources intended to govern interaction. Groups, however, will not always accept the structuring techniques presented by an EMS, or will not use them in ways intended by system designers (DeSanctis and Poole 1991, Poole and DeSanctis 1990). Relying on adaptive structuration theory, DeSanctis and Poole (1991, p. 2) have argued that a “group actively adapts the technology to its own ends, resulting in a restructuring of the technology, as it is meshed with the group's interaction system." Thus, just as EMS can restructure groups, so can groups restructure EMS. Which exerts more influence over the other is the basis of the distinction we make here between high and low structure EMS.

We suggest here that high vs. low structure EMS can be distinguished by the preponderance of influence exerted (See Figure 1). High structure systems are those designed specifically to change a group's preexisting structures. That is, these systems offer specific methods for communicating and approaching the task. The degree of change depends on the degree to which a group accepts and adopts the methods or techniques offered by the system. In contrast, low structure EMS are not designed to change group structure in any specific way. In this case, the degree to which a change in group structure (i.e., the pattern of interacting and approaching the task) occurs depends on groups creating structures for themselves, using tools provided by the system. Thus the group molds the technology more than the technology molds the group. In short, with a high structure system, the preponderance of influence is likely to be the EMS over the group, while with a low structure system, the preponderance of influence is likely to be the group over the EMS. In both types of systems, there is some influence exerted in both directions. When we refer to low and high structure throughout the paper, we mean it in terms of the preponderance of influence.

It is important to reiterate that all groups have some structure, irrespective of the type of EMS they may use. In the absence of a high structure EMS, a group could still be highly structured, and even groups that use a high structure EMS could have a weak structure. High vs. low structure EMS should be thought of in terms of the probability of changing a group's previously developed structure.

There have been some attempts in the literature to develop ways of categorizing EMS that are related to their structuring properties. DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) have proposed a model based on a three-level hierarchy ranging from low to high structure. In this model, Level 1 systems provide tools that simply remove barriers to communication and participation, Level 2 systems add structured decision modeling, and Level 3 systems induce specific group communication patterns. Nunamaker et al. (1991) argue that EMS be distinguished based on whether they offer structure or support. According to them, structure would include techniques such as decision modeling or the nominal group technique (NGT), while support would involve providing easy access to a database or allowing parallel communication. Their definition of structure appears consistent with DeSanctis and Gallupe's Level 2 and Level 3 systems, and their definition of support appears consistent with the description of a Level 1 system.

Here, we adopt the DeSanctis and Gallupe notion that the structure dimension is a hierarchical one. As features are added, the preponderance of influence EMS exerts over a group increases. Low structure EMS have the minimum features needed to facilitate group communication and task processes, while not imposing any particular process. The effect of adding EMS features is to increase the systems' influence on the group by providing more and more specific structures. Figure 2 presents a classification of the major EMS that have appeared in the literature as a function of their features and degree of structure. We discuss this classification in more detail in the following section.

Electronic Meeting Systems Features. Any EMS is a bundle of tools and characteristics, and for the most part research has not unbundled these characteristics (see Jarvenpaa et al. 1988, Easton et al. 1990 for exceptions). A particular EMS may offer both low structure and high structure tools. For example, Nunamaker et al. (1991) describe GroupSystem's Electronic Brainstorming tool as providing low task structure, while the Topic Commenter tool provides high task structure. In our characterization of particular EMS as high or low structure we consider the net effects of the system intended by its designers.

The judgments that resulted in the ranking shown in Figure 2 were based upon descriptions of designers' intended effects for these systems. The hierarchical nature of EMS structure is reflected in Figure 2 by the increasing number of features available as a function of structure. We define briefly each of these features, and then discuss each of the systems shown in Figure 2.

Decision tools refers to software that is designed to support a particular decision task or set of tasks. A stakeholder analysis tool is an example. Process tools refer to either software or software configurations specifically intended to induce a particular approach to working on a group's task. An example is a system that provides a rational problem-solving agenda (e.g., Watson et al. 1988).

Parallel communication refers to the ability of group members to input and receive ideas simultaneously with other members. Anonymous communication means that group members are not able to attribute ownership to any ideas.

Shared software refers to software that is equally available to all group members, and can thus be used for joint work. It includes, but is not limited to, software that accepts inputs from multiple sources and aggregates those inputs. Shared view means that all group members have equal access to the same view of their work.

GroupSystems from the University of Arizona (Dennis et al. 1988, Nunamaker et al. 1991) and SAMM (Software Aided Meeting Management), developed at the University of Minnesota (Watson et al. 1988, Zigurs et al. 1988) appear in Figure 2 as the highest structure EMS. Both of these systems provide all of the features currently available to structure a group's task and communication processes. They both provide individual work stations for each participant through which they can input items, and read items entered by other group members, in parallel and anonymously.

Structured decision-making tools are available on both systems. GroupSystems' tools are configured in a modular "tool kit", and they support a variety of tasks that range from brainstorming to stakeholder identification. The modules are designed so that everyone in the group works on the same module at the same time, and each module must be loaded by a designated person. In SAMM, on the other hand, individual group members have the flexibility of moving among the various tools on their own, independent of one another. For this reason, we have ranked GroupSystems as slightly more structured than SAMM.

Both systems provide process tools in the form of meeting management software. Nunamaker et al. (1991) report that GroupSystems' tool kit contains process tools that helps groups to plan, manage and debrief their sessions. Both Zigurs et al. (1988) and Watson et al. (1988) describe SAMM as providing a “rational problem-solving agenda."

OptionFinder is a keypad-based meeting system designed by Option Technologies, Inc. The University of Georgia has taken the lead in conducting research on Option-Finder (Watson et al. 1991). These researchers characterize it as providing a midlevel of technological support as compared to SAMM or GroupSystems. This system provides each meeting participant with a 10-button keypad for inputting responses to stimulus questions designed in a forced-choice comparison, or scale format. The primary decision tool in OptionFinder is a two-dimensional display of the group's responses to the stimulus materials. The dimensions, for example, can be used to rank issues on their importance for the present and for the future, or compare the views of two key stakeholders on an issue. Responses can also be displayed according to subgroupings of respondents.

The group exerts influence over the content of the stimulus materials, but has few options for question or display format. Although OptionFinder does not include explicit process tools, Watson et al. (1991) report that groups using it are more careful in premeeting planning than are groups using conventional meeting support. Similar to SAMM and GroupSystems, OptionFinder's individual keypads provide both parallel (individuals can input data simultaneously) and anonymous communication. The software, again is designed to aggregate and perform various analyses across the multiple inputs. Data are displayed for groups with a portable LCD monitor placed on an overhead projector.

The Capture Lab is the site of the current research. It was designed by Electronic Data Systems (Mantei 1988, Elwart-Keys et al. 1990, Horton et al. 1991). The Capture Lab does not provide software designed for specific tasks (each application could be used for several different tasks), nor does it provide any tools for structuring process.¹ Standard Macintosh® applications such as HyperCardR, Microsoft WordR and Excel® are available, and users may also bring their own software. These applications can be shared by group members through a common computer. The public display device is the monitor for this computer.

Individual meeting participants each have an individual computer in addition to the common computer. Although meeting participants can work in parallel on these individual computers, we want to make a distinction between parallel inputting and parallel communication. In the other EMS, users can both input data and read others' input in parallel. In the Capture Lab, users can input independently on their individual work stations, but they can only read the inputs of others, or share their own inputs, one person at a time, through the public monitor. Thus, sharing of inputs cannot be parallel in the Capture Lab. Similarly, inputting on one's individual computer in the Capture Lab is anonymous, but communicating those inputs is not. We will describe the Capture Lab in more detail in the Method section.

## Past EMS Research

Equality of participation and influence and degree of task focus have been the most frequently studied process variables in the EMS literature, and decision quality and group member satisfaction have been the most frequently studied outcome variables (Dennis et al. 1988, Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1989, McLeod, in press). Since this literature has been dominated by studies of high structure EMS, we will first review literature relevant to those systems. In the next section, we contrast these with low structure systems and present the study hypotheses.

Although there is some difficulty in comparing studies within this area because of task and system differences, some trends can be tentatively identified. We will discuss the effects of the major EMS with respect to their effects on the group process variables of participation equality and task focus, and the group outcome variables of decision quality and member satisfaction.

Equality of Participation. Nunamaker et al. (1991) have argued that equal, simultaneous access to the electronic communication channel and anonymity lead to participation equality in EMS-supported groups. Simultaneous electronic communication removes the logistic barriers to communication associated with the one-at-a-time speaking norms in ordinary face-to-face communication. Anonymity removes social barriers to participation that stem from evaluation apprehension and social status differences. The increased inputs from all group members should also result in increased decision quality.

The empirical evidence suggests that EMS use does in fact increase participation equality and reduce domination by one or a few individuals (McLeod, in press; Lim and Benbasat 1991). Nunamaker et al. (1991) have summarized a number oflab and field studies conducted with GroupSystems, and have concluded that this system does produce equal participation (see also George et al. 1990). Watson et al. (1991), in a field study of OptionFinder, reported that users perceived equality of participation to be higher than with manual support.

Zigurs et al. (1988), using SAMM, reported that influence was more evenly distributed in groups supported by that system than unsupported groups. Lewis (1982), using a similar system, reported that EMS use reduced individual dominance. Steeb and Johnston (1981), using a system that made available individual workstations to meeting participants, also reported more equal levels of participation in the computer-supported than unsupported groups. Research on dispersed groups has also found that participation in computer-mediated groups was more equally distributed than in unsupported groups (e.g., Siegel et al. 1986).

Degree of Task Focus. The research literature on group decision making has shown that structuring a group's task processes will increase task focus (e.g., Delbecq et al. 1975), which is expected to increase performance quality. The tools provided by high structure EMS are intended to have this effect. Empirical studies have found that use of structured EMS decision tools does increase task focus (McLeod, in press; Lim and Benbasat 1991).

Nunamaker et al. (1987) reported high task focus and little attention to interpersonal interaction in groups supported by the Arizona system. Nunamaker et al. (1991) summarize lab and field studies using the Arizona system and conclude that task focus does increase with this system use.

The pattern of results from the Minnesota laboratory are similar. Watson et al. (1988), using a resource allocation task, reported that task focus was greater in computer supported than manually supported groups. Using a similar system, Gallupe et al. (1988) reported greater depth of analysis in supported than unsupported groups with a business case analysis task. Recently, Watson et al. (1991) have reported that groups supported by OptionFinder have a clearer and more focused agenda and set of questions than unsupported groups. These effects seem to be due to greater premeeting planning when using OptionFinder than no electronic support.

Decision Quality. The results reviewed above would suggest that high structure EMS should increase decision quality. Input from a greater number of participants and increased task focus should provide higher quality information to decision makers (Kraemer and King 1988), should increase the amount of information exchange (Jarvenpaa et al. 1988), and increase the depth of analysis and efforts to clarify the task (Nunamaker et al. 1991), thus resulting in high quality decisions. While the empirical evidence in general supports this view, the results have been mixed. Not all researchers have found effects, and the effects that have been found have not been large (McLeod, in press). There are apparently no studies that have found that EMS hurt decision quality.²

Researchers using GroupSystems have reported limited evidence for increased decision quality (Dennis et al. 1988, Nunamaker et al. 1991). In separate field studies, Dennis et al. (1990) and Nunamaker et al. (1989) reported that users perceived their decisions to be of a higher quality when using GroupSystems than when using conventional meeting support. These results should be interpreted cautiously, however, because they are based on retrospective self-reports of users, and are not compared to responses from a control group. Further, in a laboratory study, George et al. (1990) reported no significant difference in decision quality with GroupSystems compared to manual support.

The results from laboratory studies using the University of Minnesota systems have likewise been mixed. Zigurs et al. (1988) reported no effect of technology on decision quality using SAMM, and Gallupe and McKeen (1990), using a similar system, likewise found no significant effect of technology on decision quality. On the other hand, Gallupe et al. (1988), using another system similar to SAMM, reported that the supported groups performed significantly better than did the unsupported groups.

Steeb and Johnston (1981), using a system that implemented a decision tree process, found that this support significantly increased decision quality. OptionFinder users have reported that discussions supported by this technology were more productive, effective and of a higher quality than manual support (Watson et al. 1991), but again, these are self-reported retrospective data.

Group Member Satisfaction. Past research in the small group literature has shown that structuring a group's task processes leads to higher cohesiveness and satisfaction with task and interpersonal processes among members (Hackman and Kaplan 1974). The results of EMS laboratory studies, however, has generally shown the technology to decrease satisfaction or to have no effect.

Gallupe et al. (1988) and Gallupe and McKeen (1990), both using an EMS similar to Minnesota's SAMM, and a business case analysis task, found lower satisfaction among individual group members supported by this technology. Watson et al. (1988), using SAMM, similarly found technology-supported groups reported lower levels of satisfaction. George et al. (1990) used GroupSystems and also reported that technology supported groups were less satisfied than unsupported groups. Sharda et al. (1988) at Oklahoma State University, reported that their EMS had no effect on user satisfaction in a laboratory study with a business simulation task and a group decision support system that was “essentially a financial analysis model"(p. 149).

On the other hand, results from field studies have shown users to be more satisfied with their group processes when they use EMS. Dennis et al. (1990) and Nunamaker etal. (1989) reported that users were more satisfied with using GroupSystems than whatever process they had used previously. Watson et al. (1991) similarly reported that OptionFinder users felt more satisfaction with these meetings than conventional ones. Again, these results should be interpreted cautiously because they are retrospective self-reports, and are not compared to responses from a control group. DeSanctis et al. (1991) and Poole et al. (1991) report field studies using Minnesota's SAMM These researchers report that user satisfaction depended on how the groups actually used the technology.

## Theoretical Model and Study Hypotheses

Figure 3a summarizes our model of how high structure EMS affect task groups The decision structuring tools increase task focus, and the communication structuring tools increase participation equality. Task focus and participation equality both tend to increase decision quality, and participation equality also increases member satisfaction.

Figure 3b shows the model of the expected effects of a low structure EMS on task groups. As described earlier, the Capture Lab does not permit simultaneous access to the shared monitor (Scott 1988), nor does it support anonymous communication. Although all group members have equal opportunity to control the shared public monitor, only one person at a time may. There is nothing about the technology or the facility that prohibits verbal communication, however. In fact, the verbal communication channel is important for regulating the pattern of control over the electronic channel (Mantei 1989), and the protocol that governs control over the electronic channel closely resembles the one-at-a-time speaking rule that characterizes most verbal communication.

Other research using the Capture Lab has suggested that the pattern of control over the public display is related to preexisting social structures in the groups (Austin et al. 1990, Mantei 1989). Austin et al. reported that higher status individuals in student groups took control of the electronic channel more often than did lower status individuals. Mantei (1989) found that the patterns in groups of managers varied, but tended to preserve already existing power relationships. In the current study, we thus did not expect Capture Lab use to affect participation equality.

H1. Groups supported by a low structure EMS will not differ in equality of participation from manually supported groups.

Although low structure EMS do not provide tools designed to support specific tasks or specific task processes, we do not mean to argue that these systems are not capable of structuring a group's process. Rather, we argue that there is no particular structure that is inherent in these systems. It is up to a group to use the system to impose a structure on itself, A group may adopt new structures, or use the system in ways that preserve preexisting structures. Similar to high structure EMS, there are appropriate and inappropriate ways for groups to adapt these systems, and the quality of a group's outcomes will depend on appropriate usage.

Shared flexible tools combined with a shared, directly accessible workspace can also lead to increased task focus. In a low structure EMS such as the Capture Lab, groups have complete flexibility to use jointly any tool that can run on the system. This flexibility may force a group to consider the nature of their task and their approach to it when they choose software to support their work. Further, it is up to the group to choose a communication pattern that is suitable to the task and the group's working style. For example, some groups may choose to designate one member to assume the role of scribe, while other groups may find it more effective to rotate that role. This decision depends both on the nature of the task and certain characteristics of the groups themselves. We argue that making these decisions consciously will increase a group's focus on their task.

We also expected the shared workspace to increase task focus. In the Capture Lab, the public display is actually the monitor to a computer that is equally accessible to all group members. The public display in the Capture Lab is salient and contains the only copy of the group's common work. Thus, any changes that are made to this shared information draws the attention of all group members. Working on the shared monitor is public, and changes generally must be accompanied by tacit or explicit consensus. In short, the Capture Lab technology makes it easy, even compelling, for group members to pay attention to how their work takes shape. We would thus expect that more attention will be paid to the task in this environment than in a conventional meeting facility.

Jarvenpaa et al. (1988) and Rein and Ellis (1988), reporting data from the same study, used similar reasoning in predicting the impact of an EMS configuration that was similar to the Capture Lab. At MCC, they conducted an experiment with three conditions: electronic bulletin board (EBB), electronic workstation (EWS) and control. Their EBB condition provided an electronic “public view surface" which group members could walk up to and draw or write on using electronic tools. The EWS condition provided electronic workstations for each participant, but a flip chart provided the public view. Although neither of these conditions matches exactly the Capture Lab environment, these researchers found that the EBB condition produced significantly more task-oriented remarks than did the EWS condition. We also expected the increases in task performance quality that are associated with increased task focus.

H2. Groups supported by a low structure EMS will show more task-oriented communication than will manually-supported groups.

H3. Groups supported by a low structure EMS will produce higher quality decisions than will manually-supported groups

We expected the Capture Lab to have little or no effect on user satisfaction with their task or communication processes in a laboratory experiment. Since this facility provides minimal task structure, we would not expect to find the increased satisfaction associated with this structuring. Moreover, since we do not expect changes in participation patterns, there should not be the increased satisfaction associated with participation equality. Finally, we would not expect any of the negative effects associated with technological obtrusiveness, again because of the minimal structure imposed by this facility.

H4. Groups supported by a low structure EMS will not differ in satisfaction from manually-supported groups.

## Method

This study consisted of two experiments that followed the same research design, but used different tasks and software. Since this was an exploratory study for this particular EMS, we used two different tasks that each took advantage of different Capture Lab software, though we had no specific hypotheses about the effects of these different tasks. We present first the general method that applied to both experiments. then describe the specific tasks and measures for each experiment separately. The results for both experiments will be presented together.

Subjects. The subjects were recruited from graduate and undergraduate Organizational Behavior classes in the Business and Engineering schools of a large university. As part of their class requirements, these subjects were assigned to groups of 4 or 53 members for an entire semester (14 weeks), and 34 of these intact groups volunteered for the experiment. There were 10 groups from an undergraduate engineering school class, 10 groups from an undergraduate business school class and 14 groups from a graduate business school class. Individual students were initially placed in groups by their instructors in a random manner. The groups from each class that volunteered for the study were then assigned randomly to one of the experimental conditions— EMS-supported or manually supported—so that the number of groups from each class was equivalent in the conditions. The experimental sessions began five weeks after the groups formed and ran until approximately two weeks before the end of the semester, thus spanning a period of seven weeks.

There was clearly variation across groups. Groups who participated in the experiment early in the semester were undoubtedly less far along in their group development than groups who participated in the later sessions in the semester; engineering majors are different from business majors; graduate students differ in work experience and maturity from undergraduate students. In one sense this heterogeneity is helpful as it increases the generalizability of the results, but it could contaminate the results if there were systematic differences in the types of groups assigned to the experimental and control groups. The process of random assignment within class type resulted in an equivalent distribution of groups according to type and group age in the two experimental conditions. Analyses of the data showed no significant differences between these group types on any of the dependent variables.

Experimental Design and Setting. Figure 4 summarizes the experimental design and procedures. Groups were assigned randomly to either the EMS-supported or the manually-supported condition for a total of 17 groups in each condition. An important question is what the appropriate comparison condition to EMS-supported is, and how that condition should be labeled.

Our “control" condition provided the tools that would normally be available in a conventional meeting room. As will be described in detail below, all groups in this condition were provided with paper and pencil for individual group members and a common pad of paper (8.5″ × 1 1") for group responses. Thus, we label this condition as “manually-supported." The support we provided in this condition was similar to what other researchers have described as manually-supported. For example, Gallupe et al. (1988) described their manually-supported condition: “a paper agenda substituted for the computer agenda and a flipchart substituted for the public screen"(p. 631). They contrasted it with their unsupported (or baseline) condition: “. . . not provided with any manual or technological support. They constituted naturally interacting groups, free of any intervention or instruction on how to approach the task" (p. 631). We did not provide a flipchart, but did provide individual paper and pencil and a common writing space. Thus, we believe this condition was closer to a manually-supported than an unsupported condition.

The EMS used was the Capture Lab (Mantei 1988, 1989), a computer-supported meeting room located in a research center that belongs to Electronic Data Systems. Figure 5 presents a diagram of the Capture Lab's layout. It contains an oval conference table with eight Macintosh $\mathbf { I I ^ { T M } }$ microcomputers recessed into the table so that meeting participants can easily view each other and the public screen. Users have access to any available software that runs on a Macintosh. Information can be easily manipulated (e.g., through cutting and pasting), with the added advantage that all group members have equal access to the information display, while remaining in their seats.

Each participant has the option to control at will, with his or her personal keyboard and mouse, a ninth computer. This ninth computer has as its monitor a large public display screen located on the wall at one end of the room. A preemptive protocol (Scott 1988) is used to gain control of the public screen. Only one person controls the public workstation (computer and screen) at a time. Whenever they wish, participants take control by pressing a button on their own keyboard, and then their mouse and keyboard operate the public workstation until someone else takes control. The public workstation is separate from all other participants' workstations. At any given time, each participant can control only the public workstation or a private workstation, but not both. For this study only the shared computer was used and private screens were disabled. Thus, participants used the keyboard and mouse at their workstation only to control the public computer and display screen, and only one partici-. pant at a time could have control.

Microphones and video cameras are hidden behind panels and a one-way mirror joins the conference room to an observation gallery. All experimental sessions were videotaped. Groups in both experimental conditions met in this room, but all the equipment was turned off in the manually supported condition. Since the computers are recessed in the table participants could see each other without obstruction (Mantei 1988).

Computer-supported groups were given ten minutes of general training on how to use the technology. They were shown how to take control of the public monitor, enter information on the keyboard, and manipulate information using the mouse. Each participant had a chance to try this once. Ten minutes of training seemed adequate as the software was simple to use and a pre-experimental questionnaire found that most members had prior experience with Macintosh computers. Immediately preceding each task, these groups received additional training on using the specific software for the task.

Dependent Measures. The dependent variables were equality of participation (H1), degree of task focus (H2), decision quality (H3) and member assessments of group performance and satisfaction (H4). These variables will be described for each of the two experiments. Table 4 summarizes the study hypotheses and the measures used to test each one.

## Experiment 1

Equality of Participation and Task Focus. These variables reflect group processes that are directly observable. For two reasons, we measured these variables using outside observer ratings in real time. First, subjects are busily engaged in the task and cannot record their own processes in real time. Second, subjects are likely to be biased in making ratings of their own behaviors, especially retrospective ratings.

We used SYMLOG—A System of the Multiple-Level Observation of Groups (Bales and Cohen 1979)—to make the ratings of participation and task focus, because it is a proven group process observation tool with known reliability. SYMLOG measures three bipolar dimensions of interpersonal behavior: Dominant-Submissive, Task-Socioemotional and Friendly-Unfriendly. In the current study we focus on the first two dimensions as they capture the variables that are relevant to the current study.

Equality of participation (H1) was measured by the Dominant-Submissive dimension. Bales and Cohen (1979) have defined this dimension in terms of participation To the degree that group members are active, talkative and forceful in their style, observers were trained to rate them as dominant. Members that are not active, participate little, and are not forceful would be rated as submissive. When some individuals dominate most of a meeting, while others are withdrawn and submissive, we define this as low participation equality.

We derived two measures from each group's scores on this dimension. First was dominance distance, defined as the absolute difference in the dominance scores, computed from observers' ratings, between the most and least dominant group members during each experiment (i.e., the range). The greater the distance between the most and least dominant group members, the less equal the participation. The second score was dominance dispersion, measured as the standard deviation among the dominance scores of group members. The wider the dispersion (i.e., higher standard deviation), the less equal the participation.

Task focus (H2) was measured by SYMLOG's Task-Socioemotional dimension. This dimension captures the degree to which the group is focussed on task performance as compared to socioemotional behaviors such as joking around and making light conversation unrelated to the task. One can think of certain socioemotional behaviors that directly contribute to the task, such as encouraging an otherwise quiet member to speak up and then praising that person. We instructed the observers to code such supportive behaviors that directly serve task performance as task behaviors. The number of each person's task-oriented coded acts as a percentage of their total coded acts was calculated, and a mean score of this measure for each group was computed.

Bales and Cohen (1979) obtain the interrater reliability of SYMLOG measures by computing the intraclass correlation from a rater by group mber analysis of variance. Using the Spearman-Brown formula (Rosenthál and Rosnow 1984), we obtained an interrater reliability across the 34 sessions of r = 0.85.

A HyperCard™ program was written by staff members at the Capture Lab that accepts SYMLOG codes directly on-line and analyzes them. Using a mouse, 'observers point to icons on a computer screen that represent group members and specific SYMLOG codes. With a click of the mouse, observers can record “messages" that convey who speaks to whom, what time and what type of behavior. Observers can, if necessary, further annotate these “messages" using the keyboard.

Eleven people were trained as observers for this study. Only one had been familiar with SYMLOG prior to the study. They received 20 hours of training and practice with SYMLOG, including how to use the computer interface. Three-person observation teams were assigned to each experimental session. The membership on these teams varied, and no attempt was made to randomly assign observers to experimental session because of scheduling difficulties. The final schedule did not result in any observer being assigned disproportionately to any experimental condition.

The observation teams watched the groups from the observation gallery behind the one-way mirror. Although the observers obviously were not blind to the condition of computer support, they were blind to the purpose and hypotheses of the study. Group members were not able to see the observers, but were told of their presence. At the end of each session, groups were given a tour of the observation room and were introduced to the observers.

Performance Quality. Experiment 1 used a project planning task (Lafferty and Associates 1975, Kernaghan and Cooke 1990), referred to as Task 1. It is an item ranking task similar to the NASA Moon Survival problem (Hall and Watson 1970). It is also similar to the task used by Turoff and Hiltz (1982). This kind of task is commonly used as an experiential learning exercise to demonstrate the benefits of group decision making. Subjects are given a list of 20 management activities arranged in random order that would be used to manage a hypothetical project (e.g., find qualified people to fill positions, develop possible alternative courses of action and coordinate day-to-day activities). They are told that they have been assigned to a newly formed task team which is to take over a secret project presently being handled by research and development. Their task was to reach consensus on the proper sequence of the activities. Unlike other uses of this type of task, the subjects did not first solve the task individually.

The manually supported groups received hard copies of the list, one for each member, pencils and a single blank 8.5″ × 1 1" form on which to record the group rank ordering. No other support was provided (e.g., whiteboard, flipchart, etc.). The list appeared on the public display screen for the computer-supported groups. The MORERoutlining program was the software used for this task. The subjects were given specific training on how to manipulate the item list in this program. Using the mouse, any group member at will could cause each item to move to any position on the list by “grabbing" it and “dragging" it to the desired position. In both conditions, groups were given 30 minutes to complete the task.

We expected the advantages of the Capture Lab for this task to be the shared view, equal access to manipulating the workspace, and the particular suitability of MORER for this ranking task. The task involves sequencing activities and MORE® is designed to allow one to grab and move items in varying sequences for constructing outlines. The ability to diredfy manipulate the items on the public screen provided a direct graphical analogy to tħe cognitive task of trying different sequences. By contrast, the manually supported groups had to write the rankings on the hard copy and could not easily change or share a view of the order. Each member had their own $8 . 5 ^ { \prime \prime } \times 1 1 ^ { \prime \prime }$ copy of the list of activities, so they were all initially focused on different documents.

Performance was measured (H3) as the absolute difference between the subjects rank ordering and the rank order determined by experts in project planning. Thus, the lower the score, the better the performance. These correct answers were provided by the management development firm that created the task (see Lafferty and Associates 1975 for further information). While the correct answers are based on judgments, there is some clear logic to the sequence.

The task instructions indicate that the activities should be ordered in the proper sequence for Planning, Organizing. Implementing, and Controlling the project. These words were emphasized by the researchers in their instructions to the groups. Thus. groups who did well generally recognized the significance of these categories and first sorted the detailed activities into these four categories. Beyond that, there was generally a clear logic to the order of activities. For example, they should have recognized the activity “gather and analyze the facts of the current project situation" as a Planning activity which is a necessary first step before any other planning can take place. An example of an Organizing activity is to “establish qualifications for new positions"which must precede “determining the allocation of resources."The Implementing activity “training and developing personnel" cannot take place until "qualified people are found to fill the positions." By and large, the sequencing of activities is quite logical and if any member recognizes the logic the “right answer"is apparent to all. Thus, the lower the score, the better the performance. According to McGrath's (1984) task typology, this is an evaluative task.

Participant Assessment. Participant views of the meetings (H4) were measured with a questionnaire, completed at the end of the experiment. The questionnaire contained items that measured Hackman's (1989) “intermediate criteria" for group effectiveness, adapted from his model of group effectiveness. He argues that to the extent that group interaction meets these criteria the group is likely to be effective in meeting task and member goals. The scales included in the questionnaire were: (1) level of member knowledge and skill applied, (2) level of member effort applied and (3) appropriateness of task strategy. The respective reliabilities of these three scales, measured by Cronbach's alphá were: 0.57, 0.61, 0.79. We will take into account the low reliabilities for the first two scales in interpreting the results.

The questionnaire also contained items adapted from Schein (1969) that assessed satisfaction with the group's task effort and social relations. These eight items were measured on 5-point Likert scales. The items, all highly correlated, were combined into a single scale with reliability of 0.82 as measured by Cronbach's alpha.

## Experiment 2

Experiment 2 immediately followed Experiment 1, and the same subjects participated. Participation equality, task focus and member assessments were all measured in the same way as in Experiment 1. We here describe the task and measures of decision quality used in Experiment 2.

In this experiment we used an in-basket simulation task. The task required subjects to take the role of a manager, with limited time and no assistance, faced with an in-basket full of correspondence requiring response. The version used here, adapted from Whetten and Cameron's (1984) Tampa Pump & Valve case, consisted of nine pieces of correspondence. The subjects' task was to reach consensus on what response to make for each piece of correspondence, and to write out those responses. The responses ranged from drafts of documents to instructions to subordinates. McGrath (1984) would classify this as a generative task. Fifty minutes were allotted for this task in both conditions.

Groups in both conditions received the hard copies of the background description of the Tampa Pump & Valve company, which explained the manager's role in the company and that of other key managers and subordinates. After reading the background material the groups in the EMS-supported condition were then presented with the nine pieces of in-basket correspondence on the public display screen. We programmed a HyperCard™ application so that group members could view one in-basket item at a time and scroll through the items. A table of contents of the items was provided at the beginning of the HyperCard stack and when members pointed to items on this list with the mouse, the selected item was shown on the screen in its entirety. We provided a blank, scrollable window directly to the right of each item where groups typed their responses. The original pieces of correspondence remained visible while the responses were being composed. Groups received specific training on how to move between the items and to use the simple word-processing features they would need to type their responses in the blank window (i.e., scrolling, cutting, pasting, deleting and inserting).

In the manually supported groups, after reading the background information, each group member received a hard copy packet of the in-basket items, each on a separate page. A single packet of ${ 8 . 5 ^ { \prime \prime } } \times { 1 \bar { 1 } ^ { \prime \prime } }$ blank pages for the group responses and pencils were provided.

In-basket simulation tasks are most frequently used as tools to assess management aptitude (Meyer 1970, Gill 1979) where good and bad performance is determined by criteria that vary with the specific organization or position. The assessment of performance quality on the in-basket task used in this study was based upon both the nature of the hypothetical company as described in the exercise's background information, and basic standards of document appearance, grammar and spelling. Thus, responses that showed awareness of underlying problems in the company (e.g., that job roles were not clearly understood), that fostered communication among people (e.g., specifically requested certain people to meet together), were thorough and had a neat appearance were judged as better than responses not showing these characteristics.

A scoring protocol for the in-basket responses, consisting of six scales, was developed for this experiment. Three scales judged the overall Writing Quantity and Quality and three scales judged the “Depth of Response."Depth of response measures assessed how carefully groups had analyzed the case. The scales are as follows:

## Writing Quantity and Quality

(1) Number of words in responses.

(2) Formatting—degree to which responses were complete and formatted, e.g., using a proper memo format.

(3) Error Free—degree to which responses were free of spelling, grammatical and logical errors.

## Depth of Responses

(1) Meeting Planning—degree of detail in arranging any meetings called for in the response.

(2) Number of People Addressed—how many people the responses were directed to.

(3) Problem Awareness—degree to which the responses showed an awareness of underlving problems in the company (i.e., did subjects use responses only to address the symptoms in the particular piece of mail they were currently responding to, or did they use responses to address underlying causes evident by reading across the in-basket items?).

Two graduate students in business. blind to the conditions and purposes of the experiment, coded each set of responses. In order that the coders would not be influenced by the difference between computer-printed and handwritten responses produced in the sessions by group members, all the responses were retyped with the same type-face, preserving the spelling and actual formatting used by the subjects (i.e., line-spacing, paragraphs, caps vs. lower-case, etc.). The two coders agreed on 81% of their judgments. Differences were resolved by one of the authors, who was blind to the experimental condition of each response.

In general, we expected that high scores on each of these measures reflected good performance and that the computer-supported groups would score higher than the manually supported groups on the measures. We expected Writing Quantity and Quality to benefit from the word processing features and the shared view which allowed multiple eyes to see the document as it was being composed. Thus, we expected more contributions which would lengthen the document, more suggestions for formatting, and more document editing to remove errors. We did not make spelling or grammar checkers accessible to the computer-supported groups.

We expected greater Depth of Responses because of the shared view of the in-basket items and of the responses being composed as well as the ease of moving between in-basket items. We expected that the ability for all to see the same view of the items and move easily between items would facilitate richer discussions about items as compared to the manual groups in which individuals had to, in effect, interrupt the meeting to individually read the items from their own hard copy. Thus, the computer would facilitate greater collective thinking. Meeting planning and thinking about who should be on the distribution list for the communication might benefit from these richer discussions as well as from the shared view of the responses as they are being composed. Awareness of underlying problems would be fostered by the ability to jointly view and discuss the in-basket items and quickly move among them.

## Discussion

Table 4 summarizes the findings with respect to each hypothesis. Although H1 was supported and H3 partially supported, the study results taken as a whole do not support the model underlying the study. We found that for two different tasks, Capture Lab use affected group process in the same way, but affected task performance in different ways. On a simple, evaluative task, despite lower task focus and no effects of EMS on participation, EMS-supported groups performed better than manually-supported groups. On a complex, generative task, effects on group process were the same, but the EMS led to inferior task performance. Neither of these results is consistent with the study model.

The finding of no EMS effect on the distribution of participation departs from previous research on computer supported and mediated communication (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1990. Zigurs et al. 1988. Kiesler et al. 1986) which has found EMS technology to equalize participation. In fact, on the average, the participation of groups in both conditions in the present study was quite unequal. That is, the dominance and dispersion scores were generally high, and did not vary by condition of technology support. Austin et al. (1990) reported that individuals who were rated as influential by teammates prior to meeting in the Capture Lab held control of the shared screen disproportionately more time than did less influential members. These results suggest that in this low structure environment, groups retained the communication patterns that had existed previously. The Austin et al. findings are consistent with the current argument that in a low structure EMS environment groups exert more influence over the technology than the technology exerts over groups. Groups in the Capture Lab appeared to use the electronic communication channel so that it reinforced existing communication structures.

Our finding that manually supported groups displayed a higher percentage of taskoriented behavior than did the computer-supported groups also departs from previous research that has generally found increased task focus with EMS use (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1987, Watson et al. 1988), and with the theoretical model of low structure EMS effects we have presented in Figure 3b. One might argue that the technology made the EMS supported group more efficient, leaving more time for joking and small talk. However, in both experimental conditions the degree of taskoriented behavior was high. Moreover, both EMS-supported and unsupported groups on the average took the full time allotted, and in post-experiment interviews subjects in both conditions reported feeling time pressure to finish.

In retrospect, the greater socioemotional behavior of EMS-supported groups can be interpreted as consistent with the intention of the designers of the Capture Lab who were attempting to design an environment that facilitated interaction but did not interfere with groups'“normal" working patterns (Mantei 1988, 1989). The supported groups had their information in public view and could easily shift their focus from the screen to each other. Based on our observations of the experimental sessions, it appeared that this promoted lively interpersonal behavior right from the start. When an individual was changing the sequence of activities in project planning or typing responses to in-basket correspondence, others watched and commented on that activity. Since the manually supported groups had all of their information on paper the first thing they did was individually read the list of projects in the planning task and the memos in the in-basket task. There were long periods of silence, and interactions tended to proceed slowly as participants shifted their focus from reading to discussion. Documentation was also an isolated activity; other group members tended to wait silently as the scribe recorded their ideas, also silently. We suspect the low-structure technology therefore supported easy movement between task and social behavior.

We found the results concerning the technology's effects on task performance surprising and interesting. As expected, the EMS-supported groups performed better on the project planning task than did the manually supported groups. We suspect that the graphic display of the list of activities that allowed meeting participants to see the same list simultaneously at all times, and to see the items physically change order in the sequence, accounted for superior and more consistent performance by the computer-supported groups.

On the in-basket task, however, EMS-supported groups' performance was somewhat poorer in terms of physical appearance. Their responses were shorter, less formatted and tended to be cryptic and abbreviated as compared to the manually supported groups' responses. Figure 6 presents examples of typical manual and EMS group responses.

The public, nonanonymous nature of working in the Capture Lab suggests a possible explanation for this result. Perhaps we are seeing the results of“social facilitation" (Zajonc 1965, 1972; Berkowitz 1975). Research on this phenomenon has shown that generally, the presence of other people has a positive effect on performance of easy. well-known tasks, but tends to disrupt performance of difficult, poorly understood tasks. The students who served as the subjects in this study were familiar with ranking tasks from their organizational behavior classes (e.g., NASA Moon Survival). but the in-basket task, requiring composing and typing novel responses to ambiguous situations, was a more complex and unusual task than the project planning task. Thus, the public nature of working with the display monitor may have been more disruptive to in-basket than project planning performance.

The results showed a tendency, marginally significant, for EMS-supported groups to display lower awareness than manually supported groups of general problems at the hypothetical company. This finding was surprising. We expected the ease of moving between the in-basket documents in HyperCard® would help subjects make connections between the memos, and to notice particular issues that recurred. One question these results raises is whether this kind of task, one that requires groups to process a lot of information, is well suited to 100% collaboration. Perhaps as group members all read a document together, they do not process the information as completely as they would with each member individually responsible for single documents. When reading jointly, each individual may skim, assuming that others are reading more thoroughly, or will pick up what they may have missed.

Finally, we found no effects of EMS on member assessments of their meetings. Although this result is consistent with our hypothesis, we think it should be interpreted cautiously because the reliability of some of these measures was low. The satisfaction measures, adapted from Schein's (1969) work, had high reliability, and it appears that EMS had no effect on these measures. The measures derived from Hackman (1989) similarly were not affected by EMS, but the reliability of those measures was low. It appears that low structure EMS has little effect on user satisfaction with task and interpersonal processes, but this needs further investigation, with more reliable measures.

The results obtained here suggest a modification of the study's theoretical model. One hypothesized modification is shown in Figure 7. This revised model hypothesizes that without providing any specific structure, shared tools and a shared view create shared understanding among group members of their work. This shared understanding, in turn, contributes to task performance quality. The way in which groups reach a shared understanding of their work depends on the nature of the task. For example, when working on a simple task such as the project planning task, shared views and shared flexible tools may create a common understanding in a relatively straightforward way. On the other hand, the greater information processing needs of a more complex task such as the in-basket task may require the addition of structure before a shared understanding can be achieved.

This hypothesized model is consistent with the past literature on high structure EMS which has argued that task performance gains are due to the addition of particular structures. The results of the current study suggest that as tasks become more complex, the need for structure may increase. This implies that users of low structure EMS should explicitly develop structures appropriate to their task. We do not believe that this hypothesized model or the findings from this study imply that low structure EMS are necessarily inappropriate for complex tasks. Rather, we would suggest that groups using low structure EMS receive training or facilitation that will help them to develop appropriate structures for themselves.

A related future research direction would be to compare the effects of EMS support with the effects of group process training and human facilitation. One question would be, when starting with a low structure EMS, how much additional structuring would be needed to improve performance on a variety of tasks?

This study has a number of limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. One, the study had only one comparison to the EMS-supported condition. The manual condition in the current study differed from the manual conditions in other EMS studies (e.g., Zigurs et al. 1988; Watson et al. 1988) in that we did not provide a board or flip chart as an analogue to the public display monitor. Having such a condition would have enabled us to separate the effects of the software from the effects of the common view. The comparison condition we used nevertheless mirrored a common style of meeting. In fact, item ranking tasks such as the Project Planning Situation or NASA Moon Survival are almost always administered in exactly the way done in this study's manual condition (e.g., Turoff and Hiltz 1982). Future work might also include a high structure EMS condition to, enable direct comparison between low and high structure systems.

A second limitation is this study has treated the EMS as a bundle of features, similar to nearly all the EMS research that now appears in the literature. We thus can only say that the effects of the Capture Lab in this study depended on a combination of the shared workspace and the shared software used. This may have been a particular problem in Experiment 1 in which we used MORER for the project planning task. Since MORER is specifically designed to facilitate list manipulation, this was an ideal task-technology match. The software could have provided more structure to the task than we intended, and hence the EMS-supported groups in Experiment 1 may have been more structured than the EMS-supported groups in Experiment 2. This possible difference could also account for the different effects on task performance between the two experiments. Since we are not able to isolate the effects of specific elements, this question cannot be addressed with the current data. A fruitful direction for research would be to isolate the effects of specific EMS features.

The generalizability of this study's findings is also limited because we examined student groups for a single session. Although we did not use ad hoc groups, we know very little about how the effects of this technology differ during the various stages of group development. Further, we cannot apply confidently the findings of this study outside of a laboratory setting. A small number of researchers are beginning to collect data that can address these issues (e.g., Poole et al. 1991, DeSanctis et al. 1991, Nunamaker et al. 1991).

A final caution should be sounded due to the exploratory nature of this study. Since it was the first study of its kind conducted in the Capture Lab, we accepted a less conservative criterion for significance testing than is conventional. Thus, our findings should be considered suggestive. Despite its limitations, the study has made a theoretical contribution by suggesting that the commonality of focus in addition to degree of focus on the task is a result of EMS use and affects task performance. A practical contribution is that for some types of tasks, low structure EMS can improve group task performance without providing specific structuring mechanisms. If future research demonstrated that minimal additional structuring that groups provide for themselves would improve performance, then some organizations could reap these benefits without investing in special facilities, equipment and software.\*
