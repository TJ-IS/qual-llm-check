Group decision support systems have been advocated as mechanisms for facilitating conflict management in groups. Two noted shortcomings of research on GDSS effects motivate this study: (i) Most researchers have compared the effects of computer-based and manual delivery of structures for supporting group decision making. By treating the GDSS as a "black box,' researchers have neglected attention toward examining the effects of specific capabilities delivered by a GDSS. (ii) Despite the volume of accumulated research, scant attention has been paid to examining GDSS impacts on the group interaction process itself. This research proposes a conceptual view of a GDSS as providing communication and consensus capabilities for supporting the cognitive conflict management process in group decision making. Through a manipulation of the delivery of communication and consensus structures to groups working on a strategic planning task, several exploratory research questions were examined. The GDSS used in this study was the SAMM system. Key results obtained were: (a) despite using the same GDSS structures, groups exhibited a variety of patterns of conflict management processes; (b) the delivery of communication and consensus structures together, as opposed to primarily communication structures, however, did result in higher confrontiveness, or the ability of groups to confront their conflicts and resolve them in positive ways; (c) higher confrontiveness resulted in higher levels of post-meeting consensus; and, (d) computerization of structures enabled groups to confront their conflict and resolve it more positively than when groups were provided with equivalent manual structures. The results demonstrate the value of process-oriented methodological approaches to investigating effects of GDSS designs.

## Introduction

Mtd gies to knit rapidly changing structures together (Child 1987; Huber 1984, 1990; Goodman and Associates 1986; Miles and Snow 1986; Naisbitt and Aburdeen 1985). The post-industrial organization will have to cope with an information explosion accompanied by with increased complexity and turbulence. One key characteristic of such a context is the increased levels of uncertainty and equivocality (Daft and Lengel 1986; Huber and Daft 1987) and the attendant pressures upon decision-makers to interpret and make sense out of the information signals (Daft and Weick 1984; Weick 1979). As a consequence, the need for group decision-making increases because a single person's perspective and expertise are too narrow to address complex and knowledge-intensive deliberations and judgements (Brodwin and Bourgeois 1984; Ireland, Hitt, Bettis, and de Porras 1987).

However, the effectiveness of group decision-making is contingent upon the group's ability to (i) extract a range of competing interpretations about the key issues framing the decision context so that the group can avoid the potential problems of groupthink (Janis 1972) and type III error' (Mitroff and Featheringham 1974) and (ii) synthesize these diverse interpretations into a consistent and uniform interpretation acceptable to the group members (Mason and Mitroff 1981). A key problem facing groups in this respect is conflict management. To take advantage of group strengths, conflict must be handled in a way that diverse perspectives are not stifled, members' commitment is maintained, and group cohesiveness is built

Researchers have increasingly recognized that communication behaviors occupy a central role in the conflict management process (Putnam and Poole 1987; Thomas and Pondy 1977). To help groups manage communication in complex, informationrich conflicts, increasing attention is being paid to procedural structures, particularly those delivered via computer support systems (Poole 1991). A structure may be defined as a particular arrangement of rules and resources that enables groups tó perform some decision-related activity (Poole and DeSanctis 1990). Recently, attention has been focused upon the delivery of structures for group decision making through computer technologies, such as group decision support systems, or GDSS.

A GDSS integrates communication and computer technologies with group decision structures to support various aspects of group work in face-to-face or dispersed meetings (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987). The growing body of empirical GDSS research has targeted attention primarily on the potential of GDSS teċhnologies as a supplement to, or as a replacement for, the conventional forms of group interaction in face-to-face meetings (use of flipcharts or free interaction). Researchers have utilized different types of designs for investigating GDSS impacts (DeSanctis, Sambamurthy and Watson 1989; Dennis, George, Jessup, Nunamaker and Vogel 1988). An underlying characteristic of research to date has been to view the GDSS as a global entity rather than as a bundle of capabilities for supporting different aspects of the group decision-making process (Gutek 1990). While such an approach is suitable for investigating the relative effects of delivery of structures through a GDSS versus manual means, unanswered questions remain: What specific capabilities of a GDSS are responsible for the particular effects observed? When should the effects observed with a particular GDSS design be replicable with another GDSS design? The existing base of empirical GDSS research needs to be extended in order to answer such research questions.

The current research attempts to generate insights into these questions by viewing a GDSS as incorporating two specific capabilities for supporting the group conflict management process: communication support and consesus support. We explore the following research question: What are the relative effects of delivery of communication and consensus support capabilities on the conflict management process of GDSS supported groups? The rest of this paper is organized as follows. A conceptual model for the present research, along with definitions of the key constructs, is presented in the next section. The subsequent section lays out the exploratory research questions. Next we describe the methodology, analysis strategy and results, and discuss the implications of the study.

## Background and Conceptual Model

The use of GDSS has been hypothesized to result in a variety of beneficial group outcomes (see Dennis et al. 1988; Pinnsonneault and Kraemer 1989, or Benbasat and Nault 1991 for reviews of the various outcome variables). Despite some mixed findings, the growing volume of research has evidenced some support for the superior effects of GDSS relative to manual structures. However, attention to the process by which these effects are produced has been relatively sparse (see Zigurs, Poole and DeSanctis 1988; Poole, Holmes and DeSanctis 1991 for exceptions). The group interaction process is the centerpiece to any explanations about group performance (McGrath 1984). Process-oriented research should enable us to understand how the group interaction process mediates the effects of GDSS structures and, therefore, develop better insights into the nature of group outcomes observed with GDSS use. Hence, this research employs the group interaction process as the lens through which to examine GDSS effects.

Following McGrath (1984), we distinguish between two types of conflicts: cognitive conflicts and conflicts of interests. A cognitive conflict arises due to differences in interpretations and judgements brought about by high levels of equivocality surrounding the decision issues facing the group. A conflict of interest stems from differences in motives and goals. The current research focuses primarily on cognitive conflicts. The model employed in the study assumes that group conflict management behaviors are reflected in the interaction process as groups progress toward decisions. Our analysis focuses upon the temporal sequences of communication exchanges that unfold among group members from the beginning to the end of the group meeting.

## The Conceptual Model

Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual model utilized to frame our exploratory study. A variety of task and group characteristics influence the level of existing conflict in a group at the start of a decision-making episode. Further, the GDSS structures present certain capabilities to support the group's attempts at conflict management. The quality of group outcomes reflects the extent to which a group has been successful in conflict management. Given our research focus upon the group interaction process, the conflict management process is expected to be influenced by the level of existing conflict and the capabilities of the GDSS structures presented to the group. In turn. the conflict management process should permit explanations about the quality of the group outcomes. Each one of these elements of the model is elaborated below in greater detail.

Conflict Management Process. Communication behaviors occupy a central role in the conflict management process (Putnam and Poole 1987; Thomas and Pondy 1977). Groups manage communication in complex, information-rich conflicts through production of individual interpretations, sharing of interpretations, and synthesis of individual interpretations into a shared group interpretation through problem-solving attempts (Blake and Mouton 1964; Burke 1970; Thomas 1975; Wall, Galanes and Love 1987). Cognitive conflict arises as members draw upon their past experience, training, or departmental affiliation to produce initial interpretations; individual differences in these areas may result in a range of competing interpretations in the group. Walton (1969) (Folger, Poole and Stutman 1992) argues for a two-phase progression as a characteristic of effective conflict management processes: during the first phase, differentiation, parties air viewpoints, surface differences, and define issues; during the second phase, integration, parties work out a resolution that takes various positions and issues into account. During differentiation, members attempt to communicate their initial interpretations and the underlying assumptions in order to increase other members' awareness of the range of interpretations. During integration, members attempt to synthesize their interpretations into a shared set of group interpretations.

If either phase of differentiation or integration is truncated or ineffectively managed, a less satisfactory result is likely to ensue. If the parties do not differentiate their positions, they may not develop an understanding of the conflict issues or a sense of how committed the other participants are to their positions; as a result, they may not be motivated to prosecute negotiations vigorously. If parties never move into an integration phase, they can easily get caught in continuous cycles of conflict and fail to achieve a resolution. This rather simple and general pattern can also be discerned in more complicated descriptive models of conflict and negotiation (Pondy 1967; Thomas 1975: Gulliver 1979; Morley and Stephenson 1977).

The normative two-step model described above also implies two less successful conflict management patterns (Folger et al. 1992): (1) Parties may never develop opposition or surface differences and maintain a false consensus where some issues are never raised and some needs go unmet; or (2) parties may surface differences, but be unable to resolve opposition, in which case the conflict is “resolved" by forcing one party to accede, or by dropping the subject.

The preceding discussion raises the scenario of different patterns of conflict management processes that differ in the degree to which groups surface conflict and deal with it constructively. Following Putnam and Wilson (1982), we can rate these three options in terms of their degree of confrontiveness. The degree of confrontiveness in cognitive conflicts refers to the extent to which group members confront the conflict manifest in their initial interpretations and resolve differences in their viewpoints. The least confrontive strategy is to ignore the conflict or avoid it. Next on the scale is to surface the conflict, but resolve it through competition, so that one side “wins." This is confrontive in the sense that members acknowledge the presence of the conflict. However, they do not fully confront their differences if they force one party to accept a solution. Finally, full confrontation means that the group acknowledges the conflict and tries for a solution acceptable to all parties. This is confrontation in Putnam and Wilson's sense, also termed as problem solving by Blake and Mouton (1964). Thus, the focus of our exploration is now narrowed down to examining variations in the degree of confrontiveness across groups using GDSS structures for decision making.

Level of Existing Conflict, Groups approach a decision-making situation with a certain level of existing conflict in their interpretations about the issues framing the decisions. The level of existing conflict is brought about by a combination of task and group characteristics: (a) Task characteristics, such as task variety and task analyzability, increase the degree of equivocality surrounding the decision-making issues and lead group members to formulate a divergent array of interpretations (Daft and Lengel 1986). Higher amounts of equivocality result in potential for higher conflict due to greater divergence in the initial interpretations formulated by group members. (b) Group characteristics, such as group size, can affect the level of existing conflict. Large group size can increase the range of initial interpretations and the complexity of communication exchanges and, thereby, enhance the extent of existing conflict (Huber. Cummings and Arendt 1974). Though the primary focus in our research is upon the effects of GDSS structures, the level of existing consensus is included as a control variable.

Capahilities of GDSS Structures. Based upon the description of the two phases in the conflict management process, we view GDSS structures as incorporating two specific capabilities necessary for supporting the conflict management process: communication support and consensus support.

Communication support structures within a GDSS facilitate differentiation of positions through provision of a medium and set of procedures for group interaction. Face-to-face meetings have been recommended as the most appropriate medium for facilitating communication of interpretations and enhancement of mutual understanding at high levels of equivocality (Van de Ven, Delbecq and Koenig 1976). In this research, we will consider the effects of a computer-based medium for use in face-to-face meetings. With regard to procedures, communication support structures aim to steer groups away from behaviors that inhibit effective group communication (McGrath 1984; Hirokawa 1982). For example, a structure, such as that found in the Nominal Group Technique, should be useful in achieving this goal and providing support for achieving differentiation of positions (Delbecq and Van de Ven 1971). The GDSS used in this research provides idea generation and voting structures that are similar to the Nominal Group Technique.

Consensus support is required for facilitating integration. Consensus support structures should channel group communication exchanges toward behaviors such as frank and open exchange of opinions and interpretations, examination of the validity of alternative interpretations, discarding of inappropriate interpretations, and refinement of useful ones (Deutsch 1973; Walton 1969). Additionally, it is important for these structures to steer groups away from behaviors such as one side giving in, differences in viewpoints being suppressed, or a few group members covertly manipulating others into submission (Folger et al. 1992; Sillars, Colletti, Parry and Rogers 1982). The current study incorporates elements of the Strategic Assumptions Surfacing and Testing (SAST) technique into a GDSS consensus support capability (Mason and Mitroff 1981).

The focus of our investigation is upon the relative effects of the delivery of communication and consensus structures upon the conflict management processes ofGDSSsupported groups.

Quality of Group Outcomes. The degree of success of the conflict management process can be assessed by the extent to which group members eventually move toward consensus on a final set of interpretations (Deutsch 1973). Thus, one of the important outcome variables is the level of post-meeting consensus, which refers to the extent to which group members are able to converge in their judgments about the relevance and importance of issues in the task context. In addition, groups' perceptions of their outcomes and process, such as confidence in recommendations, perceived quality of recommendations, and satisfaction with the decision process are important indicators of the degree to which conflict is managed effectively. When the group is successful in managing conflict in a way that individual members' opinions are not stifled and group cohesiveness is built or maintained, group members should have favorable perceptions about their group outcomes.

## Research Questions

The conceptual model described in the previous section forms the basis for identifying our key exploratory research questions as follows:

(1) How do variations in the capabilities of GDSS structures influence the degree of confrontiveness of the conflict management process? Poole and Roth (1989) found that the nature of the group interaction process was influenced by a variety of factors related to the group and task context. Our conceptual model suggests that the presence of communication plus consensus capabilities will enable group members not only to differentiate their initial interpretations, but also to achieve better integration of the various interpretations into the final group decisions. On the contrary, though the presence of communication capabilities alone may be adequate for facilitating differentiation of positions, they may not prove effective in providing support for the integration of the divergent interpretations in the group. Thus, our focus is upon examining if the addition of consensus support capabilities to communication support capabilities results in a higher degree of confrontiveness in the conflict management process

(2) What is the relationship between the degree of confrontiveness and quality of group outcomes? The conflict management literature suggests that productive conflict management outcomes are associated with clear differentiation of positions for identifying the conflicting issues and integration of divergent opinions through problem solving or negotiations (Burke 1970; Guetzkow and Gyr 1954; Putnam and Poole 1987; Wall et al. 1987). When group members do not freely reveal their divergent viewpoints, the level of conflict surfaced will be low. Since differences never surface, the group may enjoy its false consensus and fail to move toward consensus on common interpretations (Fisher and Ury 1981). On the contrary, surfacing of disagreements could, at the least, make members aware of rival viewpoints and provoke them into examining these viewpoints. Further, an open discussion of differences could be expected to enable members to test the validity of their individual interpretations and move toward a common set of valid interpretations (Argyris and Schon 1978). Hence, we might expect that, the higher the confrontiveness of the conflict management process, the higher the consensus change. Further, because “problem solving" and other open approaches have found their way into common parlance as desirable behaviors, groups engaging in these processes might be expected to exhibit more confidence in recommendations, perceptions of higher quality, and greater satisfaction with their decision process.

(3) Does the delivery of communication and consensus structures through computerized versus equivalent manual means influence the nature of the conflict management process? Our intent is to explore the possible effects due to computerized versus manual delivery of GDSS structures. Previous research has found no significant effects due to the delivery of structures through computerized versus equivalent manual means (Cats-Baril and Huber 1987; Watson, DeSanctis and Poole 1988). Thus, the evidence to date suggests that the effects of a GDSS may be more due to the capabilities of the embedded structures rather than computerization per se. However, we are aware of no study that has focused attention upon the group interaction process. If conflict management processes in computer-supported groups differ from those in manually supported groups, there would be some evidence that computerization per se makes a difference. Such a finding may suggest that the real differences due to computerization occur in the group interaction process. Thus, our focus here is to explore the effects due to alternative modes of delivery of structures on the conflict management process.

The next section of this paper describes how the conceptual model elaborated in the preceding sections was operationalized in a specific research context to explore the research questions related to the patterns of conflict management processes in GDSS groups.

## Method

Data for exploring the above research questions were obtained as part of an earlier study by Sambamurthy (1989). The focus of his investigation was on examining effects of variations in the capabilities of GDSS structures on group decision-making outcomes. In an experimental research design, he employed two computer-based designs to manipulate the delivery of communication and consensus support capabilities to groups working on a strategic planning task. One design, a level 1 GDSS, was designed primarily to deliver communication structures. The other design, a level 2 GDSS, was designed to deliver communication and consensus structures.² Based on a sample of 39 groups, he found that GDSS structures providing communication and consensus capabilities significantly outperformed GDSS structures providing primarily communication capabilities in terms of the level of post-meeting consensus and the group's confidence in their recommendations. However, the focus of his investigation was primarily on the effects of GDSS structures on group outcomes, and he treated the conflict management process itself as a “"black box."The focus of the current study is to unravel the "black box" by explicitly focussing attention upon the conflict management processes that unfolded in those groups. During that study, group meetings were video- and audio-taped, and these tapes are used as the primary source of data for this study.

From the population of 39 groups in the original study, 36 groups were selected as follows: 18 level 1 GDSS and 18 level 2 GDSS. In addition, Sambamurthy also gathered data on 17 manual groups; tapes for 12 groups were utilized for analysis in this study. Two of the original 20 level 1 GDSS groups and one of the original 19 level 2 GDSS groups from Sambamurthy's dataset were dropped from analysis due to difficulties in deciphering the recorded tapes. On the average, a meeting lasted about 80 minutes, and it took two to two-and-a-half hours to code the corresponding tapes. Only 12 of the 17 manual control groups were coded in order to economize on cost. Since the level 2—manual comparison was primarily a follow-up check and since observed conflict behavior in the 12 groups was remarkably consistent, it did not seem to warrant the additional expenditure of time and funds.

## Subjects

The groups were composed of undergraduate students enrolled in introductory Management Science courses at a large midwestern university. These groups worked on various assignments within their enrolled course, and they had met together several times before participating in the study. Participation in the research was voluntary, and the participants were asked to write a reaction paper to their experience in using computers for supporting group work. An equivalent alternative assignment was made available for students who chose not to participate in this study. The students received 5% of their course credit for participation in this study and writing a reaction paper or for working on the alternative assignment. During the experiment, the administrators were asked to respond periodically to a questionnaire that assessed the subjects' level of enthusiasm and seriousness with respect to the task, GDSS technology, and their meeting. Analysis revealed that the subjects took the experiment seriously, participated in it with high levels of commitment, and used the GDSS or manual technology to the extent required for testing of effects. A total of six different administrators participated in the conduct of the experiments; statistical analysis revealed that there were no systematic outcome differences across the individual administrators (at p < 0.05).

The groups were randomly assigned to the three treatment conditions: level 1 GDSS, level 2 GDSS, or the manual support. Analysis of group members' responses on a background questionnaire that assessed their computer literacy, job experience and attitude toward working in groups revealed no significant differences across the treatment conditions. Given the disadvantages of using zero-history groups (McGrath 1984), groups worked on several other assignments prior to their participation in the study. On the average, groups met on four occasions prior to participation in the experimental study to discuss tasks such as project planning, systems design and business case analyses. Further, the same groups met another three to four times on similar assignments after their participation in the research. Thus, the groups participating in this research had a history of prior interaction and anticipated further interaction beyond the research.

## Task

Groups performed a task that was adapted from a strategic planning case (Whetten and Cameron 1984). The case describes several problems facing a small college, Tidewater College, due to dissatisfaction among its stakeholders. Six competing strategies were presented to the group members for evaluation and selection of the strategy that was most consistent with the members' interpretations about crucial expectations of the college's stakeholders. The task was designed to be amenable to the use of stakeholder analysis as a strategic planning technique (Mason and Mitroff 1981). Stakeholder analysis involves a clear sequence of steps for groups engaged in strategic planning: (a) identification of stakeholders of the organization, (b) utilization of individual knowledge and experience in formulating a wide range of interpretations about possible stakeholder expectations and behaviors with respect to the organization, (c) selection of a “core" set of interpretations about stakeholder expectations and behaviors that are highly likely to occur (important to the stakeholder) and are crucial for organizational attention (important to the organization), and (d) formulation of strategies and action plans that are most appropriate in light of these “core" interpretations.

We can argue that the existence of equivocality causes group members to formulate different interpretations about stakeholder expectations and behaviors and strategies. Conflict arises because individual group members differ in their interpretations and judgments about which stakeholder's expectations are crucial for the success of the college and ought to be the basis for choice of strategies. In addition, conflict also arises over the choice of appropriate strategies for the college. Communication structures should facilitate the extraction and communication of these interpretations within the group, whereas consensus structures should facilitate the synthesis of various interpretations into group agreement over “core" stakeholder assumptions and appropriate strategies. The task was chosen because pilot testing revealed that students could draw upon their college experiences to formulate interpretations about stakeholders, stakeholder expectations, and strategies for Tidewater College (Sambamurthy, 1989). The task materials were iteratively modified through four rounds of pilot testing with student groups to stimulate adequate levels of cognitive conflict among the groups. Cues within the original case were modified by incorporating six different strategies as choices facing Tidewater College. These strategies were developed in consultation with experts in strategic planning who were familiar with the Tidewater College case. Pilot testing revealed that individual group members selected different strategies and that the task materials evoked adequate levels of cognitive conflict (Sambamurthy 1989). Some additional details regarding the appropriateness of the task are reported in the analysis section

## Capabilities of GDSS Structures

The delivery of communication and consensus structures for performance of stakeholder analysis was manipulated through two alternative computer-based designs. Both designs were incorporated as separate modules in the GDSS used in this study, the Software Aided Meeting Management³ (SAMM) system (DeSanctis et al. 1989). As discussed earlier, these designs were labelled as a level 1 GDSS (primarily communication support) and a level 2 GDSS (communication and consensus support). As summarized in Table 1, the similarities between these two designs were: (a) they both provided a combination of verbal and electronic channels for group interaction and (b) they provided similar communication structures, such as simultaneous input of ideas and interpretations about stakeholders, stakeholder expectations, and strategies, large screens for instantaneous public display of these ideas and anonymous input of ideas and interpretations (Benbasat and Nault 1991; DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987; Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1989; Connolly, Jessup and Valacich 1990).

The major differences between these designs were: (a) Higher levels of consensus structures were provided in the level 2 GDSS by enabling group members to evaluate stakeholder assumptions explicitly against two criteria (importance to stakeholders and importance to organization) (Figure 2a) and displaying the evaluations on a two-dimensional grid, with the criteria as its axes (Figure 2b). In contrast, the level 1 GDSS provides limited consensus structures for group members to perform summary evaluations of stakeholder assumptions, in the form of rating, ranking or voting, without explicit reference to the individual criteria; further, the public display of group evaluations provided a feedback only of the summary scores (Figure 3). (b) In order to ensure that our investigation of the effects of consensus structures was not confounded by an inadequate use of the GDSS, groups using the level 2 GDSS were required to use each one of the features displayed in Figure 2a at least once. However, they were free to use some features more than once. On the other hand, while groups using the level 1 GDSS were required to follow the procedure of stakeholder analysis, they could choose their own approach to evaluation of core stakeholder assumptions —verbal discussion, rating, ranking or voting.

Both designs were extensively pilot tested to ensure their reliability and appropriateness for the research. Groups were given training and an agenda that was appropriate for their GDSS; all the other factors such as task and meeting guidelines were kept constant.

Groups in the manual condition were provided with flipcharts, index cards, calculator, and procedures for performing stakeholder analysis. On the flipchart, the separate dimensions of evaluation were clearly laid out in order to keep the structure equivalent with that of the level 2 GDSS. In addition, the two-dimensional grid for feedback of evaluations was also laid out on a separate sheet and made available to each control group. Similar to the groups in the level 2 GDSS condition, groups in the manual condition were required to use each one of the steps in the stakeholder analysis procedure at least once. In this manner, we sought to ensure that the level 2 and manual groups were equivalent in their level of use of the consensus structures and that the only significant difference between the two conditions was the mode of delivery of the structures.

## Procedures

Groups assembled for two separate sessions in a decision room. The first session lasted about one hour and was devoted to training the groups in applying stakeholder analysis and in the use of features within the SAMM system. Training materials on stakeholder analysis and the SAMM system were tested and refined prior to data collection. The training procedures were tailored to the type of design, level 1 or level 2, assigned to the groups.

The second session lasted about two-and-a-half hours and proceeded as follows: (a) group members filled out a consent form and a background questionnaire; (b) they received the task materials and individually evaluated the appropriateness of six strategies as choices for solving the problems facing Tidewater College; (c) members worked together as a group to use the SAMM features made available to them for performing stakeholder analysis and evaluating the six strategies; at the end of their meeting, each group submitted a recommendation about their evaluation of strategies and the supporting “core" stakeholder assumptions; (d) members then filled out a post-meeting questionnaire that asked them to re-evaluate individually the six strategies in light of their group meeting. In addition, the post-meeting questionnaire included items to measure their perceptions about their recommendations and their meeting process (confidence in recommendations, perceived quality of recommendations, and satisfaction with the decision process). Each group's meeting was recorded on audio- and video-tapes.

## Measurement of Quality of Group Outcomes

Group members' individual evaluations of the six strategies were collected prior to the meeting and used to compute level of existing consensus. Similarly, their individual evaluations after the group meeting were used to compute post-meeting consensus. Consensus was computed with the help of a computer program based on Spillman, Bezdek and Spillman's (1980) method. ## Measurement of Confrontiveness

McGrath (1986) has advocated the need for employing methodological approaches that focus on group as the unit of analysis and do not rely primarily on self-reports of group members. In our research, confrontiveness in the conflict management process was assessed through an analytic system developed by Poole (Poole 1983; Poole and Roth 1989): the Group Working Relations Coding System (GWRCS). This system is based on direct observation of the group interaction behavior. The GWRCS codes short segments of group interaction (30 to 45 seconds) and describes the overall nature of the interaction in these segments in terms of seven different types of global interaction patterns.

(1) Focused work: periods when members are task-focused and do not disagree with one another, as in brainstorming

(2) Critical work: periods when members disagree with each other, but the disagreements are centered on ideas, and no opposing sides are differentiated.

(3a) Opposition: periods in which disagreements are expressed through the formation of opposing sides; conflict is personalized during these periods.

(3b) Capitulation: one of the three modes of resolution of opposition in which one side gives in.

(3c) Tabling: a second mode of resolution of opposition in which no resolution occurs, but the subject is dropped.

(3d) Open discussion: a third mode of opposition resolution that involves problem-solving discussions, negotiation or compromise.

(4) Relational integration: periods when the group is not task-focused; these exhibit tangents, joking and positive socioemotional behavior.

These seven interaction patterns indicate various degrees of confrontiveness in the management of conflict during the segment (see Poole, Holmes and DeSanctis (1991) for an application). The lowest level of confrontiveness, failure to surface conflict, is indicated by group communication exchanges classified as focused work, in which members are task-focused and do not disagree with one another (e.g., as in brainstorming), or relational integration periods, in which the discussion is devoted to tangents, joking, or positive socioemotional behavior. A somewhat higher level of confrontiveness is indicated by periods classified as critical work, in which members disagree with one another, but no opposing sides are differentiated relative to an issue. Here, differences are dealt with in an implicit manner. Still more confrontive are periods classified as opposition, during which members acknowledge and surface conflicts. At this point, the method used to manage the open conflict becomes relevant. Groups that deal with conflict through capitulation (in which one party accedes to the other), or through tabling (in which parties drop the subject), acknowledge their conflicts but do not confront them in the most constructive manner (Deutsch 1973). The most confrontive method of conflict management occurs when parties surface opposition and, then, resolve opposition through open discussion. In this case, conflict is managed through negotiation or problem-solving discussions in which all parties' concerns are addressed. Hence, the GWRCS enables us to distinguish periods of low confrontiveness (Focused work, Integration), moderate confrontiveness (Critical work), moderately high confrontiveness (Opposition followed by Capitulation or Tabling), and high confrontiveness (Opposition followed by Open Discussion). In this study, we are interested in the overall pattern of conflict management processes throughout a decision-making session. Confrontiveness of the conflict management process was measured by developing “phase maps" of group conflict management processes. Phase maps are temporal sequence of phases that depict how groups managed their conflict over the course of their discussion. The phases are organized around the seven categories described above, and a phase map is composed of a series of these phases. For example, we might observe a group pass through four phases: (1) Members engage in a period of Focused work (FW), followed by (2) a period of Critical work (CW), during which it becomes evident that members have some differences in viewpoints relative to an important issue; as a result (3) an Opposition (OPP) phase occurs, during which members openly confront one another, finally moving into (4) a Capitulation (CAP) phase, when one side gives in to the other. The four phase sequence FW-CW-OPP-CAP constitutes a phase map of how conflict emerged and was dealt with. Of course, this is not the only sequence that can be detected with this classification scheme. Other sequences are possible, and it is also possible for groups to recycle through phases. For example, a group with the sequence FW-OPP-TAB-FW-OPP-TAB-CW-OPP-OD has gone through two cycles of surfacing opposition and tabling them (TAB) before finally resolving them through open discussion (OD) in a third cycle.

Poole's (1983) conceptual work related to group working relations was utilized as the basis for identifying the patterns of conflict management processes and developing measures of confrontiveness. The following sequence of steps was followed:

Step 1. Each group's discussion was divided into short (30-second) segments to identify which of the seven phase types each segment reflects. This was done with the Group Working Relations Coding System (GWRCS) (Poole and Roth 1989), which operationalized the seven phase types.4 Studies have supported the representational and construct validity of the GWRCS coding system (Poole 1983; Poole and Roth 1989; Folger, Hewes and Poole 1984). The coding was done by the first author after an adequate level of interrater reliability was attained with an independent coder. Interrater reliability was 0.90 for 300 segments, measured with Cohen's kappa. The result was a series of codes for each group.

In view of the fact that this was an exploratory study with no strong a priori expectations about the results, the first author's service as a coder did not seem to present a serious threat to the study's validity. The first author coded the discussions before the research questions outlined above had been specified. Interrater reliability was initially established on a different set of discussions classified by coders not acquainted with the research questions, setting an independent classification standard. To check whether the first author's codes were biased, five groups were subsequently recóded independently and the resulting Kappa was 0.89. Further, the phasic analysis procedures provide some protection against bias, because the individual codes do not serve as the basis for identifying conflict management phases. Phase identification depends upon an analysis of the longitudinal patterns of acts, and the complex rules underlying this process mitigate bias introduced in making individual classifications.

Step 2. The series of GWRCS codes for each group were converted into a normalized timeline, which standardized the lengths of various discussions and permitted comparison of phase maps across groups. The timeline shows how the total discussion was divided up into successive phases from beginning to end, with the length of each phase proportional to the percentage of the total discussion it represented. A phase was defined as a sequence of contiguous codes that shared the same value (for example, a series of contiguous INT codes connote an integration phase). A computer program was written to identify phases divisions; the program passed down the series of codes, grouping contiguous codes into phases and demarcating boundaries between phases (see Holmes and Poole (1991) for a description of this program). In order to avoid giving individual acts too much influence on phase classifications, a cutoff value was set so that a phase was included on the timeline only if it took up 5% or more of the total discussion time. If the phase took up less than 5% of the total meeting time, it was either smoothed into the surrounding phase or, if there were two or more consecutive phases with 5% or less, they were combined into a composite phase. The result of this procedure was a set of comparable timelines that could be sorted into various typical phase progressions. These timelines served as one source of information to guide the development of a conflict management process typology.

Step 3. A second piece of information utilized in developing the typology was generated through statistical analysis of the series of GWRCS codes generated in step 1. The nonparametric statistic gamma was used to determine the general order in which the GWRCS codes tended to occur, following the procedure described by Pelz ((1985); see Holmes and Poole (1991) for the description of a computer program used to conduct this analysis). The gamma analysis yields two indices: (1) the order of precedence of phases and (2) the degree of separation among phases, i.e., whether the interaction process was characterized by distinct phases or whether the phases were mixed with each other. These two indices can be used to determine the general ordering of phases, whether the same phases occurred more than once in the discussion (i.e., whether there was recycling), and the general number of statistically distinct phases. Unlike the detailed timelines, the gamma analysis provides a general overview of the longitudinal trends in the sequences. This statistical analysis provides a useful supplement to visual inspection and classification of timelines from step 2.

Step 4. Information from steps 2 and 3 was used to develop phase maps of conflict management processes. We combined the information obtained from timeline generation and gamma analysis to develop phase maps for each group. Timelines were often too detailed to allow a global characterization of phase progressions; some timelines had as many as 15 distinct phases, and it was difficult to discern overall patterns. The statistical information from the gamma analysis, which indicated the degree of separation or overlap between the various phases, was used to smooth the timelines into a final phase map. These phase maps provided the final pictures of groups' conflict management processes and were used to evaluate confrontiveness.

Step 5. The phase maps were inductively classified into related patterns (Table 4). Note that these patterns were not determined a priori; rather, given the exploratory nature of this study, they were discovered through induction. Next, the rationale outlined in Table 3 was used to assign scores on the degree of confrontiveness for each pattern; this procedure is explained in a later section.

## Results

Given a sample size of 48 and three treatment conditions, a significance level of 0.05 was chosen for statistical testing of differences between conditions. The power of an ANCOVA test for this sample size was found to be 0.85 for a medium effects size.

The process by which the task for this research was developed and validated was discussed in an earlier section. As a final means of verifying that the task did generate adequate levels of cognitive conflict in groups, the scores on pre-existing consensus were examined. For the sample of 48 groups included in this study, pre-existing consensus scores ranged from 0.14 to 0.40, with a mean value of 0.21 on a scale from 0 (which indicates perfect disagreement) to 1 (which indicates perfect agreement) (see Table 2); these scores provide confirmation that the task did evoke adequate levels of cognitive conflict in the groups.

Our presentation of the results is organized as follows: First, we present descriptive details of the various patterns of conflict management processes observed in our research. Next, we present analysis to explore each one of the research questions presented earlier.

The traditional focus in research has been to portray the group conflict management process as occurring through a set sequence of phases or stages that groups pass through (Pondy 1967; Thomas 1975). Historically, researchers have implicitly assumed a unitary sequence of phases of conflict management and sought to identify more-or-less static “styles." However, recent evidence suggests that groups actively structure their decision making processes in ways that depart from the unitary sequence model. Hence, there is a growing recognition of the validity of a “multiple sequence model," or the idea that groups exhibit a wide range of patterns in the temporal sequencing of their conflict management process (Poole 1983: Poole and Roth 1989; Gersick 1988, 1989; Walton 1969; Sillars 1980; Folger et al. 1992: Putnam and Poole 1987; Papa and Natalle 1989).

The significance of the patterns in Table 5 is their implication that several different patterns of conflict management processes resulted in the groups included in our study. And within each GDSS condition, groups exhibited a considerable range of patterns of cognitive conflict management processes. Thus, a multiple sequence model of conflict management patterns seems to be appropriate: despite using the same GDSS structures, groups display variations in their conflict management processes. These patterns stress the validity of the multiple sequence model as the basis for understanding group conflict management processes in GDSS-supported groups. ## Discussion

Group decision support systems have been advocated as mechanisms for facilitating conflict management in groups. While a significant volume of research has targeted attention upon investigating the beneficial effects of GDSS structures, two noted shortcomings motivated this research: (i) Most researchers have been concerned with comparing the effects of computer-based versus manual delivery of structures for supporting group decision-making. Attention toward the effects of specific capabilities delivered by a GDSS for supporting various aspects of the group decision-making process has been relatively sparse. (ii) While a significant volume of research has accumulated relative to the effects of GDSS structures on the quality of group decision-making outcomes, scant attention has been paid to examining impacts on the group interaction process itself.

This research proposed a conceptual view of a GDSS as providing communication and consensus capabilities for supporting the cognitive conflict management process in group decision-making. Through a manipulation of the delivery of communication and consensus structures to groups working on a strategic planning task, several exploratory research questions were examined. The GDSS used in this study was the SAMM system. Key results obtained were: (a) Despite using the same GDSS structures, groups exhibited a variety of patterns of conflict management processes: (b) The delivery of communication and consensus structures together, as opposed to primarily communication structures, however, did result in higher confrontiveness, or the ability of groups to confront their conflict and resolve it in positive ways: (c) Higher confrontiveness resulted in higher levels of post-meeting consensus: (d) Computerization of structures was observed to enable groups to confront their conflict and resolve it more positively than when groups were provided with equivalent manual structures.

These results suggest that decision support technologies which incorporate capabilities matching the demands imposed by the group decision-making process may heighten a group's ability to deal with conflict constructively and productively. How can we explain this effect? Two necessary prerequisites for effective conflict management are: (i) differentiation, or surfacing disagreements so that members are made aware of one anothers' positions and of the need to work on the conflict; and (ii) integration, so that groups can positively resolve their disagreements and synthesize their various initial positions into a shared group interpretation. The communication structures of a GDSS include features for anonymous input of ideas and public display of ideas and appear to be appropriate for facilitating differentiation. Poole et al. (1991) found some evidence of this effect for groups working on a budget allocation task. However, in our study, the communication capability does not appear to be sufficient for creating opposition. Only four of the 18 groups using the level 1 GDSS (that delivered primarily communication support) surfaced conflict openly. The level 2 GDSS differed from the level 1 GDSS in that it incorporated higher levels of consensus support (two-dimensional evaluation and graphical display) for stakeholder analysis in addition to the communication support capability. Observation of level 2 GDSS groups indicated that members used the graphical display to identify key assumptions they agreed on, paving the way for integrative solutions. Level 1 GDSS groups, with no straightforward way to manage open disagreements, seem to have been reluctant to acknowledge opposition. Instead, they did not surface conflicts at all or dealt with disagreements through critical work. Our results suggest that GDSS structures should explicitly incorporate both communication and consensus structures in order to enable groups to confront their differences and resolve conflict positively.

It is also noteworthy that within the overall pattern of results, there is some variation in the group conflict management processes. Eight level 2 GDSS groups exhibited low or moderate confrontiveness, despite the fact that the system was designed to promote surfacing and open discussion of conflicts. This underscores the fact that technology is not deterministic; groups have considerable latitude in how they use the structures within a technology to respond to potential conflicts (Poole and DeSanctis 1990). Members may smooth over or suppress conflict, even with extensive technological support. The ability of groups to “restructure" computerized procedures so as to adapt them to their particular needs or proclivities must be taken into account in forecasting the impacts of structures on group work.

The study also provided evidence that confrontiveness of conflict management processes is related to a measure of productive outcomes, level of post-meeting consensus. Whereas most previous research has used self-report or observer ratings on questionnaires to measure conflict management styles (Burke 1970; Wall et al. 1987; McGrath 1986), this study employed direct behavioral observation and detailed analysis of the group interaction process. Of interest are the variations in patterns of conflict management processes that were identified. There were, for instance, at least five distinctive patterns by which groups could engage in high confrontation and resolve conflict positively.

Differences between the computerized and manual modes of delivery of communication and consensus structures suggest that merely providing structures to groups in the form of stakeholder analysis was not sufficient to create more effective conflict management. Recall that groups in the manual condition were provided with all the features (such as separate dimensions for evaluation and a two-dimensional feedback) provided to the level 2 GDSS groups and required to use these features at least once. The only difference between the two conditions was the mode of delivery: level 2 GDSS groups were delivered the features through a computer medium, whereas the manual groups were delivered the features through a flip-chart. All but one of the manual groups failed to surface conflict. Moreover, for the manual groups, there was no correlation between confrontiveness and the amount of consensus change. So there was a net benefit from computerization of structures. This finding differs from the results of earlier research which indicated that structures alone accounted for the impacts of a GDSS and that computerization added nothing (Watson et al. 1988; Zigurs et al. 1988; Sambamurthy et al. 1992). The contradiction can be explained by the fact that earlier research did not incorporate the mediating role of conflict management processes into the research models for investigating effects of alternative structures on post-meeting consensus. The current study's findings underscore the need to develop input-process-output models which allow for the mediating effects of conflict management and other group interaction processes.

On a methodological note, our research demonstrates the value of conducting process-level investigations of the effects of GDSS. Such examinations complement and strengthen insights gained from traditional GDSS research that has tended to treat the group interaction process as a blackbox.

## Limitations of the Research

Several limitations of this research should be noted. The study was conducted with the SAMM version of GDSS; it remains to be determined whether the results are generalizable across other structures and GDSS implementations. Second, the coding procedures employed may have restricted what we can infer about group conflict interaction. Third, the study utilized only one type of task. Task variation could produce differences in GDSS effects. Fourth, the study used business school students as subjects. Additional replications in the field are required before the current results can be generalized.

## Directions for Future Research

Our research has provided some illustration of the value of differentiating the capabilities embedded within a GDSS and comparing their relative effects. One obvious direction for future research would be to replicate our research with a different design of a GDSS, for example, GroupSystems (Dennis et al. 1988). Differentiation of the capabilities provided by a GDSS should permit integration of research findings across different implementations of GDSS structures. Another extension for future research would be to examine the effects of GDSS structures in the context of dispersed rather than face-to-face meetings. A third extension would be to replicate the research in a different task context, for example an intellective task (Zigurs et al. 1988), or creative software design (Jarvenpaa, Rao and Huber 1988).\*
