# The Information Age Confronts Education: Case Studies on Electronic Classrooms

Dorothy E. Leidner Information Systems Department

Department of Management Science and Information Systems CBA 5.202

Information technology is slowly becoming a part of educational classrooms and corporate training facilities. The current study examines the use and outcomes of computer-based instructional technology in the context of graduate business education. Case study data is gathered to explore how computer technology is used in the university classroom, and how computer-based teaching methods differ from traditional teaching methods in terms of class interaction and in-class learning. The study found that there are many potential computer-based teaching methods and that the methods can have different outcomes. The use of computer-based teaching methods requiring handson student use appear to offer an advantage over traditional methods and over computer-based methods not requiring hands-on student use in providing a forum for exploratory analysis during class and for acquiring technical procedural knowledge. A model of in-class learning is developed for future research.

## 1. Introduction

Droblems abound in American education. At the secondary level, problems include low average reading proficiency and high dropout rates with an associated high rate of unemployment for dropouts (US Congress 1988). At the university level, problems typically revolve around motivating a large group of students with wide disparity in knowledge and familiarity with the subject, extending knowledge beyond the mere grasping of facts into an ability to synthesize and analyze new situations and helping students develop reasoning skills (Elder 1990). American businesses can expect to spend \$25 billion a year for remedial training programs for new employees (Kearns 1990). This is all above and beyond traditional training for supervisors, new employees, and management development.

Information technology may have a role in improving American education. The potential uses of information technology in education share the very characteristics of information technology which businesses have used to gain competitive advantage and make productivity improvements: the interactiveness of computers, the distribution of information, the provision of analytical tools, the elimination of distance barriers, and, to a lesser extent, the replacement of repetitive tasks. The effects of information technológy on classroom activity have, however, received only limited attention in higher education settings. This may begin to change with the advent of electronic classrooms. An electronic classroom is a classroom equipped with computer facilities which an instructor and/or students can use to store, manipulate, or retrieve information during class for the purpose of presentation, analysis, or communication. Examples of electronic classrooms have been reported in many disciplines including chemistry, engineering, physics, English, foreign languages and business (Lyman 1988).

This paper examines the use of computers in university classrooms to enhance student learning. This is a descriptive study conducted in the context of graduate business education designed to build an initial model of the impact of computerbased teaching on classroom interaction, student motivation, and student cognitive development. The current study, and the resultant model, is limited to instructional situations where a teacher and a class of students meet at the same time and same place, and hence, will exclude open learning or distributed facilities where students are at one location and the instructor in another location.

## 2. Information Technology Use in Classrooms

Research examining classroom use of computers consists almost exclusively of experiments examining a specific application of a computer-assisted instruction. a videodisk, or an interactive video. Research on computer-assisted instruction (CAI) dominates. CAI is an interactive software program that provides information in sequential or nonlinear modes to challenge a student's knowledge and understanding of a subject matter (Lay 1989–1990). CAIs vary according to type (drill and practice, test taking, games, simulations), subject matter, and feedback mechanisms, as well as according to whether the student or the computer controls the speed and sequence of the material presented (Beech 1983).

The findings are inconclusive. CAI is generally shown to lead to more time efficient learning and attitude improvements but not to any significant difference in performance (Gist et al. 1988/1989, McAleese 1980). CAI has also been shown to lead to increased student perception of learning with less effort, and more active engagement in learning (Krendl and Lieberman 1988). Increased performance for low-achieving students has been shown to occur when the computer controls the speed and sequence of presentation, whereas increased performance for high-achieving students occurs when the student has control (Schloss et al. 1988, Grubb 1977, Fry 1972). For young students, CAI has not been shown to significantly accelerate cognitive development (Krendl and Lieberman 1988). Simulations have been shown to increase college chemistry students' test and lab performance (Smith et al. 1986). Elsewhere the impact of simulations have been shown to depend on the accuracy of the system, how it is used, its place in the total course, and the instructor's attitude toward it (Barnes 1980, Marante 1980).

Overall, the empirical results on CAI appear to have many similarities with those obtained in other areas of information technology. Much as the impact of decision support systems on decision makers is shown to depend on the nature of the task or decision, the cognitive preference of the user, and the capabilities of the system (e.g., Keen and Scott Morton 1978, DeSanctis 1984), the impact of computer use on students depends on the cognitive aptitude of the student, the nature of the material, and the degree of student control over the program. Most importantly, much as the success of an information system in an organization is thought to depend on how well it is linked to the organization's strategy (e.g., Jarvenpaa and Ives 1990), the success of computers in education depends on how well they are integrated with the instructional objectives.

## 3. Research Questions

Much prior research on computers and learning has addressed children in primary school.' In addition, prior research examining the impact of computer use on student learning rarely examines the computer as an instructional tool integrated into rather than apart from the classroom, neglects such higher-order learning as problem solving skills and analysis, and lacks a theoretical base. This study addresses these research voids. We examine electronic classrooms at the graduate student level, investigate electronic classrooms in their natural context as an instructional tool integrated into a course, examine higher-order learning (problem solving and analysis as opposed to factual material), and develop an initial theoretical model within which to examine the effects of electronic classrooms on learning. The specific research questions addressed are: (1) How is information technology incorporated into university classroom activity?, and (2) What are some of the outcomes of the classroom use of information technology?

## 3.1. How Is Information Technology Incorporated into University Classroom Activity?

The fact that a course may take place within an electronic classroom may not necessarily imply that computer-based technology is used or used similarly by different instructors as part of classroom activity. The use of the technology is likely to depend on what specific computer technology is available and the instructor's method of using that technology. For example, in another context—that of group decision support systems (GDSS) where a group typically uses computers to facilitate the manipulation and sharing of information—George (1989) showed that the availability of technology does not necessarily lead to use; however, use is necessary for technology to influence outcomes. The use and the subsequent impact are also likely to depend on the instructional content of the course. Different subject areas are likely to provide varying opportunities for computer use. Where the instructional content revolves around procedures and problem solving, more opportunity for computer use might result than when the instructional content focuses on facts. In this study we are interested in understanding when the computer-based technology is used in the electronic classroom and how the instructor's style might affect the use.

## 3.2. What Are the Outcomes of the Classroom Use of Information Technology?

Our second question explores the outcomes of incorporating computer-based technology into a university classroom activity. We are interested in understanding to what extent outcomes such as a teacher-student interaction change when computerbased technology is used and how much these changes are due to the instructor's particular method of using the technology. We will specifically explore if computer technology increases the students' interaction in the classroom and how and why this increased interaction occurs.

Current theories, as well as empirical research, echo the view that active involvement in the learning process is critical. For example, Smith (1989) suggests that when students are actively engaged in a discussion, there is a greater likelihood of creative and critical, as opposed to factual, thinking. Interaction is a part of overall student participation.2 Research on college classes, however, suggests that only a small portion of most classes elicit student interaction. In a study of traditional lectures. Karp and Yoels (1983) found that instructors dominated classroom activity and that the interaction that did occur between instructor and students was dominated by the same four to five students. Traditional lectures have been shown to be unable to stimulate broad interaction (Smith 1983). In contrast, some evidence has been accumulated that electronic classrooms might induce student interaction. In an electronic English classroom with communication facilities, Butler (1990) recorded 200 comments in a two-day period of which only 10% were instructor comments. Twentyeight percent of the student comments were student-to-student, 61% were directed towards the whole class, and only 13% were of the student-to-instructor variety. Observing in the same classroom, Bump (1990) noted that the electronic classroom allowed all members of the class to contribute to class discussion and Slatin (1990) found that in a class of 18, students generated 100 messages, with approximately 60% being addressed by students to other students rather than to the instructor. The interaction was strongly encouraged as part of course requirements. We will explore whether computer-based technology leads to similar interaction in cases where class participation is not part of a student's grade.

A study conducted by the Air Force Academy (Gist et al. 1988–1989) found that students taught in classrooms equipped with instructor workstations and videodisks had more positive attitudes about the instructor, about the teaching method used in the course, and about how the instruction had affected their own attitudes than students who were taught in a traditional classroom via a lecture method. Likewise. Horowitz (1988) found in his observations of IBM's electronic training facility that although the lecture mode of teaching was much more time efficient in terms of the material covered, the attentiveness of the students was much less than with a “facilitation" mode in which frequent student responses were elicited via the computer technology. These findings probably resulted more from the fact that students were constantly being asked questions (via the computer) demanding attention rather than passively listening to a lecture. Had the lectures involved frequent questioning, the results may have been different.

Educators agree that critical and creative thinking, and reasoning are part of "higher-order thinking" (Elder 1991). Factual information, considered essential in most subjects, is only the first level in the process of thinking (Fisher and Grant 1983). Although the higher-level processes should be the goal of college and graduate education, research suggests that college instructors tend to emphasize factual information and to neglect higher-order cognitive development. Research on within class activity at the college level typically points to the same disconcerting conclusion: Barnes (1983) found that instructor questioning occurred only 2.6% of the class time and 62.7% of the questions involved factual information. In another study, Smith (1983) found that although 14.2% of class time was spent on discourse involving students, 49% of this time involved factual information and only 17% involved higher-order discussion.

Computer-based teaching methods may facilitate greater depth of analysis and, hence, discussion on the higher cognitive level. In research conducted at a training facility, Gist et al. (1988–1989) found that the teaching method involving computerbased modeling and practice significantly enhanced the trainees'mastery of the subject. In terms of higher-level thinking, Bump (1990) observed that the questions asked in the computer-supported classroom appeared better thought out than those asked in traditional classrooms. Hence, potential appears to exist for computer-based teaching methods to positively impact learning outcomes.

## 4. Research Design and Method

To examine the research questions, we conducted observational case studies over a fifteen-week period of three courses meeting in electronic classrooms. The three courses observed were all part of the same MBA program. No student attended more than one of the courses observed. King, et al. (1990) argue that research examining computer-supported teaching requires a complete understanding of the context in which the computer is used. Furthermore, the need for observational studies of computer-based teaching in their natural settings has been noted by educational psychologists (Papert 1987, Pennypacker 1981).

Case study research is appropriate in situations where the research question involves a "how", "why", or exploratory "what" question, the investigator has no control over actual behavioral events, and the focus is on contemporary as opposed to historical phenomenon (Yin 1989). The research presented in this paper fits all three criteria. The questions in this study revolve around how information technology is used in university classrooms and what some of the effects of the use are.³ We did not have the means to control the behavior of the professors and students. We will, therefore, report how electronic classrooms were actually utilized during three courses throughout the semester of Spring 1990. Because the desire was to explore the use of computer-based teaching methods in their natural environment (i.e. the classroom), a controlled experiment such as that used by King et al. (1990) was inappropriate.

## 4.1. Research Design

The following describes the case study approach used. The primary unit of analysis was a course. An embedded unit of analysis was the students in each course. The study was a descriptive one. Computer-based teaching was the intervention to be described; the context was graduate business education; the possible outcomes to be described included classroom activity (including teaching methods and student interaction), and student reactions to the technology.

The sites selected were electronic classrooms used in graduate business education at a large Southwestern state university. Two such classrooms existed and both were used. A multiple-case design was applied using literal replication logic to choose the second case (a maximally similar case which should therefore have predicted similar results to the first case) and theoretical replication logic to choose the third (a maximally different case that should have produced contrary results but for predictable reasons) (Yin 1989, p. 53). External validity was addressed by using such replication logic in selecting multiple-case studies.

Two of the three cases involved two sections of the same course, taught by two instructors who collaborated extensively. The other course observed, taught by a third instructor, was unrelated to the first two. Thus, considering the student, teacher, classroom, and course as variables defining the case, the second case differed from the first in that the students were different and the teacher was different (the course material and the classroom were the same). The third case, a maximally dissimilar case, involved different students, a different teacher, a different classroom, and different course material from the first two.

The study was longitudinal over a semester-the natural time choice with a class as the unit of analysis. Ten sessions were observed for each of the three classes: one observation per class per week, excluding the first two weeks and the last week of class and the periods for tests and exams. The first two weeks were used to refine the coding scheme. For each case, student attitudes were also surveyed at the end of the semester. The responses were used only to interpret the activity in a given course and were not (indeed theoretically cannot) pooled across the classes.

## 4.2. Coding Scheme for Case Studies

Reliability for a case study was addressed by developing and using a detailed case study protocol to aid in the documentation of observations. A detailed coding scheme was developed to ensure consistent observations across the cases (see Appendix A).

The coding scheme included the type of material (i.e., content) being discussed, the teaching method used for five-minute class intervals, and the interaction in class (see Appendix A). The possible types of content material included factual, procedural, conceptual and exploratory. These correspond to the four categories of information used by Williams (1977). The type of material being covered in class was obseryed in order to gather information concerning the nature of in-class learning that occurred. Conceptual and exploratory material are considered higher-order learning. Factual material is considered lower-order learning. Procedural material is middle-order learning. We considered teaching method to be the means by which an instructor chooses to communicate ideas and information to students during class. A computerbased teaching method is a teaching method used within an electronic classroom in which computer-based technology is used to store, retrieve, manipulate or present information, or to facilitate communication. The possible teaching methods included traditional (instructor lecturing or guiding discussion), computer-based (instructor and/or students using computers during class as part of instruction), and other (student presentations, guest lecture, or other methods not included in traditional or computer-based). Should an instructor change teaching methods midway through a five minute period, the interval would be split in half and coded as such. Detailed notes were taken about the teaching method, including how any technology was used, so that more specific categories could be determined later if necessary.

Interaction was an active student involvement in classroom learning through verbal, written, or electronic communication of ideas or information between students and the instructor or among students. Interactions recorded included (1) the number and content of questions asked by the instructor and answered by students, (2) the number and content of questions answered by the instructor himself, and (3) the number and content of instructor questions answered by several students at once. A summary was also kept of the number of different students participating. The content was coded according to whether the question dealt with the subject or the technology, and whether the question was factual, procedural, conceptual or exploratory. These correspond to the four categories of information as defined by Williams (1977). Student questions were coded according to the same content scheme as instructor questions.

## 4.3. Observer

Because this was a case study and not an experiment in which the researchers had control over the instructor and teaching methods, it was desired by the instructors teaching the courses that one and the same observer attended the classes to reduce the potential distraction of the study to the class. In order to eliminate possible bias and increase consistent and accurate coding, the observer chosen had previously taken the same or a similar course from the instructors observed. Thus, the course content was familiar to the observer for the courses taught. In total, the observer spent thirtyeight hours observing the courses for a total of 450 five-minute intervals.

## 4.4. Background of the Cases

4.4.1. The Classrooms. Two of the courses met in a classroom that had 15 IBM ATs on desks facing the instructor. The instructor had an AT on his podium. The classroom was actually a meeting room into which computers had been installed specifically for the two courses. Displays were made from the instructor's AT to an overhead screen. The other classroom, much more sophisticated, had 24 IBM PS/2s and an elaborate instructor console (Klingman and Phillips 1988). This classroom was specifically designed to house state-of-the-art teaching and learning technologies. Each instructor had the option to use or not to use the technology during class.

4.4.2. Case I: Financial Statement Analysis. The objective of this elective course was to provide an introduction to the techniques used to make investment decisions based on financial statement data. The 23 students, 21 male and 2 female, were second-year masters of business administration students, professional public accounting (PPA) students or senior undergraduate CPA students. All students had used personal computers prior to the course and all students had used at least one spreadsheet package and one statistical package. The course was taught in the classroom with 15 IBM ATs. Students worked in pairs on the computers. Participation during class was encouraged but not part of a student's grade. Although he had not taught in an electronic classroom, the instructor was a tenured faculty with extensive teaching experience and experience using computers for his personal research.

4.4.3. Case II: Financial Statement Analysis. This class was a second section of the course described above but taught by a different instructor. The instructor worked closely with Instructor 1. The instructors jointly developed the syllabus, assignments, and exams. However, they graded individually and developed their own lecture material. The instructors appeared to spend a great deal of time outside of class preparing for computer exercises. The preparation involved gathering financial data at the library and entering the data into a database for class use. All the students in this section were graduating MBA or PPA students. There were no undergraduate students. All students had used personal computers prior to the course and all students had used at least one spreadsheet package and one statistical package. Students generally worked in pairs on the computers, but occasionally worked individually. There were 16 males and 1 female in the class. Participation during class was encouraged but not part of a student's grade. The instructor, also tenured with many years of teaching experience, had taught for several years in an electronic classroom.

The data was analyzed both quantitatively and qualitatively. Quantitative analysis produced the means and percentages of the items coded for the three case studies. The quantitative data was used to support the qualitative insights from the observations. The benefits of combining qualitative and quantitative research have been noted by Kaplan and Duchon (1988)

## 5. Results

The observational data from the three case studies will be discussed on a case-bycase basis. For each case, we will first describe the specific traditional and computerbased teaching methods used by the instructor and the outcomes of the use, including the type of material covered and the nature of interaction. We will present student reactions to each of the methods as indicated by their responses to questions on the survey completed at the end of the semester. Where appropriate, we will make comparisons among the cases. The discussions will answer the questions “how is computer technology used in university classrooms?" and "what are some of the outcomes of computer-based teaching methods?".

## 5.1. Case 1: Financial Statement Analysis with Instructor 1

5.1.1. The Teaching Methods and Their Outcomes—Case 1. Instructor 1 used one type of traditional method: instructor-led question-answer sessions and three types of computer-based methods: unstructured computer exercises, structured computer exercises, and computer lab sessions. These methods and their outcomes will be described and compared.

During the traditional sessions,5 the instructor's style was one of frequently asking factual, short-answer questions—he averaged 5.2 questions per five-minute interval. Instructor 1's traditional style can best be described as an instructor-led question answer session. The same several students tended to respond to the questions, most of which required only a “yes" or “no" answer. Fourteen students participated during traditional sessions on average, although an average of only four students answered more than one question. On numerous occasions, the instructor had to repeat the question several times before a murmured response of several students was obtained. An example of typical interaction during traditional methods is shown in Table 2.

Sixty-one percent of the 237 questions asked by the instructor during his traditional methods were factual questions. Students, in turn, asked few questions during the traditional methods (a total of 24). There were 11 exploratory questions asked, although these were asked throughout the semester by the same student, who seemed very interested in the material. Instructor 1 used this traditional teaching method during 51% of the observed time.

There were three types of methods in which the instructor employed the computer technology, One was computer sessions during which no handouts were given, but the students were expected to follow the instructor's command sequences projected from his computer to the overhead screen. These sessions had as their purpose both to familiarize students with important commands and to serve as a basis for class discussion. We will refer to these as unstructured computer exercises. A second type of method was a more structured session in which the students had handouts showing them exactly the sequence of commands to enter. These sessions were designed to familiarize students with the software package to be used for assignments. The students worked at their own pace. We will refer to these as structured computer exercises. The final computer-based method was lab sessions during which the students worked on homework assignments and the instructor was available to answer questions. No new course material was presented during these computer lab sessions. In all three methods the students worked in teams of two. For the structured and unstructured exercises, the students chose their own partner. The instructor assigned pairs at random for lab sessions.

During both the structured and unstructured exercise sessions, it was observed that if one student in the team of two was familiar with the technology, this student took charge. Such behavior may have hindered the learning of the less familiar student who observed the team member rather than himself interacting with the computer. It was also observed that when students failed to properly execute a command on their computer, they would frequently ask the adjacent team for help, rather than asking the instructor. At times, the class would appear to descend into a state of pandemonium with no team paying attention to the instructor and virtually all the teams at different points in the exercise. The instructor attempted to prevent this by working the exercise on his own computer at a slow pace and asking questions after a series of commands. However, many teams chose to ignore the instructor and work at their own pace.

The third type of use—lab sessions—gave the students the opportunity to begin their assignments and encounter initial problems solved with the instructor's help. These sessions always occurred one or two class sessions after a computer exercise session had already familiarized students with the software they would be using to complete an assignment. Nevertheless, the instructor was kept busy during these labs with almost every team at some point encountering a problem. The fact that the students encountered many problems suggests that despite the preparation on the technology and with the software during the computer exercises, they had difficulty transferring their knowledge of the technology procedures to different areas of the subject. Perhaps if the students had paid attention to the instructor's discussions during the structured exercises, they would have encountered fewer problems during the labs. The instructor typically spent between three and seven minutes answering one team's question, indicating that the problems encountered by the students were not trivial. In fact, it may be because of the preparation from the exercises that the students did not have to ask simple questions but had more complex problems during the lab sessions. The problems did not become less frequent as the semester progressed. This is because each lab session was devoted to a different assignment using a different software. The problems encountered were specific to the technology and the assignment.

In summary, Instructor 1 used the computer-based methods for primarily two purposes: (1) to familiarize students with computer-based technology used to solve accounting problems, and (2) to enable students to experiment with the technology, encounter unanticipated problems, and seek help from the instructor in order to enable them to make better use of their time spent on assignments out of class. In fact, 87% of the total course time spent on procedural material coincided with computer-based teaching. Thus, there was a distinct separation between conceptual and factual material, covered during traditional sessions, and procedural and exploratory material, covered during computer-based sessions. Because of this distinct separation between material type by technology, the students may have learned more about how to solve problems during the computer-based methods while learning more about concepts during the question-answer lecture sessions.

In addition, the computer-based methods appeared to offer opportunities to cover a wider gamut of material as indicated by the fact that all types of questions (except technical exploratory) were represented in computer-based sessions, whereas traditional sessions were devoted mostly to subiect factual questions. ## 5.2. Case 2: Financial Statement Analysis with Instructor 2

Case 2 served as a close literal replication of Case 1. Both the course material and the electronic classroom were the same. Only the student and instructor differed. Consequently, we expected similar uses and outcomes as from Case 1. The results were, however, quite different.

5.2.1. The Teaching Methods and Their Outcomes—Case 2. Instructor 2's class sessions were much less formal and less intense (in terms of the amount of material covered each period) than those of Instructor 1. Class frequently did not begin until 10 or 15 minutes into the period and a great deal of class time was spent discussing projects due and projects being returned. Instructor 2 used one type of traditional session—lecture, and two types of computer-based methods—structured computer exercises and computer labs.

Instructor 2's traditional method, which he used 32% of the observed class sessions, was a lecture in which he did not use notes, but rather discussed issues as they seemed to arise in his mind. While Instructor 2 did not engage the students with nearly as many questions as Instructor 1, a greater percentage of those asked were exploratory in nature which required greater thought among the students to reply. Table 8 illustrates typical questioning from Instructor 2. Sixty-five percent of Instructor 2's questions were exploratory (compared to 30% for Instructor 1) and 33% were subject procedural. The questions took longer to ask compared to Instructor 1. The questions also seemed ad hoc rather than preplanned, and were interspersed with comments by the instructor. This instructor covered factual material rather quickly and rarely asked factual questions (only 1 of the 44 questions was factual). Instructor 2 assumed that the students knew the facts—an assumption rarely made by Instructor 1. For example, in a session when the class was looking at ratios, Instructor 2 asked such questions as “How do you evaluate the Debt/Equity Ratio? Is 0.8 big?" After someone responded that it depends on the industry, the instructor went on to lead a discussion about which industries should be expected to have a high ratio and why. In fact, this instructor spent 64% of the time during traditional sessions discussing conceptual material.

The instructor frequently opened the lectures up for students to ask questions, although they were also free to interrupt him at any point with a question. When the instructor specifically allocated 10 or 15 minutes to answering student questions, the students focused on basic questions about the assignments, or conceptual questions concerning the material.

This instructor used the computer technology for two types of methods. One was structured computer exercises. These sessions were notably procedural focusing on how to manipulate the data into a model rather than on how and why to choose a certain model for analysis and how to interpret the results. Whereas the lecture sessions were very unstructured sessions in which the instructor did not use notes but rather proceeded to discuss issues as they arose and occasionally used homework assignments as the starting point of discussion, the computer exercise sessions were very structured in which the instructor handed out copies of the screen displays the students would see on their terminals and the exact commands they should enter. Compared to the same structured exercises of Instructor 1, where the students often had difficulty following, Instructor 2's students had little difficulty. One reason may be that Instructor 2 rarely asked questions during these sessions. Another may be that Instructor 2 was more familiar with and knowledgeable about the technology (he in fact did much of the work in setting up the classroom) and thus encountered few problems disrupting the structure and sequence of the computer sessions.

The second method of use was identical to the lab sessions of Instructor 1. However, the students in Instructor 2's class had far fewer technical problems requiring the instructor's attention during these sessions (10) than the students in Instructor 1's course (21). Because the procedures learned during the exercise sessions were supposed to be used to solve problems during the lab sessions, the fewer problems encountered by Instructor 2's students during the lab sessions may indicate that Instructor 2's exercise sessions were more effective. In addition, the lab sessions of Instructor 2 tended to evolve into exploratory discussions in which a team asked the instructor a particularly intriguing question that the instructor then opened up for the entire class to think about. These discussions lasted on average 15 minutes.

Since there were only 18 students and 15 computers, most students worked alone during the exercise sessions although some opted to work in teams even if there were extra computers available. The instructor assigned the teams for the lab sessions. Similar to Instructor 1's class, technically experienced students appeared to dominate the team's activity on the computer. Table 9 summarizes the computer-based methods and outcomes of Instructor 2.

In total, computer-based teaching accounted for 33% of the observed class time. During the computer-based methods, 73% of the time procedural material was being covered. Hence, Instructor 2 favored traditional methods for conceptual teaching, computer-based teaching for procedural material, and split exploratory analysis among traditional and computer-based methods (see Table 10). Although roughly equal time was spent in traditional and computer-based teaching methods, 40 of 44 instructor questions were asked during traditional methods and only 4 asked during computer-based methods; 20 of 29 student questions were asked during traditional methods and only 9 of 29 during the computer-based sessions. However, the exploratory interaction that occurred during the computer-based methods was always the result of students asking questions during the lab sessions that the instructor decided the whole class should discuss. Hence, during the computer-based lab sessions, the students appeared to be more in control of their learning and asked exploratory questions that led to further analysis and discussion.

5.2.3. Summary. However, Instructor 3 did not conduct real-time manipulation of the software. Nor did his students experiment with the computers during class. Thus, despite having the more sophisticated technology at his disposal and teaching a highly technical/procedural subject matter, Instructor 3 employed computer technology less often than Instructors 1 and 2 and used the computer-technology in a rather limited fashion. We do not attribute this to the different classroom, since he had the more sophisticated room, nor to the difference in the material, since the technical material seemed to be appropriate for IT support, nor to the instructor's experience with the technology, since the instructor was an expert in the area of databases and decision support systems and had several years teaching experience in the electronic classroom, but rather to the difference in the instructor's attitude toward computerbased teaching methods.

## 5.4. Summary of the Three Case Studies

5.4.1. How is Information Technology Incorporated into Classroom Activity? The three case studies evinced four primary uses of computer-based technology: (1) a means of interactive guided learning of technical procedures necessary to use in mastering the analysis of a subject, (2) a means of real-time manipulation of data and analysis of data to provide the basis for conceptual and exploratory learning during class, (3) a lab where students can work individually or in pairs until they need the instructor's help, and (4) a display mechanism—a replacement for transparencies, slides or blackboards. The first two uses involve both instructor and student use of computers during class; the third involves student use only; the fourth instructor use only. The first three methods involve interaction between the student and the computer. The last method involves no interactive student use of the computer. Besides illustrating several uses of information technology for computer-based teaching, the cases demonstrate problems associated with various computer-based teaching methods. These include the confusion that can arise when students must both emulate an instructor's actions on a computer and listen to his questions, the difficulty of ensuring that students working in pairs both learn the material, the time spent getting the class ready for computer use, and the tremendous time commitment required of instructors to adapt their course material for interactive computer applications during class. Instructors 1 and 2 had the benefit of sharing material so that they could split the work of inputting financial figures into software packages for students to manipulate.

Although the computer-based methods of Instructors 1 and 2 resulted in student interaction with the computer, there appeared to be less class interaction during the computer-based sessions than during traditional sessions. The content of the questions seemed to become more procedural in nature during the computer-based teaching methods. Perhaps if electronic communication media such as electronic mail had been available, class interaction would have been greater. Interactive computer use requires student participation and would therefore be expected to increase interaction by virtue of getting the students involved in the instruction. However, more students asked questions when the computer teçhnology was used as a display mechanism (Instructor 3's class) than when it was used interactively by the students (Instructor 1 and 2's classes). This may indicate that the students in Instructor 3's class had to ask simple procedural questions that they might have been able to quickly discover on their own had they been working simultaneously with the instructor on their PCs as opposed to merely watching. Most likely, the students in classes 1 and 2 were able to solve their own problems and questions when working on the computers during the interactive computer-based teaching methods, and therefore class interaction was not increased. The class interaction that occurred as a result of the computer-based teaching seemed to revolve around complex problems and questions in Cases 1 and 2.

The computer-based teaching methods requiring interactive student use during class appeared to help students assimilate the information and acquire insights not stated by the instructor. Also, the computer exercises and lab sessions introduced to students the procedures necessary to complete out-of-class assignments. This may have enabled the students to make more effective use of their time spent outside of the class. The above results are in accordance with prior research that suggests that students are motivated and learn more when they are given more control over their material (Perry and Magnusson 1989, Schloss et al. 1988, Fisher and Grant 1983). Certainly the lab sessions gave the students the most control of any of the teaching methods. It was always à student rather than an instructor who provoked exploratory analysis during computer sessions. Table 16 summarizes the outcomes of computerbased methods requiring hands-on student use and computer-based methods not requiring hands-on student use.

Comparing traditional methods to computer-based methods, in all three cases the instructors' primary use of computer-based methods was to cover procedural material whereas the traditional methods were used to convey factual and conceptual material. A question arises from the heavy use of computers to cover procedural issues in the two classes: why were the computers used by instructors as merely a vehicle for teaching procedural material rather than as a vehicle enabling a broader role in teaching concepts? A major reason may be that, as noted previously, it takes a tremendous amount of time to redesign a course for computer-based teaching methods. Another reason may be the newness of electronic classrooms: as different methods of use are recognized and developed, conceptual learning may become an important objective of computer-based teaching. ## 6. Implications, Future Research, Limitations, and Conclusions 6.1. Implications and Future Research

This study provides insight into how computer-based technology is used in university classrooms and how computer-based teaching methods may affect classroom interaction. A major observation is that interactive student use of computers is a prerequisite for effective computer-based teaching methods. As a presentation technology only, the computer appeared to offer no advantage over traditional display mechanisms. But as a device to be manipulated for analysis and procedural learning, the technology appeared to provide a new method for stimulating exploratory or higher-order discussions

Another observation is that the level of sophistication of the classroom does not appear to be a factor in effective utilization of the technology, assuming that a threshold level of technological sophistication has been attained. This is evinced in the fact that while one classroom had much greater technological capabilities in terms of both hardware and software, it was the classroom with lesser use. It is therefore the method of using the technology rather than the capabilities of the technology that is of primary importance.

There appears to be a rather strong dependency between the type of material covered during class and the teaching method: in-class learning tended to be procedurally focused during computer-based methods and conceptually or factually focused during traditional methods. There also appears to be a dependency between available technology and method (with the computer-based methods used almost half of the observed class time). A relationship between the method and the type of questions asked seemed to exist. Procedural and exploratory questions dominated computer-based methods whereas conceptual and factual questions dominated traditional methods. Interaction appeared to become more procedural during the computer-based methods, yet over time Instructor 1 was able to conduct exploratory and conceptual analysis using computer-based methods and Instructor 2's students frequently turned computer sessions into exploratory discussions.

In all cases teacher style appeared to be an important factor. Instructors 1 and 2 appeared to change their styles to make better use of the computer technology. Instructor 1 altered his style from one of asking frequent factual questions to one ofless frequent, exploratory questions during the computer-based methods where the students were engaged in interactive computer use. Instructor 2 altered his low-key, unstructured lecture style to a very structured computer-based method style. Instructors not willing or not noticing the necessity of such change might have vastly different effects from using the technology. This concurs with research that has shown instructor attitude to be a factor influencing successful innovation in education (Watson 1990, Bliss et al. 1986).

Student preference for one teaching method over another appears to be an important moderating variable between the method and its effect. The student rankings of the methods suggested varying preferences from one teaching method to another. While no course can be tailored to suit the preferences of each student, it would be interesting to determine the characteristics of the students that determine their preference for a certain teaching method. In the context of programmed instruction, Matta and Kern (1991) found that there are student personality characteristics that affect learning success with different teaching methods. In particular, they found that introverted students performed better than did extroverted students. Student familiarity with the technology and familiarity with the subject may be important to their attitude. Thus future research needs to determine the important student characteristics that make in-class learning by one computer-based method more effective than another. It is also possible that because graduate students tend to be highly motivated and intelligent, the appropriate method for them may differ from the most appropriate method for other levels of students. This should also be considered as a “student factor" in future research.

Another important relationship concerns the effect of computer-based teaching on out-of-class learning. Certainly the case studies indicated that the computer-based methods tended to shift the interaction towards procedural material during class. Perhaps computer-based methods can supply students with the necessary procedural and technical knowledge during class which they need to learn more productively on their own. Some component of out-of-class learning should therefore be included in future models even where the desire is to examine in-class learning. In addition, the link between in-class activity and out-of-class learning as well as course performance should be examined in future research.

We thus propose our Research Model for use in future research examining electronic classrooms and student learning. The model is shown in Figure 1.

The model suggests that course content, available technology, and instructor factors (style, experience, attitude) influence the type of teaching method used. Student factors (experience, attitude, personality) and instructor factors mediate the impact of the teaching method on class interaction and in-class learning (i.e., the type of material covered during class—factual, procedural, conceptual, or exploratory). In turn, in-class learning and class interaction affect out-of-class learning and class performance.

## 6.2. Limitations

Several boundaries to the generalizability of the study warrant mention. First, the study relied on one research method—observational case studies. Second, the course content of all three cases required quantitative or technical skills. Prior research has suggested that subject matter is related to the effectiveness of instructional innovations (Kulik et al. 1982). Different types of computer-based methods may be best suited for different subject matters. This can be further examined in future research. Third, the students in this study were graduate students (except for two), all of whom had experience with computers. Undergraduate students or students without computer familiarity might react different to computer-based teaching. Fourth, the class size in all cases was around 20 students. There might be problems associated with larger class sizes. In particular, students would not be able to receive as much personal attention when they encountered problems during the computer-based sessions. Finally, it is possible that the observor had a preference for a particular teaching method that might have biased the observor's data collection.

## 6.3. Conclusion

This study has shown that there are many potential computer-based methods and that the methods can have different outcomes; it is therefore the method of using the technology and not the technology itself that has an effect on classroom activity. Future research should be careful to describe the exact nature of the computer-based method being examined. In this study, the most preferred methods were those that not only gave the students interactive use of the computer, but that allowed the students to work independently of the instructor on the computer to encounter their own problems in a classroom environment. This may sound like a more appropriate situation for an out-of-class lab, but the instructor's presence seemed to add a unique and important dimension, enabling these sessions to become a vehicle for subject exploration.

The current work suggests that there is a potential for computer-based teaching methods to improve classroom learning but that their potential sometimes remains untapped by the inability to use them effectively; this can be disruptive to the class. Part of the problem lies with the limitations of computer-based technology itself—it takes considerable time to set-up, prepare, and use the technology and to change applications during class. In addition, the importance of user-friendly and ergonomically-designed classrooms should not be overlooked (Schneiderman 1988). Where overhead screens are difficult to read or computers are positioned in a visually obstructive manner, students are likely to have difficulty following the instructor, especially in cases where they are trying to emulate the instructor's work on their own computer.

Studies on computer-supported meeting environments have found that people must not only have been trained how to use the hardware and software, but must also have been trained in meeting strategies in order to effectively use these resources (Hiltz and Johnson 1990). Similarly, this study of electronic classrooms suggests that new teaching methods may have to be learned in order to effectively use the technology. Certainly, given the research orientation of many universities, it cannot be expected that instructors themselves will desire to spend the time learning or developing new teaching methods that complement the available technology, regardless of how sophisticated the technology may be.

Overall, the case studies reported tentatively suggest that computer-based teaching methods might be encouraged as a means of enhancing classroom learning, although it may require trial-and-error or experimentation to determine the most effective uses of the technology. In addition, electronic classrooms need not be expensive and sophisticated arrangements to be effective. Although it is not possible to generalize from this study of graduate business students to other educational and training domains with different goals, we do feel the results have important implications for designers of electronic classrooms and business training facilities, instructors desiring to use such facilities, and information technology researchers interested in the impact of information technology on learning.\*

Appendix A: Coding Scheme.

Teaching Method

(1) Traditional: Neither instructor nor students using computers during class.

Traditional Methods Observed:

Lecture: Instructor talks until interrupted by a student question. Instructor-led question answer session: Instructor asks questions requiring short responses (typically “yes" or “no" or a short phrase).

Discussion: Instructor asks questions requiring substantial thought to answer. Answers much more in-depth than short phrases

(2) Computer-based: Instructor and/or students working on computers during class.

Computer-based Methods Observed:

Computer-supported Lecture: Instructor uses workstation to display his lecture notes.

Class Computer Exercise: Instructor and students use computers to go through a planned exercise.

Computer Lab Session: Students use computers to work while the professor is available for help. (3) Other

Student Presentation: Students as individuals or in teams present homework projects to the class. Administration: Handing out assignments, setting up materials, discussing homework assignments . .

## Material

(1) Factual: Generally explains "what". Examples: definitions, introduction to topics, people, history. (2) Procedural: Generally explains "how". Examples: how formulas are computed, how analysis is performed, how software is programmed.

(3) Conceptual: Generally explains "why". Examples: principles used to interpret situations, methods of analysis.

(4) Exploratory. Compares knowledge across domains, applies knowledge to a particular situation (as in the case a homework problem). Examples: why one method is superior to another in analyzing a particular situation.

## Questions

Subject Factual: The facts about the course material, analytical methods, or procedures used.

Examples: "What two methods of forecasting does Forster (the accounting textbook author) discuss?"

"What is the difference between ROA and After-Tax ROA?" “What is the accounting procedure used to evaluate revenue growth?'

Subject Procedural: The methods and formulas used to accomplish analysis, solve problems, or complete assignments.

Examples. “How do analysts aggregate forecasts effectively?" “How does increasing depreciation allowance affect net income?"

Subject Exploratory: The concepts behind the subject material. Requires students to think beyond the material presented. Often compares materials to other domains or looks at particular cases/problems.

Examples: "When is it advantageous to forecast?" "What explanation can you give for the forest products industry having such a higher XXX ratio than the other industries?"

Technology Factual: The facts about the technology or the software.

Examples. “Does Lotus allow you to import non-ascii files?" “Where is Profit Before Tax located on your spreadsheet?"

Technology Procedural: The technical procedures: how to get the software to give the desired results.

Example: "What command do I use if I want to get a report combining records from two different tables in the database?"

Technology Exploratory: The concepts behind the technology. Should rarely occur, but may occur when comparing one software package/language to another or when discussing when certain methods are appropriate.

Example: “Is there a more efficient way to have the database search for the desired records?"

Repeated Question: The instructor revises or repeats a question after getting no response to the original question.

Self-answered Question: The instructor awaits a response from a student but is forced to answer the question himself when no one responds

Group Responses: Three or more students simultaneously answer (or mumble as the case may be) the desired answer. Frequently occurs for simple questions, especially when there is a time delay so a few students mutter the awaited “yes" or “no".

Individual Responses: The number of responses to instructor questions made by a single student.

Total Number of Different Students Responding: Number of students answering questions during a class session. Only the first response of each student is counted. Thus the cumulative number of different students responding is calculated

Student Problem: When working on the computers alone or in groups of two during group exercises, students may have problems which can not be addressed without individual attention by the instructor.

Number of Students Asking Questions: Calculated the same way as “total number of different students responding".
