Phillip Ein-Dor

Eli Segev

Faculty of Managemen Tel Aviv University Tel Aviv. Israel

Faculty of Manageme Tel Aviv University Tel A viv, Israe

Seventeen major types of information systems are identified and defined by vectors of their attributes and functions. These systems are then classified by numerical methods. The quantitative analysis is interpreted in terms of the development history of information system types. Two major findings are that the numerical classification autonomously follows the chronological appearance of system types and that, along tbe time line, systems have followed two major paths of development; these have been termed the applied artificial intelligence path and the human interface path. The development of ne types of systems is considered within the framework of a theory of technological evolution. It is shown that newer types of systems result from gradual accretion of new technologies on one hand, and loss of older ones on the other. Conclusions are drawn concerning the value of taxonomy in studying information systems, in suggesting possible research directions, and the desirability of rationalizing research efforts within the IS discipline.

## Introduction

The changes in organizations that have followed successive waves of new information technology posed new challenges to managers and scholars who study the phenomenon. The introduction of each definable type of system was followed by a stream of research on the impact of that type on the organization. Streams of research focused on distinct types of systems which came into being, for example, EDP, MIS, DSS, OA, Expert Systems, and robotics. As a result, the Information Systems (IS) area is today fragmented into multiple, disjoint interest groups, each focused on a unique type of IS, with little cross-fertilization and much duplication of research efforts. Occasionally, when developments render boundaries between systems vague, comparative analyses of different systems and syntheses of knowledge about them are undertaken (e.g. Rish et al. 1988, Segev and Gray 1989, Turban and Watkins 1986). A good example is the development of the annual conference on DSS into a conference on DSS, GDSS, and Expert Systems (DSS-89).

The objective of this paper is to attempt to understand the diversity of the IS phenomenon and to provide a conceptual framework for discussing the diversity. Given this goal, it is necessary to define IS as broadly as possible, while yet excluding any entities not generally recognized as IS.

Our definition of IS is: any computerized system with a user or operator interface an information system, provided the computer is not physically embedded

The intention is to distinguish between what are generally considered information systems and other computerized systems in which the computer functions without human intervention or is part of a larger physical system. Examples of systems not considered IS are computerized ignition systems and self-guiding missiles. Manufacturing robots are perhaps a borderline case; we consider them to be information systems because they provide interfaces for human operators and the computer is generally distinct from the manipulators. Office automation systems and decision support systems are examples of mainstream information systems.

As stated above, the goal of this paper is to understand and introduce some concep tual order into the phenomenon of proliferation of information system types. The problem was perceived as one of evaluating the relationship between the aggregations of technologies which constitute information systems and the names or labels applied to those aggregations—does each label denote a unique kind of system (i.e., of technology aggregation) or are essentially similar systems distinguished by different labels? Furthermore, the question was raised whether the process of system differentiation might be identified and described. These goals were addressed in this study via the classification of IS and the history of their development.

## Methodology

## System Identification

The study began by combing literature on IS and computers and noting any general system types in order to identify as many major types of information systems as possible. This search yielded 17 systems, beginning chronologically with early computation and data processing and culminating in group decision support systems and manufacturing robots. A list of the systems identified and sample definitions from the literature appear in Appendix 1. A few examples with references are also provided for each type of system; these appear in Table Al at the end of the Appendix. The examples are provided in order to demonstrate that they represent recognized classes of systems and that they are actually installed types, not merely figments of researchers' imaginations.

There is an element of arbitrariness in the list of systems adopted in the sense that there are many intermediate systems which were not included. In general, in those cases where there is a long developmental history of a given type of systems, we included the beginning and current points only. Thus, only early and mature DP are represented when there are several intervening developments that could be identified—see e.g. Nolan (1973, 1979). C'l appears without its antecedents, C^ and C^ because no definitions were found in the literature we surveyed. Furthermore, subgroups of major types were not explicitly included; examples are marketing information systems, which are considered MIS, and statistical systems—a form of scientific computing. This was deemed permissible for both theoretical and pragmatic reasons. On theoretical grounds, complete enumeration is not necessary in order to produce a classification; ideally, a rohust taxonomy permits prediction of typ>es unknown to the taxonomist, or otherwise omitted, as did Mendeleyev's periodic table of elements. One does run the risk, however, that the addition of new types may require reclassification. On pragmatic grounds, the list was trimmed for economy of presentation— essential in this study. An advantage of such omissions is that they may be used to test the validity of the classification scheme; a few such tests which we performed indicated no need to revise the taxonomy.'

## System Definition

Information systems are generally defined either in terms of their functions or attributes (Ein-Dor and Segev 1978). The first kind of definition focuses on what the system does; the second pays more attention to the components of the system. Consider, for example, the two definitions of Executive Information Systems in Appendix 1; the first is expressed in terms of the hardware and software attributes of EIS while the second focuses on the functionality required. There is some overlap between the types of definition—for example, one or more attributes can provide a given function (e.g. input) or a function (e.g. data retrieval) may require several attributes—but the attributes and functions are generally not exactly coextensive. Ideally, it seems that complete definitions should encompass both functions and attributes. In this study it was decided to maintain completeness by using both attributes and functions to define systems, but to do so in two parallel sets of definitions in order to permit eross-validation. The attributes and functions, then, are the taxonomic characters used in classifying systems in this study.

The next step was to identify and define the attributes and functions which describe systems and which serve as taxonomic characters. In order to minimize arbitrariness in the choice of characters, we did not provide any characters ourselves, but proceeded by scanning a large number of system definitions and noting all the attributes and functions mentioned. Definitions of the characters api>ear in Appendix 2 for the 31 attributes and 27 functions identified. The list of characters is complete in the sense that any character encountered was included. It is, of course, quite possible that definitions we did not survey include additional characters and that some of the characters included in the definitions are not really relevant; this is the price paid for making the process as objective as possible. The choice of taxonomic characters and the issue of completeness are discussed in somewhat greater length in the Methodological Note appended to the paper.

Given the attributes and functions, each of the information systems was described by two bit-vectors, a vector of attributes and a vector of functions; presence of a feature was denoted by 1, and absence by 0. These vectors were developed as syntheses of various definitions and descriptions of the systems as they appear in the literature. The definitions in Appendix 1 are examples of the much larger number of definitions which were used to develop the definition vectors; space does not permit replication of all the definitions used.

## Technological Evolution Interpretation

(a) Concepts of Evolution. To this point, we have employed quantitative method to classify types of information systems into groups with similar characters. It is now our intent to apply concepts of technological evolution to the results ofthe quantitative analysis.

This is not the first time that evolutionary ideas have been used in the context of information systems. In most cases, however, that use concerns the development of single systems, not of types of systems, which is our focus. It seems appropriate to briefly discuss some ofthe prior developmental interpretations in order to precisely delineate our interpretation. One ofthe earliest attempts to put information systems in the framework of a developmental process is Nolan's (1973. 1979) stage hypothesis; this proposes a model of the development of information processing within an organization. In analyzing Nolan's work, King and Kraemer (1984) point out that, in Van Parijs's (1981) terms. Nolan's approach is "evolutionist" rather than "evolutionary." The main distinction is that evolutionist theories occupy themselves with the direction of change and its final destination, while evolutionary theories are indifferent to these characteristics and concentrate on the mechanisms which produce change. Most ofthe IS papers with developmental explanations deal, as do Nolan's, with the development of a specific system within a given organization and so are evolutionist in nature. This is amply demonstrated by alluding to some papers in the proceedings of a conference on Evolutionary Information Systems (Hawgood 1982).

As the title ofthe conference and papers presented suggest, the above distinction between the terms "evolutionary" and "evolutionist" is not strictly maintained.

Swanson (1982) suggests "the design as genotype, the implementation as phenotype, the utilization as natural selection." Land (1982) states that "The evolutionary model for an application assumes that in general the basic system in terms of the system's functions changes very slowly through time . . .". More specifically, Wasserman and Shewmake (1982) describe an "automated tool . . . as an aid to the development and evolution of user/program interaction in an interactive information system." Thus, these are examples of evolutionist, rather than evolutionary approaches. A notable exception to the generally evolutionist approach is Dickson's (1981) examination ofthe evolution of various aspects ofthe MIS field from its inception to the date of writing. In particular, the paper ends with a discussion ofthe origin of DSS and their affinity to MIS; although not stated in explicitly evolutionary terms, it can easily be so interpreted.

From the preceding discussion, it follows that virtually all developmental explanations in the IS field to date have been evolutionist in nature rather than evolutionary. We believe that this is the first attempt to place the full range of IS phenomena within an evolutionary context.

Evolutionary concepts are sometimes employed outside their original domain of biology and evolutionary explanations have been found useful in other fields such as the social sciences (Van Parijs 1981). In this study we have drawn mainly on sources utilizing the natural selection evolutionary model as applied to biological diversity. In applying the concept of evolution, a careful distinction must be made between the sense in which biologists regard evolution and that in which it is employed here. The analogy must be recognized as just that and should not be interpreted literally. As Basalla (1988) puts it in his application of evolutionary concepts to the history of technology:

Emphatically, I do not propose the establishment of a one-to-one correspondence between these markedly different domains (the world of the made and the world ofthe born, au.). I employ the evolutionary metaphor or analogy selectively, with the expectation that this metaphor will give us insights otherwise unavailable to the history of technology.

Given that we, too, are addressing the historical development of a particular subset of technology, Basalla's framework is particularly apposite and, in the following discussion, we examine the four basic concepts of his framework—(//"v^r5/fy, continuity, novelty, and selection—in the context of information systems

The concept of continuity, in the technological domain, is that new artifacts are no unique, but that each is related to its predecessors. This is the foundation of the evolutionary analogy, as of evolution itself. Without continuity, there can be no evolution. No attempt will be made here to answer the question whether the first computerized information systems fit this mold, but, given the first information systems, we believe that continuity is amply demonstrated by the quantitative analysis presented above. Later types of systems tend to be characterized by more attributes and functions than earher types. This is shown in Table 3, which exhibits a very strong and highly significant correlation between year of first mention and number of characters. Thus, technological progress in information systems seems to proceed largely by accretion of technologies. The older characters are generally maintained as new ones are added, although some are occasionally sloughed off in the development of new kinds of systems and others atrophy or disappear; batch processing is an example of an attribute which appears in Appendix 3 in tbose systems which have fewer attributes in general and then tends to disappear from systems wbich have more attributes. Paralleling biological evolution, older system types persist after new ones have been developed; DP and CAM are good examples.

Diversity is essential to natural evolution in that it provides the richness of types which make feasible the selection of some and the rejection of most. Basalla presents the view that diversity in the technological domain is not the result of existential imperatives, as bas often been argued, but arises rather from a conscious choice to pursue novelty, even though existence is quite possible without new artifacts. Auto mobiles did not emerge from a shortage of horses and carriages, but from a culturally inspired choice between options. These options were equally viable from the technical point of view and in fact coexisted for many decades, and still coexist in many cultures.

Analogously, organizations functioned quite well before each ofthe new types of information system was introduced and could have continued to function without them. GDSS and expert systems, for example, were initially developed because ofthe interest of researchers, not because organizations found them to be existential imperatives. Thus, with infonnation systems as with means of transportation, new types arise not because they are indispensable, but because novelty is a motivating force for human activity, albeit of different strength in different cultures. As with other inventions, once the use of a given type of information system becomes customary it does tend to become indispensable, as anyone who has become accustomed to depend on a personal computer can testify.

With the introduction of a new type of system, each organization is faced with the choice to adopt it or not. Some types of systems are either not accepted, or accepted only slowly, because they are not well adapted either technically or economically. Tbe very slow adoption of expert systems, first developed in the 1970s, appears to be a case in point. With the advent of new types of systems, organizations tnay also choose to discard old ones; early DP applications, MRP and C^ are examples of systems frequently replaced by later types. These organizational choices lead to the selection of systems and their consequent survival or demise

The nature of selection for information systems is quite different from that of living organisms; here there are no chance mutations and natural selection; diversification and selection are both functions of conscious choice on the part of men and women seeking to achieve predetermined goals. For this reason, we hesitate to push the evolutionary metaphor too strongly in regard to selection. Furthermore, with information systems, it seems much more a question of rate of adoption rather than outright rejection of new types. We are hard pressed to think of any kind of computerized information system which appeared on the scene and then totally died out as did tbe stone hammer, but this may well be because of the yet very short history of information systems. Automobiles also appeared originally in steam, electric, and internal combustion versions; many years elapsed before the first two types left the field completely to the third.^

' Perhaps the gradual reemergence ofthe electric automobile demonstrates another difference between natural and technological selection, namely that, unlike biological species, technologies may reemerge after a period of extinction.

Evidence for the eventual reduction ofthe number of system types through extinction is provided by past experience. The mechanism is consolidation of features from a number of systems into a single new one. One example is the current tendency to integration of MRP II and CAD/CAM into computer integrated manufacturing (CIM), where CAD/CAM is itself an integration of two types of systems. C^I is an even more extreme case with the initial two C's representing command and control. To these was added the third C, communications, and then I for intelligence.

(b) Application of Evolutionary Concepts. Given that the framework of technolog cal evolution seems to apply quite well to information systems, it is now possible to explore our findings in these terms. This begins by imposing an evolutionary interpretation on the findings reported above in the Taxonomic Interpretation section. As we indicated there, the systems are distributed in the two-dimensional space in such a way that, generally, the chronologically earliest systems are on the left and the latest on the right. The systems on the left are also those with the fewest attributes and functions while those on the right have the most; we stress again that the number of characteristics does not cause the distribution, but the differences between them. Just as more complex organisms are considered more highly developed than less complex ones, and that the more complex ones generally evolve later, so the systems on the right are the more highly developed temporally, functionally, and by number of characteristics. It is an interesting result that a plausible evolutionary classification is provided by the quantitative analysis.

We may now also reinterpret the partitions in Figures I and 2. In the taxonomic interpretation, the orientation ofthe partitions was presented as differentiating earlier from later system types; in the evolutionary interpretation, these same partitions differentiate systems that are more highly evolved in terms of number of attributes and functions from those that have fewer features.

Applying what we know about the various systems and their development, one may attempt intuitively to infer some ofthe patterns of evolutionary development. One such path which appears in Figure 1, is early DP, to MRP, to MIS, to DSS, to EIS and GDSS. As one ofthe elements of DSS is the application of mathematical and statistical techniques, we find DSS and scientific computing close together; we may think of DSS as MIS which have adopted some ofthe qualities of scientific computation. Similarly, we find the offshoots of DSS—EIS and GDSS—in close proximity to Office Automation, elements of which are crucial to the two later types of systems. A similar kind of development observable in Figure 2 is the development of manufacturing robots from early computation through CAM and scientific computing. Both figures show MRP as one ofthe first developments of early DP in the direction o manufacturing systems.

We believe that a more systematic identification of development paths can be developed with tbe help ofthe results ofthe quantitative analysis. No one single phase ofthe analysis provides all the necessary information, but all of them taken together can provide considerable insight.

(1) The multidimensional scalings suggest the chronological sequence of system development. This is not sufficient, however, because (a) the coefficients of stress indicate that the distributions of systems do contain some inconsistencies, which are reflected in the less than perfect correlations of Table 3, and (b) the MDS-derived chronological sequence indicates which systems may be antecedents of others, but does not determine this. In cases where the MDS-derived chronological sequence may appear inconsistent, we can turn to year of first mention in the literature (Table 3) for assistance.

(2) The distances between systems provide information on the similarity between systems. However, the taxonomical characters have been divided into attributes and functions and applied separately; this implies that the system definition vectors in terms of either type of character are incomplete and in fact there are differences in degree of similarity between some pairs of systems depending on whether the differences are measured by attributes or functions. Thus a complete analysis requires that both classifications be used.

(3) Finally, as stressed in the Methodological Note, it is also necessary in all such interpretations to apply commonsense knowledge ofthe historical process of IS devel opment in order to interpret the numbers.

Basically, we suggest that the evolutionary path to any type of system can be found by tracing backwards, following the shortest distances to relevant ancestors, until early computation; in this process, only those shortest distances are considered which conform to the chronological order determined by the multidimensional scaling and which do not violate common sense.

This is now demonstrated with respect to two examples, which were chosen because of the problems they demonstrate. The discussion refers to Figure 3 which contains partial graphical representations ofthe attribute and function vector dissimilarities of Tables 1 and 2; the analysis can be performed without the graph, but i highlights some relevant points.

GDSS are closer to CAD/CAM than anything else; this, however, is simply contrary to what we know about the development of GDSS and is also inconsistent with the function differences in Figure 3b. Thus we may conclude that GDSS are a development of DSS, with similar attributes to CAD/CAM but different functions. From DSS, the path leads back through MIS, and eariy DP to eariy computation."\* Thus, the forward path is early computation-early DP-MIS-DSS-GDSS.

The second example is CAD/CAM. The shortest path back from CAD/CAM in the attribute mapping is to CAD and in the function mapping to CAM. ## Discussion

To this point, the data clearly support the thesis that new types of systems are generally formed by adding features to existing systems and there are few quantum leaps, if any. Development occurs when a new feature is adopted, or an existing feature is adapted from one kind of system to another, resulting in a new type. A quantum leap would be reflected in a significant discrepancy between time of first mention in Table 3 and rank order in MDS by both attributes and functions. The inference would be that the system appeared chronologically earlier than could be predicted on the basis of its attributes and functions and that it appears late in the scaling because, following its appearance, features not originally included have been retrofitted as they became available. OIS/OA, mature DP, CAD, and expert systems exhibit considerable discrepancies, but only in one of the character types. If both types of characters were considered simultaneously as a combined set, the discrepancy would obviously be much smaller. The case which comes closest to a quantum leap is manufacturing robots, number 9 in order of mention, but 13 by rank order of attributes and 16 by functions. We have not defined this as a quantum leap as the discrepancy arises, in part, because the article on which the year of first mention was based predicted future developments rather than describing the then current state of the art.

Some interesting features emerge from a comparison of the two scalings. First, two fairly distinct paths of development can be identified. One is from early computing through scientific computing, CAD/CAM, and manufacturing robots; as it is eharac terized by increasing application of artificial intelligence techniques, we might call this the "applied artificial intelligence" path. The other is through early DP, MIS, mature DP, GDSS, EIS. and OIS/OA; we might call this the "human interface" path as it is characterized by increasingly diverse and natural user interfaces.

## Quo Vadis?^

The discussion to this point leads us to believe that one can use the identification of two distinct paths of development to anticipate the development of new types of information systems in the not too distant future. It seems that the development is toward two types of systems, each an extrapolation of one ofthe development paths; both rely heavily on artificial intelligence, but on different aspects of that discipline. Essentially, one type of system is destined to do much ofthe world's physical work, the other its information processing; we refer to the next step on the first, applied artificial intelligence path, as autonomous intelligent machines (AIM) and the second, human interface path, as the universal interface computer (UIC).

Autonomous intelligent machines are already being researched and reported in the literature; the special issue of Computer (June 1989) is a good example. Such machines derive their name from the intention that they be capable of locomotion necessary for the performance of many physical tasks. They must be endowed with sophisticated sensory capabilities—especially vision, sensitive manipulators, and the problem-solving intelligence necessary to free-moving entities. Although this description brings to mind the androids of science fiction and film, there seems to be no necessity to encumber such machines with human form, which may be functional for organic beings but can probably be improved for automata. Presumably such machines will come in many forms adapted to their various functions.

The second path of development is toward an advanced data processing, data base machine providing virtually all possible interfaces—the universal interface computer. The prime characteristic of such machines will be their ability to provide convenient communications with naive users by means of spoken and written natural language. Their function will be to put the world's information at the fingertips of any user in the most convenient way possible. This concept is very close to that of the Japanese Institute for New Generation Computer Technology which housed the Fifth Generation Computing Systems project. The goals of the project were: to produce machines easy enough to use. and intelligent and fast enough in their respons to come close to the kinds of transactions intelligent human beings are used to havin with each o/Zier (Feigenbaum and McCorduck 1983).

## Conclusions

Mayr (1982) posed three questions relevant to classification—the methodology of this study:

(1) How is classification best done? For the entities classified in this study, quantitative analysis seems to yield the most interesting results.

(2) What classification criteria should be used? We believe that the validity of attributes and functions as classifying criteria has been established in general. The complementary nature of these two sets of characters is emphasized by the medium correlation between rank orders of systems by attributes and functions in Table 3 compared to the high correlation of each with order of first mention. Each type of character clearly provides information not supplied by the other. Using both as a single set should improve the accuracy of the analysis.

(3) What is the ultimate purpose of such a classification? We have used the classification in an attempt to understand the diversity and the development of IS and to suggest the direction of future developments. Furthermore, we have indicated how the classification might be utilized to improve our understanding of the field and to rationalize IS research efforts.

The analysis in this paper demonstrates that information system types evolve by accretion of technologies. Thus, information systems of various types are essentially on a continuum of technology accretion. The point at which a set of technologies is considered distinct and requires a new name is somewhat arbitrary, and largely a matter of convenience. Thus, there are really two types of evolution involved in information systems—one, the evolution of technology and its use and the second, the evolution of our naming or labelling of particular combinations of technology. Perhaps the most important conclusion is that, too frequently, the labelling drives our perception ofthe field and its constituents rather than the content.

The idea that information systems advance by merging technologies, in itself, is not new. Thus, for example, Cash et al. {1983) write of "Merging the Islands of IS Technology" and provide numerous examples; their presentation, however, was intuitive and restricted, essentially, to business data processing activities. The contribution here is in basing the analysis on taxonomic principles and quantitative analysis, placing the phenomenon in the wider perspective of technological evolution in general, and in indicating the pervasiveness ofthe phenomenon throughout computerized information systems of all types. Consequently, all types of information systems may be seen as variants ofthe same underlying phenomenon rather than as a plethora of disconnected phenomena, as the labelling process might suggest.

Furthermore, the analysis and discussion point to a number of far-reaching conclusions concerning information systems in general, and what is recognized as the IS discipline in particular. First, the phenomenon of IS evolution and its mechanisms are common to information systems of all types. Consequently, it is possible to anticipate general trends. In particular, it is reasonable to assume that any new technology which becomes available will be absorbed into most extant types of systems.

Second, the information system field is fragmented in several directions. The major split is that between what we have denoted as the applied Al and human interface paths of evolution. Practitioners and academics involved in one of these two major processes are largely oblivious of the existence of the other. In fact, there are two distinct literatures with very little cross-referencing or cross-fertilization. Thus, on reading the IS, or human interface literature, one is hardly aware ofthe applied Al area, except for its one broad interface with expert systems.

Conversely, those involved in applied Al, outside expert systems, largely ignore the business-oriented information systems. As an example, an article on factory automation (Komoda et al. 1984) contains a figure with a box for a "Business Computer", but there is no reference to it in the paper itself. This is significant in light of the genesis of manufacturing systems as MRP, quite close to MIS; in the course of development, the links have been severed. (This comment is in no way intended to east aspersions on the paper quoted, which is very interesting in its own right.) Our conclusion here is that everyone involved would benefit from an awareness of this situation and from efforts to rectify it.

The applied Al path has had the advantage that those involved in it have held the specific goal of integrating technologies to achieve very advanced autonomous intelligent machines. The traditional IS field, on the other hand, has a distinct tendency to fragment whenever a new merger of technologies takes place, with new specializations, journals and conferences. While the technological issues cleariy require reclarification for each new type of system, this is hardly the case for the human interface issues. Nevertheless, one finds issues such as organizational integration or behavioral aspects which have been studied for earlier systems such as MIS, or DSS and are then restudied for each new type of system. An example is a new book on human issues in expert systems (Berry and Hart 1990). Given that expert systems evolved from eariier types of systems, one doubts that the human issues will be any different to, say, human issues in office automation. This doubt is strengthened by the fact that some ofthe special interest groups are starting to merge; in spite ofthe obvious common interests, papers in these fields all tend to relate to one particular kind of system. It is our conviction that the IS field would benefit greatly if its participants took a broader view of their terms of reference, with respect to both existing systems and future target systems. It is suggested that the universal interface machine is a desirable focus for such a view, one which was adopted by the Japanese in their Fifth Generation Computing Systems project.^ More specifically, research could profitably be directed to the development and delivery of those functions and attributes which are not yet readily available and on their integration into the IS development process.

An additional conclusion concerning IS research arises from the labelling problem discussed above and the consequent possibility of classifying IS into groups of technologically similar systems. There would be clear advantages to conducting research for such groups as a whole rather than for each type of system separately. The combination of DSS, GDSS and expert systems into a single conference as mentioned in the Introduction indicates an intuitive recognition of this fact by researchers in the field The systematic establishment or redefinition of conferences and journals for groups of system types would, we believe, yield considerable dividends in terms of reducing research redundancy and increasing the realm of applicability of research results.\*

## Methodological Note

The problem of choice of characters is a universal problem in all classification studies. According to Mayr (1942) "We understand by taxonomic character any attribute of an organism (or better, of any group of organisms) by which it may differ from other organisms." In biology, the choice of characters is admittedly arbitrary to some extent and becomes a pragmatic issue. ". . . not only is a complete species diagnosis impractical, it is also unnecessary, since even a small fraction ofthe morphological differences is sufficient, in most cases, to insure the correct diagnosis.. . . The most practical diagnostic characters are those that relate to some easily visible structure with low variability. This character may be, and very often is, of no particular importance to the species, but it serves as a marker to the taxonomist."

One source of arbitrariness in the present study is in the characters used in the literature to define systems—no one definition contains all characters and it is quite possible that all the definitions we reviewed omitted some characters. As Mayr indicates, this is of no great concern. A second possible source of arbitrariness would be if we injected ourselves into the choice of characters. In order to prevent this we used

the literature as a sole source and let the definitions we found dictate the characters used.

Three potential problems arise in the choice of characters (a) including irrelevant characters, (b) omitting important characters, and (c) redundancy or coUinearity of characters chosen. Following is our evaluation of these problems in our study and steps taken to mitigate their effects.

(a) In this context, by irrelevant characters we mean characters which do not meaningfully distinguish between systems; color of the computer or size of the computer room might be examples. As our analysis is based on dissimilarities between systems, the inclusion of irrelevant characters could distort the results of the analysis. Our defence against this problem was to rely exclusively on published definitions of systems which have presumably been reviewed for their relevance. As none of our panel of experts noted the inclusion of irrelevant characters, we assume that we have been reasonably successful in this regard. It should be noted, however, that some of our characters (e.g. hardware, software, and speech input) are irrelevant in the sense that their values are the same for all systems and they do not assist in distinguishing between types of systems; they were included because they appear in the hterature.

(b) The steps taken against omission of important characters are the same as in (a) above. As Mayr indicates, this problem is theoretically less serious as it is never possible to consider all characters, except in trivial cases. The problem thus reverts to choosing a sufficient number of characters to produce a meaningful classification. Diagnostic efficiency of the characters chosen and accuracy of classification may be improved in successive analyses as it becomes apparent which are the relevant characters. This is precisely what happened in biology from Linnaeus to the present day as some taxonomic characters were discarded and others added.

(c) There is indeed redundancy and coUinearity in the characters chosen. This, again, is well known to biologists; "It will usually be found on closer examination that good characters are correlated to a number of other characters" (Mayr 1942). For this reason multidimensional scaling (MDS) was adopted as the analytical technique because it does not assume lack of dependence among the variables. Unlike very rigorous methods such as multivariate regression which are invalid under coUinearity, MDS does not suffer from this restriction.

Following is a succinct description of the multidimensional scaling technique quoted from Wilkinson (1986):

"Multidimensional scaling is a powerful data reduction procedure which can be used on a direct similarity or dissimilarity matrix or one derived from rectangular data via (correlation). Although there are various algorithms to perform multidimensional scaling, all perform a similar function: to compute coordinates for a set of points in a space such that the distances between pairs of these points fit as closely as possible to measured dissimilarities between a corresponding set of objects. The family of procedures called principal components or factor analysis is related to multidimensional scaling in function, but multidimensional scaling differs from this family in important respects. Usually, but not necessarily, muhidimensional scaling can fit an appropriate model in fewer dimensions than can these other procedures. Furthermore, if it is implausible to assume a linear relationship between distances and dissimilarities, multidimensional scaling nevertheless provides a simple dimensional model."

The coefficient of stress computed by the procedure represents the degree to whic the algorithm was able to position data points in the space in a manner consistent with the positions of all other data points. The lower the coefficient, the greater the consistency. In general, a coefficient of stress of 0.15 or less is considered acceptable.

Given a multidimensional scaling of a data set, interpretation of the outcome consists of inferring meaning from the distribution of systems along the axes, or rotations of those axes. A " . . . gain in understanding may be achieved through the identification of (new) directions or axes through the spatial representation that can be given compelling substantive interpretations. Thus, we may note that, as we move farther and farther in some particular direction through the space, the points that we successively encounter correspond to objects that possess more and more of some particular, identifiable property. We can then conclude that this property . . . played an important role in whatever process gave rise to the data" (Shepard et al. 1972, p. 4). "With respect to the problem of interpretation, it is essential to understand that . . .the orientation ofthe configuration of points generated by the iterative process is often completely arbitrary with respect to the coordinate axes.. . . This means that the axes will generally be properly interpretahle, if at all, only after a suitable rotation" (Shepard et al. p. 10). In this study, the only rotation applied was to orient systems from left to right starting with early computation.
