# Interactivity and Communication Mode Choice in Ongoing Management Groups

Michael H. Zack

College of Busiņess Administration

Northeastern University

Boston, Massachusetts 02115

Management is communication intensive and, therefore, managers may derive benefits from computer-based alternatives to the traditional communication modes of face-to-face (FTF), telephone, and written memo. This research examined the use of electronic messaging (EM) by ongoing management groups performing a cooperative task. By means of an in-depth multimethod case study of the editorial group of two daily newspapers, it examined the fit between the interactivity of the chosen communication mode (FTF vs. EM) and the mode of discourse it was used for (alternation vs interaction/discussion). Two propositions were derived from this exploratory study. The first proposes that FTF, being highly interactive, is appropriate for building a shared interpretive context among group members, while CMC, being less interactive, is more appropriate for communicating within an established context. Groups exhibiting effective communication will use FTF primarily for interactive discourse and EM for discourse consisting primarily of alternating adjacency pairs. The second proposes that to the extent that the appropriate communication modes are chosen, communication will be more effective.

## 1. Introduction

Maaem is communica io intesie, and manaers establish and maintain Lcomplex communication networks for performing their work (Barnard 1938, Kotter 1982, McKenney et al. 1992, Mintzberg 1973). While managers appear to prefer face-to-face (FTF) communication (Luthan and Larsen 1986, Mintzberg 1973, Kurke and Aldrich 1983, McCaskey 1982), computer-mediated communication technology (CMC) also may play a useful role in this context. The use of electronic mail, computer conferencing, shared databases, and other computer-mediated forms of communication is proliferating in today's business organizations. Research examining computer-mediated communication modes (cf., reviews by Culnan and Markus 1987, Kerr and Hiltz 1982, Rice and Bair 1984, Steinfield 1986, Williams 1977) suggests that CMC can increase the range, capacity, and speed of managerial communication. Whether or not CMC will improve managerial or organizational performance, however, may depend on the particular circumstances under which these electronic media are used (Daft and Lengel 1986).

A growing body of research has focused on examining differences between computer-mediated and nonmediated communication channels. The literature on group decision support and computer-supported cooperative work (cf., Dennis et al. 1988, Galegher and Kraut 1990, George et al. 1990, Jessup et al. 1990, Kraemer and King 1988, Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1989, Seigel et al. 1986, Smith and Vanecek 1990, Walther and Burgoon 1992) has reported much experimental research on media differences in collaborative decision making tasks. Little formal empirical work, however. has been done in this area (Dennis et al. 1988, Nunamaker et al. 1989). The research has typically used artificial groups—groups with no shared history, context, or knowledge about each other—performing artificial tasks, and has not allowed for free choice of medium, thus it may have limited generalizability to the field, where the technology ultimately is used (Culnan and Markus 1987), and especially to ongoing groups of managers having free choice of various communication modes. Only by systematically observing the antecedents and consequences of managers unrestricted choices of communication mode can we begin to understand the pragmatic differences in these modes of communication.

A second important line of research has focused explicitly on media richness, suggesting that the richness of the medium should match the equivocality or richness of the message being sent (Daft and Lengel 1986, Daft and Weick 1984, Daft and Wiginton 1979, Trevino et al. 1987). This line of research has examined, via survey, the media choices of samples of individual managers in large organizations (Daft, Lengel and Trevino 1987. Daft and Macintosh 1981, Russ et al. 1990, Trevino et al. 1990). The media richness research has suffered from inconsistent findings (see Fulk and Boyd 1991, Schmitz and Fulk 1991, Walther 1992, Walther and Burgoon 1992), and this may be because the survey approach has tended to decontextualize the research and distance the researcher from the phenomenon (Walther and Burgoon 1992). Steinfield's (1986) review of the CMC literature concluded with a call for more field-based CMC research, and Schegloff (1987, p. 222) proposed that

"Alny discipline that takes the understanding of human interaction as its goal must be answerable to such microanalysis as seems to offer a rigorous account of the details of social interaction in its own terms. [Emphasis in original.]

Goodman et al. (1987) similarly suggested that only by performing in-depth fieldbased micro-studies of work groups could we understand the causality—the hows and whys—of the phenomena under consideration. This was echoed by Hackman (1990a), who stated that developing useful and practical normative models of group behavior required in-depth field work to identify those factors which were easily observed, had a relatively large effect on outcomes, were salient to group members and were controllable by the group.

Only a small amount of CMC research has observed ongoing work groups in natural settings where history, routine, norms, social relationships, and deeply shared interpretive and behavioral context may play a large role in determining choice of communication mode and procedure. Interpersonal communication is inherently a social act, and attempts to understand this phenomenon using social theories of communication (Fulk et al. 1987) should benefit from studying complete, intact social groups to better understand the social influences on communication behaviors.

Exploratory research on the role of CMC in management groups in natural settings (McKenney 1986; McKenney et al. 1989; McKenney et al. 1992; Reder and Schwab 1989, 1990; Trevino et al. 1987) suggests that shared cognitive and social context may be the most important factors influencing communication behavior and media choice in work groups' and that field-based micro-study is the appropriate method for studying them. For example, McKenney et al. (1992) observed that “lean" EM was used effectively to exchange rich messages by employing an elaborate metaphorical code which emerged within the group over time and formed part of each member's shared interpretive context. Zack (1991) found that a group's shared communication routines, norms, and procedural expectations may be as important as richness in choosing an appropriate medium.

The research being reported here examined the use of electronic messaging (EM) vs. traditional modes of communication (i.e., FTF, telephone, and written memo) in ongoing management groups performing a cooperative task. Ongoing means that the groups had an established culture and set of routines, and held an expectation of continuing to work together for the foreseeable future. By means of an in-depth multi-method field study, the research explored when and why these managers selected and used a particular communication mode, and how their choices related to communication effectiveness.

The research studied the managing editorial group of two daily newspapers, one a medium sized evening newspaper (to be referred to as The Evening News) and the other a large morning paper (to be referred to as The Morning Times.)² Figure 1 presents descriptive data comparing the sites. Each group comprised the senior and middle-level newsroom managers responsible for producing the “hard" news section of the newspaper. The Evening News's editorial group had 14 members, and The Morning Time's 18.

At both sites, stories were written, transmitted, edited, and the process managed using EM³ together with FTF. Each site had been using its current editorial EM system for about seven years. Editors occasionally employed shouting across the newsroom as an additional mode of communication. While managers had both written memo and telephone readily available, memos played little or no observed role in the day-to-day production of either paper and were reserved solely for infrequent, formal administrative communication among the top executives. Telephone accounted for less than 3% of the observed communication events at The Evening News and 3.7% at The Morning Times, primarily for communicating to others outside the newsroom and where no alternative existed (e.g., to talk to a reporter at home or on location). Thus the study focused on EM vs. FTF. Both groups conducted daily scheduled meetings to coordinate efforts and to negotiate newspaper content and story placement, and communicated on an ad hoc basis throughout the publishing cycle using both EM and FTF. Both organizations were considered by the industry to be innovative and to produce a high quality newspaper.⁴

## 2. Background

## 2.1 Media Richness

Managers typically have several communication modes⁵ to choose from. Choosing a mode with the appropriate information processing characteristics relates positively to managerial effectiveness (Daft et al. 1987; Russ et al. 1990). The work of Daft and colleagues (Daft and Lengel 1984, 1986; Daft, Lengel and Trevino 1987; Daft and Macintosh 1981; Daft and Weick 1984; Daft and Wiginton 1979) suggests that communication about ambiguous or equivocal events, often arising when shared interpretive context among communicators is lacking (Gerrig 1986, Trevino et al. 1987), requires rich media. Daft et al. defined rich media as those providing (1) multiple channels, (2) the capacity to transmit high-variety (or “natural") languages, (3) message personalization, and (4) capacity for immediate feedback. Rich channels have high levels of each. FTF would be very rich, while EM would be less so.

Richness has also been related to social presence (Short et al. 1976). Social presence represents the degree to which the mode of communication allows participants to experience each other as being psychologically close or present (Fulk and Boyd 1991). Each of the interactors is salient to the other, as is their interpersonal relationship. Social presence is conveyed by the use of multiple, nonverbal communication channels and cues and continuous feedback (Rogers 1986). Communication modes high in social presence enable the communication of status or other social cues, while those with low social presence filter out those cues (Sproull and Kiesler 1986). Again, FTF offers the greatest degree of social presence, while EM, although not completely devoid of the ability to carry socio-emotional content (Rice and Love 1987, Walther and Burgoon 1992), offers less.

Richness theory implies that greater media awareness should lead to more appropriate media choices and thus more effective performance. However, little research has attempted to link the appropriateness of media choices to measures of effectiveness, focusing instead on media selection and use (Fulk and Boyd 1991). Steinfield's (1986) review of the CMC literature describes studies of media choice and of EM impacts, but none linking the two. Three articles reporting a positive link between appropriate choices and performance effectiveness (Daft et al. 1987, Lengel and Daft 1988, Russ et al. 1990) are all based on the same data, and Russ et al. (1990) advised the reader to interpret the results with caution because of the small sample size. Their use of internal corporate performance ratings as a measure of effectiveness may be too indirect to be meaningful. Rice et al. (1989) criticized the measure as unoperationalized and too organization-specific and narrow to measure task performance effectively. They expanded the construct to measure increases in communication flow and in the rate, quality and quantity of work, which taken together they labeled “augmentation" effects. They found that greater media awareness did relate to greater self-reported EM use, but they did not directly relate media awareness to measures of work or communication effectiveness.

Rather than assuming a relationship between communication and performance, O'Reilly and Roberts (1977) explicitly tested this hypothesis and provided evidence linking communication effectiveness to perceived performance effectiveness for task groups. Hackman (1990b) similarly found that groups which communicate more effectively about their work tend to perform better. Given the group focus of the research reported here, focusing on measures of group communication effectiveness seemed to provide a more useful approach to linking media choice to outcome effectiveness.

Media richness theory has been criticized for being overly rationalistic (Fulk et al. 1987, Fulk and Boyd i991) and has shown weak or mixed results in linking the richness of the chosen medium to the ambiguity or equivocality of the task or communication episode (Fulk and Boyd 1991, Schmitz and Fulk 1991, Walther 1992). Fulk et al. (1987) proposed extending the theory and its rational expectations concerning media choice by linking it to more socially-oriented theories such as social information processing (Salancik and Pfeffer 1978) and symbolic interactionism (Stryker and Statham 1985). Regardless of the particular social theory employed,6 however, research suggests that media richness should be linked to some social theory of communication (Schmitz and Fulk 1991, Walther and Burgoon 1992).

The richness construct is highly similar to that of interaction discussed in the sociology literature (e.g., Chapple and Coon 1942, Goffman 1967, Homans 1950, Levinson 1983, Newcomb 1953, Parsons 1937, Sacks et al. 1974). This literature has addressed the issues of human interaction in a systematic manner for over fifty years and provides a solid foundation for grounding the richness construct.

## 2.2 Interaction and Interactivity

[T]his gamelike back-and-forth process might better be called interplay than dialogue. And with that, the dance in talk might finally be available to us. (Goffman 1981, p. 73)

The noted sociologist Erving Goffman placed human interaction at the forefront of communication research (e.g., Goffman 1967). Goffman viewed interaction as an arrangement by which individuals come together and sustain matters having a rati fied, joint, current and running claim upon attention which lodges them together in “some sort of intersubjective, mental world." (Goffman 1981, p. 70). Building on this perspective, Rogers (1986) developed an interactive model of the communication process, which he defined as one in which

participants create and share information with one another in order to reach a mutual understanding. Such information sharing over time leads the individuals to converge or diverge from each other in their mutual understanding of a certain topic. (Rogers 1986, p. 199)

Roger's (1986) model, and the field of communication pragmatics in general (Brown and Yule 1983, Gerrig 1986, Levinson 1983), places shared interpretive context in a primary role. Effective communication requires participants to share enough background knowledge or interpretive context to render their messages mutually meaningful. Roger's perspective was echoed by Tannen (1989) who described conversational interaction as an iterative process leading to jointly produced meaning-in-interaction.

[C]onversation is not a matter of two (or more) people alternately taking the role of speaker and listener, but rather that both speaking and listening include elements and traces of the other. Listening, in this view, is an active not a passive enterprise, requiring interpretation comparable to that required in speaking, and speaking entails simultaneously projecting the act of listening. (Tannen 1989, p. 12)

Schegloff (1992) similarly viewed conversation as providing for displays of mutual understanding, which he considered one basis for the cultivation and grounding of intersubjectivity.

Rogers (1986) expanded the model to include modes of communication other than FTF. While all two-way communication can be considered interaction, Rogers suggested that some media are more interactive than others. Rogers described communication technologies along a continuum of interactivity (see also Rafaeli, 1988), which he defined as the degree to which the technology could support or enable interaction that resembles human conversation. For example, TV and other mass broadcast media support little or no interactivity, while electronic mail and teleconferencing support a much higher degree of interactivity. FTF represents the most interactive mode of communication. Schegloff(1987) proposed a related continuum of interactivity based on the flexibility of the turn-taking system. He pointed out that

. . . [I]n ordinary conversation determination of both who shall speak next and when that one should speak (i.e., when current turn should end) is accomplished in a local, turn-byturn manner and not by some predetermined pattern. . . Many ceremonies, rituals, and formal debates on the other hand, may fully specify the order and length of all turns, being thereby at the opposite end of the 'local allocation' versus preallocation' spectrum. (Schegloff 1987, p. 222)

Goffman (1967) also believed that interactivity may lie on a continuum and that interaction applied to immediate and mediated encounters, however mediated interaction was likely to be more attenuated.

The interaction literature identifies several key characteristics of interactive communication which might be useful for differentiating communication modes.

The Simultaneous and Continuous Exchange of Information. Interactive communication allows for simultaneous exchange (Schegloff 1987) and continuous feedback (Goffman 1967). This may be accomplished by two parties talking (and listening) at the same time or by use of so-called back-channel (nonverbal, paralinguistic) cues. Backchannel remarks are used to display active and continued listenership and to signal comprehension, reception, and cursory agreement or disagreement (Goldberg 1990). The significance of continuous, simultaneous feedback is that these cues are given while speakers are speaking so that they can judge whether they are succeeding or failing to get their message across and adjust accordingly as they speak (Goffman 1981, Schegloff 1992).

The Use of Multiple, Non-verbal Cues. The essence of interactivity for Goffman was the ostensibly minor yet highly meaningful cues—the “glances, gestures, positionings" (Goffman 1967), “tone of voice, manner of uptake, restarts, and the variously positioned pauses" (Goffman 1981)—that are conveyed only during faceto-face encounters (Goffman 1967).

During direct personal contacts. . . unique informative conditions prevail and the significance of face becomes especially clear. The human tendency to use signs and symbols means that evidence of social worth and of mutual evaluations will be conveyed by very minor things, and these things will be witnessed, as will the fact that they have been witnessed. An unguarded glace, a momentary change in tone of voice, an ecological position taken or not taken, can drench a talk with judgmental significance. . ."(Goffman 1967, p. 33)

The Potentially Spontaneous, Unpredictable, and Emergent Progression of Remarks. Interaction implies joint, spontaneous involvement (Goffman 1967). The path of conversation is indeterminent and “progressively realizable through the further course of the conversation" (Garfinkle 1964). Even where there is a very high probability of predicting the nature of the forthcoming response, participants must still wait to be sure (Goffman 1981). The present significance of what one person says often is clarified by waiting to hear what the other says at a later time (Garfinkle 1964).

The Ability to Interrupt or Preempt. Interruption is one means for giving simultaneous or immediate feedback using, for example, asides, parenthetical remarks, or well-placed quips (Goffman 1981). It is a way to initiate minor side conversations to repair or explain something before continuing with the main conversation (Goldberg 1990). The capability to interrupt or preempt other participants is of particular interest to this study as FTF and EM differ significantly on this dimension. Highly interactive exchange allows the communication process to be interrupted by the participants at a small grain of interaction. Interacting FTF speakers can interrupt each other at any point in their exchange. They can see how others are responding to their messages before they are even finished, and alter them in midstream to elicit a different response (Goffman 1981, Schegloff 1987). Skillful interruption can utilize nonverbal back-channels, enabling listeners to speak without formally being given their turn (Goffman 1981). Issues requiring immediate attention or demanding immediate juxtaposition to be meaningful and coherent need to be said as soon as possible after the point at which they become relevant. To wait one's turn may be too late to achieve the precise placement of comments required to guarantee their “sequential implicativeness"(Goldberg 1990). EM, in contrast, limits interruption or response to the grain of an entire message. While some forms of CMC (although not those used at the research sites reported on here) do support interruption, its nature is unique in a FTF context.

Although the literature on interruption views the phenomenon primarily from a power perspective, Goldberg (1990) found that interruption may also be triggered by the interrupter's enthusiastic interest and involvement in the discourse. These socalled rapport-based interruptions may occur for many other reasons, including expressing empathy, affection, solidarity, interest, or concern, and conveying one's rapport, cooperation, or camaraderie with the interrupted speaker (Goldberg 1990). Rapport-based interruptions are viewed as acts of collaboration. They aid in establishing mutual orientation by providing immediate feedback, additional information, and elaboration on the topic or theme, thereby allowing speaker and listener to co-develop common topics while displaying their joint enthusiasm for, involvement with, or understanding of each other and the issues or goals of the interaction (Goldberg 1990). The ability to interrupt is thus linked to the ability to create the level of involvement and mutual understanding described by Rogers' (1986) model.

Mutuality. Interactive communication enables messages to be mutually constructed and coordinated (Goldberg 1990; Schegloff 1987, 1992). For example, one speaker may help the other find the right word or fill in the sentence (Goffman 1981). Additionally, the entire course of interaction is mutually determined, and the outcome is mutual understanding or intersubjectivity (Rogers 1986, Schegloff 1987).

Patterns of Turn-taking, and the Use of Adjacency Pairs. Speakers typically take turns in making remarks, and managing turns is an important part of conversation (Sacks et al. 1974). Interactivity implies that turn-taking is flexible, rather than structured and deterministic (Brown and Yule 1983, Levinson 1983.) Well-structured turns (i.e., one person speaks, then the other, then the one, then the other, etc.) might be better described as reaction (Rafaeli 1988) or alternation (Schegloff 1987) rather than as interaction.

Communication can similarly be characterized by the use of adjacency pairs (Goffman 1981; Levinson 1983; Schegloff 1987, 1992.) The concept of adjacency pairs is analytical formalism that describes those exchanges which occur as ordered sequences of two utterances that are adjacent, produced by different speakers, and classified so that a particular first part requires a particular second part. For example, an offer is usually followed by an acceptance or rejection; a question requires an answer (or other “legitimate" response.) Adjacency pairs can be likened to scripts (Gioia and Poole 1984), and provide shared “prepackaged meaning"(Goffman 1981) so that the mutual knowledge required for interpreting an exchange does not have to be contained within the messages themselves.

Interactive conversation often deviates from strict adjacency in terms of the order or sequencing of remarks and the predictability of response (Brown and Yule 1983 Goffman 1981), and the basic adjacency pair organization is often difficult to determine (Brown and Yule 1983). According to Goffman (1981), adjacency pairs

## . . . are found in the artful dialogue of the theater and in novels. . . Ordinary talk ordinarily has less ping-pong. (Goffman 1981, p. 35)

With interactive conversation, remarks typically are made out of sequence or embedded within other sequences. Additionally, it may be quite difficult to predict which of a range of responses will be forthcoming (Levinson 1983). For example, a question may elicit a challenge to the basic premise of the question. The increased structural complexity of communication patterns caused by divergent responses such as this requires the ability to repair, that is, to respond in a mutual, instantaneous and flexible manner to steer the conversation back to convergence (Levinson 1983). Repair is a major resource in maintaining and restoring intersubjectivity or mutual understanding (Schegloff 1987). FTF provides mechanisms to transcend turn-taking and adjacency-pair expectations, and to repair interaction as needed.

Goffman (1981) explicitly listed the requirements for a system that would support highly interactive exchange, and which summarize much of the discussion above. His requirements are: two-way communication; the capability for back-channel feedback to inform on message reception while it is occurring; contact signals as a means to indicate seeking a channel connection, ratifying that the channel is open, and closing the channel; mechanisms to signal turns at talk; preemption/interruption signals for inducing a rerun, holding off channel requests, or interrupting a talker in progress; framing capabilities providing special cues sent simultaneously with the message to alter or temper the meaning of what is being said, and signals to verify the results; norms of proper conversation; and constraints on nonparticipants (e.g., to prevent eavesdropping, noise, blocking view, etc.)

## 2.3. FTF vs. EM

FTF interaction, then, provides a standard by which interactivity and richness' can be measured. FTF is highly interruptible at a small grain. It provides mutual, continuous, and instantaneous feedback about the interaction and utilizes multiple communication channels simultaneously, providing the ability to respond to unanticipated or unpredictable remarks, engage in repair, and resolve divergence. These characteristics are vital to dynamic and adaptive evolution of discourse, required to generate new concepts and meanings, enact interpretations, reach consensus, give explanation, and resolve conflict. Schegloff (1987) proposed that all other turn-taking systems are variants or modifications of FTF conversation which he defines as the "primordial organization of talk-in-interaction"(Schegloff 1987, p. 222). Any variation in the setting or context which affects the turn-taking system would in turn affect the organization of repair and ultimately the process for “defending intersubjectivity" (Schegloff 1992).

EM provides a clear contrast to FTF. EM is not interruptible.8 Rather it supports alternating turns at the grain of an entire message. In contrast to FTF, EM tends to enforce strict turn-taking. Lacking the capability for interrupting at a fine grain, communicating simultaneously, employing multiple paralinguistic cues, or engaging in instantaneous feedback, it may be inappropriate in circumstances where high-level repair might be required to build mutual understanding. EM may be more appropriate for strict adjacency pairs where the remarks are predictable in sequence and content, are to a high degree already mutually understandable and meaningful because of that structure (Goffman 1981), and adhere to a low number of discretely alternating exchanges.

A distinction can be made between context-building and context-using communication (McKenney et al. 1992, Stryker and Statham 1985, Trevino et al. 1987). When the communication process diverges from mutual understanding, shared context may have to be created—a special form of repair. FTF, because of its interactivity, would be expected to support complex, iterative exchanges appropriate for context-building and sharing (Goffman 1981, Goldberg 1990, Rogers 1986, Schegloff 1987, Tannen 1989, Trevino et al. 1987). EM, in contrast, is a text-based, alternating communication mode which might be most appropriate for simple exchanges within a strongly shared and developed interpretive context previously set by culture, habit, social structure, or prior FTF conversation (Brown and Yule 1983, Schegloff 1987 Trevino et al. 1987).

## 3. Research Framework

This discussion suggests that the extent of shared interpretive context determines an appropriate form of discourse (interaction or alternation) and thus an appropriate communication mode (FTF or EM). Specifically, a highly shared interpretive context should enable the efficient and effective use of EM, a lean or low-interactivity communication mode, while low levels of shared context should require FTF, a rich and highly interactive communication mode capable of building and sharing interpretive context. While groups might attempt to employ EM to build interpretive context, or FTF to discuss a well-known, mutually understood issue or to support a highly-focused exchange of unequivocal facts, effective groups should exhibit the appropriate fit (or match) of chosen communication mode to required form of discourse (Figure 2). The notion of fit used here is similar to that of Gestalt proposed by Venkatramin (1988), whereby congruence among a set of variables is observed for organizations exhibiting a particular level of performance. Fit between discourse form and communication mode was expected to influence communication effectiveness, with effective groups exhibiting the communication patterns illustrated in Figure 2.

## 4. Research Method and Design

As an exploratory study, the objective was to amass data addressing the research themes, leading to the formulation of research propositions for future study. Realworld context was essential to the research, thus a field study was required. Goodman et al. (1987) suggested that useful research on organizational effectiveness required in-depth “micro-studies." This notion, together with the exploratory nature of the research, suggested a case-study approach. That is, the primary unit of analysis was the site itself, and data (both quantitative and qualitative) were collected and analyzed on a site-by-site basis, rather than pooled. Data were collected at each site from several sources and by several methods including interviews, observation, questionnaire, and electronic mail capture, and analyzed to identify convergent findings about that site.

The particular case design used was the multiple site replication with embedded units (Yin 1984). Rogers (1986) proposed the information exchange relationship (or interaction event (Fulk and Boyd 1991, Goffman 1967, Sigman 1987)), rather than the individual. as the appropriate unit of analysis for CMC research. The interaction event was used as an embedded unit of analysis within the case site.

At The Evening News, 18 interviews of 45 to 90 minutes each were conducted with most of the management group and several nongroup managers. The publishing process was observed (usually shadowing a central actor) for 30 hours, distributed throughout the 24 hour publishing cycle.9 Four news meetings were attended. All electronic mail messages sent during each observation were obtained directly from the editorial computer system. After conducting all interviews and observations, a questionnaire was created, tested, and administered ¹0; and completed questionnaires were received from the entire core group of 14 editors.

At The Morning Times, 19 interviews were conducted. All phases of the publishing process were observed during a total of 110 hours of observation. Again, particular editors were shadowed during most observations. Twelve news meetings were attended. Electronic messages were obtained directly from participants at the end of each observation period. The questionnaire, modified slightly to reflect the particular process and jargon at this site, was administered to the 18 managers. Fourteen questionnaires (78%) were returned. Newsroom blueprints and editing system user manuals were reviewed.

## 5. Data Analysis

## 5.1. Shared Context and Communication Mode

Observation and interview data suggested that the greater the shared interpretive context among communicators, the less ambiguous the exchange and the less interactive a channel required to support it. FTF conversations provided the rich, interactive channels used to enact a consensus about newsworthiness, importance, angle, etc. for upcoming stories. This enactment continued within the daily news meeting. As the cycle progressed and editors began to talk about and read particular stories, shared context grew. As the process moved from editing to layout, a greater volume of terse, simple messages were sent via EM, reflecting the large amount of tacitly shared background knowledge and implied meaning built earlier in the cycle.

The editors tended to match the interactivity of their communication mode to the extent to which they shared interpretive context. They generally used FTF for building shared context and EM when communicating within a shared context. Reporters and editors typically used FTF to negotiate and establish a shared interpretive context about stories to be written, followed by EM for simple logistics and status-oriented exchange of facts. One editor from The Morning Times described an example of his communication with copy editors:

In general, I talk face-to-face early and send messages late. For example, I keep the slots appraised about what [stories] will be moving to the A section so they can balance that with their own work. This is usually done early on using lots of face-to-face followed later on by [electronic] messaging for updates, rewrites, timing, length, and so forth.

An editor from The Evening News provided another example of matching communication mode to extent of shared context;

If you are dealing with the regular person on the desk [i.e., the person who usually edits his section], you can send an electronic message describing each page: a list of the slugs, whether or not the page will use a town logo, which story goes on top. Otherwise, communicating this to the copy desk will be mostly face-to-face.

Figures 3a and b illustrate an example from each paper where context was built early in the exchange via FTF, while subsequent communication within that context occurred over electronic mail. When clarification was needed, the mode switched back to FTF.

EM was also used effectively to communicate within the context of other electronic text. Editors often appended terse messages to prior electronic messages. For example, the copy desk might electronically send to an editor a story excerpt with “Is this ok?" appended. Notes and questions embedded within a story could be terse, as the story text itself provided the context for the message, usually sufficient for sending simple electronic messages, many requiring no response.

Several editors stated that electronic messages were rarely ambiguous, indicating that the group tended to share an adequate interpretive context for communicating electronically. Observed communication, regardless of mode, generally was unambiguous and convergent, and editors generally chose a communication mode appropriate to the extent of shared context.

Where an appropriate mode was not used, editors usually recovered quickly. For example, I observed one editor returning several electronic messages with the appended note: "What does this mean?", leading to FTF conversations to clarify the messages. As a Morning Times editor explained,

The slots are good enough editors to know that if I didn't say enough in my [electronic message], they should come and seek me out, and they will.

The editors chose an appropriate channel most of the time. Most of the editors indicated they used FTF whenever they anticipated requiring more than a simple one-way message or a message-response pair; for example, if they expected many comments or questions. Simple messages tended to be factual or those for which there was a rich, shared context, and those could be sent electronically; for example, questions about story length and status. Where more interactivity was required, FTF would be used; for example, the news editor negotiating the length or placement of a story with the copy desk. When many back-and-forth messages were required, the exchange would be slower with EM than FTF, and convergence might not occur. Or if it did occur, it might take an exceedingly large number of messages. With FTF, the parties would quickly converge to a consensus on meaning, decision, or action.

Occasionally, editors started an electronic exchange and terminated it when the iterations grew beyond a small number and it became obvious that they would reach closure only via FTF.

Occasionally you get volleys of [electronic] messages, but you can always cut it off and speak face-to-face. Or if it gets too confusing, pick up the phone. [Night News Editor, The Morning Times]

Editors continued with EM exchanges as long as they felt they were making adequate progress towards closure.

Senior editors also used FTF to obtain a diversity of opinion, for example, to discuss the angle of a story or the planned content of the paper. The inherent interactivity of FTF tended to encourage divergence, exchange of opinion, and discussion, avoiding premature closure. EM, on the other hand, would usually preclude discussion. The business editor at The Evening News provided an example:

FTF gets reporters thinking about their own questions about the story. I could [electronically] send them a list of my own four questions and they could answer them, but this might not address their questions. And if we could get together to discuss the questions, we might find that two of my four were resolved and that two of the reporter's questions were more important. And we may come up with two more during our discussion. Then we could all decide how to answer those questions and plan our attack.

The editor-in-chief at The Evening News chose a communication mode in a similar manner. When he wanted information to be taken as a directive with no discussion anticipated or requested, he would use EM. For example, he often sent electronic messages to the news editor directing him to change page one. However, when the editor-in-chief wanted discussion, reaction, or opinion, he would either use FTF, or follow his electronic message with a visit to the newsroom floor for a FTF conversation. The national editor at The Morning Times, while training a new night editor, routinely communicated the daily story plan to the new editor via EM until the national editor was comfortable enough with the new editor to value his input. The national editor then began to meet daily to discuss the story plan face-to-face with the night editor during their shift overlap. Several editors stated that they often sent potentially contentious directives or requests via EM to limit the amount of discussion or negative reaction from the recipient, especially on deadline.

Shouting was a unique form of“face-to-face" interaction and in some ways resembled electronic messaging. Editors often shouted to one another when within earshot. It was appropriate for quick, simple, one-way exchanges because it was too intrusive, public, and susceptible to noise and interference to use for longer back-and-forth exchanges. However, it provided the benefits of a broadcast medium by keeping all editors in earshot simultaneously informed and co-oriented. Shouting often occurred between editors and the copy desk, but almost never between editors and reporters. The former often communicated about simple, factual logistics issues (e.g., shouting a story length), while communication between reporter and editor usually involved a lot of interactive discussion (e.g., negotiating a story angle).

Questionnaire responses from both sites indicated strong agreement (and with almost complete consensus) that FTF should be used when lots of back and forth exchange was required. However, the editors at The Evening News were neutral to somewhat in disagreement that EM should be used only when communicating about well-defined issues requiring simple responses. Thus FTF was clearly the preferred choice for interactivity at The Evening News, however, this did not preclude the use of EM for more than simple exchanges (although only one occurrence of this was observed). This is probably best explained by the long-term stability of the group's members, their roles, and the group's environment, leading to a highly shared interpretive context among the group, and enabling EM to be used for more complex exchanges when FTF was not an option (e.g., for cross-shift communication). McKenney et al. (1992) discovered a similar finding where members of a programming team developed an elaborate code over time to describe the status of the program being tested. The code was employed to send “rich" messages using EM. Responses from The Morning Times editors were even more in line with expectations The Morning Times represented a more complex and dynamic organization and environment. The editors at The Morning Times worked hard every day explicitly to build and renew their shared context. Therefore the distinction between communication modes was more salient and important to that group.

A structured analysis of observed communication events strongly supported these findings. At The Evening News, 404 separate communication events were recorded during 12 hours of observation." At The Morning Times, 1,193 events were recorded during 60 hours of observation.12 A communication event was considered to be any number of message exchanges about a topic and which had an observable beginning and ending (i.e., reached closure). An event might range from a simple, unidirectional announcement of a fact, to a long discussion with many back-and-forth exchanges and fine-grained interruptions. Following Brown and Yule (1983, p. 230), the number of exchanges for each communication event was recorded when discrete and alternating. Where the exchange did not follow a pattern of alternation, the event was considered to be an interactive "discussion". Electronic mail messages were captured directly, allowing those exchanges to be cross-referenced and linked to communication events. For FTF exchanges, where alternating remarks were made, they were recorded directly. Where it became impossible to record the particular sequence, exact content, or speaker of each remark (i.e., during interactive discussion), the gist of the exchange was noted.

The most frequently observed combinations, as expected, were EM for one-way or simple, alternating exchanges and FTF for interactive discussion. Shouting and FTF often were used for simple exchanges, primarily because of the close proximity of the editors. the relative inconvenience of having to interrupt their work to access the electronic messaging screen, and the routine nature of the communication. The combinations not expected to occur frequently (using EM or shouting to support interactive discussion) represented a negligible portion of the observed communication events. When EM or shouting were used, it was almost always for a simple exchange. When FTF was used, it was usually for interactive discussion, and almost all discussion occurred via FTF.

These results suggested a strong observed relationship between communication mode and interactivity for effective communication, as expected, leading to the following two-part proposition:

P1a. Where extent of shared interactive context is low, interactive discourse is needed, and FTF is the appropriate communication mode.

P1b. Where interpretive context is highly developed and shared, discourse can take the form of adjacency pairs and strict turn-taking (i.e., alternation); therefore EM is an appropriate communication mode

## 5.2. Communication Effectiveness

Given that editors usually chose communication modes appropriate to the extent of shared interpretive context, observed communication should have been effective. Although miscommunication occasionally occurred, the editors believed that communication was generally effective. Communication breakdowns occurred several times per week. but this was not considered excessive. Questionnaire responses supported these findings as well as indicating that most miscommunication occurred when communicating across shifts and departments, while the least amount of miscommunication occurred when communicating within the same shift and department (Figure 4).

This pattern provided additional evidence to support the proposed relationship between shared context, choice of communication mode, and communication effectiveness. Cross-shift communication relied on EM to a great extent because of temporal separation, therefore the flexibility to match the interactivity of the exchange to the communication mode was limited, resulting in less effective communication over all. According to the editors. the highest frequency of miscommunication occurred when communicating with another department on another shift—a situation usually requiring building and sharing context, but one where FTF was not convenient.

Context was shared to a greater extent with people in one's own department than with those in other news departments, and the incidence of miscommunication varied accordingly.

Overall. the editorial group engaged in an effective communication process, and most editors believed that their communication led to a high-quality paper—or at least did not detract from that quality. Communication was more effective when FTF was used to build or share context where deficient, and when EM was used to communicate within an existing shared context. ## 6. Conclusion

This exploratory study attempted to identify factors which were important, salient, observable, controllable, and operating within a real-world context (Hackman 1990a), so as to provide useful information to managers for improving their performance through the use of EM. In addition to accomplishing that goal, it clearly demonstrated that fruitful research on CMC technology (and related technologies such as groupware and GDSSs) require a detailed understanding of how people communicate and interact on an ongoing basis and in a real-world context.

The findings of this study supported the notion that matching the richness of the medium to the ambiguity or equivocality of the message will result in more effective communication. In doing so, it addressed many of the shortcomings of prior research pointed out by Fulk and Boyd (1991) and others. The research was performed within the real world context of multiple, intact, ongoing groups (Culnan and Markus 1987, Fulk et al. 1987, Steinfield 1986, Walther and Burgoon 1992), it focused on outcomes as well as choices (Fulk and Boyd 1991), was grounded in social communication theory (Fulk and Boyd 1991), and used the communication event as the unit of analysis (Fulk and Boyd 1991).

EM within the ongoing managing editorial groups studied provided a means for efficiently and directly exchanging information with others, where the pattern of exchange required no more than a one-way message or a simple adjacency pair. These messages were communicated within a previously established and shared interpretive context and might range from simple facts to moderately rich messages depending on the extent and richness of that shared context. Many of the electronic messages were routine exchanges, and therefore took place within a rich, shared, and institutionalized context. Typically, anything requiring more than a simple exchange within a shared context would be FTF.

The editorial group tended to be aware of the different roles of EM and FTF and to choose a communication mode appropriate to their need for interactivity or alternation. The communication process, supported by the appropriate choice of communication mode and sufficiently shared interpretive context, was effective, enabling the group to produce a high quality newspaper.

These findings show how EM complements, rather than substitutes, for FTF interaction. They suggest that the interactivity of the communication mode should be of central concern to managers. They should be aware of channel interactivity and match it to the extent of interactive exchange required. Where interpretive context does not exist or is not shared, FTF is the appropriate communication mode. EM is effective and efficient within a shared context, either pre-existing or built specifically around the communication event. To sustain effective communication, managers must be aware of their communication options and the differences in their characteristics, and accordingly choose the appropriate mode. Media awareness went beyond merely choosing the appropriate communication mode, to include skill in its use. For example, occasionally it was more convenient for editors to substitute FTF for EM when sending a "lean" message. Although not in compliance with the communication mode prescriptions presented above, most editors were aware enough to “throttle down" the richness of FTF in those situations by collectively agreeing to employ terse jargon and to adhere strictly to alternating turns. They actively constrained the FTF communication mode to reduce its richness and thus avoid introducing extraneous cues and meaning to their lean, factual messages.

According to Steinfield (1986), “The trend toward defining and measuring interactivity is a promising movement. . . ." However, little has been done beyond preliminary attempts to define the construct. This research investigated the interactivity construct and its relationship to media choice so as to shed some light on the study of media richness. Table 5 compares the media characteristics addressed by these two approaches to investigating media differences. While not the primary focus of the research, observations and interview data suggested that these characteristics appear to cluster into three groups or dimensions. The first addresses the issue of channel bandwidth, which seems to be the characteristic most often associated with media richness. A second addresses the degree of personalization and social presence. The third addresses the structural organization of interaction. The dimensions appear to vary independently. For example, broadcast television exhibits relatively high bandwidth, yet relatively low social presence and personalization, and no “interactivity" in that interaction is structured to be one-way with no opportunity for feedback or interruption. A speech exhibits approximately the same level of bandwidth and interactivity as broadcast TV, but higher personalization and social presence. Electronic financial markets exhibit high interactivity, but low bandwidth and no social presence.

Both the richness and interactivity constructs address bandwidth. Richness theory suggests that multiple channels and high variety languages, similar to the multiple senses and paralinguistic cues described by interaction theory, provide the wider bandwidth required to convey rich or ambiguous information. Both approaches would thus consider FTF to be richer than EM, as FTF employs natural, high variety languages and paralinguistic cues sent simultaneously via several channels (senses).

Both constructs similarly address personalization. Interaction theory assumes personal FTF interaction as the standard by which other forms of interaction are measured. Richness theory proposes personalization of the message, which is usually maximized during FTF interaction. Both are similar to social presence, and the more salient the interpersonal relationship, the more personalized the message and vice versa. Personalization and social presence enable communicators to utilize social cues to increase the richness of the medium.

Thus both richness and interactivity account for bandwidth and personalization. The primary difference in the two constructs is in how they address the structural characteristics of interaction for a particular medium. Richness theory proposes immediacy of feedback as the representative characteristic of this dimension, while interaction theory addresses this dimension in greater scope and detail and includes the characteristics discussed earlier such as simultaneity of exchange, turn-taking flexibility, spontaneity, and ability to interrupt or preempt. The structural components of interaction are the primary focus of much of the interaction theory research, and can lend insight into the study of media richness. By decomposing the richness construct into subconstructs and augmenting the structural organization subconstruct using interaction theory, it is possible to consider, in light of the findings reported here, how each might operáte to produce a perception of “richness."

Sitkin e al. (1992) proposed that communication media exhibit two independent channel capacities, one to transmit data and another to transmit meaning. The first is a relatively invariant characteristic of the channel, while the second varies with the particular communication context and situation. The findings observed at the research sites reflected a similar division of the richness construct. Bandwidth and, to some degree, social presence varied with the extent to which context and experience were shared among members of the group, while the structural aspects of richness or interactivity were less variant and more directly tied to the interaction constraints of the particular mode of communication. For example, the language of the newsroom groups became elaborated over time to where their precise jargon and code words could evoke very rich imagery and convey rich meaning. That is, the language was rich and “high variety" in a sociocognitive sense, but not in the Daft and Wiginton (1979) sense on which the media richness construct was founded. These results suggest that the bandwidth dimension of media richness may vary with the extent of shared interpretive context, and this may help to explain the inconsistent results in studies attempting to rate the richness of various media. Although both groups, enjoying a well-shared context for effective communication, rated EM as relatively expressive and electronic messages as meaningful, the group with higher average tenure, more stable environment, and thus greater shared context rated EM as more expressive. This would suggest that groups with low shared context might rate EM and other text-based communication modes as relatively leaner with regard to bandwidth.

As for social presence, Sproull and Keisler (1986) found that where the sender of an electronic mail message was known, social presence was high relative to anonymous electronic mail messages. EM has been shown to have the capacity to convey socio-emotional and relational context, especially in groups with shared experience and history (Rice and Love 1987, Walther and Burgoon 1992). The ability to convey relational information and social presence via mediated channels is especially strong in ongoing groups, where participants' status, personality, etc. are well-known by all and salient in every message regardless of the channel used (Zack 1991). In the newsroom groups, as in most ongoing groups, all communication was signed and editors knew the habits, personalities, and experiences of each of their team members. They knew when, for example, an electronic message would be taken as humorous by one individual yet as hostile by another, and communicated accordingly. With regard to filtering of nonverbal or paralinguistic cues, the issue for ongoing groups, then, is not primarily that CMC eliminates those cues which are rich in relational information and which convey social presence (Short et al. 1976) or social context (Sproull and Kiesler 1986). The more salient issue is that CMC eliminates those social and paralinguistic cues used to regulate and structure the process of interactive conversation. Although social presence and social cues may be attenuated to some degree in mediated channels, it is the interactivity (Rafaeli 1988, Rogers 1986, Schegloff 1987) effects, not the socio-relational effects, that are the primary richness constraints of CMC in ongoing groups.

Fulk et al. (1987) proposed that richness is not an invariant characteristic of a communication medium, but rather that social context plays an important role in influencing media choice, and that the attitudes and opinions of others will cause an individual's perception of media richness to vary. Modest support has been found for this Social Information Processing model (Schmitz and Fulk 1991). The findings reported here, in comparison, suggest that richness varies with degree of shared interpretive context. They also suggest, however, that social context may influence media choice by interacting with the social presence and personalization dimension of media richness. The greater the extent to which social information is collectively known and thus “precommunicated," the less important will be conveying social cues at the time of the interaction. Thus an electronic message from a close colleague will exhibit more social presence and social cuing than the same message from a stranger. Even when communicating via FTF, we can often be more clearly expressive and better understood when communicating with individuals we know than with those we don't. Therefore richness appears to be variable as proposed by Fulk et al. (1987), but rather than varying directly (or only directly) with the perceptions of influential others in the relevant social network (Schmitz and Fulk 1991), it may vary with the extent to which communicators know about each other.

In summary, the richness construct is more complex than previous research would indicate. Some have considered it invariant, while others, variable. It appears to be both. Some have postulated a rational, objective relationship between media richness and media choice, others a more sociotheoretical approach. It may be both. In ongoing groups with history, culture, norms, and deep, shared knowledge about each other, the cues-filtered-out constraints of EM (Culnan and Markus 1987) become less important and the bandwidth constraint is mitigated by shared code and jargon that has become elaborated over time. Group norms and shared knowledge allow for much about social relations and circumstances to be assumed, providing a rich context for ostensibly lean (but meaning-rich) messages. Perceived richness then may be more dependent on the richness of the shared, precommunicated, tacit context not included in the message, then in the message itself. And thus shared context can substitute for bandwidth and social presence when needing to send a rich message. As Galbraith (1973) pointed out when discussing uncertainty and its resolution: we can either reduce the uncertainty beforehand (by use of buffers or plans) or we can reduce it when needed by the exchange of information. The same with knowledge. It can be shared either beforehand or at the time of communication. The accumulation over time of messages exchanged and situations collectively experienced provides tremendous leverage for passing rich communication through lean channels. On the other hand, in the groups studied, skillful communicators knew to use EM when a lean message would suffice. Often the context was provided for in the structure of the communication itself. For people situated in a similar, familiar, and routine task context, adjacency pairs usually sufficed as little additional context was needed to render the message meaningful. Therefore, in situations where interpersonal knowledge and elaborate jargon is deeply shared, social presence and bandwidth—the variable dimensions of richness—diminish in importance while interactivity—the invariant dimension of richness—becomes most important.

This study shows that appropriate media choices (as defined within the context of use) do improve communication and group performance. However, many questions are left unanswered. The groups studied had generally cooperative and supportive cultures. Do the findings generalize to ineffective groups or groups with less hospitible cultures? What are the effects of group size, structure, or task? Are the proposed subconstructs of richness theoretically useful, and does the variable/invariant categorization hold up? By what mechanism and weighting do the subconstructs combine to produce a perception of richness? How and to what extent are the variable subconstructs influenced by different social and sociocognitive contexts? This study indicates that the media richness is still an area that may benefit from additional research.

More and more communication is being carried over mediated channels, and getting it right will only become more important.

This study does have its limitations. Generalizability has been identified as a problem with case-based research (Yin 1984). Yin, however, suggests that each case study be treated as an experiment. Experiments generalize to a theory and not to other experiments. Likewise, each case study generalizes to the theory being built rather than directly to other sites. Based on the results of similar in-depth studies of management groups performing both within ad hoc cycles (e.g., software development teams (McKenney et al. 1992)) and performing within cycles varying in duration from days to months (leasing company management team (McKenney 1990)), the findings appear to be robust for groups managing information- and communication-intensive work in a range of circumstances. However, in all cases, computer use formed an integral part of the managers' work. One area for future research would be to study managers for whom computer system use was not as integral a part of their task.

Another potential limitation is that only effective groups were studied. However, studying groups which are effective but which occasionally do ineffective things may be the most efficient means to building normative theory. The focus was on identifying what must go right for communication to be effective, rather than what may go wrong to render it ineffective. However, the analysis of miscommunication provided the exceptions that help to prove the rule. Those data generalized to the same theory, and the resulting propositions accounted for those instances of ineffective communication.

The results provide a foundation on which to build more focused and controlled (e.g., social survey or behavioral laboratory) studies of communication context and its relationship to communication mode characteristics and communication effectiveness. This stands in contrast to typical experimental design whereby the effects of context are controlled out of the experiment (or alternately the experimental setting itself forms the context). Additionally, it suggests that CMC researchers themselves must understand and share the context of the phenomenon before employing more precise research methods.

In summary, effective communication contributes to effective group performance. EM can provide management groups with an effective and efficient mode of communication when made available as part of a set of communication modes spanning the range of interactivity and richness.\*
