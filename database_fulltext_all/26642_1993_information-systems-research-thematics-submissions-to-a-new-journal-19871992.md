# Information Systems Research Thematics: Submissions to a New Journal, 1987–1992

E. Burton Swanson

Neil C. Ramiller

The flow of manuscripts through the editorial offices of an academic journal can provide valuable information both about the performance of the journal as an instrument of its field and about the structure and evolution of the field itself. We undertook an analysis of the manuscripts submitted to the journal Information Systems Research (ISR) during its start-up years, 1987 through 1992, in an effort to provide a foundation for examining the performance of the journal, and to open a window on to the information systems (IS) field during that period. We identified the primary research question for each of 397 submissions to ISR, and then categorized the research questions using an iterative classification procedure. Ambiguities in classification were exploited to identify relationships among the categories, and some overarching themes were exposed in order to reveal levels of structure in the journal's submissions stream. We also examined the distribution of submissions across categories and over the years of the study period, and compared the structures of the submissions stream and the publication stream. We present the results with the goal of broadening the perspectives which individual members of the IS research community have of ISR and to help fuel community discourse about the nature and proper direction of the field. We provide some guidelines to assist readers in this interpretive task, and offer some observations and speculations to help launch the discussion.

Information systems research—Research questions—Research themes

## Introduction

he founding of the journal Information Systems Research (ISR) by The Institute of Management Sciences in early 1987 marked an important milestone in the development of the Information Systems (IS) field. Launched with the aspiration that it would become a leading research journal in IS, ISR's first issue appeared in early 1990. Issues have appeared quarterly since, backed by a strong flow of submissions: 4 manuscripts in its first month of operation, December 1987, then 88 manuscripts in 1988, 67 in 1989, 93 in 1990, 89 in 1991, and 120 in 1992.

We recognized in ISR's submissions stream an opportunity to consider two important questions: (1) What does the “window" afforded by the ISR submissions stream reveal about the structure and direction of IS as a field; and, (2) how well is ISR functioning as an instrument for sampling and shaping the work of the IS research community? To provide a foundation for entertaining these questions, we undertook an analysis of the manuscripts submitted to ISR during its start-up years, the period 1987 through 1992, corresponding to the first author's tenure as editor for the journal.

This paper reports our findings.' Our primary goal in offering this paper to the community is to provoke discussion and reflection, rather than to sharply draw our own conclusions. In taking this stance, our perspective is that ISR, like any academic journal, is a social construction of its community. Naturally, the editor, sponsoring organization, advisory committees, and editorial boards play key roles in determining the character of a journal. Thus, institutional sponsorship, publicity, editorial policy statements, and appointments to editorial and advisory boards help create certain expectations about the mission of the journal. However, as authors submit papers, referees review them, and a readership emerges, the journal becomes the property of the community, and a large variety of actors comes to take part in defining its vision, focus, and direction. Moreover, the journal, while itself a social construction, contributes reciprocally to the social construction of the field and its community. As the journal grows and evolves, it not only provides a record of research past but helps in turn to define the field that it represents, serving as one arena in which the members of the community negotiate what their field is about.

We hope this report makes ISR, as just such an arena, more accessible and effective. Any single individual, whether author, reviewer, board member, or reader, has a relatively narrow view of the journal, influenced heavily by the submissions and published papers s/he is given, and chooses, to see. This report gives members of the community the wherewithal to take a broader view. The panorama unveiled here can help us all to consider more clearly and deeply how the work we produce reflects on what we are, as individual scholars and as a community. Moreover—reflexivity being fundamental to the on-going creation and reproduction of the social order (Giddens 1984)—an improved understanding of what we have accomplished so far can help us in making the new choices, individually and collectively, that will shape the future of the field.

Our intention is that this paper take a place alongside other overviews of research in IS (e.g., Ives et al. 1980; Culnan 1986, 1987; Culnan and Swanson 1986; Barki et al. 1988; Alavi et al. 1989; Banville and Landry 1989; Gray et al. 1989; Culnan et al. 1993). However, it is not our ambition to advance a “framework" for the field. Rather, we limit our claims to the ISR submissions stream itself and, in a social-constructivist spirit, leave the primary task of determining what the data mean more broadly to our readers.

In our concluding remarks, we suggest some guidelines for this broader interpretation. To seed the discussion, we also venture some of our own observations. And we raise some pointed questions: Does ISR reflect a sufficient diversity of research in the field? In the face of technological and social change, do ISR submissions indicate that the IS research community is responsive to important emergent phenomena? Does the view through ISR's window reveal the IS research community to be a "fragmented adhocracy" as suggested by Banville and Landry (1989)? Can and should ISR foster a growing unity in the field and, if so, on what basis?

## Analytic Method

To achieve the objectives of this study we needed to characterize the stream of submissions received by the journal. As our interest was in substantive content, we did so by focusing on the principal research questions which those submissions addressed. The analytic process involved three steps: selecting submissions to review, identifying their research questions, and classifying those research questions.

## Selecting Submissions

To get the most panoramic view possible across domains of research and over time, we decided to include in the analysis all submissions to ISR during the period 1987 through 1992 that were not screened by the editor as inappropriate for the journal. Of 92 new submissions received during 1987–1988, 6 were screened as inappropriate, leaving 86 submissions to be analyzed. In succeeding years, 10, 21, 12, and 15 submissions were screened, leaving 57 submissions for analysis in 1989, 72 in 1990, 77 in 1991, and 105 in 1992. Thus, 397 submissions were included in the analysis.

## Identifying Research Questions

Identifying the research questions involved substantial interpretation. Not all submitted papers stated their research question in succinct form. When we began the analysis on the submissions for 1987–1988, we examined each manuscript's title, abstract, introduction, discussion section, and conclusions before writing the research question. As the study advanced, we discovered that the research question could usually be identified through the title and abstract alone. Only where the abstract was quite poorly composed did it prove necessary to look beyond it. Although authors infrequently provided a clearly stated research question that could simply be copied down, in phrasing research questions we attempted to use language provided by the authors themselves.

## Classifying the Submissions

Our objective was to classify the submissions based on their research questions, to help reveal convergence around certain fundamental issues or themes in the field. However, we considered classification problematic since a given paper might address a number of distinctive topics. For example, a paper that considered the effect of the user-computer interface on the implementation of a PC software package might fit comfortably into categories as varied as human-computer interaction, end usér computing, and IS implementation. Nevertheless, we decided to eschew the potential complexity and confusion of cross-classification, and to place each submission in a single category. At a later point, we could evaluate what problematic classifications had to say about the relationships between the categories and, hence, among the streams of research in the field.

Classification of the research questions proceeded iteratively, year by year, with the research questions for the 1987–1988 submissions providing our starting point. Each investigator independently generated a preliminary classification for these submissions. The investigator considered the research question for each submission and wrote a phrase or two characterizing the IS research area it principally addressed. The investigator then looked for common themes among these characterizations and grouped submissions accordingly, labelling the groups and setting aside isolates that failed to fit well into any group.

We then met to compare our respective classification schemes. One scheme comprised 15 categorięs and the other, 18 categories. Comparing categories pairwise across the 2 schemes, 12 pairs of categories each shared 3 or more submissions, accounting for 50 (58%) of the submissions. An additional 12 pairs of categories each shared exactly 2 submissions, accounting for an additional 24 (28%) of the submissions. The initial classification effort thus revealed a substantial amount of congruence between the investigators and boded well for achieving a unified scheme. We proceeded, again independently, to develop proposals for reconciling the two classification schemes, and then met to negotiate the final groupings, their labels, and the disposition of isolates. The result was a consensus classification of 20 categories and 2 isolates.

Using the classification scheme for the 1987–1988 submissions as the foundation, each of us independently assigned each 1989 submission to the category in which it best appeared to fit. Where a paper failed to fit well into an existing category, either a new category was proposed or the paper was left unclassified. We then met to resolve differences in the assignment of submissions and in the nomination of new categories. Submissions from 1987–1988 were reevaluated, and a number were reassigned to the new categories. Analysis of the 1990, 1991, and 1992 submissions proceeded in a similar fashion, starting with the categories existing at the close of the previous year's analysis, and assigning submissions, modifying categories, creating new categories, and reclassifying prior submissions. In the end, 391 submissions were assigned to 37 categories, leaving 6 submissions unclassified. The growth in the number of categories over the course of the analysis was primarily an effect of the increasing number of submissions considered, which permitted the identification of clusters of research questions representing subtopics within grosser categories. In a few cases, new categories appeared to reflect the emergence of a novel area of inquiry during the period covered by the study.

## Results

## How We Are Reporting Our Findings

The research questions and their grouping into categories represent the primary database in our study. However, we do not reveal the research questions here. There are two reasons for this omission, First, the sheer number of research questions would require a prohibitive amount of space to report. Second, we are concerned with protecting the proprietary interest that ISR's authors may have in their original resèarch ideas, which might be compromised by our broadcasting the research questions. In place of the research questions themselves, we attempt to give a rich accounting of their core concepts, broader patterns, and underlying themes. To characterize the individual categories we provide descriptions of each and list the key words, concepts, and associations that appear in their constituent research questions. We also examine the relationships among the categories, as suggested by research questions that point toward other categories than those to which they have been assigned. And finally, we consider how clustering in the relationships suggests higher-order themes. The presentation therefore builds in a fashion suggested by Figure 1.

We conclude the description of our findings with some observations on the distribution of research questions among the categories over time. These data can give some clues as to what, through ISR's "window,"the structure and direction of research in information systems appear to be. We also note the differences between the composition of the submissions stream and the distribution among topics of the papers ISR has accepted for publication.

Human-computer Interaction. Three themes arise in research questions raising issues at the boundary of the human and the computer. These include the design and evaluation of interfaces or interface elements (especially graphical ones); the partitioning and integrating of work between computer and human processors; and the nature and genesis of selected outcomes of human-computer interaction.³ Questions consider both the design or machine side of the interaction, and characteristics of the human processors.

Information Economics. Research questions concerned with information economics focus on fundamental issues associated with the production, collection, and use of information. Information economics shares this mission with the category information and managerial decision making, but takes an explicitly economic perspective, whereas questions in the latter category tend to be more psychological or sociological in nature. Information economics also contrasts with IS economics in that the latter category is concerned more expressly with the economics of systems themselves.⁴

Information and Managerial Decision Making. Research questions in this category explore a variety of topics having to do with the relationship between information and decision making in organizations. Unifying this work is an abiding interest, often explicit, in informing the design of systems toward improved decision making.

DSS Design. Research questions concerning the design of decision support systems focus in various ways on the issue of designing DSS for improved decision performance. While some submissions engage specific elements of DSS, others consider design overall.⁶

DSS Model Management. Research questions relating to model management readily announce themselves by the use of such phrases as “model management" or "modelbase management"or by focusing on the management of models and model components. This collection of research is preoccupied with advancing the state of the art, and considers such areas of interest as the supporting technology (especially database technologies), model formulation, model maintenance, and model management in distributed computing environments.

DSS Development and Implementation. Interests in DSS development and implementation (beyond those involving design) tend to parallel those that apply to information systems more generally (see, in particular, IS development process, user involvement. and IS implementation). Submissions inquire about the antecedents of acceptance and use, the role and effects of user involvement, and the structure and meaning of implementation lifecycles.

DSS Outcomes. One set of research questions converges on inquiry about the effects of using DSS. These questions examine outcomes, the overall value or effectiveness of DSS, and contingencies that affect the link between use and performance.7

DSS Applications. A few submissions consider how DSS might be applied. The assortment of questions in this area concern decision support for crisis management, the management of software maintenance projects, facilities planning, and marketing.

Expert System Applications. Interest in the application of knowledge-based technology is divided between addressing specific applications and raising more general issues regarding the application or applicability of expert systems. Of particular interest is the use of expert systems to support decision making and problem solving in particular domains.8

Expert System Design and Evaluation. A prominent theme among submissions concerned with the design and evaluation of knowledge-based systems is accomplishing efficacious design. Toward this end, specific research questions consider methods and approaches, overall architecture, design of specific elements and capabilities, and performance measurement.

Knowledge Acquisition. As for model management, research questions concerning knowledge acquisition for expert systems concentrate on advancing the state of the art. Papers consider specific techniques for eliciting knowledge and beliefs from experts, ways to reconcile the knowledge of multiple experts, and approaches to automated induction, and compare and evaluate methods.

Systems Development Process. ISR has collected a potpourri of research questions on the context, structure, process, and outcomes of system development projects. The subtext for many of these questions is a search for the conditions of project success. Submissions offer or compare particular ways of looking at systems development, explore antecedents of project performance, examine project management, and probe the dynamics and effects of participation and conflict.9 The appearance of such phrases as “systems development" and “system development project" tends to announce a research question's membership in this category.

Requirements Analysis and Modeling. A unifying concern of the research questions on requirements analysis is discovering what contributes to effective practice. Authors approached this from various angles, some proferring or evaluating particular techniques or approaches, others exploring the antecedents of effective analysis.1º Membership in this category is broadly signalled by the use of the word "requirements" or by allusion to the systems analysis task domain.

Data Modeling and Database Design. An underlying theme and motivation for much of the research on analysis and design for databases is improving effectiveness. Research questions consider differences in approach, characteristics of the modeler as predictors of effectiveness, and problem context.1 Work pioneering on the technical frontier explores automation of modeling and design through expert systems, and design issues relating to heterogeneous, distributed databases.

User Involvement. An abiding concern of the research on user involvement in systems development is its effects on system quality and user satisfaction. Research questions focus on issues of measurement, contingencies influencing the effect of user involvement on project outcomes, and process models of involvement. Research in this category is readily identified by the use of the phrases “user involvement." “user participation," or “user leadership."

Tools and Techniaues in Systems Development. As with other relatively “technical" categories. interests in tools and techniques center on the state of the art. Submissions make innovative proposals, examine use, or scrutinize the performance outcomes of the technology. The technologies considered include data flow diagramming, functional decomposition, computer aided software engineering (CASE), and object orientation.

System Project Estimation. The shared subtext for research questions concerned with problems of estimation is improving accuracy and reliability in prediction. Most submissions target systems development; one question addresses software maintenance, while the remainder consider software projects more generally. The focus of estimation is, variously, cost, effort, complexity, and schedule. Submissions propose methods and models, compare and evaluate approaches and techniques, and examine managers' estimation heuristics and capabilities.

Software Maintenance. Research questions defining the nascent software maintenance category raise issues concerning the overall nature of maintenance problems, the maintenance of knowledge-based systems, estimation of software maintenance effort (see system project estimation), and the administration of software maintenance projects (see DSS applications).

Data Management. Research questions in data management explore various issues in practice. Submissions sort themselves into those concerned with evaluation and measurement (data quality, database usability, DBMS benchmarking), policy (error removal, data distribution), and management practice (critical success factors).

Computer Resource Allocation. Research questions on resource allocation are united by a concern with identifving optimal arrangements. Topics of interest include alternative mechanisms for allocation, access-control policies, and allocation of resources in distributed computing environments.

IS Performance Evaluation. Questions on performance evaluation divide themselves between an interest in measuring the performance of organizational information systems and a concern with assessing the information systems function. Thus research questions ask about building models of IS “success,"the definition and measurement of success in IS planning, and the views held by organizational participants of what constitutes success.

IS Security and Control. Interests in the security and control of systems range across issues of computer abuse (such as unauthorized access), loss of computer capacity, and low computer dependability. Research questions examine the design and maintenance of controls, the comparative effectiveness of alternative controls, conditions determining the effectiveness of controls, computer backup, database security, access control, and user perceptions of security issues.

IS Ethics. Questions in this category, which complements IS security and control, consider the antecedents of ethical vs. unethical behavior among computer users, and the phenomenon of unauthorized software copying.

IS Personnel. Research on information systems personnel considers an assortment of “classic" human resources topics. These include turnover, managerial training. gender discrimination, and organizational commitment

IS-user Relationships. The unifying motivation for the research on the interaction between users and the IS function is improving this relationship. Research questions examine the outcomes of IS-user interaction, conditions affecting the relationship, communication between users and the IS function, and mechanisms for user control.12

User Perceptions and Attitudes. Research questions on user perceptions and attitudes focus on how they are formed, and why they are important in systems practice. Papers range across issues of conceptualization, measurement, and explanation. Causal studies explore the relationship between perceptions and attitudes, factors in the development of attitudes, the employment of perceptions and attitudes as predictors of future system use, and the effect of information systems on users' perceptions of work.

User Information Evaluation and Satisfaction. Submissions on the user evaluation of information (specifically, as an information system output) constitute a well-defined subtopic of user perceptions and attitudes. As for the latter category, research in this area concerns the advancement of conceptualization and measurement, and the identification of causal antecedents. Among antecedents of interest are environmental characteristics, task requirements, system functionality, and the hardware delivery system. Some efforts also place the information satisfaction construct in broader context, specifically, that of assessing the value of information or the effectiveness of information systems.

End-user Computing. Research questions on end-user computing, identifiable on the whole by the appearance of phrases like “end-user" and "end-user computing," are united in a fundamental concern with how end-user computing might be facilitated and managed. The questions disperse themselves among a variety of issues, including appropriate technology, training and support, determinants of usage, management control, and outcomes.

IS Economics. A variety of themes underlies the research questions that take an explicitly economic approach, which are united less by topical focus than by perspective. Submissions, moreover, pose questions at a number of different levels. A cluster of research questions concerning the economy at large addresses the interaction of technological innovation, cost/price, and expenditures. Extra-organizational research also explores the setting of industrial standards and the impact of information technology on productivity. Research questions relating to interorganizational transactions focus on the economics of information technology supported products. (See also interorganizational information systems.) Research at the firm level considers sources of economies in constructing information systems, correlates of information technology investment, the internal pricing of computing resources, and the effect of information technology investments on the market value of firms.

IS, Strategic Management, and Business Outcomes. Submissions that address the strategic and financial implications of information systems reflect the abiding concern of the IS research community with the relationship of information technology investments to business performance. Prominent research themes include identifying the effects of information technology on business results, determining how to organize, plan, and render decisions that can enhance the linkage of IS and business strategy, and measuring the contribution of information technology to firm performance.13

Interorganizational Information Systems. Research questions on interorganizational information systems, which exhibit a preoccupation with strategic effects, substantially constitute a special case of IS, strategic management, and business outcomes. They readily identify themselves through the use of such phrases as “interorganizational information system" and “electronic marketplace," or through reference to more specific phenomena, such as EDI and ATMs. Submissions in this category address a number of areas of potential effect, including competitive advantage, search costs, product quality, product diversity, customer costs, market share, industry profits, social welfare, and seller-buyer relationships, and examine the conditions and factors involved.'4

Information Technology Diffusion. One group of research questions shares the goal of explaining the adoption and spread of information technologies using an innovation-diffusion perspective. Most of the research questions in this category announce their membership by the use of "diffusion," “adoption," or “"decision to adopt." Submissions explore diffusion on a variety of levels, from individual end users through organizations and industrial sectors, to nations, and with respect to a diversity of technologies and practices.'s

14 Among the conditions explored are system sponsorship (industry standard vs. seller sponsored), industry structure, and local competitive and demographic conditions.

Information System Implementation. Key words like“implementation,""incorporation," “infusion,"“routinization," and “utilization" identify questions about the introduction of information systems or information technologies in organizations. Research questions in this category concern the characterization and classification of implementation situations, the conceptualization and measurement of constructs, and the identification of factors and contingencies affecting implementation processes and outcomes.

Organizational Outcomes. A substantial collection of research questions shares concerns with the impacts of information systems on organizations (beyond the issue of firms' financial performance). This category includes questions dealing with the mutual structuring or coevolution of information technology and organizational form. Submissions offer contributions to conceptualization and theory-building, or focus on specific effects, including impacts on structure, relationships, tasks, work performance, and personal attitudes.1⁶

Information Systems Typologies. Research questions concerned with the classification of information systems show two distinctive motivations. One motivation is to compare classification across the practitioner-educator boundary. The other seeks to use typology in explaining the evolution of information systems and in predicting new forms of technology.

IS Research. The research questions that look at IS research as a subject in its own right fall into four distinct subcategories. One group of submissions examines information systems as a field, raising questions concerning its character, its current status, its evolution and motivation, its philosophical foundations, and its relationship to reference disciplines. Another set of research questions focuses on issues in research design, method, measurement, and data analysis. A third group of questions considers how certain concepts, constructs, and theories (e.g., Nolan's stage model, agency theory, information systems effectiveness, organizational fit, typologies of work tasks) might contribute to research and theory-building in information systems. The fourth subcategory raises questions about the nature of the IS research community (e.g., patterns of collaboration, influence of journals).

A Comment on the Isolates. A handful of research questions do not fit well into any category. Being classified an “isolate" does not necessarily mean that a research question is fundamentally odd or anomalous. It might be the case that only one representative of a well-established research area was submitted to ISR (for whatever reason). Alternatively, a particular submission may mark an emergent category of research that will become well represented in the years ahead. For example, in 1987– 88, the year ISR began receiving submissions, only one paper each was received in the categories computer-supported cooperative work and information technology diffusion: these categories have since achieved considerable prominence.

## Relationships among the Categories, and Higher-order Themes

Not all research questions could be unambiguously assigned to a single category, as remarked earlier. Questions that might have been placed in more than one category, or that pose important implications for other categories than the ones to which they were assigned, in effect create bridges among the categories. These bridges help further to characterize the categories themselves, and, taken together, the IS field generally. In particular, the pattern of links between categories tends to suggest certain higher-order themes, and hence to reveal structure in the field.

In an effort to expose this structure, we reviewed the classification of research questions for points of ambiguity, and mapped the relationships among the categories accordingly (Figure 2). Our mapping notation employed a three-level scheme. At the highest level we identified collections of categories representing broad, overarching themes. Each diagram in Figure 2 portrays one such theme. The categories that gave primary definition to the theme are shown in bold and enlarged type in the figure, and are listed in Table 2.

The second tier in our mapping scheme comprised pairs of categories, joined in Figure 2 by double lines, which could potentially share a number of research questions. For example, IS economics and IS, strategic management, and business outcomes each contain a number of research questions that might readily have been assigned to the other category. The primary relationships in this tier helped to define the broad themes in the first tier, but did not exclusively do so. For example, as Figure 2c shows, we united categories relating to DSS in a single theme, even though not all the DSS categories were related to one another by primary (double-line) connections. The lack of primary relationships in this case does not mean that the categories fail to bear a thematic relationship to one another but, rather, that we simply had little difficulty separating those types of DSS questions.

Where only one or two research questions might be cross-classified between categories, or where a question was not difficult to classify but nevertheless bore important implications for another category, we defined a secondary relationship, indicated by an arrow in Figure 2. In each case the arrow points away from the category in which the research question(s) at issue resides. Secondary relationships represent the third and lowest tier in the mapping scheme.

The high-level themes listed in Table 2 and portrayed in the separate panels of Figure 2 do not represent islands of inquiry. Primary relationships between certain pairs of categories create important links between themes. Consider, for example, the relationship between IS performance evaluation and user information evaluation and satisfaction in linking research on “Evaluation and Control" with studies of"Users" (Figures 2e and 2f). A summary of the major linkages among the nine research themes is given in Figure 3.

The mapping exercise exposed a dense web of relationships among the categories In the discussion to follow we will attend only to the most prominent associations.

Computer Supported Cooperative Work. Computer supported cooperative work (Figure 2a) is one of the most cohesive categories and substantially constitutes its own thematic area. Nevertheless, the category replicates within a focused domain many of the research interests of the areas we have labelled Information and Interface (via common interests in collective decision making), Users (by sharing a preoccupation with user perceptions), and Introduction and Impact (through shared concerns with usage and implementation). Computer supported cooperative work contains several questions that concern the organizational and, in particular, the group effects of using CSCW technologies, and therefore bears a strong kinship with the category organizational outcomes. On the other hand, although research on group decision support systems appears prominently in the CSCW category, its pronounced distinctiveness from DSS-related research, with the latter's focus on individualized decision support, produces only an attenuated relationship between the two areas. This relationship is founded mainly on papers concerned with the appropriation and use of individualized decision support tools in group work settings.

## Discussion

We now return to the two primary questions motivating the analysis we have just reported. What do the patterns and trends in ISR submissions say about the development of IS as a field? And, what do they say about the functioning of ISR as an instrument of the IS research community? To reiterate the theme of our introduction, our primary objective in presenting this report is to help fuel the IS research community's efforts to debate, negotiate, define, and redefine the nature of the journal and of the field. Accordingly, our discussion primarily takes the form of “guidelines for interpretation" designed to alert readers to some of the opportunities and hazards in interpreting the journal's submissions stream. We do, however, venture some preliminary observations in concluding this report—to get the discussion going, as it were.

## Some Guidelines for Interpretation

Sorting out the stories that the submissions stream has to tell about IS and ISR is problematic, because their plots are fundamentally intertwined. The journal, however broad its mission, is necessarily selective. Accordingly, while the ISR submissions stream gives one picture of the research activity in the field,.work that some might label “IS" is almost certainly taking place out of the journal's sight. One clue to what might be “missing" is provided by the journal's statement for contributors. This reads, in part:

Information Systems Research (ISR) is dedicated to advancing the understanding and practice of information systems in organizations through theoretical and empirical research. The scope of ISR's interests is wide ranging. In general, submitted articles should address the application of information technology to human organizations and their management. . . No one reference discipline is viewed as inherently superior. Research based in any of the social sciences is welcome.

While broad, the statement's expressed focus on application to organizations and management, and its appeal to the “social sciences," probably tend to discourage submissions of a more purely technical nature. Moreover, although implications for practice are identified as important, the editorial statement makes clear that ISR is a research journal and does not entertain reports on practice, per se. (Contrast Management Information Systems Quarterly, which maintains sections for both “Application" and “Theory and Research.") The practice of screening submissions for consistency with the journal's mission has, we believe, substantially translated the editorial statement into policy.

Despite the deliberate bounding of the journal's scope, the editorial statement clearly signals an intent to make ISR encompassing in its interests and appeal. The composition of ISR's Editorial and Advisory boards, manifestly broad in its substantive range, provides a complementary signal to the community about the journal's ambition to reflect and promote diversity in the field.

Of course, the “management" of ISR—its editor, boards, and sponsor—does not alone determine the journal's character. How community members inferred ISR's mission in the early signals; how that affected their self-selection as authors, reviewers, and readers; and how their understanding of ISR subsequently evolved based on additional information, such as published articles, reviews written or received, and discussions with colleagues—all this has helped to define what the journal has become.

This process of social construction, complex and difficult to trace, can make it tricky to assign a pattern or trend in the submissions stream to the field or to the journal. For example, does a dearth of submissions on DSS applications mean a lack of academic interest in this area or a community consensus that ISR is poorly positioned to publish such research? Does a sudden upswing in submissions on computersupported cooperative work announce an explosion of activity in that subject area or a convergence by CSCW researchers on ISR as a promising outlet for their work?

Experts in a given research domain may be best positioned to settle questions of this kind. because of their first-hand research, their personal contacts with colleagues, and their familiarity with other outlets—conferences, journals, and so on—for research products in the area. This level of exposure gives them a quasi-independent foundation for understanding the research activity in the area and hence for determining how well ISR is sampling it. We say “quasi-independent" because the flow of relevant manuscripts through ISR may well play an important role in determining how even domain experts see the character of the area.

Here are some of the broad issues which readers might entertain in considering the data reported here, (1) Given external points of reference, as just remarked, how well does the ISR submissions stream reflect the diversity of current research activity in IS? Is ISR attracting a wide enough range of papers to serve the long-term development of the field. or are there important pockets of research that have not been adequately attracted to the journal? (2) Does the ISR submissions stream suggest that there are areas of research receiving insufficient attention from IS scholars? What do gaps in the record of research suggest are the field's key “research challenges," the messy vet critical problems with which the field must grapple? (3) How well are we directing our attention forward, toward emergent phenomena? Are we prematurely consolidating our research agendas around a standard set of issues, rendering our work unresponsive to shifting economic and technological foundations, and ceding innovation wholly to practitioners (Barley et al. 1988)? (4) Finally, and more broadly, how does a look through the ISR window inform our understanding of the structure of IS as a field? What appear today to be its relevant reference disciplines (Culnan and Swanson 1986, Culnan et al. 1993)? Does a multiparadigmatic regime seem likely to persist (Banville and Landry 1989)? And, is communication and integration across the different traditions in the field problematic?

We close by venturing some speculations on the data we have presented here, as our contribution to the interpretive discourse. Apropos the strategy of making reference to expert knowledge, we undertook a modest effort to solicit expert opinion by sending an earlier version of this report, based on submissions for 1987-88 and 1989, to members of ISR's editorial and advisory boards. Several board members responded, some in detail, and we have worked many of their observations into the remarks that follow

## Through ISR's Window: Speculations on the Journal and the Field

The overall distribution of submissions across topics (note the marginal row totals in Table 3 and Appendix 1) suggests that ISR has substantially succeeded in attracting a substantively wide range of papers. The journal has, we believe, taken some important early steps toward achieving one of its key goals, to accommodate and promote diversity in IS.

The reviewers of our preliminary report delivered substantially the same assessment. Nevertheless, they did identify a number of topics that the submissions through 1989 did not represent well. (Our reading of the submissions stream from 1990 through 1992 suggests that at least some of these “deficiences"or variances continue to apply.) Cited were information ethics, legal aspects of computing, international issues (including IS in developing countries, and comparative studies of IS penetration, transfer, and impact), the role of government policy in innovation and technology transfer, and interorganizational information systems. Reviewers also noted a lack of papers related to IS forecasting, that is, work speculating about future directions in IS applications and research, or reporting on emerging, innovative applications. One reviewer remarked that the submissions stream conveyed the impression that ISR was mainly attracting papers dealing with organizational, behavioral, and managerial issues. This contrasted with his prior impression, based on articles published in early editions, that ISR had been attracting a robust stream of analytical and technical works.

Is the perception (or even just the awareness) of ISR among IS scholars not working in business schools problematic? If this is the case, as suggested by one reviewer, it might help explain low levels of submissions taking in broader social and political contexts (e.g., few or no submissions from scholars of information science, political science, public policy, and so on) or on technical issues (e.g., few or no submissions from researchers in computer science or industrial engineering). Perhaps the expressed editorial positioning on “organizations and their management" plays a role in deflecting potential ISR authors whose primary disciplinary agendas tend to be remote from issues of organizational application.

Of course, although the gaps noted by the reviewers may indeed reflect problems in ISR's "capture mechanisms," there also may well be a dearth of research in the indicated areas. One reviewer, for example, remarking that ISR submissions on computer support tend to show a bias toward research on managerial level personnel at the expense of clerical employees, mused that this might well be true of research in the community at large. Another reviewer faulted the field, not the journal, for the lack of analytical work and formal modeling. A third reviewer attributed the lack of forward-looking work in the ISR submissions stream to the research community, and took the community to task for setting its research agendas incrementally, attempting to standardize prematurely on topics and methods, and, in effect, trying to drive while looking through the rear-view mirror. (We note in passing the irony of this comment with respect to the mirror we have provided in our own paper.)

Do ISR submissions tell us anything to the contrary about the potential responsiveness of the IS research community to emergent phenomena? Unfortunately, the small number of submissions in the individual categories (Appendix 1), or even in the aggregated categories (Table 3), do not provide a clear basis for interpreting trends in the journal's submissions stream. Nevertheless, some patterns are suggestive. For example, interest in computer-supported cooperative work is clearly on the rise, a likely response to the emerging “group work" technologies that promise to deliver enhanced levels of connectedness among organizational members. Of somewhat less obvious genesis is the rise in interest in applying the innovation diffusion framework to information technologies. Perhaps the increasing diversity and reach of new information technologies may help to explain this sudden growth in IS researchers' appeals to the diffusion literature.

Considering what the submissions stream might have to say, overall, about the structure of the IS field, we believe (intersections among substantive domains, reflected in Figure 2, notwithstanding) that the view through ISR's window reveals what Banville and Landry (1989) have called a "fragmented adhocracy." That is, the field continues to be topically diverse and based on appeals to significantly different and partly incommensurate reference disciplines (Culnan and Swanson 1986; Culnan et al. 1993). Reviewers essentially agreed, interpreting the scattering of research over many different topics as indicating a lack of major paradigms or foundations particular to IS.

Our own assessment is that four rather distinct intellectual traditions may be identified as shaping the present IS thematic structure. We characterize them as: Engineering and Design; Decision Processes; Social Processes; and Economic Efficiency and Business Performance. These strongly parallel what we recognize as the key reference disciplines of IS: computer science, management science, organization science, and a relative newcomer, economics. Communication across these disciplines continues to pose a challenge to the IS field.

Finally, we observe that the contributions to ISR as a whole have a very instrumental flavor. If there is any unity to the research stream, it may be in its consistent attention to the basic issue of how information technology may best be organized directed. and applied (now. not in the difficult-to-see future). Given the underlying disciplines and their research conventions, this focus should perhaps not be surprising. But some may ask whether it is enough to hold the field together. What are the implications for the shape of the IS research community and for ISR over time? Can we expect to grow a reasonably cohesive academic village (however far-flung geographically)? Or will IS become merely a crossroads through which travelers from economics, decision sciences, organizational behavior, and so on, pass? On this point. we are ourselves optimistic that the common bond of interest in information technology among researchers will prove as durable and lasting as information technology itself appears to be, in its continuing evolutionary and revolutionary applications. Once attracted to the IS academic village, many scholars will continue to choose to reside there, and will carry on the building of a heterogeneous, and yet congenial, community.
