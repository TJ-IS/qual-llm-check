# User Response Data: The Potential for Errors and Biases

Ellen M. Hufnagel

Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences

The University of South Florida

4202 E. Fowler Avenue—CIS 1040

Tampa, Florida 33620-7800

Christopher Conca

Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences The University of South Florıda

4202 E. Fowler Avenue—CIS 1040

Tampa, Florida 33620-7800

Surveys that require users to evaluate or make judgments about information systems and their effect on specific work activities can produce misleading results if respondents do not interpret or answer questions in the ways intended by the researcher. This paper provides a framework for understanding both the cognitive activities and the errors and biases in judgment that can result when users are asked to categorize a system, explain its effects, or predict their own future actions and preferences with respect to use of a system. Specific suggestions are offered for wording survey questions and response categories so as to elicit more precise and reliable responses. In addition, possible sources of systematic bias are discussed, using examples drawn from published IS research. Recommendations are made for further research aimed at better understanding how and to what extent judgment biases could affect the results of IS surveys.

## 1. Introduction

s computer technologies have proliferated, IS researchers have become increas-Lingly concerned with the effect information systems have on the work activities of users. What distinguishes these new behavioral researchers is a particular interest in theoretical models relating to the beliefs, attitudes, perceptions, values, and preferences of current and prospective system users. To measure constructs like these, researchers typically employ survey instruments that require respondents to make inferences and draw conclusions about the characteristics of a system and its effect on their work activities. Unfortunately, research involving user judgments has culminated in low explained variance and results that are inconsistent across studies of the same phenomenon.

Numerous reasons have been offered to explain the inconsistent results of studies involving users’ evaluative judgments. For example, weak theoretical underpinnings (Miller 1989, Melone 1990), faulty conceptualization and measurement of key constructs (Galletta and Lederer 1989, Etezadi-Amolie and Farhoomand 1991), inconsistencies in the operationalization of constructs from study to study (Melone 1990, Kim 1990, DeLone and McLean 1992), and the omission ofimportant antecedent or mitigating variables (Hawk and Raju 1991) have been cited as contributing factors. Closer attention to these theoretical and methodological issues should improve matters greatly; however, research that relies on user responses is also subject to a host of problems associated with shortcomings in human judgment processes that warrant further attention.

Most researchers know that the framing of questions and response categories affects the way people respond to surveys (Bradburn 1982). But it is often difficult to anticipate the different ways people might interpret and respond to a question and how this variability might contaminate a study's results. Fortunately, the literature on human cognitive processes has begun to shed light on these issues. For example, studies of judgment and choice indicate that seemingly inconsequential differences in the way questions are asked can affect people's perceptions and preferences (Tversky and Kahneman 1982, Slovic et al. 1988). When respondents do not have a well-developed idea of what they like or want, the questions posed by an investigator can actually shape the respondent's values or elicit judgments that are unduly extreme (Fischhoff et al. 1988). Because such effects are most likcly when the issues addressed are complex or unfamiliar to the respondents, these findings have particular relevance to IS researchers who study users' reactions to new technologies.

Understanding how errors and biases can affect user response data is only a partial solution, however. Behavioral researchers also need to know what they can do to increase the likelihood that survey questions produce reasonably accurate measures of the researcher's theoretical constructs. Although rigorous research on question wording began to emerge only recently (Marsh 1982), the results provide some specific guidance for social science researchers. For example, studies indicate that judgments involving the categorization of a stimulus object (e.g., a new system) are more likely to be stable and reproducible if contextual data is provided to ensure that all respondents are answering the same question and respondents are given some indication of the basis to be used in making comparisons (Andrews 1989). Additionally, judgments involving frequencies are more precise when numerical frequency scales are used instead of verbal scales (Hartley et al. 1984, Newstead and Arnold 1989).

In this paper, we describe some judgment deficiencies identified in the literature on cognition and social judgment processes and explain how they can lead to inappropriate conclusions when users are asked to assess the characteristics or effects of an information system. Using examples from IS research, we show how the questions used to measure users' perceptions and beliefs can elicit very different kinds of judgments from users of a single information system as well as from a single user at different points in time. We also offer suggestions for wording questions and response categories so as to minimize the likelihood of misleading results. Although the examples are largely drawn from user satisfaction research, our analysis is pertinent to most survey research in the social sciences.

In the next section, we distinguish between random error and systematic bias in terms of their effects on the reliability and validity of research data. Possible differences in the extent to which these undesirable “response effects" might occur in relation to subjective versus objective questions are also considered. We then provide a background for our discussion by differentiating between the logic that guides scientific reasoning and the logic employed by “intuitive lay scientists" who have not been trained in or do not routinely apply the scientific method. In §3, we establish a framework for thinking through the cognitive activity involved in answering different kinds of survey questions. We then use this framework to examine the inferential reasoning steps involved when current users of an existing system are asked to draw conclusions regarding (1) the salient characteristics of a particular system, and (2) its effect on work-related outcomes. Specific errors and biases associated with each of these inference activities are explored along with suggestions for reducing their impact through careful instrument design. We follow this same format in §4 in considering the judgment activities involved when prospective users of a new system or technology are asked to make predictions about the future effects of a system or their own behavior. In the final section, we provide a summary of our analysis and suggest areas for future research.

## 2. Definitions and Background

## 2.1. Definitions and Key Concepts

The practice of using standardized questions in survey research is based on the following assumptions: (1) that the meaning of the questions is shared by a majority of respondents, (2) that respondents understand the stimulus or phenomenon under investigation in roughly equivalent ways, and (3) that the responses will be given in a manner which allows the researcher to interpret and compare them (Bradburn 1982). When these assumptions are not met, the results may be unreliable and/or invalid. Reliability and validity are related issues; however, each requires special consideration during instrument design.

2.1.1. Random Error and Reliability. Survey methods, which rely on people's judgment, recall, and interpretation skills for accuracy, seldom produce perfectly precise answers. As a result, all survey data contains some amount of random error or deviation from the “true value"(actual state of affairs). Generally speaking, the more random error a survey instrument produces, the less reliably it will behave on repeated measurements and the more difficult it will be to ensure stable, reproducible results over time (Carmines and Zeller 1979)

Imprecision or measurement inaccuracy is most easily identified in factual or objective data where the responses can be verified by external observation. However, measurement error can also occur in subjective data where the researcher is attempting to assess human characteristics that are not directly observable (Marsh 1982, Buckley et al. 1990). Researchers sometimes resort to tests of internal consistency as evidence that these subjective measures are reasonably reliable. However, because there are no generally-accepted criteria (“true values") against which to evaluate such measures, it is seldom possible to ensure complete accuracy and some degree of imprecision is inevitable.

Random error in survey data can be dealt with in two ways: First, the researcher can exercise care during instrument design so as to avoid problems that commonly affect reliability (Fowler 1984). Second, the researcher can use statistical methods to estimate the size of the error component and increase sample size to obtain the desired statistical power (Lessler 1984). Although some researchers place greater emphasis on statistical testing, this is the more costly solution as larger samples (especially random samples) can be expensive and difficult to obtain. Much time and effort can be saved by ensuring that questions regarding similar phenomena result in similar responses and that differences in a respondent's answers at different points in time reflect a change in the phenomenon under investigation, rather than the effect of chance disturbances (Fowler 1984, Rust and Golombok 1989).

2.1.2. Nonrandom Error (Bias) and Valıdity. Random error is not the only type of error that can affect the measurement process—nonrandom error or “bias" is also possible. Bias exists when some unmeasured variable skews the results of the measurement process in a systematic way (Reynolds 1982). Because a biased measure can produce stable and internally consistent results, the presence of bias affects validity rather than reliability (Rust and Golombok 1989, Gable 1986). It is possible to have a highly reliable measure that does not accurately assess the phenomenon we set out to measure (Carmines and Zeller 1979). Consider what would happen if we administered a satisfaction survey to two groups of users, one of which was quite knowledgeable or had substantial experience with a system, while the other had little experience with that system. We might be measuring satisfaction among the experienced users, but the results could be contaminated or confounded as a result of a lack of competency or computer literacy among the inexperienced respondents. Thus, the validity of the results would be different for the two groups and we would expect different aggregate results if the questionnaire were administered to mixed samples that included different proportions of experienced and inexperienced users.

A number of types of validity are identified in the social sciences literature (see, for example, Crano and Brewer (1986) and Cook et al. (1990)), and all are important to survey researchers. However, the issue of bias introduced by question wording relates most directly to the notion of construct validity. Establishing construct validity involves ruling out alternative interpretations of what is indeed being measured (Cook et al. 1990). Unlike reliability, which relates to the measurement instrument itself and can be tested, construct validity is defined in relation to the theory being tested and can only be evaluated in that context (Bradburn and Sudman 988). Furthermore, evidence pertaining to the validity of a particular measure is accumulated over time based on its performance relative to researchers' theoretical expectations about how the construct or variable in question should behave. Thus, to say that a question or instrument produces a valid (and unbiased) measure of a particular construct implies a sort of consensus or agreement amongst researchers as to what is (and is not) actually being measured.

Although bias is relatively easy to identify in objective or factual data where a “true value" can be established or agreed upon, it is more difficult to assess in the subjective domain--the focus of most survey research. As Turner and Martin (1 984) point out, subjectivity can enter a survey in two distinct ways, leading to different interpretations of what constitutes bias. First, when the method of obtaining data about a given phenomenon calls for an estimate, impression, or “best guess" as a proxy measure of the true state of affairs, there is bound to be subjectivity involved in answering depending on how familiar individual respondents are with the facts. To the extent that there are systematic discrepancies between the respondents' answers and some objective reality, the results of such a survey are biased.

Second, some research deals with phenomena that are intrinsically subjective, such as attitudes, opinions, beliefs, and intentions. Here, bias must be defined, not in relation to some objective external reality, but rather in terms of the respondent's true internal state. Thus, the issue is whether or not people can accurately assess their own feelings. Many psychologists believe that people have less self-knowledge than researchers typically assume (Taylor 1975, Wicklund 1975, Nisbett and Wilson 1977, Oskamp 1991), i.e., that they come to know themselves and their reactions to outside stimuli through self-observation and inference, rather than through direct and intimate understanding of their own thought processes (see, for example, Ryle (1949) Bem (1972), Arkin (1980), Pryor (1980), Carver and Scheier (1981)). If an individual, when asked about his/her attitude or belief, has to stop and think—to construct a response based on whatever evidence comes to mind at the time—perceptual data may be subject to the same sorts of inferential errors and biases that are known to occur with more factual response data (Anderson 1974). In other words, if the responses produced by an intrinsically subjective survey are an artifact of the research process (e.g., the way the questions were asked or simply the fact that they were asked at all) rather than a reflection of “naturally-occurring" perceptions or feelings, the results may be biased or distorted in some way.

For simplicity's sake, our discussion focuses primarily on the first scenario where the research goal is to obtain a reasonably accurate picture of the objective state of affairs by surveying users. However, many of the issues are pertinent to the second scenario as well and should be carefully considered by researchers interested in understanding the purely subjective domain.

2.1.3. Response Effects. There are two general classes of errors and biases in survey data: those that result from improper sampling procedures (sampling errors) and those that result from faulty data collection procedures (nonsampling errors) (Bradburn and Sudman 1988). Nonsampling errors are typically attributable to poorly designed survey instruments; vague, inconsistent or misleading administration procedures; and respondent reactions to the research process itself (e.g., lying or giving socially-acceptable answers). This paper addresses only nonsampling errors associated with survey instrument design.

To avoid confusion, we refer to nonrandom or systematic error as “bias" and reserve the unmodified term “error" for random error. Although error and bias are conceptually distinct issues which affect the results of measurement in different ways, in practice, it can be difficult to distinguish between the two. What appears to be random error in one study may later prove to be systematic bias when the research model is expanded to include additional variables. In our discussion, we address error and bias as separate issues wherever a reasonable basis for distinguishing them can be articulated. Where the evidence is less clear, we use the more inclusive term “response effects" to refer to both random and systematic errors (Sudman and Bradburn 1974).

In surveys, both types of response effects can result from poorly-worded or ambiguous questions that elicit different answers from different respondents or from the same respondent under different circumstances (Carmines and Zeller 1979, Fowler 1984). Recognizing this, researchers sometimes ask the same question in different ways to increase the likelihood that the content domain of the construct has been adequately sampled and the chosen items are reliable (Gable 1986). However, when the average correlations between presumably-related items are low, the troublesome items must be eliminated and data are lost in the process. Thus, time and effort can be saved if researchers understand how wording can affect response consistency and take steps to eliminate or correct potentially confusing items during instrument design.

## 2.2. Users as Lay Epistemologists

When researchers seek to understand how users respond to information technologies, they begin with a theoretical framework describing anticipated relationships between the constructs of interest. Hypotheses are then generated, and a sample of data is collected to test them. Typically, some data are gathered by surveying a subset of actual or intended users. The responses are aggregated and statistically tested to evaluate the fit between the research model and the data. Finally, the relationships of interest are evaluated by applying a set of formal rules and statistical principles which specify the appropriate evidential basis for drawing conclusions about the larger population of users. Thus, the pursuit of scientific understanding involves the exercise of inferential reasoning and is guided by the rules of scientific inquiry.

When users respond to a survey question, they select from the range of possible answers the one they judge to fit best with the sample data provided by their own experience. Because people seldom have ready cognitive access to alí the details of their past experience, this sample is generally limited to instances or events which come to mind most readily (Tversky and Kahneman 1973, 1974). Thus, survey respondents act as lay epistemologists insofar as they draw conclusions based on a limited sample of data. Although their reasoning may be guided by theories of cause and effect, the researcher must exercise caution in assuming that the inferences respondents make necessarily conform to the rules of scientific reasoning. A growing body of evidence suggests that people's inferential judgments are often normatively inappropriate and/or biased, leading to conclusions that are difficult to reconcile with empirical reality (Dawes 1988). Thus, “people often look for the wrong data, often see the wrong data, often retain the wrong data, often weight the data improperly. often fail to ask the correct questions of the data, and often make the wrong inferences on the basis of their understanding of the data" (Nisbett and Ross 1980, p. 12).

Faulty reasoning may be especially troublesome when survey respondents have had little training in logical inference and little experience with the technology in question (i.e., when they have a very small sample of data on which to base their conclusions). But a recent review of the literature on heuristics and biases in auditors judgments (Smith and Kida 1991) suggests that training and experience reduce rather than eliminate faulty reasoning. Because experienced auditors are typically wel trained in sampling methods and logical inference, it is reasonable to expect that they would be less prone to errors in inferential reasoning. However, bias has also been found in the responses provided by professional auditors performing familiar, job-related tasks (Smith and Kida 1991). Thus, the issues identified in this paper may have greater relevance to studies involving novice users who lack formal training in logical inference, but they could also affect the reliability and validity of survey data gathered from other users.

Although the issues raised in this paper are pertinent to all survey research, we have chosen to focus on studies where users' judgments about a system serve as a basis for drawing conclusions about the effectiveness or acceptability of a particular system or technology. This domain was selected because of its historical importance in IS research (Doll and Torkzadeh 1988, Galletta and Lederer 1989) and the fact that most readers are likely to be familiar with the examples.

## 3. Judging a System and Its Effects

Researchers who study users' reactions to information systems have tended to focus on attitudes and behavior, largely ignoring the cognitive activities involved in the evaluation process (Melone 1990). This is a notable omission as our data collection methods typically require users to make judgments about the system in question and to respond in some fixed format (e.g., by marking a scale). Depending on the wording, the user's task may involve categorizing a system based on its attributes or explaining cause and effect relationships. Both categorization and explanation are cognitive processes involving attributions. Categorization involves the attribution of dispositional properties to a particular object/person, while explanation involves causal attribution or the attribution of observed effects to some precursor (Ajzen and Fishbein 1983). In this section, we describe the cognitive activities involved in each of these judgments as well as errors and biases that may occur due to the wording of items or response formats and the prior experiences of individual respondents. Suggestions are offered for improving the wording of questions so as to reduce the likelihood of undesirable response effects.

## 3.1. Categorization

3.1.1. Cognitive Processes. Much of the IS evaluation research relies on standardized instruments for assessing users’ perceptions of a system or, in some cases, the entire IS function. In these studies, users are expected to classify or categorize an information system (or IS services) along predefined dimensions based on how the perceived characteristics of the target system compare with the characteristics of other systems and/or prior expectations. To categorize an object means to consider it, for purposes of that categorization, not only equivalent to other objects in the same category, but also different from objects not in that category (Rosch and Mervis 1975, Rosch 1978). Thus, categorization can involve judging the probability that a system belongs to a certain class of systems (e.g., those that are reliable) or judging the attitudes or dispositions of a specific object, person or group (e.g., IS staff are helpful cooperative, friendly, etc.) (Ajzen and Fishbein 1983). In either case, inferential reasoning is required as respondents must infer general characteristics of the population in order to judge the likelihood that a particular observation is representative (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Nisbett and Ross 1980).

A number of popular user satisfaction instruments ask respondents to make categorization judgments, but they do so in different ways. For example, the Bailey and Pearson (1983) instrument asks users to rate characteristics of the system and the IS staff on seven-point semantic differential scales such as the ones shown below for measuring reliability:

3.1.2. Potential for Errors and Biases. Researchers who study the cognitive activity associated with categorization have found that classification judgments are often context-specific or socially-defined, rather than absolute and objectively-defined (Medin and Schaffer 1978, Roth and Shoben 1983). As a result, an individual's response to a particular item can vary depending on the contextual cues provided by past experience, the setting, the researcher, the instructions, or the item itself (Einhorn and Hogarth 1988).

3.1.2.1. Context. When an attribute derives its meaning from the context in which it typically occurs, it may not make sense to individuals who lack prior knowledge of the object or situation (Rosch 1978). For example, a user whose prior experience with computers has been confined to receiving batch reports may not understand “processing speed'’ or may not perceive speed as a salient characteristic of information systems. In cases like this, failure to supply adequate contextual background as part of the survey instrument can result in different interpretations of the questions and response data that are highly variable from one adminıstration of the questionnaire to the next (Turner and Fiske 1968, McCloskey and Glucksberg 1978, Bradburn 1982).

Contextual clarity is important because vague wording and unfamiliar terms can produce both errors and biases in the response data. Random error is likely to increase if an individual responds differently to the same item at different times due to difficulty understanding the wording of the item or applying the meaning to his/her own experience (Cronbach 1951, Kuncel 1981). A user who today interprets the word “system" to mean the organization's information systems may focus on a particular application when answering the same question tomorrow. Fortunately, this sort of variability can often be identified via a carefully planned pretest using a subset of actual respondents. Furthermore, poorly-worded or ambiguous questions that escape early detection can be identified using statistical techniques which evaluate interitem correlations (e.g., Cronbach's alpha) (Straub 1989). These procedures will not eliminate random error but can certainly reduce it to an acceptable level.

Problems in question wording can also lead to biases that are more difficult to detect. For example, experienced users might consider the processing speed of a particular system relative to other systems they have used in the past, arriving at a negative evaluation. Less experienced users might evaluate the speed of the same system against the manual procedures previously employed, judging the system to be an improvement. Bias like this is hard to detect via pretesting and relabilitv statistics since inexperienced users may, on repeated tests, respond in a consistently different way than experienced users.

Research on question wording suggests that the likelihood of context-related errors and biases can be significantly reduced by including definitions of potentially ambiguous words (e.g., system) and by specifying the population to which comparisons should be made (Trabasso 1982). To be effective, this contextual information should be as brief as possible. The question “Is this application relíable relative to previous methods of performing this task?" should result in more consistent response data than “Is the system reliable?" Furthermore, wording questions in the comparative form has been shown to produce more precise response data, possibly because the provision of an anchor point establishes a point of reference against which judgment can be made (Andrews 1989).

3.1.2.2. Frequency Expressions. Empirical evidence suggests that context-related response inconsistencies may be especially problematic when frequency expressions are incorporated in the item stem or the response categories (Pepper and Prytulak 1974), as is the case with the Doll and Torkzadeh (1988) instrument. Terms like “Usually,"“Sometimes," and “Rarely" have meanings which can vary depending on the situation and the individual's perceptions of what is normal in that setting—a factor which may change with experience (Pepper 1981). Thus, both errors (context effects) and biases (experience effects) mav be present in the response data.

The specific terms used to label response categories can also affect the response data. For example, Schriesheim and Schriesheim (1974) found that high frequency scales (ranging from “Occasionally" to “Very Often") and low frequency scales (ranging from “Seldom" to “Generally") tended to produce similar numerical scores. In other words, respondents who selected the first position on one scale (“Occasionally") tended to select approximately the same position on the other (“Seldom") irrespective of the labels provided. Thus, the position of a given frequency term relative to the rest of the scale may have more meaning than the actual label assigned to it.

Recent studies of the properties of numerical vs. verbal frequency scales indicate that numerical scales with precise percentage labels (e.g., 25% of the time) produce significantly better results than verbal scales. Percentage scales produce less variability on repeated testing as well as more precise and accurate responses when used to measure objectively verifiable phenomena (Newstead and Arnold 1989). Percentage scales are also more likely to achieve true interval scaling (Hartley et al. 1984).

Although frequency scales have not been widely used in IS research, one recent study (Davis 1989-Study 1) used a verbal frequency scale to measure self-reported usage of an electronic mail system. The response categories were labeled as follows:

Don't Use Use Less Than Use Several Use About Once Use Several At All Once a Week Times a Week Each Day Times Each Day

Without instructions as to what to count, this scale seems especially prone to ambiguity insofar as users may experience difficulty deciding what to count. Some respondents might interpret sending multiple messages at a single sitting as using the system “About Once a Day," while others might view this same activity as using the system “Several Times Each Day." In cases like this, frequency expressions can pose serious difficulties for those who attempt to interpret respondents' answers and may be especially troublesome when the results of studies conducted in different contexts are compared.

3.1.2.3. Relevance and Availability. Categorization research also suggests that the heuristics people use in making classification judgments are subject to certain biases and can produce answers that are inconsistent with the preponderance of evidence (Nisbett and Ross 1980). Because people think most naturally by analogy with past experience, an individual's response at a given point in time may depend on the experience or frame of reference that comes to mind most readily (Fischhoff et al. 1988). Furthermore, the availability of relevant dispositions sets limits on the kinds of hypotheses people actually consider (Tversky and Kahneman 1973, 1974). For example, if a user can recall instances in which a systems analyst helped him out of a crisis (available evidence), a dispositional inference about the helpfulness of IS staff is more likely to be made. But if the help involved fixing a flat tire (irrelevant evidence), the user is less likely to see the analyst's actions as relevant to the question that appears on a user satisfaction survey.

A user's ability to take relevant evidence into account when completing a survey is likely to be affected not only by the volume of evidence, but also by the individual's ability to recall such evidence at the appropriate time. Two factors that are particularly important in this respect are recency and salience or vividness. Studies of recency effects suggest that events stored in short term memory may be more available to the respondent than those stored in long term memory and thus may exert a disproportionate influence on the dispositional judgments that are made (Wyer 1981). For example, Fischhoff et al. (1978) found that respondents who first engaged in a decision-making task concerning the benefits of new technologies subsequently judged the risks associated with those technologies to be significantly lower than did those who worked on a problem focusing on the hazards associated with those same technologies. Thus, recently encountered problems may overshadow a long period of stability and lead to negative assessments of a system, simply because they are more easily recalled. If the researcher is interested in understanding how perception or behavior is affected by an unexpected or unusual disruption of service, salience might not be a serious concern. But if the objective is to obtain a reasonably accurate measure of overall satisfaction with a system, the salience of unusual or extreme events might skew the results, leading to an erroneous conclusion.

Other studies demonstrate that the emotional interest or vivıdness of a particular instance has a more pronounced effect on inferential reasoning than does the number of actual instances that can be recalled (Nisbett et al. 1982). Furthermore, when more thought is directed toward an object, attitudes become more polarized (Tesser and Conlee 1975, Wicklund 1975). Thus, questions that inadvertently focus the respondent's attention on an especially vivid occurrence can potentially affect both the evaluation of the object and the extremity of the response, whether positive or negative. If these results are generalizable, a single instance of poor system performance at a critical time is likely to be more easily recalled than routine events and can lead to an evaluation that is more negative than might normally be the case

Galletta and Lederer's (1989) results with respect to the test-retest reliability of the Ives et al. (1983) instrument provide some support for the hypothesis that recency and salience can affect users' dispositional judgments. In this study, subjects completed the IOB, discussed MIS successes or failures, and then completed the IOB a second time at the end of the two-hour period. Subjects who were presented with information about MIS failures had significantly lower scores on the second round of testing. Although Galletta and Lederer attributed the reduction in satisfaction scores to poor test-retest reliability, their results could indicate that the construct itself is unstable (Hawk and Raju 1991) or that the treatments had a systematic effect on the respondents in each group. In other words, we cannot reject the alternative hypothesis that factors such as the availability and vividness of new evidence introduced during the discussion, caused the respondents in each group to alter their dispositional judgments systematically and to view the performance of their own IS departments in a different light.

Because the heuristics people use and the data on which they base their judgments are outside the researcher's control, there may be times when researchers can do little more than acknowledge the possibility of errors and biases. But if the goal is to obtain a reasonably accurate picture of system performance as opposed to respondents' subjective impressions at a particular point in time, the effects of recency and vividness can be reduced by providing instructions that cue respondents to consider the normal state of affairs. For example, the questionnaire might be prefaced by a statement such as:

In answering questions about the system's performance, please consider how the system has operated under normal conditions during the last three months, ignoring isolated events and one-time problems you might have had.

If the respondents' attention is directed to a wider sample of experiences, the judgments should be more representative and more stable over time (Arkes 1991).

3.1.2.4. Lack of Knowledge and Nonattitudes Another source of instability in response data is closed format questions which force users to evaluate dimensions or take a position when they have little knowledge about an issue or have not developed a consistent, well-defined attitude, belief, or opinion. The most popular IS instruments require respondents to answer every question, providing no opportunity to select a “Don't Know" or “Not Sure" alternative. Although this is common practice in the social sciences, studies suggest that people who do not have an understanding or opinion about an issue may respond by simply guessing or randomly selecting a position on a scale (Converse, 1976–1977). Those with less experience in the domain of interest may be especially prone to guessing. If their responses are different on a subsequent administration of the test, it is difficult to know whether the inconsistency reflects true change (e.g., learning) or measurement error (Smith 1984, Turner and Martin 1984).

Research on this issue is still in the early stages; however, evidence suggests that allowing respondents to opt out if they lack the requisite knowledge tends to produce both higher validity and lower residual error (Andrews 1989). Allowing respondents to reply “Don't Know" can help to segregate those who do not understand, lack knowledge or have not formed an opinion. This is only a partial solution, however, as studies indicate that the number of“Don't Knows"may still underestimate the true number of “nonattitudes" or responses based on incomplete knowledge (Markel 1972, Smith 1984). Another approach is to identify and eliminate questions that are particularly troublesome for a majority of respondents by measuring both the attitude/opinion and its strength, since random response effects due to guessing appear to be related to low attitude strength (Schuman and Presser 1981).

## 3.2. Explanation

3.2.1. Cognitive Processes. IS researchers have recently begun exploring models of user behavior that take into account users' perceptions of the usefulness or instrumentality of a system or technology. To operationalize these constructs, instruments have been developed which ask users to draw conclusions about the relationship between system use and work-related outcomes. For example, Davis (1989, Study 1) and Moore and Benbasat (1991) constructed scales to measure perceived usefulness or relative advantage using the following items:

Using [a particular technology] gives me greater control over my work

Using [a particular technology] increases my productivity,

Using [a particular technology] enhances my effectiveness on the job

Here, users must do more than categorize the system; they must also develop an understanding or explanation of how the technology affects work-related outcomes.

From a cognitive perspective, both categorization and explanation begin with dispositional judgments of a target object; however, explanation is considerably more complex as the user must venture beyond mere description to arrive at a judgment regarding relationships between objects and/or events (Ajzen and Fishbein 1983). Explanation requires additional inferential reasoning (1) to detect covariation between early symptoms (causes) and later events (effects), (2) to estimate the magnitude of the covariation, and (3) to arrive at a conclusion about how hypothesized causes are linked to observed effects (Nisbett and Ross 1980). These inference activities are carried out with great speed and little effort, and without conscious or deliberate thought as to the heuristics used (Hogarth 1980). In other words, people apply what they perceive as commonsense logic in responding to questions about cause and effect relationships in their daily lives (Lloyd-Bostock 1983).

Efforts to tap into users' perceptions of causal relations may eventually move us closer to understanding users' behavioral responses, as well as the conditions under which new technologies are accepted or rejected by their intended users. The attributions people make are important as they can affect not only their expectations, but also their affective reactions and persistence following a performance failure (Russell et al. 1987). Furthermore, to the extent that users base their judgments on appropriate evidence, they may provide important information about the technology under investigation. However, the literature on judgment suggests that researchers should exercise caution in interpreting this type of response data as indicative of true cause and effect relationships or as evidence of technical/design problems. Because explanation begins with an assessment of the dispositional properties of a system, the errors and biases associated with categorization can occur here as well, establishing an inappropriate foundation for causal reasoning. But other biases can also occur when untrained observers draw conclusions about cause and effect relationships.

3.2.2. Potential for Errors and Biases. Understanding how people arrive at causal attributions or explanations for observed outcomes has been a dominant theme in the cognitive science literature in the last 25 years (Weary et al. 1989). In addition, Weiner and his associates (Weiner et al. 1971, Weiner 1986) have generated an impressive body of research focusing on the consequences of attributions or the effect attributional tendencies have on people's attitudes and behavior in achievement-oriented settings.

3.2.2.1. Attributions for Performance Outcomes. Numerous studies indicate that when causal relationships are ambiguous or unclear, people tend to attribute their successful performance outcomes to internal factors such as their own ability and effort, while unsuccessful performance outcomes are most often attributed to external factors such as unclear instructions, task difficulty, or bad luck (Streufert and Streufert 1969, Bradley 1978, Zuckerman 1979, Arkin et al. 1980, Kelley and Michela 1980, Riess et al. 1981).

The IS literature provides evidence that users are also prone to make biased attributions. For example, Lin and Ashcroft (1990) describe a controversial implementation project where, despite evidence indicating that a majority of errors in the data originated in the user departments, the users criticized the system and its developers, blaming the problems on external factors beyond their control. Hufnagel (1990) reports similar results based on an experiment where the relationship between system use and performance outcomes was ambiguous by design and payoffs were based on performance relative to other participants. Although outcomes were largely a matter of luck, participants with the highest scores attributed their outcomes to their own effort and understanding of the problem (internal causes); those with the lowest scores attributed their results to poor system quality and bad luck (external causes).

3.2.2.2. Prior Expectations and Attributions. Some researchers have linked expectancy theory with attribution theory (Feather and Simon 1971, McMahon 1973, Kovenklioglu and Greenhaus 1978), arguing that the way people evaluate their own performance may be a function of both the outcome and the prior expectancy of success or failure. Empirical evidence indicates that, where congruence exists between an individual's expectations and performance outcomes, attributions are more often made to factors that are stable across performances. Incongruence between expectations and achieved outcomes results in more frequent attributions to causes that are unstable across performances (Feather and Simon 1971, Frieze 1976). These results suggest that a user who expects to do well but does poorly on a system-aided task might attribute the disappointing outcome to a problem with the input or a “bug" in the system (unstable causes), while a user who expects to do poorly and then does so might attribute the same outcome to system quality (stable cause), whether or not these were the true causes of performance.

Although these attributional hypotheses have not been tested in an IS environment, there is some evidence that expectation-outcome incongruence can bias users’ evaluations of a system. For example, Ginzberg (1981) found that users were less satisfied with their information systems when the final product was significantly different from what they expected, regardless of whether the system was better or worse than anticipated.

3.2.2.3. Differences in the Attributions of Subgroups. As Barki (1990) and Hufnagel (1990) point out, attributional bias is a potentially troublesome issue for researchers who seek an objective assessment of system effectiveness or a method of evaluating a system's performance impact. Attributional bias may be somewhat less of a concern for researchers whose theoretical models deal with subjective or perceptual factors since people may act in accordance with their causal inferences, whether or not these inferences are based on appropriate evidence (Russell et al. 1987). But attributional errors and biases may be problematic here too if they are an artifact of the survey process—if the survey process leads users to draw conclusions they would not otherwise have drawn, even seemingly subjective measures could be affected. Unfortunately, the evidence suggests that, when asked to respond to questions they had not previously considered, people tend to search for possible causes, selecting the first plausible explanation they encounter (Jones and Davis 1965). Here again, the causes that most readily come to mind are likely to be those that are salient or available in short term memory (Taylor and Fiske 1975, Pryor and Kriss 1977). Thus, a questionnaire that focuses attention on the system as a possible cause of work-related events may trigger a disproportional number of attributions to that system. If so, responses may fluctuate over time as respondents think of other explanations (Mowday 1983), with a resulting increase in random error and instability in the measures obtained.

The validity and generalizability of a study's results can also be affected if other unmeasured variables have a systematic effect on the attributional processes of respondents in a given sample. For example, McDade's findings (reported in Mowday 1983) indicate that high-performing managers are more likely to take responsibility for undesirable outcomes than are low-performing managers, who are apt to blame factors outside their control. Thus, high-performing managers might see themselves as at least partially responsible for unsuccessful system-related outcomes, while lowperforming managers might blame the system and its designers rather than assume any responsibility themselves. If this were the case, the proportion of high versus low-performing managers in a sample might skew or confound the results of a particular survey.

Other research indicates that experts may make different attributions than novices (Lord and Maher 1990, Smith and Kida 1991). Studies have shown that expert computer programmers have better recall of relevant information than novices (Glaser 1984), and that the information experts can recall is less biased (Fiske et al. 1983). Furthermore, people with knowledge in a given domain seem to make different attributions than those who lack such knowledge (Mitchell and Kalb 1982). These findings also suggest that the relative expertise of users in a given sample could have a systematic effect on the response data when the questions asked require causal inferences to be made.

Evidence also suggests that the actions people can or cannot take in a given situation affect their attributions (Mowday 1983). For example, Duval and Hensley (1976) note that, in low-perceived choice situations, more external attributions are made for negative outcomes; but in high-perceived choice situations, the most plausible causal factor is the person himself. Consistent with these observations, Sogin and Pallak (1976) found that subjects who chose to perform a task that resulted in unforeseeable negative outcomes were more disposed to take responsibility for those outcomes than were subjects who were induced to perform the same task. These findings should be of interest to IS researchers, as many users may feel that they have little or no choice regarding the use of organizational information systems. Studies that mix volitional and nonvolitional users without testing for differences in their response patterns may produce results which are weak or difficult to reconcile with prior research due to differences in the attributional tendencies of these two groups.

These sorts of issues are an inherent problem in survey research since it is impossible to anticipate every variable that might influence users’ response patterns (Nunnally 1978). While the nature of the subgroup differences to consider will vary depending on the topic of study, IS researchers might find it useful to consider characteristics of the system and its users, as well as the task and organizational environment. Researchers who recognize potential sources of bias during instrument design can ask additional questions and use the responses to segment the data and test for between-group differences. While this will not eliminate faulty reasoning, it may help us reconcile our results with prior findings and explain low correlations between logically-related constructs. In addition, simply providing as full and accurate a description of the respondents as possible may allow reviewers and readers alike to judge the generalizability of the findings to other settings or subpopulations, and may eventually help to account for conflicting results across studies of the same or similar phenomena.

It might also be productive to begin a program of research using one of the more popular IS instruments to identify and measure differences in the response patterns of various subgroups such as experienced vs. inexperienced users, managers vs. clerical users, volitional vs. nonvolitional users, etc. Nonsignificant between-group differences would indicate that these variables do not have a biasing effect on users' evaluations of a system. However, if the differences were significant, the findings could serve as a guide for other survey research and might also help us to reconcile the mixed and inconclusive results produced by prior studies. Furthermore, identification of the sources of bias may provide the impetus for additional research investigating why factors like those cited above affect users’ judgments of information systems and technologies.

## 4. Judging the Likelihood of Future Actions and Preferences

Understanding how users characterize a system and explain its effects is an important step that moves us closer to understanding why systems are accepted or rejected by organizational users. However, to avoid costly system failures, practitioners also need to know how to recognize early warning signs so that corrective action can be taken as early as possible (Ginzberg 1981, Doll and Torkzadeh 1991). Thus, another goal pursued by IS researchers is the ability to predict the behavior of prospective users, with reasonable accuracy, based on a limited number of variables that can be measured in advance of that behavior.

Although it is possible to develop powerful predictive models that do not involve user judgments, a number of researchers have tried to predict system usage based on users' beliefs and expectations after a brief exposure to a new system (Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989, Mathieson 1991). Research involving users' predictive judgments differs from traditional evaluation research in a number of ways: First, the respondent pool is typically more diverse as both potential users and nonusers are included, which may increase the likelihood that respondents will vary in terms of their previous experience with seemingly-similar systems (frame of reference). Second, some research involving users' predictive judgments has been conducted in laboratory rather than field settings. However, as in field surveys, users are typically asked to complete a questionnaire concerning their likely future behavior toward or preferences for a particular technology or system. Third, the respondents generally have had little experience with the particular system or technology under investigation. Although they may have had a brief exposure to the system (via a demonstration or hands-on exercise), they must engage in probabilistic reasoning to respond to questions about how the system is likely to behave and how that behavior will affect their future work efforts. Fourth, users' evaluations of the system and its effects serve, not as a dependent variable, but rather as an independent variable that is expected to correlate highly with future behavior (usage).

## 4.1. Prediction

4.1.1. Cognitive Processes. Researchers interested in predicting acceptance of a new technology or intention to use a system have drawn on social-psychological theories that link prior beliefs and expectations about an issue/object with subsequent behavior involving that issue/object (Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989, Thompson et al. 1991, Mathieson 1991). In these studies, beliefs and expectations are elicited by asking prospective users to estimate the likelihood that system use will lead to specific (presumably favorable) outcomes. For example, several recently published studies included the following item stems

“It would take me (Much more/Much less) time to do the assignment with [the spreadsheet]

than to do the assignment with my calculator" (Mathieson 1991)

“Use of the PC will increase the amount of variety on my job" (Thompson et al. 1991)

“Using WriteOne would increase my productivity in the MBA program" (Davıs et al 1989).

From a cognitive perspective, making predictions like these requires complex judgments involving both dispositional assessment (categorization) and causal reasoning (explanation). However, prediction also requires the respondent to estimate the probability that relationships between cues/events observed in the past are good indicators of what will happen in the future (Hogarth 1980). Thus, a user who is asked to judge the likelihood that “The system will help me get my work done faster" might think about the characteristics or dispositional properties of the system relative to the method currently used (e.g., Is it reliable, accurate, easy to use?), the causal relationship between use of a system with these properties and the achievement of work-related objectives (e.g., Can system use affect the speed of task performance?), and the probability that using the system will increase his/her own speed of performance in the future. Not surprisingly, prediction problems can be both consequential and exceedingly difficult in their cognitive demands (Nisbett and Ross 1980).

In addition to predicting work-related outcomes, respondents are sometimes asked to make predictions about their own future behavior, for example:

My intention would be to use [the spreadsheet] rather than my calculator to do the assignment (Mathieson 1991).

Assuming Pendraw would be available on my job, I predıct that I will use it on a regular basis (Davis 1989).

Although people may answer questions about their future activities with less conscious thought, they are still making judgments about their own dispositions (I am favorably disposed to use computers/this system) and the possible causal relationships involved (Using this system will produce benefits I value), as well as predicting their reactions to similar events or objects in the future.

When the judgment process is decomposed in this way, it is easy to see how errors in assessment and explanation can affect predictive judgment. However, even when people have correctly assessed the situation and identified causal relationships between the relevant variables, they often make predictions about future cases that are inconsistent with the evidence (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Bell et al. 1988). Not only do they have trouble assessing the likelihood of future events and outcomes not under their direct control, but they are not even good at predicting their own behavior. Davis et al. (1989) report a correlation of only 0.35 between behavioral intentions and self-reported usage measured 14 weeks later. Even more surprising, a second measure of behavioral intention captured at the same time as the usage measure evidenced a correlation of only 0.63. These correlations are consistent with those reported by behavioral researchers in other disciplines. Understanding how such shortfalls in predictive accuracy occur may help to explain why the stated intentions of prospective users do not always correlate highly with their actual behavior and what can be done to increase the correspondence.

4.1.2. Potential for Errors and Biases. Responding to questions about one's future actions involves making predictions or guesses not only about the consequences of future actions, but also about one's future preference for those consequences. Neither guess is necessarily easy to make, and both are subject to errors in judgment (Hogarth 1980, March 1988).

4.1.2.1. Predicting Future Behavior. Because predictions of future consequences build on categorization and explanation judgments, erroneous inferences about a system's dispositional properties or its potential to affect the respondent's performance can bias prediction (Nisbett and Ross 1980). In other words, question framing (context), the availability of relevant evidence, the recency and salience of that evidence, and the causal attributions used to explain outcomes can carry forward, affecting predictive judgments as well. Studies involving prospective users of new technologies also appear to have some built-in bias as respondents must generalize from a very small sample of data.

Sometimes participants are required to make predictions on the basis of a short well-orchestrated demonstration or trial designed to point out (and make salient) specific features of the system (see for example, Davis (1989) and Davis et al. (1989)) By increasing the salience or cognitive accessibility of particular features, however, researchers may be “priming" participants to respond in ways that reflect the circumstances rather than their underlving beliefs (Fischhoff et al. 1988). If the researcher does not present both the positive and negative characteristics of a particular technology, it is likely that the respondents will have little understanding of the downside of system use. Thus, the research design can lead to overly-optimistic judgments of the system and its usefulness or ease of use—judgments that are not always borne out when respondents later attempt to use the system.

One promising method for overcoming the biasing effects of cognitive priming is a “consider the opposite" strategy (Lord et al. 1984), whereby respondents are asked to consider the evidence in favor of and opposing a particular course of action. For example, after a system demonstration, respondents might be asked to list reasons why they would and would not use the system. This procedure not only provides an opportunity for respondents to express any discomforting thoughts that occurred to them during the demonstration, but eliciting predictions of future behavior after such a “debiasing" exercise should also result in more accurate predictions since the “priming" effects of the demonstration would be counterbalanced to some extent' (Arkes 1991).

4.1.2.2. Predicting Future Preferences. Predicting one's own future behavior is always difficult because it is impossible to anticipate all the factors that might impinge on one's actions. However, as Shafer (1988) points out, the task of predicting and reporting future preferences is also difficult:

“(P)eople generally do not have ready-made preferences. When asked to make choices, they look for arguments on which to base these choices. The ways in which the alternatives are described can suggest arguments and therefore influence these choices. This means that people's choices in response to one query may be inconsistent with their choices in response to another query" (Shafer 1988, p. 203).

Expressing one's true preferences may be especially difficult when the decision involves making trade-offs between or among multiple, conflicting dimensions. Formal theories of choice tend to “treat such terms as values, goals, preferences, tastes, wants, and the like as either equivalent or as reducible to a single objective function with properties of completeness and consistency" (March 1988, p. 49). Yet, there is growing recognition that people may have clear and relatively stable preferences for some outcomes, but have difficulty ordering their preferences when forced to choose between alternatives that satisfy different preferences (Bell et al. 1988). Thus, it may be hard to choose between a system that ranks high in functionality but is very difficult to use and one that has less functionality but is very user-friendly. Furthermore, expressing the felt conflict between these alternatives on a questionnaire may not be possible. Although some argue that users who select a particular response category (e.g., “Strongly Agree") are also expressing their evaluation of or preference for the outcome in question (Davis et al. 1989, Doll and Torkzadeh 1991), respondents who indicate a strong preference for a system that has high functionality and is easy to use tell us nothing about the choice they would make if both preferences could not be satisfied simultaneously.

Changing the way we ask questions involving preferences for certain features could make the trade-offs clearer to respondents and elicit a more accurate picture of their relative preferences, which might correlate more highly with predicted or actual behavior. For example, having the respondent rate specific features and then rank them in terms of their relative importance would certainly yield more data about the individual's preference for one feature versus another. The approach used by Fishbein and Ajzen (1975), which involved assessing both the individual's belief about a behavior and his/her evaluation of the consequences, might serve as a model for doing this. Measuring the respondent's confidence or certainty with respect to future preferences (or actions) might also provide a basis for distinguishing between prospective users and nonusers. But these methods still assume that people know their own values and preferences and that direct elicitation is an unbiased method for converting subjective responses into “analytically usable expressions" (Slovic et al. 1988). Studies of people's expressed preferences indicate that they too may be “susceptible to the way the data are presented, and to implicit suggestions and other biases" (McNeil et al. 1988). Until more is known about how and why people's preferences change, IS researchers are probably well advised to try different methods of presenting systems to prospective users and to assess the sensitivity of respondents preferences when pilot testing their instruments.

## 5. Summary and Future Research Directions

Survey instruments are frequently used by IS researchers who are interested in understanding how individuals perceive and evaluate information systems and technologies. Whether custom-designed or standardized for more general use, the instruments used in collecting data are meant to elicit comparable answers from a majority of respondents and to provide a reasonably accurate assessment of the phenomenon being investigated. If they do not, it is highly unlikely that the relationships observed and inferences drawn from the data will be stable and reproducible over time.

Throughout this paper, we have identified reasons why these goals might not be achieved and suggested ways that survey researchers might address these concerns. The discussion has focused on IS research, not because it is necessarily more prone to errors and biases than other social science research, but rather because field-specific examples are of greatest interest to IS readers. However, in cases where the phenomena studied are technically complex and not well-understood by user respondents the likelihood of errors and biases may indeed be greater. By sensitizing researchers to the cognitive activities involved in responding to survey questions and shortcomings in users' inferential reasoning, we hope to encourage more careful instrument design and testing, and increased awareness of the effect question wording can have on the stability and comparability of survey results

Numerous suggestions are offered for improving the precision of survey questions and identifying potential sources of bias. Some of these suggestions can be readily implemented by survey designers. For example, more contextual data can easily be added to existing instruments, and questions can be worded in the comparative form so that respondents better understand the judgments they are asked to make. Verbal frequency scales can be replaced by numerical percentage scales to improve precision, and a “Don't Know" category can be included to allow less knowledgeable respondents to indicate their uncertainty. These refinements should reduce random error and improve the reliability of our measures.

The issue of potential biases requires more long-term study. To know whether or not survey questions produce valid measures of specific theoretical constructs, evidence must be accumulated over time in different settings and evaluated as a whole. When different studies using reliable instruments produce results that are inconsistent or difficult to reconcile, researchers should be alert to the possibility that the measures are biased in some way, i.e., that they elicit different judgments from different sub-groups. To deal with a specific bias, the researcher must first identify its source. In other words, additional theorizing about the construct in question is required to identify previously unmeasured variables that might have skewed the response data.

As Nunnally (1978) notes, “there are so many different types of potential response biases that only some general guidelines exist as to how to avoid them" (p. 657). Furthermore, only through experience with the instrument in question can the researcher hope to identify and address specific biases. Thus, our suggestions for addressing validity issues are necessarily general. We do, however, identify a number of factors that might lead to differences in the way different groups respond to IS surveys. In particular, factors like experience with automated systems, relative expertise, amount of discretion with respect to using or not using a system (volitional vs. nonvolitional use) and perceived control/responsibility for the outcomes could affect both perceptions of a system's salient characteristics and the causal attributions made by different groups of users. Empirical testing is needed to determine whether or not these variables do indeed have a significant biasing effect.

We urge researchers to begin exploring the extent to which these factors might affect responses to commonly-used IS questionnaires, as studies of this sort might give us clues as to what to expect with similar instruments in the future. The results may also be useful in resolving some of the inconsistent findings that have characterized past research in IS. While it is tempting to leave matters like these to trained psychologists or those who study survey methods, what we are likely to get from their efforts is more general advice as to appropriate question forms or response categories. As cognitive psychologists develop a better understanding of human judgment processes, they can alert us to possible sources of errors and biases. However, if we wish to know to what extent specific instruments do or do not suffer from the errors and biases described here, additional effort should be devoted to testing our instruments in different settings, experimenting with different ways of asking questions, and evolving more complete theories to explain users' reactions to complex information technologies.

Conducting high quality survey research is not easy because so many extraneous factors can affect the results in ways unanticipated by the researcher. But more attention to methodological issues and more explicit recognition of the limitations of survey research should increase the credibility of our findings and, ultimately, may lead to the development of more powerful explanatory models. Recognition of systematic effects in psychological research has led to the identification of new psychological theories (Fischhoff et al. 1988). Similar efforts in the IS domain may suggest new ways of interpreting and integrating past research findings and may also point the way to new theories relating users' perceptions and responses to information technologies.\*
