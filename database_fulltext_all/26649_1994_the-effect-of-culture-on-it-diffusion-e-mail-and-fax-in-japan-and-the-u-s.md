# The Effect of Culture on IT Diffusion: E-Mail and FAX in Japan and the U.S.

Detmar W. Straub

College of Business Administration

Department of Computer Information Systems

Georgia State University

Universıty Plaza

Atlanta, Georgia 30303

Few cross-cultural studies have investigated how firms diffuse new information technologies (IT). Still fewer have advanced a theoretical perspective on possible cultural effects. In a world moving rapidly toward corporate multinationalism, this oversight seems notable. As foreign managers locate plants and offices in the U.S. and as American managers establish foreign subsidiaries and offices abroad, it is important for these managers to know in advance as much as possible about the impact of culture on technological innovation. Japan and the U.S. are cases in point. Both have subsidiaries and actively market goods and services in the other country, far flung enterprises for which IT seems to be a natural coordinating mechanism. Yet while U.S. companies exploit the advantages of IT such as E-mail, Japanese firms do not. The Japanese, however, do utilize FAX extensively. Culture is one fruitful explanation for these differences. To examine these two markedly different cultures and the effect of these differences on technological innovation, a large Japanese airline and financial institution were chosen as representative Asian sites. The IT experiences of 209 Japanese knowledge workers are contrasted with those of 711 knowledge workers in comparable firms in the United States on certain dimensions. Using Hofstede's work on culture and social presence/information richness theory as grounding, it was hypothesized that high uncertainty avoidance in Japan and structural features of the Japanese written language could explain Japanese perceptions about new work technologies such as E-Mail and FAX. Furthermore, the theoretical conceptualization in the paper attempts to account for Japanese departures from the U.S experience. Results from empirical tests verified many, but not all of the predicted differences between Japanese and American knowledge workers. In general, cultural effects seem to play an important role in the predisposition toward and selection of electronic communications media. Surprisingly, responses to traditional media such as face-to-face and telephone were remarkably similar between cultures.

## Introduction

hile scholarly attention has been directed at understanding how U.S. firms diffuse new information technologies (Moore 1987, Brancheau and Wetherbe 1990, Hoffer and Alexander 1992), few cross-cultural studies have been directed toward learning how firms select, adopt, and use new information technologies (IT) and still fewer still have advanced a theoretical perspective on possible cultural effects. Ho et al. (1989) investigated cultural effects on GDSS use/outcomes, but their work is one of a handful of studies to date that specifically looks at micro-level connections between culture and information technology (IT) diffusion.

In a world moving rapidly toward globalization of markets and corporate multinationalism, this oversight is notable. As foreign managers locate plants and offices in the U.S. and as American managers establish foreign subsidiaries and offices abroad it behooves these managers to know in advance as much as possible about cultural impacts on technological innovation (Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen 1990, Couger 1986). In fact, more than half of the 300 SIM managers surveyed by Ives and Jarvenpaa (1991) felt that global IT issues were very important in their firms' future success. Such knowledge can make the difference between success and failure in implementing new IT. It can also increase the chances that IT investments are well thought out and do not result in lost opportunities to dramatically increase productivity.

From the standpoint of theoretical development, it is important to identify factors that will moderate key theoretical relationships (Ho et al. 1989). Cultural factors could have significant influence in many situations (Hofstede 1980, 1984, 1985, 1991). For these reasons, among others, it is imperative that we learn as much as we can about the effects of culture on organizations and, in particular, about the effects of culture on IT adoption and use.

Prior research has found that cultural differences between Asiatic and Western-English speaking cultures can be pronounced (Ho et al. 1989). Japan, therefore, was chosen as an initial Asian site for exploring the effects of culture on technological innovation. Japanese IT experiences are contrasted with those of the U.S., a prototypical Western industrialized nation. Both have subsidiaries and actively market goods and services in the other country, far flung enterprises for which IT seems to be a natural coordinating mechanism. Yet while U.S. companies exploit the advantages of IT such as E-mail, Japanese firms do not. The Japanese, however, do utilize FAX extensively. Cultural differences are one fruitful explanation. Specifically, it is posited that uncertainty avoidance in Japan and structural features of the Japanese written language are significant factors in explaining perceptions about new work technologies and departures from the U.S. experience.

To test research hypotheses, knowledge workers in Japanese firms using FAX and using or considering the use of E-Mail were sampled and compared with firms matched by industry and size in the U.S. Results from the empirical tests verified many, but not all of the predicted differences between Japanese and American knowledge workers. In general, cultural effects seem to play an important role in the predisposition toward and selection of electronic communications media. Responses to traditional media such as face-to-face and telephone were remarkably similar between cultures, however.

## Background

IT Diffusion in Japan

Within the Asian community, Japan represents an interesting counterpoint to IT adoption and use patterns in the U.S. As in the industrialized West, we would expect the diffusion of new technologies to derive from their relative advantage over other technologies (Rogers 1983) and we would particularly expect that the Japanese would be evaluating gains in productivity as an impetus for adoption. Yet, these advantages have not been sought out so much so that Japan lags the U.S. in the exploitation of IT by at least five years (Gillin 1990, Pearce and Valigra 1990).

While significant Japanese productivity gains have been made through productions refinements and reengineering of manual processes, little is known about the effect of advanced information technologies on Japanese productivity. In one of the few academic studies on the subject, Drummond (1990), however, found that while IT did not influence efficiency and effectiveness, it had a pronounced impact on employee morale.

Until quite recently, Japanese interest in IT has been limited to transactional systems (Nakanomyo 1990a, 1990b). But in 1990, the Ministry of Trade and Industry (MITI) began a national policy initiative which took a more aggressive posture on the use of IT for advanced applications in professional and managerial work. As a result of this initiative, it is possible that more advanced uses of IT will be considered in the future and the information will come to be viewed as an organizational resource (Sato and Horiuchi 1988).

Thus, in spite of a wide range of published works on the subject of Japanese productivity, management practices, and national policy initiatives, little serious academic attention has been devoted to studying the micro-level impact of IT on the workplace or on the varying patterns of technology choice.

## Diffusion of High Impact Communications Technologies

In studying cross-cultural IT diffusion, it is useful to examine technologies that demonstrate markedly different patterns so that hypotheses explaining these differences can be articulated and tested. From the management perspective, it is also valuable to study technologies that are positioned to achieve major organizational impacts.

Electronic communications technologies such as E-Mail and FAX meet both of these criteria. In the U.S., they have diffused throughout the workplace over the last decade and are predicted to be significant forces for change during the 1990s (Straub and Wetherbe 1989). They are thought to be especially valuable in the latter part of the millennium (Grover and Goslar 1993) as a means by which to streamline organizational communications, improve the substance of coordination, and dramatically increase productivity (Culnan and Markus 1987, Rice and Associates 1984, McKenney et al. 1986).

Moreover, E-Mail and FAX are well suited for study because their diffusion patterns have been so different in the two countries. In the United States, E-Mail has had a long, slow growth pattern, but has now been adopted by a significant number of firms. FAX, on the other hand, has had a rapid, exponential growth in just the last few years. Although FAX diffused very rapidly in Japan, E-Mail remains in an early growth period (Japanese Institute of Office Automation 1986, 1990). There has been speculation, in fact, that E-Mail will never diffuse widely throughout Japanese corporations.

What are viable explanations for these differences in diffusion patterns, apart from national policy initiatives (Gurbuxani et al. 1990)? There are at least two major cultural factors that can be offered by way of explanation. First, the Japanese cultural proclivity to avoid uncertainty by relying on traditional, information-rich channels for communication work against the acceptance of asynchronous, lean electronic channels such as E-Mail. Second, the nature of the Japanese language gives certain structural advantages to facsimile and image-based communications media.

## Theory Base

Cultural Avoidance of Uncertainty and Media Choice Theories

Japan differs markedly from the U.S. on all four of Hofstede's cultural dimensions —uncertainty avoidance, individualism, power distance, and masculinity (Hofstede 1980, 1984, 1985). Among these, uncertainty avoidance has the most direct bearing on preference for and use of communications media, and, therefore, is the dimension that will be discussed in connection with media choice theories.

Uncertainty avoidance is defined by Hofstede (1984) as: “the degree to which members of a society feel uncomfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity" (p. 83; boldface and italics added). The inclination of members of a culture to avoid uncertainty and ambiguity profoundly affects the way in which institutions are organized and managed (Hofstede 1984). Consistent with this logic, uncertainty avoidance will also likely affect the way in which individuals choose media for their communication tasks.

With respect to organizational communication behavior, researchers have also focused specifically on uncertainty and ambiguity as principal factors in predicting media use. Daft and Lengel's (1984) information richness theory posits that individuals choose media based on task characteristics of equivocality and ambiguity. To accomplish a particular task, individuals select the most appropriate communications channel, i.e., that channel which possesses the required level of information richness (Daft and Lengel 1984). Richness in a medium depends on the interactive nature of the feedback, channel type (e.g., body language, facial expression, and tone of voice), the personal quality of the source, and the ability of the interface to express linguistic innuendos. Face-to-face and telephone communication are informationrich channels because they can convey a wide variety of social cues to the listener (Sproull and Kiesler 1986). Electronic media such as E-Mail, FAX and mail services are lean channels because they carry only verbatim information and not the personal presence or force of the communicator. Daft et al. (1987) propose that communicators choose a medium by matching information requirements of the task, requirements such as the uncertainty and ambiguity of the task, to the information richness of the media. For tasks high in uncertainty and ambiguity, like negotiations and conflict resolution, it is thought that people will use face-to-face and telephone channels. For tasks low in uncertainty and ambiguity, like information memos and simple information exchange, leaner channels suffice.

An established theory which closely parallels this line of reasoning and has developed validated measurement scales is social presence theory (Short et al. 1976). Like information richness theory, this theory posits that media are chosen for specific types of interaction and for how well media “fit" task information requirements (Short et al. 1976). Media differ in social presence or “the degree to which a medium permits users to experience others as being psychologically present" (Fulk et al. 1987) and media like face-to-face meetings and telephone have been found to be high in social presence, whereas media like mail services, E-Mail, and FAX have been found to be relatively low in social presence. Socially-present media, thus, are known for their ability to “transmit information about facial expression, direction of looking, posture, dress and nonverbal cues" (Short et al. 1976, p. 65). The theory maintains that when a task is interpersonally involving and sensitive, as in the case of a negotiation or a conflict resolution, then media with high social presence like face-to-face interaction will be selected, but that, for a less sensitive task such as a straightforward information exchange, the social presence of the medium is not as important as its efficiency (Rice and Case 1983, Steinfield 1986, Sherblom 1988). ## Cultural Attributes of the Japanese Written Language

Because of their relatively high level of discomfort with uncertainty and ambiguity Japanese knowledge workers are predicted to view certain technologies as more information-rich and socially-present than American workers and that this disposition, in turn, will affect their choice, use, and evaluation of communications media. These cultural explanations provide one line of argument for how culture affects IT diffusion, but there is another aspect of Japanese culture that could be a significant factor That is the nature of the Japanese written language itself (Unger 1987)

Written expression in the Japanese language takes one of two forms: symbols written in kana and symbols written in kanji. Kana symbols, specifically hiragana and katakana, are approximately 100 in number. They are roughly equivalent to the Roman alphabet in that this relatively limited set of symbols can be used to express all of the sounds of the language. Japanese school children learn the kana in the early grades so as to be fully literate in kana within two years.

If kana were the only language system that Japanese knowledge workers had to work with, then the cultural impact of the language on IT use would very likely be minimal. There are, arguably, marginal differences between keyboarding 52 upper and lower case Roman characters versus the approximately 100 kana characters in Japanese. But kana is virtually never used by itself in business settings by adult Japanese. Noun, verb, and modifying root words in Japanese are written in another set of characters known as kanji. Formal Japanese written expression, thus, requires the use of kana and kanji symbols, illustrations of which, along with kana symbols appear in Figure 1.

Kanji characters, based on Chinese ideograms, are far more extensive than the kana. Japanese school children learn 2,000 of these symbols by the sixth grade and many thousands more to become proficient in their professional work. Thus, technology to support the written expression of Japanese knowledge workers must be capable of producing the 100 kana symbols, but also thousands of kanji symbols in regular use. Computer keyboards representing such a large number of characters are clearly impractical. And, interestingly enough, software remedies for creating electronic textual symbols may not be possible because users must still choose among many kanji options for representing every sound.⁴ Such difficulties, thus, are thought to place textual systems like E-Mail at a marked disadvantage (Boyd 1988).

While it is clear that the cultural attributes of the Japanese language may make E-Mail difficult to use, these same attributes have the opposite significance for FAX use. Facsimile machines digitize and transmit images rather than textual symbols, and, therefore, can readily transfer messages handwritten in Japanese. Although, as discussed above, high levels of uncertainty and ambiguity avoidance in Japanese culture would lead individuals to view electronic media in a less favorable light, the ability of FAX to convev subtleties through written images will likely more than compensate for other conceivable deficiencies. While technological advances such as expert systems that convert speech to kanji/kana (Rifkin 1991) could some day alter perceptiors of IT usefulness, the nature of the language at this time favors the use of FAX and disadvantages use of E-Mail for most tasks.

## Research Hypotheses

The discussion thus far suggests that cultural factors have a significant effect on several phases of the diffusion process that leads from evaluation through adoption use, and finally performance (Karahanna and Straub 1990, DeLone and McLean 1992, Goodhue 1992), and, thus, the research hypotheses are arranged according to these diffusion phases. The chain of events starts with tasks and communications options, as portrayed in Figure 2. Before a communications medium is selected, characteristics of the medium are assessed, both in an overall sense and on a task-bytask basis (Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989, Goodhue 1992, Karahanna and Straub 1990), as indicated by Diffusion Phase I effects. Following the determination that a medium “fits" a particular task, individuals form perceptions about the usefulness of that medium (Diffusion Phase II). These beliefs lead to choices of that medium and, aggregated across many such individual choices, to a pattern of usage (Diffusion Phase III). Finally, as indicated in Diffusion Phase IV in Figure 2, use of media are associated with productivity benefits (or decrements).

Diffusion Phase I hypotheses postulate that Japanese knowledge workers, who react more strongly to uncertainty and ambiguity than American workers, will perceive traditional communications media, face-to-face and telephone, to be higher in social presence (SP) and information richness (IR; when combined with SP, the acronym becomes SPIR) than U.S. workers. Because of their high level of discomfort with uncertainty and ambiguity, E-Mail, by the same token, will be viewed as low in SPIR. As an asynchronous, information-lean channel, FAX would ordinarily also be viewed as low in SPIR, but its differentiating ability to convey subtleties of the written forms of Japanese expression argue that perceived SPIR of FAX will be higher than that of American workers.

## Research Approach

The methodologies chosen to study cross-cultural effects of technological innovation included field interviews, questionnaires, and policy capturing. Such multimethods and sound theoretical underpinnings are recommended by Bhagat et al. (1990) to overcome the artifactual limitations of cross-cultural studies and to increase robustness of findings.

## Sample

Knowledge workers from four large Japanese and U.S. firms participated in the research. The firms, matched by industry and size, were in transportation and finance. A comparison of the firms, including sample characteristics, are shown in Table 2. The sample drew from workers across the managerial, professional, technical, and clerical ranks.

To deal with the possible confounding effect of variability in the organizational experience with E-mail, the large Japanese airline was selected precisely because it, like its American counterparts, was a mature E-mail user. Whereas PCs were more widely diffused in the American airline than in the Japanese airline, the primary means by which employees in both firms accessed E-Mail was through terminals, or PCs emulating terminals, linked to mainframes. According to the background information collected in the U.S. and Japan, this condition did not differ markedly across countries. Computers linked to mainframes, hence, have been used in the entire industry for many years to coordinate global sales and operations. Many managers and knowledge workers heavily use the computerized reservation systems and, for particular needs, can choose to supplement other forms of communication, i.e., media such as E-mail. In a certain sense, this environment was the most hospitable Japanese environment that could be selected for studying E-mail diffusion, and if there are patterns of perception and utilization that differ here from the American experience, then we could expect differences to be even more pronounced in other Japanese firms.

To control for exogenous variables that might confound results, sampled firms were selected so that they were as alike as possible with respect to the context within which they used FAX. For E-Mail, however, which is not widely diffused in Japan (Japanese Institute of Office Automation 1990), it was not possible to match firms in the financial industry. Nevertheless, knowledge workers in the Japanese financial industry were given an extended description of E-Mail technology and its capabilities so they would be able to evaluate, via the questionnaire, the SPIR of the medium. As a belief set that potential adopters are able to formulate prior to actual system use (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975),⁵ SPIR can be assessed by potential adopters on the basis of even brief descriptive information. Although it would have been desirable for respondents from the financial institution to have a more thorough understanding of the technology, and the lack of this level of understanding is still a limitation in the study, the presence of a second industry permitted testing for an industry effect in at least one phase of the diffusion process. For that reason, too, the sample was retained for data analysis.

FAX machines, which are discretionary purchases by departments in all cases, are available to users on each floor of all sampled corporate facilities. Since use of electronic media is not mandated, individuals may choose to use or not use the electronic media.

## Pretesting of the Research Instrument

The instrument was pretested before being administered to the sample group. Interviews were conducted with 32 knowledge workers in 17 different organizations across a broad organizational spectrum in the U.S. and Japan. Cooperating organizations in the U.S. came from manufacturing and financial services, state government, nonprofit organizations, and education. In Japan, interviews were conducted with machinery and tool manufacturing firms, computer firms, insurance, and education institutions. The purpose of the semistructured interviews was to refine constructs and develop measures for each construct on the questionnaire. One of the important outcomes of the pretesting process was the qualitative validation of constructs and measures (Straub 1989). Likert scales were pretested for their ability to measure key variables.

To ensure that the instrument was compatible with the unique cultural characteristics of Japanese and Japanese firms and that bias and artifacts were minimized (McHenry et al. 1990), five months of on-site research was carried out in total during 1990 and 1991. The instrument was translated into Japanese by a business school professor, a native speaker with significant experience in translating academic, business books in the information systems arena. The translation was reviewed by another business school professor whose native tongue is English, but who speaks, reads, and writes Japanese fluently.

## Creation of Policy Scenarios

Another important outcome of the pretesting was the development of eighteen policy capturing scenarios that measure respondent choices of certain media in certain situations. Since pretest interviews indicated that respondents found it difficult to accurately estimate face-to-face meetings or telephone calls in an average day or week, policy capturing scenarios were used for Diffusion Phase III measures for traditional media. Policy capturing, also known as “scenario creation," allows researchers to gather evaluations from a large number of respondents as they evaluate a set of feasible real-world alternatives presented by the researchers. Variants of this use of scenarios can be found throughout the management literature, including Fedor et al. (1989), Janis and Mann (1977), Ashford and Northcraft (1989), and Cosier (1983), as well as in the IS literature (Daft et al. 1987). Since one of the objectives of the study was to assess media choice across a wide range of respondents in the workplace as well as a wide range of real-world task situations, policy capturing seemed to be an appropriate methodology.

Task scenarios, which cued respondents on generic managerial and administrative tasks such as scheduling, budgeting, negotiations, personnel issues, coordinating, and control, were based on task types in the literature (Mintzberg 1973, Yukl 1987, Page 1985) and activities that typified knowledge work in both Japanese and American firms. Item 23 illustrates the form and format of the scenarios, which were randomly ordered in the final, administered form of the questionnaire.⁶

Given your current environment, please select the medium or media you would use. If you would use more than one, please rank order with “1" as your first choice.

Cronbach alphas for the social presence scales for E-Mail (0.83) and FAX (0.84) were found to be acceptable by the standard proposed by Nunnally (1967). ## Tests of Rival Hypotheses

To ensure that E-mail findings were not moderated by factors other than the cultural factors specified here, several rival hypotheses were tested. It is possible, for example, that (1) lack of prior experience with E-mail, and/or (2) lack of keyboarding skills account for differences in results. These factors would establish an experiential basis for varying perceptions of Japanese knowledge workers, an explanation which may or may not itself be related to culture.

While it is highly desirable to test these alternate explanations against the data, there are prima facie reasons for believing that these explanations would not turn out to be significant, as argued above. The choice of the large Japanese airline was based partly on the fact that this organization, like its American counterparts, was a mature user of E-mail. Since this environment was very likely the most hospitable environment that could have been chosen, then differences from the American experience could be predicted to be even more pronounced in Japanese firms not yet using E-mail.

To serve as measures of the independent variables for the rival hypotheses, four surrogates were used. One item on the questionnaire measured the extent to which knowledge workers found their computer software difficult to use to prepare messages for electronic distribution. This and a second measure of how easy the E-mail system itself was to use are thought to capture elements of both prior experience with E-mail and keyboarding skills. Since these experiential components are also likely to be a generational phenomenon as well as a function of formal education in computers, variables for age and number of years with the firm were also entered into the regression equations.

Appendix E presents the results of regressing all diffusion phase dependent variables on the four surrogate independent variables. None of the rival explanations approaches significant levels, thus tentatively ruling out these factors.

## Discussion

A main contention of this paper has been that two aspects of Japanese culture— uncertainty avoidance and complex written language symbols—predispose Japanese knowledge workers against E-Mail, but in favor of FAX. Empirical evidence suggests that these cultural effects are real, especially for E-Mail. In spite of a strong interest on the part of Japanese managers in company communications networks (Senn 1990 Japanese Institute of Office Automation 1990), this study has found that Japanese knowledge workers rate E-Mail lower than other media, and in all phases of the diffusion process. This finding suggests that there may be resistance to the introduction of LANs for the purpose of enhancing communications. This cultural inclination suggests that the Japanese view E-Mail as poorly suited for much intragroup communication, perhaps explaining why this technology does not appear to be growing in Japan. Since U.S. firms are experiencing performance gains from E-Mail use (Straub and Karahanna 1990), a further implication might be that much more extensive U.S. utilization could lead to productivity gains vis-a-vis Japan.

The ambiguity in the FAX results are surprising. FAX was perceived as being significantly higher in medium SPIR by Japanese workers, but not perceived as being more useful. FAX usage rates were likewise not significantly different between Japan and the U.S. even though Japanese workers saw more productivity benefits to this form of electronic messaging. With the exception of number of FAXes received per week, all effects were in the predicted directions and it is possible that the predicted cultural effects in the case of FAX are being offset by a greater cultural acceptance in the U.S. than current theories would suggest. Alternatively, it is possible that Japanese linguistic factors are not as powerful in their motivating force as the theoretical arguments predict.

Overall, the study affirms that cultural effects can moderate theoretical relationships in the IT diffusion process. This implies that researchers should include culture as a key variable in studies which draw on an international sample base. The present study verified many predicted differences between Japanese and American cultures, but to generalize across larger cultural units such as region, it will be necessary to sample from numerous countries in each region and to make no untested assumptions about regional character.

The results of this study tentatively suggest actions by managers attempting to introduce new technologies such as E-Mail into Japanese firms. For Japanese managers, decisions to invest should be made after a careful examination of the social impacts of the technology. It may be that the benefits of the technological innovation will not offset the burdens of cultural change and a difficult and prolonged adaptation. Multinational firms operating in Japan and countries with similar cultural predispositions or patterns will need to consider how to coordinate their international sites without doing violence to the cultural needs of their employees.7 It may be possible to springboard from the present Japanese inclination toward FAX to an argument for the value and productivity benefits of E-Mail. PC-based FAX technology is now making strong gains in the marketplace and adoption of this technology might make the transition to E-Mail an incremental rather than radical change (Rogers 1983).

By the same token, Japanese managers in the U.S. should realize that E-Mail can be an effective tool in this culture. In all phases of the diffusion process, American knowledge workers demonstrate a strong proclivity for E-Mail as a communications medium. Encouraging the effective use of both E-Mail and FAX in appropriate situations and exploring the usefulness of complementary technologies such as voicemail makes good business sense. Japanese managers should expect to see roughly equivalent use of FAX compared with their Japanese employees

Finally, with respect to traditional media, the cultures do not appear to be radically different. Managers should not expect patterns of communication utilizing face-toface meetings and telephone to vary significantly between cultures. This result is very surprising in that it suggests that managers can structure group work and social interaction through these media without regard to cultural differences. This in no way implies that the nature of the social interaction is similar between cultures, but at least the form of the interaction appears not to be a complicating factor. That is, Japanese and American workers may use the same media form just as frequently, as in face-to-face meetings, for example. Hence, while the form of the media may not differ between the cultures, the content of the meeting may be very different. Meetings in Japanese firms are often held for team building and information sharing purposes (Ouchi 1981) rather than for decision making, as in American firms.

## Accounting for Rival Explanations in Future Cross-cultural Research

While cultural effects do seem to explain reasonably well the different patterns of IT diffusion observed in this study, no one study can definitively rule out every conceivable rival hypothesis (Dubin 1976). Moreover, there are at least three conditions within the telecommunications environment that need to be considered by cross-cultural researchers in order to make the strongest possible case for the dominant, or at least contributing effect of culture on the diffusion of IT. Whereas these sources of extraneous variation have either been controlled or judged to be nonproblematic in this study, they do pose a risk to the ongoing credibility of our work and should be handled in future research, as completely and convincingly as possible.

In the first place, there is a risk that, apart from culture, IT accessibility will affect beliefs and attitudes of usefulness as well as actual use of a system (Culnan 1984. 1985). In the case of Japan, for example, the lack of accessibility to PCs in Japanese firms (Japanese Institute for Office Automation 1990, Sato and Straub 1993) could explain relatively lower scores in various phases of the diffusion cycle, especially when the software being studied runs exclusively on PCs. In the present study, this factor was not deemed to be a threat since E-Mail was accessible via mainframe terminals, which were sufficiently diffused across the two cultures. If, however, there are differences in the technological environments between the countries, then the diffusion of requisite hardware of software is an important covariate.

In the case of low diffusion of requisite IT, there is a second and related threat. Researchers must decide whether the circumstances of sharing computer access will itself mitigate against favorable attitudes toward and use of an electronic medium. If privacy is an important cultural value, then the requirement of shared use could severely affect all phases of the IT diffusion cycle. In Japan-U.S. cross-cultural studies, for example, it can be argued that this may not be a problem although there is widespread sharing of computers in Japanese offices (Sato and Straub 1993) and a concomitant inability of Japanese workers to compose and send messages in private While the U.S. experience is a marked contrast in that nearly every U.S. knowledge worker has a computer workstation for his or her exclusive and private use, this difference may not be critical since the Japanese culture does not place a high value on individualistic issues such as privacy. Hofstede's work (1984, 1991), in fact, shows that Japan and the U.S. are at nearly opposite ends on the individualistic-collectivist index.

When studying countries in which the diffusion rates of certain IT differ markedly, researchers should attempt to disentangle, through whatever means possible, the reciprocal effects of low IT diffusion rates and low proclivities to respond to and use IT. Matching industry and firm characteristics across cultures is a step in the right direction, but even stronger cases for the effect of culture will result from partialling out variables that reflect the specific technological context.

With respect to the third threat, researchers should ensure that differences in IT diffusion are not the result of a lack of a crıtical mass of users. Markus (1987) has argued effectively that communications technologies require a sufficient base of electronic correspondents, i.e., a “critical mass," for the medium to be effective. In the present study, this factor was controlled by choosing airlines in both countries who were mature in E-Mail use. Another, probably preferable manner of handling this threat is to poll respondents as to how many of their regular correspondents are available through the electronic media. Such empirical controls will give greater assurance that users and potential users think the system can meet their basic need of connection to a functioning network.

The spectre of rival hypotheses always looms large in feld work (Stone 1978), even when there is an attempt to control for extraneous variation, as in this study. Thus, while there is a distinct possibility that certain causal effects are unaccounted for, it was felt that the topic was of such importance that research should proceed even if the circumstances for ruling out alternative explanations were not ideal. Cook and Campbell (1979) espouse this general point of view when they argue for a practical “critical realist" proof of causality which overcomes the deficiencies of a single study through streams of research in which researchers triangulate via alternate study methods and replicate with variation across settings, populations, and times

## Technological Advances and Future Studies

Advancements in technical capabilities of the electronic media are also very likely to affect future research. As electronic media become more sophisticated, gaps between E-Mail, FAX, and other electronic technologies will disappear and the technologies will merge (Craig 1991). In practice, E-Mail is currently used for memoing and transmitting machine-readable textual documents, while FAX is being used for transmitting exact images, such as signed papers, short documents, drawings, and graphs. Even though the technical capability to incorporate image scanners into workstations and use the electronic messaging systems to send facsimiles through E-Mail packages already exists, this feature has not been widely disseminated. So, for the moment and perhaps for as long as a decade, differences in how these technologies are viewed and used will be relevant.

What we might expect to see when a technological merging does take place is a new medium that meets a variety of needs and one, which from the perspective of Japanese workers, compensates for the deficiencies of each medium. At that point, the electronic medium would no longer be subject to the same cultural biases and there should be much broader usage.

Clearly, globalization is rushing forward at a rapid pace. It is imperative, therefore, that knowledge about the effects of culture on the way organizations function is communicated to managers. This project has attempted to move in that direction and should provide a baseline and data point to help researchers and managers alike determine whether findings about IT diffusion in one culture are generalizable to other cultures.\*
