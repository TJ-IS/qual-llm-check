# Computer-mediated Communication for Intellectual Teamwork: An Experiment in Group Writing

Jolene Galegher

Department of Englısh

Carnegie Mellon University

Robert E. Kraut

Puttsburgh, PA 15213

School of Computer Science

Carnegie Mellon University

Pittsburgh, PA 15213

Contingency theory predicts that using computer-mediated communication to accomplish complex collaborative work will be difficult, especially for tasks that require interactive, expressive communication. This proposition was examined in an experiment in which 67 three-person groups of MBA students completed two collaborative writing projects under either Computer Only, Computer + Phone or Face-to-Face communication conditions. The effects of these manipulations on group processes and performance were examined using data obtained from questionnaires and scores on the projects themselves. Although communication modality did not affect project performance, being restricted to computer-mediated communication made completing the work more difficult and diminished the participants' satisfaction with their work and with the other members of their work groups. The results also provide partial support for the idea that tasks that require more intensive communication, such as project planning, were more difficult than those that can be completed more independently, but this premise was not consistently confirmed. Taken together, these findings tend to confirm the contingency hypothesis regarding the difficulty of accomplishing work that involves ambiguous goals, multiple perspectives, and information that is susceptible to multiple interpretations without an interactive multiperson communication medium, such as face-to-face meetings. However, the results also suggest that modifications of contingency theory are required to incorporate the evidence that individuals can, to some extent, adapt to restricted communication channels. Further research designed to examine patterns of adaptation under various task/technology combinations is recommended.

Collaborative writing—Computer-mediated communication—Contingency theory—CSCW

n most kinds of work, success depends on matching talents, tools and procedures to the demands of specific tasks and projects. The development of new information technologies designed to aid communication and task performance has raised new questions about fit, and stimulated new research programs intended to assess the appropriateness of these technologies for tasks of many different kinds. In the investigation reported here, we assess the effects of a particular information technology computer-mediated communication—on the experiences of people involved in a complex collaborative writing project. The purpose of this investigation is to determine how well the opportunities and constraints provided by computer-mediated communication fit the demands of this kind of work, when compared to face-to-face communication.

In a project of this sort, success is ordinarily viewed as multidimensional, involving not only nstrumental achievements, but also individual satisfaction and the maintenance of the group as a performing unit (Hackman 1983, McGrath 1990). This distinction between the dimensions of small group effectiveness suggests that a comprehensive assessment of the impact of computer-mediated communication should also be multidimensional. Thus, our analysis focuses on multiple outcomes, including the task activities of group members as they carry out their work, the resulting performance, and the attachment of group members to each other.

Our interest in the effects of computer-mediated communication is both theoretical and practical. Communicating by computer has the potential to alter ordinary patterns of communication and task performance in many ways. Identifying those effects would enable us to expand and update existing theoretical descriptions of the relationship between patterns of coordination and communication and workgroup outcomes. On the more practical side, computer-mediated communication could reduce or eliminate the expense and inconvenience associated with distributed work. But, although research about its effects has grown rapidly in the recent past, there are still few studies that have examined sustained, project-oriented teamwork of the sort that is important in most real-world organizations. Furthermore, studies that do look at longer-lived project groups typically involve too little control to identify the variables of interest (Hiltz 1984). Thus, a rigorous analysis of the effects of computer-mediated communication in a sustained project seems warranted

To make possible such an analysis, we have chosen to study groups engaged in collaborative writing projects. By this, we mean projects in which the group must formulate a response to a problem and create a document that communicates that response. The rationale for this choice is, again, partly theoretical and partly practical. On the theoretical side, there are significant group process problems involved in completing the project. These include negotiating the meaning of the facts with which the group is presented, establishing consensus as to an appropriate solution, dividing tasks among group members, coordinating the execution of work, and confronting questions of authority within the group as it attempts to insure both on-time completion and product quality.

The practical importance of research involving projects of this sort stems from their frequency in the world of work. Although definitions of collaboration differ, research by Couture and Rymer (1989), Ede and Lunsford (1990), and Faigley and Miller (1982) has demonstrated that collaborative writing in business and other professional contexts is frequent, and that it plays an important role in organizational activity. Recognizing the importance of collaborative writing and the potential to enhance this process technologically, some investigators (Forman 1991; Horton et al. 1991) have begun to examine the use of computer support for these activities. The present study differs from the Forman study in that it is experimental, rather than observational, and from the Horton et al. study in that it involves a more complex project carried out over a longer period of time; this project also differs from these earlier investigations in the specific nature of the computer support provided. In the following sections we review the theoretical foundations for our investigation.

The Interrelationships of Communication Modality, Task Type and Group Performance

Assessing the effects of information technology on the execution and outcome of particular tasks requires a conceptual model that identifies important task dimensions and the theoretically relevant features of particular information technologies. Several such models have been proposed. Although they differ in emphasis and in the terminology they employ, they have in common the idea that work on open-ended problems requires interactive, expressive communication. Interactive communication involves the ability to exchange information rapidly, adjusting messages in response to signals of understanding or misunderstanding, questions or interruptions from one's communication partners. Expressive communication involves the ability to convey ideas fully, presenting not only their semantic content, but also using intonation and gestures to reveal intensity and physical artifacts to clarify or amplify meaning (Kraut et al. 1992).

One example of a model that embodies this linkage between task type, a technique or medium for managing the task, and task outcome is structural contingency theory (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967). According to this formulation, the critical task variable is uncertainty and the task management process is a coordination practice of some kind. Van de Ven and his colleagues (Van de Ven et al. 1976), as well as other researchers working in quite different settings (Tushman 1978, 1979) have found that when task uncertainty is high, people engage in higher levels of informal communication to coordinate their work than when task uncertainty is low. This finding has been interpreted as evidence that coping with uncertainty requires a level of mutual adjustment within and between work groups that is unattainable when individuals are relying on formal policies and procedures or other less flexible means of coordinating their work.

The conclusion that expressiveness and interactivity are required for communication about complex problems can also be derived from the results of research that explicitly examines the effects of variations in communications media (Short et al. 1976). In this research, the crucial task variable is the extent to which the task requires negotiation over meaning or resources, and rather than coordination mechanism, the variable linking task type to task outcome is the social presence of the communication medium. Social presence is the extent to which a communication medium elicits the same psychological experience as unmediated, face-to-face communication. In our terms, media that provide social presence do so because they permit interactivity and expressiveness. To test their version of the contingency hypothesis, Short et al. (1976) manipulated communication modality and task type, and observed the effects of these variations on task outcomes. Although complex, the results of these experiments were congruent with the correlational findings of Van de Ven et al. (1976) When tasks involved straightforward information transmission or problem-solving on cooperative tasks, the outcomes were unaffected by variations in communication modality. But when the task involved conflicts over resources or point of view, variations in communication modality had significant effects. In particular, carrving out tasks involving conflict via communications media that were low in social presence produced negative outcomes.

These observations anticipate a more recent analysis of the contingencies between task characteristics, ways of managing work, and performance. In the model proposed by Daft and his colleagues (Daft and Lengel 1986, Trevino et al. 1990), equivocality is the task variable, and media choice is the “task management" variable linking task type to task outcome. According to this analysis, richer communications media permit communicators to modify their messages in response to cues from their partners, to amplify their messages using nonverbal and paralinguistic channels, to speak naturally and colloquially and to direct their messages to a particular individual or group. For our purposes, these attributes can be summarized in terms of interactivity and expressiveness. Examinations of the relationship between media richness and media choice have shown that more equivocal tasks are typically associated with high levels of direct, informal, face-to-face, i.e., interactive and expressive, communication (Daft and Macintosh 1981, Daft et al. 1987). According to Daft and Lengel (1986), people understand media differences in carrying capacity and, when allowed to choose, select a communication modality that maximizes the fit between the richness of the information they seek to convey and the richness of the information that particular communication modalities can carry.

Recall, now, that we have described collaborative writing as involving negotiation about the meaning of facts, a demand for consensus as to an appropriate solution, division of labor based on concerns for fairness and quality of work, coordination of individual contributions, and resolution of questions about authority within the group. Whether labeled uncertain, conflictful or equivocal, such an enterprise clearly involves considerable cognitive and interpersonal complexity. Further, comparative ratings of various communications media have shown that computer-mediated communication is generally perceived as low in terms of its ability to support interactive, expressive communication (Zmud et al. 1990). Thus, taken together, the conceptual analyses and previous research described here predict that using computer-mediated communication for collaborative intellectual work will be quite difficult. This prediction stems from the mismatch between the properties of the medium and the nature of the tasks involved, a mismatch that may manifest itself in several ways.

First, the lack of interactivity associated with asynchronous computer-mediated communication may reduce the efficiency of the sequential communication needed to clarify complex ideas. Because communicators cannot immediately assess their partners' understanding, they cannot easily tailor their utterances to insure comprehension. T'he absence of interactivity may also lead to uncertainties about the activities of others. To keep the flow of information moving, individuals must check their computer accounts regularly and must also signal receipt of new messages to the sender because the sender cannot observe directly whether the message was received or acted upon. Second, computer-mediated communication reduces expressiveness, not only in the familiar sense of expressiveness as the communication of nuances of feeling, but also in the sense of expressiveness as the full expression of an idea, including perhaps, its representation in text, drawings, diagrams or marginal comments. Because most commercially available computer-mediated communication systems do not permit users to view and manipulate a shared document or other artifact, or to coordinate their conversation with attention to specific parts of the object, their ability to express their ideas fully is limited.

It is important to remember, however, that an extended project is likely to involve a number of tasks, and that these tasks may differ in the extent to which they require interactive, expressive communication. Thus, the impact of computer-mediated communication may depend on the specific task the group is working on at a given time. Typically, long-term projects involve several loosely bounded phases (Kraut et al. 1987-1988, Kraut et al. 1990, McGrath 1990). At the outset, the participants may have only the vaguest ideas of what they should be trying to achieve and how to go about it. Thus, project initiation—settling on a shared interpretation of the problem, defining goals and planning work—is likely to require intensive communication. Once the group members begin to execute their plans, communication between group members is likely to shift from lengthy, highly interactive sessions to brief conversations in which group members exchange progress reports or otherwise coordinate their separate activities. Finally, as the project moves toward completion, the participants enter an integration phase during which they may again require extensive interaction to weave together the results of their individual work.

This model of the temporal sequence of communication between project members implies a differential impact of computer-mediated communication across time. Specifically, we would expect that the negative effect of computer-mediated communication on task performance will be most dramatic during the most communicatively intense phases of the project. Applied to the example of collaborative writing, this means the deleterious effect of computer-mediated communication should be greatest for planning—during which group members are establishing a sense of direction, defining goals, and agreeing on procedures for carrying out their work—and for revision, when group members are reining and integrating independently created drafts. Thus, in addition to our prediction that computer-mediated communication will adversely affect work on these projects, we expect that the strength of that effect will vary with the communication requirements of project components, with the most negative impact occurring when group members are planning and revising their work. Finally, because the group's achievements depend on its ability to carry out its tasks, we expect the documents produced by groups whose communication is restricted to be inferior to those created by groups that are able to communicate faceto-face.

## Communication Modality and the Social Experiences of Workgroup Members

As noted above, the outcomes of collaborative work include not only the group's performance and the efficiency of the processes by which that performance is achieved, but also the affective experiences of the group members. Although not necessarily correlated with performance in the short run, these affective experiences can be important determinants of the group's longevity and long-term productivity (Hackman 1983). Thus, both for the sake of creating or maintaining a benign work environment, and for the sake of preventing any detrimental effects of negative social experiences on work performance, it is important to consider how the reduction in interactivity and expressiveness imposed by computer-mediated communication might affect the social experience of group members.

In the Short et al. (1976) studies comparing interactions carried out face-to-face with those carried out over a video channel and an audio-only channel, participants in the more restricted communication conditions typically rated their interaction partners more negatively. In addition, assessments of subjects' metaperceptions— their sense that their communication partners understood them and that they understood their partners—revealed a greater sense of uncertainty about the quality of their interactions. One interpretation of these results is that the reduction of behavioral cues and the resulting difficulty of assessing a communication partner's reactions preclude the development of smooth social interactions. Although these studies do not involve computer-mediated communication, they suggest that the social effects of using electronic communication for group work may be problematic

Because computer-mediated communication is even leaner, even lower in social presence than the audio and video modes examined by Short et al. (1976), we should expect to observe similar, or perhaps even greater, disruptions in patterns of social interaction. A recent summary of research on the effects of using computers to communicate (Sproull and Kiesler 1991) suggests that such disruptions do. in fact, occur with some frequency. While acknowledging the advantages of computer networks for establishing connections across time and space, and for reducing isolation and compartmentalization within organizations, these authors point out that computer-mediated communication nonetheless involves a substantial reduction in the social cues that regulate and facilitate communication. This reduction leads to more socially inappropriate behavior and to a greater focus on the task at the expense of focus on the audience, the target of the communication.

Based on these observations, then, we anticipate that using computer-mediated communication for a task such as collaborative writing will interfere with the development of positive social relationships between group members. Because communicating by computer makes it more difficult to share jokes, to soften the presentation of critical feedback, and to interject irrelevant, but entertaining, comments, the members of these groups are likely to be less attached to each other than are members of groups who work without these impediments. Unlike in the task domain, however, we do not expect these differences to vary with the task structure of the project Although tere may be variations in the social “work" that group members need to do over time (e.g., getting acquainted versus smoothing out conflicts), we have no a prtori grounds for predicting a particular pattern of social experiences over the course of the project. Thus, we expect that the social experiences of group members will be negatively affected by restrictions in communication modality. but do not expect specific time- or task-related variations in these experiences.

## The Experiment: Overview and Hypotheses

In the previous section, we have argued that two important, socially relevant properties of communication modalities are interactivity and expressiveness. To examine the effect of communication modalities that differ with regard to these characteristics, we designed the experiment reported here. In this experiment, we compare the effects of Face-to-Face, Computer + Phone, and Computer Only communication conditions on the work processes, performance and affective experiences of small groups engaged in collaborative writing projects. The Face-to-Face condition embodies traditional communication modalities, and thus provides a baseline according to which the effects of restricted interactivity and expressiveness can be assessed. The Computer Only condition is intended to provide a sort of lower bound, an extreme, but theoretcally relevant, test of the effects of limited interactivity and expressiveness. The Computer + Phone condition, which simulates the communication possibilities that might be available to the members of a distributed work group, occupies an intermediate position. Adding the telephone means that groups in that condition have better mechanisms for interactive and expressive communication than do those in the Computer Only condition, but this condition nonetheless embodies some qualities that might be expected to be particularly problematic for collaborative writing. In particular, telephone conversations are typically pairwise, and thus may not be as effective as face-to-face meetings for achieving consensus or establishing a state of mutual knowledge among all the members of a work group. In addition, the absence of a shared visual channel may make communication about interim drafts quite difficult.

In this experiment we expect to find that these restrictions on face-to-face communication impede work on the collaborative writing project, and that these negative effects will be most problematic early and late in the project when group members are planning their work and integrating their individual contributions. As a result of these problems, we expect that group performance will be adversely affected. Further, we expect that limiting opportunities for face-to-face interaction will undermine normal patterns of social interaction, resulting in lower levels of attachment to the group on the part of individual group members.

## Method

## Design

All 117 first-year MBA students (68 men and 49 women) enrolled in a managerial communication course conducted by the first author participated in this experiment. The students were randomly assigned to three-person groups, which were, in turn, randomly assigned to experimental conditions. Each student participated in two groups, one for each of two projects.¹ For each project, there were three communication conditions (Face-to-Face, Computer Only, and Computer + Phone). For the first project, half of the groups were assigned to the Face-to-Face condition and the rest were evenly divided between the Computer Only and Computer + Phone conditions. For the second project, new groups were formed with the restriction that work groups were made up of different individuals than in the first project. Students who had previously been in Face-to-Face groups were assigned to one of the two Computer-mediated groups, and students who had previously been in Computer-mediated groups were assigned to Face-to-Face groups.²

## Procedure

Students were required to carry out two projects, each of which involved responding to the dilemmas presented in a business case. For the first case, drawn from Micheli et al. (1984), students were to announce and explain a large-scale corporate layoff to the employees who were to be terminated, to the remaining employees, to the company's stockholders and to the community in which it operated. The second case, drawn from Schuler and Youngblood (1986) described an organization facing a number of complex personnel problems. The students were to analyze these problems and recommend solutions in a report to management.

Each case was discussed during a regular class session. Following the discussion students were given a set of guidelines that defined the substantive goals they were to try to achieve and a two-week deadline for completing the project. In that interval, they were free to work on the project in any way that did not violate the terms of their experimental conditions. Packets containing blank copies of daily work reports were handed out, and students were instructed to complete one form on each day of the project. Post-project questionnaires were completed during class sessions.

Students assigned to the Computer-Mediated conditions communicated via a system called /CoSy, a program that supports both electronic mail and computer conferences. Using the E-mail function, individuals could send private messages to other individuals or to several people at a time. Using the conference function, an individual could post entries, much as one would post notices on a bulletin board. These entries could be of any length or type (e.g., personal messages, outlines, draft versions of documents, or comments on entries submitted by other group members). The system also contains a reference function which enables users to follow a sequence of entries (i.e., comments on a draft document and revised versions can be linked to the entry containing the draft). In addition, mail messages could become conference entries and vice versa by copying the relevant message to a “scratchpad," and forwarding it to the appropriate destination. Finally, the system supports uploading and downloading, making it possible for individuals to create and manipulate documents in their own word processors and transmit ASCII versions of those documents to the conference space.

To complete their work, students in the Computer-mediated conditions could use either their own computers or computers in a university lab open only to MBA students. Eighty-four percent owned their own computers; modems and communications software (either KERMIT or ProComm) were provided for any student who needed them. The students were reasonably experienced computer users; on a 3- point scale of previous experience with word processing (with 3 high) the class mean was 2.41³, and 37% reported some previous experience with electronic mail. Many, however, were new to computer-mediated communication, and most were new users of /CoSy. Thus, a training session was held to demonstrate the use of the computer conference, and participants were given specially created documentation to guide them as they acquired the necessary computer skills. In addition, students could obtain follow-up advice about the use of the computer conference on-line in the open conference or through mail messages, by telephone, or in person, from the experimenter, from university computer consultants or from their classmates. For practice using the conferencing system prior to beginning work on their projects, all students joined an open conference. For project work, however, each group had its own computer conference. Thus, each individual was a member of two conferences—an open conference that remained accessible throughout the course as a vehicle for communication among students and between students and the professor, and a closed conference accessible only to the student and the other members of his or her work group.

Participation in this research was voluntary, and all students signed consent forms indicating their willingness to participate. Executing the experiment within a regular class meant that students were aware of the variations in communication conditions. To minimize their concerns about the potential effect of these variations on their performance (and the biases that such concerns might introduce into the research findings), the instructions pointed out that any advantages or disadvantages associated with communication condition would be evenly distributed because each individual would participate in one Face-to-Face group and one Computer-mediated group. In addition, students were told that the instructor would eliminate any effects of the experimental manipulations in determining grades. To preserve the students privacy, they were also told that none of their responses would be examined until after the end of the course.

## Manipulation of Communication Modality

In the Computer Only condition, students were required to conduct all project-related interactions, including both substantive and procedural discussions of their work and the exchange and review of interim documents, through the ICoSy system. As noted above, each work group had its own conference, and any member of the group could read any message posted in its conference.⁴ In the Computer + Phone condition, students were allowed to use the ICoSy program and the telephone. In the Face-to-Face condition, the participants could meet, conduct telephone conversations, and exchange documents, but were not allowed to use ICoSy

## Dependent Variables

Time Series Data.The time series data were derived from the students’ daily work reports. On these reports, students used a checklist to record the type of work they had done each day. They also reported how many “communication occasions" they had had with other group members that day, and provided detailed information (time of day, type of contact, duration, identity of individuals present, topics discussed, and assessments of enjoyableness and productivity) about a selected communication occasion.⁵ In addition, the students provided daily evaluations of several aspects of their experience including the difficulty of coordinating work, the fairness of the division of labor, the quality of their communication procedures, and their attachment to other group members. These evaluations, as well as the evaluations of the enjoyableness and productivity of the focal communication occasion, were reported on 7-point Likert scales.

To create scales for the analyses, we first grouped these items in conceptually-relevant categories. For instance, activities were classified as planning (e.g., Planned how to complete the assignment; Planned who would do what), drafting (e.g., Did new writing o project documents), revising (e.g., Evaluated initial drafts; Revised another's writing), or social (e.g., Discussing other school work; Discussing social activities). The internal consistency of each scale was then assessed using the Spearman-Brown measure of reliability. The items that make up each scale and the results of these reliability analyses are shown in Table 1.

Retrospective Data.After each of the two projects, students completed questionnaires containing items about the group's work procedures and performance, their own experiences during the course of the project, and the interpersonal dynamics of the group. After completing both projects, students compared face-to-face communication and other modalities used in the experiment in terms of their usefulness for work and social activities.

Perforrance Data.Each group's project was scored by three people, the first author and two individuals who were employed as graders for the course. These individuals were blind to experimental condition. Their evaluation of the completed projects followed the usual practices in the course. That is, after the projects had been turned in, the first author scanned the submitted papers and selected a small number of examples (without regard to experimental condition) to illustrate successful and unsuccessful responses to the assignment; these were then discussed in a meeting with the graders. Subsequently, each rater worked independently, assigning holistic scores to each paper. The effective reliability (Rosenthal 1973) was 0.77, and the mean of the scores assigned by the three raters was used as the measure of performance.

## Analysis

Both our experimental method and the theoretical analysis on which it is based treat groups as the unit of analysis. That is, we are interested in group-level phenomena such as coordination difficulties and performance on a jointly created product. Thus, the research participants were assigned to the experimental conditions as groups, rather than as individuals. Some dependent variables, such as group performance, are naturally group-level outcomes. Others, such as time spent on various tasks, were measured at the level of the individual. To create the group-level measures needed, it was necessary to calculate group means for these dependent variables. First, however, we assessed the statistical (as opposed to conceptual) appropriateness of treating groups as the unit of analysis by computing the effects of group membership on individual scores for five dependent variables (coordination difficulty, attachment to the group, perceived fairness, perceived quality of the focal communication occasion and duration of the focal communication occasion). These analyses indicate that group membership accounted for between 43% and 64% of the variance in these dependent variables, a powerful demonstration of the effect of the circumstances the group shared on the individual experience of its members. Thus, where appropriate, the analyses that follow are based on group means.

The goal of this research was to assess the effects of computer-mediated communication on a group's ability to perform sustained intellectual teamwork and on the interaction between group members as the work is carried out. In addition, we wanted to determine whether any such effects depend on project phase. As we have discussed, the consequences of variations in communication condition are expected to vary depending on the communication requirements of the specific tasks being performed at a particular point in the life of the project. For instance, if computermediated communication interferes with the interactive communication required to carry out communication-intensive tasks such as planning and revision, we would expect to see the largest differences between the Face-to-Face and Computer-mediated conditions early and late in the life of the project. Thus, the data analysis focuses on the effects of Communication Modality, and on the interaction of this variable with time.

The time series measures described in the previous section were created by finding the mean values within each group for each day of the project. These values were analyzed using a 3 (Communication Modality) ×15 (Days) analysis of variance, with days as a within-group factor. As noted above, Communication Modality × Time interactiors are taken as evidence that the effects of computer-mediated communication depend on the phase of the project. Significant interactions indicate that behavior (e.g., planning) and subjective experience (e.g., perceived difficulty in coordinating work) depend on communication modality and project phase. For most analyses, the Computer Only and Computer + Phone groups are combined, and then contrasted with the Face-to-Face condition. In this study, as will be shown below, a significant Communication Modality × Linear Time interaction typically reveals that a difference between the Computer-mediated and Face-to-Face groups increases over time. Similarly, the Communication Modality × Quadratic Time interaction typically shows that such differences are greatest during the middle of the project. when groups are most actively working, and a Communication Modality × Cubic Time interaction suggests that these differences are greatest both early and late in the projects, when groups are planning and revising.6 In general, these effects reflect interactions between the characteristics of particular communication modalities and time-based task variation

## Project Performance

Despite the difficulties that students in the Computer-mediated conditions experienced, their performance was not affected by communication condition (F(1, 75) $= 0 . 1 7 5 , p = 0 . 6 4 7 )$ . The average scores on the reports they produced were not significantly different than the scores of students who were able to meet face-to-face. Apparently, the greater costs they endured (greater investment of time in communication and in work on the project) paid off in product quality. But the students' own views of their work were more negative than the views of the raters. They perceived the quality of their work less favorably in the Computer-mediated conditions than in the Face-to-Face condition $( F ( 1 , 7 4 ) = 6 . 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . ## Social Effects

Additional negative consequences of restricted communication appear in the social domain. In response to questions (on the daily questionnaire) about whether they had talked to other group members about other school work or about other topics, students in the Face-to-Face groups reported more social communications than did students in the Computer-mediated groups $( F ( 1 , 7 5 ) = 2 3 . 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ), and this difference persisted over the course of the two-week project. Adding a more interactive medium did not seem to increase the level of social interaction between students; those in the Computer + Phone groups didn't have significantly more social conversations than those in the Computer Only groups $( F ( 1 , 7 4 ) = 2 . 2 , p = 0 . 1 4 )$

Students' affective reactions to their groups also showed main effects of communication modality. The scale measuring satisfaction with the work group (see Table 1) revealed that students in Computer-mediated groups were less positive toward the other members of their group than those in Face-to-Face groups $( F ( 1 , 7 4 ) = 1 0 . 6 , p$ < 0.01). Again, this difference remained constant throughout the project. The two Computer-mediated groups did not differ from each other.

A scale measuring student perceptions that they and other members of their group were contributing fairly to the joint project showed that students in the Face-to-Face group rated the work process as more fair than did those in the two Computer-mediated groups $( F ( 1 , 7 4 ) = 9 . 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Moreover, this difference was largest in the middle of the project, presumably because, compared to other phases, individual work during the drafting phase was less visible and this problem was heightened by computer-based communication (for the Face-to-Face vs. Computer × Quadratic interaction, $F ( 1 , 7 4 ) = 1 4 . 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

Taken together, this evidence—lower levels of social interaction, lower levels of reported liking for other group members, and a lower level of perceived fairness in the distribution of labor—suggests that social relationships surrounding the project were strained by channeling all communication through the Computer Only or Computer + Phone combination. (See Kiesler et al. 1984, for a discussion of the social effects of computer-mediated communication.)

These effects, however, seem to be specifically associated with work on the project Other evidence indicates that the computer conference supported social interaction on a broadly distributed, albeit less intense scale. Throughout the class in which this experiment was conducted, students used the computer conference extensively for social purposes. Class members exchanged hundreds of personal and social E-mail messages with other students outside their project teams. and, during the 10 weeks of the term, the 117 members of the class posted more than 2,500 messages devoted to such topics as sports, outdoor recreation, politics, and school gossip in the open computer conference. Although the computer conference did not seem to provide an appropriate vehicle for the flashes of humor and goodwill that can help to alleviate strain on difficult projects, its utility as a community forum was confirmed in postproject evaluation, in which students reported that the computer system was superior to telephone and face-to-face communication in keeping up with school gossip.

## Discussion

Based on previous conceptualizations of the communicative demands associated with collaborative intellectual work, we hypothesized that using computer-mediated communication to carry out a project of this type would affect both task and social outcomes. With regard to the work process, we hypothesized (la) that computer-mediated communication would impede work on the project, (/b) that the impact of computer-mediated communication would vary over time, and (Ic) that its negative impact would be most severe when the demand for interactive, expressive communication was believed to be highest. With regard to group performance, we hypothesized (2) that the performance of students in computer-mediated groups would be inferior to that of students who could communicate face-to-face. With regard to social experience, we hypothesized (3a) that computer-mediated communication would undermine relations among group members, but (3b) because social relations have a less well-defined temporal structure, this degradation would be unrelated to time-based task variation. Our results reveal substantial congruence with these hypotheses, but some discrepancies as well. Below, we discuss the fit of the data to the hypotheses.

## Effects of Communication Modality on Task Execution

The most general hypothesis——that computer-mediated communication would impede project work—was confirmed. Computer-mediated groups required more time to complete each stage of their work, and also spent more total time working and more time communicating with each other than did the students in the face-to-face groups. But, despite this investment, they still reported greater difficulty in coordinating their work. We also obtained clear evidence that the impact of the communication modality manipulation varied over the course of the project. In particular, the quadratic and cubic components of the Time × Communication Modality interaction suggest that the negative impact of computer-mediated communication is a function of the communicative requirements of particular tasks.

The more specific assertion that computer-mediated communication was especially poor at supporting more integrative tasks was, however, only partially supported. The idea that the significant quadratic and cubic components of the Time × Communication Modality interaction reflect the detrimental effect of restricting communication at periods of maximum interdependence is supported by the pattern of task activities shown in Figure 1. Nonetheless, in the time series analyses, the link between task activities and reported experiences can only be established by inference. And, contrary to the contingency hypothesis, more direct analyses revealed no differential impact of communication modality on coordination difficulty across tasks. The correlations between reported task activities, the proportion of the group working and the proportion involved in communication indicate that tasks defined as requiring more intensive interaction do, in fact, evoke that interaction. But these measures of involvement in specific tasks were not associated with differential levels of coordination difficulty for groups in different communication conditions. Self-reports of the extent to which groups were working were associated with the negative effects of computer-mediated communication, but the predictions were not stronger for the more integrative tasks (planning and revising) than for the more individualistic task (drafting).

It seems unlikely, however, that task characteristics are irrelevant to judgments about the utility of particular communications media. More likely, the absence of task-specific effects of communication modality is a function of imperfections in the link between the theoretical concepts on which our hypotheses are based and the concrete properties of collaborative writing. In particular, the framework used here breaks collaborative writing into phases thought to reflect the variations in the need for detailed coordination of individual activity described by the contingency theorists cited earlier (i.e., Van de Ven et al. 1976, Short et al. 1976, Daft et al. 1987). But analysts of individual writing projects have shown that planning, drafting and revising are likely to comingle and to be recursive (Flower and Hayes 1978). In group work, the necessity for joint planning and allocation of tasks to individuals might impose a more linear process than has been observed with individuals (note, for instance, the orderly sequence of peak activity levels in planning, drafting and revising portrayed in Figure 1), but a close-up view would likely reveal that this process encompasses considerable task-cycling. For instance. groups might plan, draft, and revise an outline, but within the outline, they might plan, draft, and revise the introductory argument, the body of the document, and their conclusions. This task-cycling may dampen the effects of high-level differences in the components that make up a long-term project.

This line of argument suggests the need for more controlled research with better measures of the components of intellectual teamwork. If group members are rapidly shifting from planning to drafting, from drafting to revision and back again, activity measures that only permit respondents to characterize their work at a general level may be too gross or insensitive to portray the sequence of group work accurately.

Thus, rather than seeking contingencies between communication requirements and projects, work groups or organizational level (Daft and I.engel 1986; Rice et al. 1989; Tushman 1978, 1979), researchers should be encouraged to look for contingencies at a more molecular level of analysis. Intellectual progress is likely to depend on matching detailed features of tasks with detailed attributes of the communication modalities (cf. Zrnud et al. 1990).

The idea of task-cycling should not, however, be seen as merely a rationalization for failure to confirm our hypothesis. Rather, the idea that collaborative writing, as well as other forms of intellectual teamwork, involves intricately interwoven task variations may help to explain not only the absence of task-based effects of communication modality, but also the presence of the substantial negative effects of communication modality that we observed. To the extent that such rapid task-cycling exists. group members are likely to need a highly flexible communications medium. Indeed the advantage of face-to-face communication might not be that it makes possible specific forms of communication that no other medium can provide, but that it makes possible all, or most, of the forms of communication that other media can provide. In face-to-face groups, members can move easıly from indiv idual thought, to group discussion, to initial sketches of their plans, back to discussion and modification of those plans, all within the space of a single meeting. When opportunities for interactive and expressive communications are constrained, movement between these activities becomes much more difficult. Group members are likely to endure delay in obtaining responses to their ideas, to have difficulty establishing that a goal has been reached and a new activity can be initiated. and to feel constrained by reductions in bandwidth that prevent the full expression of ideas.

## Effects of Communication Modality on Project Performance

As noted above, despite the many frustrations they endured, the students in the computer-mediated communication conditions produced documents that were similar in overall quality to those produced by the students who were able to meet face-toface. Although the process data make it clear that this achievement was hard-won, the absence of an effect of communication modality on project performance reveals the limitations of contingency theory. In particular, contingency theory holds that particular tasks demand particular ways of communicating, and that the inability to communicate in the theoretically prescribed fashion should undermine performance. But this proposition ignores the possibility that, faced with an inappropriate task/technology match, people can nevertheless carry out their work by adjusting their behavior to meet their goals. The equifinality of results observed here implies that the greater amounts of time that the students in the restricted communication conditions spent working and communicating about the project can be seen, not merely as liabilities, but as adaptations to a difficult set of circumstances. This interpretation suggests that rather than focusing solely on questions about performance and user satisfaction, future research about human uses of technology should also focus on questions about patterns o adaptation (Orlikowski and Robey 1991, Yates and Orlikowski 1992)

## Effects of Communication Modality on Social Experience

As we anticipated, restricting communication not only interfered with the students' ability to do their work efficiently, but also undermined their ability to establish amicable relationships. However, in contrast to the temporal variation observed in the task domain, our measures of social experience reveal a simple main effect of communication modality. Many studies have shown that ease in communication among individuals increases the likelihood of friendship formation and contributes to group cohesiveness. Thus, the most likely explanation for the detrimental effect of computer-mediated communication on social relations is simply that it interferes with the smooth flow of conversation, making it more difficult for individuals to come to know and appreciate each other.

## Methodological Considerations

One might argue that the results reported here—especially the effects on work processes—are artifactual, resulting from differential levels of experience with faceto-face and computer-mediated communication rather than from the constraints imposed by the technology. However, the structure of the experiment makes it unlikely that lack of experience with computer-mediated communication per se can account for our findings. The two class projects involved in this experiment were several weeks apart. During this interval, many students used the computer conference regularly to exchange E-mail messages and to post notices meant to entertain the professor and other members of the class. If the results were entirely an artifact of experience with the medium, this extended opportunity to practice should surely have led to some difference in the pattern of results obtained on the two projects. As noted earlier, however, the results showed no changes in the effects of technology over time between projects; rather, there were variations in the effects of communication conditions over time within projects. These within-project temporal variations make it unlikely that the results are solely, or even mainly, a result of limited expertise or dislike for computer-mediated communication.

A somewhat more serious criticism of this experiment is that, rather than reducing obstacles to communication as it might in a distributed work situation, computermediated communication interfered with familiar and reasonably effective ways of working. Students' reactions to the computer-mediated communication technology might have been more positive if, as in authentic distributed work, they had been unable to communicate about their work without it. Even in this more artificial situation, however, computer-mediated communication could potentially have served student needs. The projects they were working on were, for them, their real work, not a task designed for and limited to the experimental context. The effort they invested in completing their work makes it clear that they believed performance mattered. Furthermore, despite common membership in the MBA community, arranging out-of-class face-to-face meetings was often problematic. In this class as well as others, students frequently complained about the difficulty of squeezing group meetings into schedules that were already crowded with coursework, part- or fulltime jobs and, quite often, family responsibilities as well. In sum, their motivation to perform well, the scheduling problems associated with group work and the enthusiasm students showed in using the computer conference for less demanding communication tasks suggest that computer-mediated communication would have been viewed more favorably if it had provided an effective mechanism for communicating about project work.

Still, both the concern about lack of experience and lack of real benefit of electronic communication are, almost certainly, valid to some extent. The projects in this study were much longer and more complex than the tasks used in many experimental studies of computer-mediated communication, but they are still, when compared to many “real world" forms of intellectual teamwork (cf., Kraut et al. 1987-1988). short-term projects. In longer projects, and in situations where computer-mediated communication becomes an integral, taken-for-granted aspect of organizational life, people undoubtedly become more skilled in using it. Thus. we applaud efforts such as those in which McGrath and his colleagues (1993) are examining the effects of computer-mediated communication over longer time periods and across a broad range of tasks. Projects such as this will help to identify which of the effects of computer-mediated comrnunication we have observed here endure over time, and which dissipate as this way of communicating becomes more familiar. They will also help to distinguish tasks that seem to require the interactivity and expressiveness of face-to-face communication from those which distributed workers can perform easily and efficiently using leaner media.

Our own analyses of communication among collaborating scientists hint at what such clarifying research may reveal. For instance, we have found that, despite access to and experience with computer-mediated communication, the researchers we have interviewed typically discuss their work face-to-face, and are especially likely to choose this medium during the early stages of a project. In more than 100 interviews with researchers in several disciplines, only one has reported initiating a new project and carrying out the primary planning electronically. Moreover, many have reported that, despite a history of successful joint work and, often, strong personal relationships, they find it difficult to begin new projects when once colocated colleagues move to new institutions. In our view, these difficulties are not a matter of lack of experience; rather, they reflect fundamental difficulties in conducting the complex conversations required to launch a new project when interactivity and expressiveness are reduced (Galegher and Kraut 1992). Recent observations of distributed teams in a multinational corporation have produced similar findings; computer-mediated communication plays an important role in helping members of these teams coordinate their activities, but discussions of complicated problems, especially during the early stages of project work appear to demand face-to-face communication. In addition, the participants claimed that their experience had taught them that, despite the great differences between them and the considerable expense of travel, the project tended to go “off track" without face-to-face meetings at regular intervals (Johnson 1992). We believe that these two examples illustrate both the extended connectivity that computer-mediated communication makes possible, and its limitations as a medium for approaching new problems.

## Summary

The essence of contingency theory is its claim about the importance of the task/ technology fit. Our results provide partial confirmation of this basic premise; when constrained to using technologies inadequate for their tasks, group members experienced substantial difficulties. But, as McGrath (1990) and others have argued, understanding behavior in groups and the impact of technology on that behavior requires both observations over time and attention to multiple outcomes. When the full range of outcomes, and their temporal patterns, is considered, the results are much more complicated than could be predicted solely on the basis of contingency theory. In particular, this experiment revealed an interesting combination of interrelatedness and independence in the relationship between various measures of group performance. The quality of group performance was apparently unrelated to the process that generated it, and although the negative effects of computer-mediated communication on the work process were also reflected in group members' social experiences, the temporal pattern of results differed substantially across the two domains. These results suggest that theoretical statements that postulate simple relationships between tasks and technologies are almost certain to be wrong, at least with regard to some aspects of group effectiveness. Clearly, alternative theoretical formulations based on more sophisticated understandings of tasks, technologies and human behavior are required. In our current work, we are attempting to develop such formulations.

Practically, the results of this study suggest some of the limits of computer-mediated communication systems. As recent news reports show (Kolata 1990), these systems permit speedy collaboration across barriers of time and distance. And, as we have noted, students in the computer-mediated groups in this experiment were able, despite considerable difficulty, to complete their work and to produce reports similar in quality to those produced in more traditional ways. The computer conference also allowed students to keep in touch with a wide network of information and gossip, and they acknowledged that computer-mediated communication could be satisfactory as a means of handing off independently completed interim products. But the results of this study indicate that electronic groups have a more difficult time carrying out their work and form weaker bonds for member support than do face-to-face groups. As a result, at least until more satisfactory technologies become available, complex, longterm collaborative work is likely to involve a recurring need for face-to-face interaction.\*
