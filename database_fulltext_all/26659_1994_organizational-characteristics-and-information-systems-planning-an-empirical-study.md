Information Systems (IS) planning has gained considerable interest among researchers and practitioners in recent years because of the large investments that firms have made in IS and the increasingly strategic nature of the impact of information systems on organizational performance. Since IS planning is performed in an organizational context, characteristics of the organization may have a significant influence on the quality and effectiveness of the IS planning process. A research model is developed that links two major dimensions of IS planning—the quality of the planning process and planning effectiveness--with a set of eight organizational factors derived from contingency research in IS planning, strategic business planning, organizational studies, and technology innovation. The model is validated using data collected from a field survey of 249 senior IS executives. Canonical correlation analysis is used to test the research hypotheses and validate the model.

The results of the study indicate that the two planning dimensions respectively reflecting the “means" and “ends" of IS planning are equally important. The results also indicate that planning resources, the intended strategic impact of IS on future business operations, the quality of facilitation mechanisms, the quality of implementation mechanisms, and the quality of strategic business planning are significantly associated with the quality and effectiveness of IS planning.

The implications of the results to further research and to managerial practice are highlighted.

The increased awareness of the strategic impact of information systems (IS) on business has made research on information systems planning increasingly important to the IS field (Boynton and Zmud 1987). As investment in IS has increased, IS planning has become a key management issue for practitioners as well (Niederman et al. 1991).

Since IS planning is conducted in an organizational context, various organizational factors have naturally been studied and are believed to influence the efficacy of the IS planning system (Pyburn 1983, Davis et al. 1984, Cash et al. 1988), and it is being increasingly recognized that IS planning approaches must be adapted to fit with the characteristics of the organization (Cash et al. 1988, Sullivan 1985). The study of the relationship between organizational factors and IS planning is particularly important since there is “no one best way" to perform this important organizational activity (Pyburn 1983, Sullivan 1985, Jang 1989).

Empirical research in IS planning has examined these organizational-IS planning relationships to a limited extent. Various organizational factors such as the quality of strategic business planning, organizational support mechanisms, top management and user involvement, the resources devoted to planning, the organization's size, and management styles have been found to influence IS planning (Pyburn 1983; Cash et al. 1988; Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988, 1989, 1990; Galliers 1991; Lederer and Sethi 1988; Goodhue et al. 1988; Earl 1989; Premkumar and King 1991).

These studies follow the general contingency theory paradigms that relate technology, innovations, organizational and IS functional characteristics and organizational and IS effectiveness to their organizational contexts. However, such studies in the IS planning context have generally involved case studies of a few organizations (McFarlan et al. 1983, Pyburn 1983, Lederer and Mendelow 1987, Goodhue et al. 1988, Brancheau et al. 1989, Adrians and Hoogakker 1989), thereby greatly limiting the generalizability of the results. The few large-scale empirical studies have, individually and collectively, focused on only a small set of organizational factors.

There is, therefore, a need for a comprehensive framework that examines the relationship between IS planning and a broad set of organizational factors. This will facilitate further research that builds on prior work in IS planning, the development of common measures and permit the comparison of the results of various studies.

The specific objectives of this study are:

• to identify the salient dimensions that can be used to characterize the efficacy of the IS planning system

• to identify a comprehensive set of organizational factors that are importantly related to the nature and efficacy of the IS planning system, and

• to empirically assess and validate the relationship of these organizational and IS planning system factors.

## Conceptual and Research Background

The conceptual model underlying the study is based on contingency theory, an idea introduced by Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) and later expanded by Kast and Rosenzweig (1973). Contingency theory, which has been the basis for a great deal of research concerning the technology-organization interface (Weill and Olson 1989b), is based on the idea that there is no single best way to achieve the necessary fit among organizational factors and, in this instance, IS planning systems and processes. It attempts to understand the interrelationships within and among organizational subsystems and emphasizes the multivariate nature of organizations.

This specific contextual use of contingency theory traces back to the work of Galbraith (1973), who emphasized the need to match information processing requirements, which are determined by environmental and organizational factors, with information processing capabilities.

Most of the research that is relevant to the development of the research model for this study has been conducted in the contexts of IS planning and organizational-IS functional relationships

## Prior Research in IS Planning

Research in IS planning consists of both conceptual and empirical research streams. Research in assessing IS planning performance is distinct and is treated separately here.

Conceptual IS Planning Research. Most of the early research in IS planning was theoretical, essentially developing better conceptual models for IS planning (Zani 1970, McFarlan 1971, Bluementhal 1973, IBM 1975, McLean and Soden 1977, King 1978). These models have evolved over time in light of the changing organizational role of IS (Ward et al. 1990). The objectives and focus of IS planning also evolved over this period to meet the varying needs of organizations: system integration in the EDP era, databases and support systems in the MIS era, and strategic systems and information architectures in the “information age" era (King 1988b). Many commercial methodologies have been developed based on these theoretical models (Hackathorn and Karimi 1988).

A primary theme of these planning models is the need to integrate IS planning with business planning so that information systems provide support for the business strategies of the organization (King 1978, Venkatraman 1985).

Although there are many different specific methodologies for IS planning (Earl 1989, Martin 1989), a generic description of the process would involve first identifying the firm's business objectives, strategies, and critical success factors using information obtained from senior management and business plans. Based on these informational inputs, a mission and broad objectives are formulated for the IS function. Then, a detailed business process analysis is performed and an information architecture is developed to meet the requirements of the business processes. Subsequently, the inforrnation architecture is mapped into existing systems and a series of plans at different levels—strategic, tactical, and operational—are formulated

Most of the conceptual research on IS planning is based on the rational top-down approach to planning (King 1978. Henderson and Sifonis 1988). Commercial planning methodologies and consultants use this approach to IS planning and there is some empirical evidence to indicate that this approach is the predominant mode of IS planning (Vitale et al. 1986). However, some researchers have argued that rational IS planning models ignore the individual initiatives at the grassroots level (Ciborra 1991). They draw upon the literature on logical incrementalism in strategic business planning (Quinn 1980, Mintzberg 1990) to highlight the incremental aspects of planning. Others argue that since a comprehensive planning exercise is a necessity, these two approaches should be complementary to each other (Camillus 1982). Other approaches to IS planning have only recently been highlighted (Earl 1993) and further empirical research is required to validate those approaches. Since there is strong conceptual and some empirical evidence to support the rational top-down planning model and only some support for the contrary, that model is extensively used in most research studies.

Empirical Research in IS Planning. Most of the early empirical studies on IS planning were exploratory in that they focused on identifying the issues and problems in IS planning (Martino 1983, McLean and Soden 1977, Lederer and Mendelow

1986, Galliers 1987). These studies identified factors that could potentially influence the efficacy of the planning system.

Some of the important organizational factors and issues that were identified by these exploratory studies are: an inappropriate planning time horizon, a lack of communication and integration between business and IS planning, a lack of top management and user involvement in IS planning, overly optimistic expectations, a lack of adequate commitment to planning, a lack of resources for planning and plan implementation, the volatility of the business environment, the status of the IS manager and the stage of IS maturity (Karimi 1988).

More recent empirical research on IS planning has focused on understanding the relationships between various organizational factors and IS planning. The primary objective of IS planning is to align IS plans with business plans (Benson and Parker 1985, Earl 1989) and therefore the information outputs from business planning are critical inputs for IS planning (King 1978, King 1988a). Many researchers have found significant positive correlations between the overall quality of business planning and the quality of IS planning (Premkumar and King 1991, Zviran 1990a, Lederer and Sethi 1988, McLean and Soden 1977, McFarlan 1971).

Although the link between business and IS planning has been well recognized at the conceptual and general level, it has been found to be a weak link in practical terms (Lederer and Mendelow 1986). Organizations therefore often use various support mechanisms such as active interaction between business and IS planners, a steering committee, or the active participation of the IS manager in strategic business planning to improve the information flow between the two planning systems (Lederer and Mendelow 1987, Galliers 1987, Lederer and Sethi 1988, Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988). The increasing use of strategic information systems and interorganizational systems that facilitate value-added business partnerships (Konsynski and McFarlan 1990) make these mechanisms even more important.

Among the other organizational factors that have been empirically determined to significantly influence the effectiveness of IS planning are a closer proximity of the IS function to top management and an enhanced status of the IS executive (Pyburn 1983), and the planning time horizon, which influences the link between business and IS planning and the focus and scope of planning (Karimi 1988, Martin 1989, Galliers 1991). Studies in business planning, IS planning, and IS have found that firms with longer planning horizon have better planning and performance (Bracker et al. 1988, Lederer and Sethi 1988, Raymond 1990).

The primary resources used in IS planning are the planning methodology and human resources (IS staff, users, and top management). Many firms consider planning as a resource drain (McFarlan et al. 1983) and therefore the inadequacy of resources has been identified as a significant problem in IS planning (King 1988a. Lederer and Sethi 1988, Goodhue et al. 1988). Various studies have emphasized the importance of top management and user involvement, the planning skills of IS staff, and the quality of the planning methodology to the success of IS planning (Adrians and Hoogakker, 1989, Brancheau et al. 1989, Galliers 1991). The need for top management and users' continued commitment during implementation in spite of its implications on their operations, and the need for effective feedback and adequate resources for implementing the plans have also been identified as important (Goodhue et al. 1988, Raghunathan and King 1988, Galliers 1991, Wilson 1989).

The role played by information systems in an organization has been found to be importantly related to IS planning characteristics. Information systems evolve through various stages/roles and serve different purposes in various organizations (Nolan 1979, McFarlan et al. 1983). A variety of models have been developed to categorize organizations based on their IS infrastructure and their mode of usage of information technology (IT) (Nolan 1979, McFarlan et al. 1983, Sullivan 1985, Lehman 1985, Zmud et al. 1987). Researchers have found that planning characteristics differ in the various stages and have identified appropriate planning characteristics for the stages of Nolan's stage model (Earl 1989, Galliers and Sutherland 1991) Sullivan ( 985) posited that planning characteristics must be matched with the extent of infusion/diffusion of IS within the firm. The “strategic grid," used by McFarlan et al. (1983) highlights the planning characteristics of organizations with different roles for IS. Recent empirical studies have confirmed the relationship between the role of IS and IS planning (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1990, Premkumar and King 1992).

It has also been found that industry characteristics influence the value chain, and thereby the potential to exploit information technology, resulting in variations in the nature and impact of IS planning across industries (Porter and Millar 1985). Firms in service-oriented industries tend to have higher information content in their products and services and therefore depend more on information technology compared to firms in manufacturing-oriented industries (Cash et al. 1988). Studies have confirmed the differences in strategic uses of IS among industries (Johnston and Carrico 1988, Buday 1986, Ives and Learmonth 1984).

Research in IS Planning Performance. Research on IS planning has not adequately addressed the performance of planning systems. However, the significant investments in IS and growing claims for deriving strategic advantage through IS has brought the planning performance dimension into prominence (Weill and Olson 1989a, Galliers 1991). Comprehensive frameworks for the evaluation of specific IS planning systems have been developed (King 1988a). However, empirical research on assessing IS planning performance across organizations is sparse. A few empirical studies (McFarlan 1971, Raghunathan and King 1988) have tried to assess performance of planning system using measures such as user satisfaction and system effectiveness, but have had limited success.

Some IS planning benefits, or outcomes, such as improvements in communication with top management and users, better appreciation of role of IS within the organization, and better integration with business planning are difficult to assess using objective measures (McLean and Soden 1977, Galliers 1987). In strategic business planning contexts, objective performance measures, such as increase in sales, ROI, etc., have not proved to be useful, probably because even strategic business planning is only one of a large number of determinants of business success (Pearce et al. 1987). Indeed, it has been demonstrated that there are advantages in using well-defined subjective measures of performance (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987).

IS planning researchers have had better success with perceptual measures such as using the fulfillment of planning objectives as a measure of planning effectiveness (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1991). Although the overall outcome of planning is planning effectiveness, some research suggests that “quality of planning process" and “planning effectiveness" dimensions prove to be most useful in evaluating IS planning because of the iterative nature of planning with feedback from outcomes serving to refine the planning process (King 1988a, Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987).

## Prior Research in Organizational-IS Functional Relationships

In the domain of studies on the relationships of the IS function to the organization, Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) developed a framework that identified ten important organizationał variables and hypothesized their relationship to IS success. This framework provides a description of the aspects of the organizational environment that are most relevant to the IS function. It has been successfully used in contexts such as the study of end-user computing (Cheney et al. 1986).

In a meta-analysis of contingency research in MIS, Weill and Olson (1989b) identified seven contingency variables—strategy, structure, size, environment, technology task, and the individual—that have been proved useful, of which an organization's strategy, structure, and size are relevant to this study. While the relationship between IS planning and business strategy has been well articulated (as discussed earlier), the relationship between IS and organizational structure has not been well established (Olson and Chervany 1980, Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, Robey 1981, Huber and McDaniel 1986, Feeney et al. 1987, Ahituv et al. 1989). The existence of complex and multiple organizational structures in a single organization and the extensive permeation of computers in organizations necessitates multiple IS structures to suit different conditions within the same organization (Earl 1989, Ahituv et al. 1989, Swanson and Beath 1986), thereby making it difficult to measure and relate it to other IS variables. Most studies have found that size is positively related to MIS use and success (Delone 1981, Raymond 1985, Lehman 1985), but a few studies have only found a weak relationship between the two variable (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, Raymond 1990).

Other variables that have been found to be positively related to IS success and use are: EDP maturity, IS resources, and technical sophistication (Delone 1981, 1988; Cheney and Dickson 1982; Raymond 1985, 1990; Alpar and Kim 1990; Raymond 1990).

In comparing these studies with those of organization-IS planning relationships, one can note the use of some variables such as organizational size, IS resources, and EDP maturity that have not been considered in prior studies of the organization-IS planning relationship. Since these variables have been found to be related to various aspects of the IS function, we consider them in formulating a comprehensive model of organization-IS planning relationships.

## Planning Dimensions of the Research Model

A “goal centered” approach to measurement of planning success assumes a rationally-designed planning system and does not consider other organizational models such as political, system resources, and strategic constituencies (Lewin and Minton 1986). Cameron (1986), summarizing research on organizational effectiveness, concluded that it is a problem-driven construct rather than a theory-driven construct, and recommended that researchers decide on the appropriate model based on a school of thought and use measures that are relevant to that model. Although research in strategic business planning uses approaches other than the rational approach in a limited fashion, as in logical incrementalism (Quinn 1980) or deliberate and emergent strategies (Mintzberg and Waters 1985), research in IS planning and models of IS planning have predominantly subscribed to the rational approach to planning (McLean and Soden 1977, King 1978, Henderson and Sifonis 1988, Earl 1989) and practitioners have incorporated these models into the structure of their practical IS planning procedures and methodologies (IBM 1981, Martin 1989). This viewpoint has also been empirically determined to be the most prevalent planning practice in organizations (Vitale et al. 1986). Hence, the rational approach to IS planning has been assumed in the research model developed here.

Since planning provides both tangible and intangible benefits (King and Cleland 1978, King 1983, Lorange 1979), it is useful to expand the conceptual domain of planning success beyond the narrow focus of economic benefits (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987, Wood and LaForge 1979). This approach is particularly relevant in the context of functional planning systems, such as IS planning, where the link to overall organizational performance can only be indirect and mediated by many intervening factors.

Recent research in strategic planning has conceptualized planning system success in terms of two interrelated dimensions: planning system capabilities and the fulfillment of key planning objectives (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987). The first dimension represents the process aspects of planning and is based on Lorange's (1979) contention that an evaluation of the system's capabilities is an important measure of the system's effectiveness. The second dimension, the fulfillment of planning objectives, is based on goal-centered approach to the evaluation of effectiveness (Cameron and Whetton 1983), and is consistent with King's (1983) approach to the evaluation of planning systems and Steiner's (1979) notion of“measurement against purpose." The first dimension reflects the “means" aspect of the planning system; the second dimension measures the “end," or benefits, from the planning system.

Based on this conceptualization, the research model in Figure 1 represents IS planning in terms of two dimensions: the “means" dimension is represented by the “quality of the IS planning process" and the “ends" dimension is represented by “planning effectiveness." Below, each of these is explicated in terms of its rationale and operationalizations. Discussion of the operationalizations of the other variables of the research model is deferred to the subsequent hypotheses section.

Quality of The IS Planning Process. The IS planning process consists of a set of activities that transform the information inputs from business plans, external sources, and IS users into plans that guide the development of the IS function.

The quality of the planning process is operationalized in terms of the extent of detailed analysis in the three major areas of the planning process: internal, external, and technology (McLean and Soden 1977, Selig 1983, Boynton and Zmud 1987, Karimi 1988). Internal analysis deals with identifying internal strengths and weaknesses in IS, analyzing business processes and user's requirements, developing information architectures, and analyzing telecommunications requirements. External analysis is concerned with analyzing business opportunities and threats as related to IS, exploring opportunities for identifying strategic IS applications, and linking business plans with IS plans. Technology analysis deals with analysis of technology trends and their impact on the firm, the assessment of the hardware/software market, and the analysis of hardware/software requirements.

The planning process is also evaluated for its “process" characteristics based on prescriptions for good planning practice such as the integration of various levels of plans, the evaluation of multiple alternatives, and the analysis of resource constraints (Henderson and Sifonis 1988, Grant and King 1982, King and Cleland 1978, Rumelt 1979, Steiner 1979).

Eighteen items (Appendix 2) were used to measure these four dimensions. Each item assessed the extent of planning analysis on a seven point Likert-type scale ranging from “little detail" to “great detail."

Planning Effectiveness. The research model shows that the “ends" aspect of planning success is described by a construct called “planning effectiveness." The implementation of plans results in outcomes in the organization that determine the effectiveness of the planning system.

Planning aims to fulfill both tangible and intangible objectives (King and Cleland 1978). A “goal centered" approach to planning evaluation (Cameron and Whetton 1983), assesses the effectiveness of the planning system in terms of the extent of fulfillment of key planning objectives (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987, Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1991)

In this study, planning effectiveness is operationalized as the extent of fulfillment of a set of seven common IS planning objectives such as better integration of business goals and strategies with IS plans, greater exploitation of IS opportunities for strategic advantage, and better planning and control of hardware and software resources. A detailed list is provided in Appendix 2.

## Organizational Variables in The Research Model

In the research model for this study, the framework for IS-organizational relationships suggested by Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) has been augmented and adapted to the IS planning context. This framework has been widely used in earlier studies linking organizational factors and the IS function (Raymond 1990, Cheney et al. 1986, Ein-Dor and Segev 1982). Table 1 shows the variables used by Ein-Dor and Segev (1978), and those used in this study. The difference in the lists in Table 1 reflect the different contexts (IS planning versus the IS function) and the changing environment and research results over the decade that has passed since they were proposed.

Organizational size has been an important variable in innovation research (Schultz and Slevin 1975) as well as an important variable in studies addressing IS characteristics (Weill and Olson 1989b). Since a larger organizational size has been found to be related to more formal planning processes (McFarlan et al. 1983, Cash et al. 1988) this variable was incorporated into this study. Planning time horizon was included in the model since it has been found to be an important variable in studies on business planning (Bracker et al. 1988, Rhyne 1986), IS function (Ein-Dor and Segev 1978), and IS planning (Karimi 1988, Lederer and Sethi 1988, McFarlan et al. 1983, McLean and Soden 1977). Findings regarding the importance of adequate resources to effective planning (Premkumar and King 1991, King 1988a. Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1989, Goodhue et al. 1988) motivated the inclusion of the “resources" variable from Ein-Dor's framework. The empirical evidence in recent studies on differences in IS planning among industries (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1990) motivated us to include “industry” as a variable in this study.

“Organizational maturity," is redefined for this study, as the “role of IS in the organization," to reflect the recent recognition of the strategic role of IS and to better suit the context of strategic IS planning. This adaptation is also based on research in diffusion, where the later stages of diffusion are concerned with obtaining strategic benefits from the innovation and improving organizational effectiveness (Cooper and Zmud 1990). The recent empirical evidence on the influence of the “role of IS" on IS planning (Premkumar and King 1992, Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1990) also justified our choice. The “role of IS" subsumes another variable from Ein-Dor's study: psychological climate. As originally defined, climate addresses an individual's attitude toward the adoption of a specific information system, which is too narrow a perspective for an organizational-level study. The extensive use of computers in firms, the growth in end-user computing, and the critical necessity of computers for performing most organizational activities have reduced the resistance to the adoption of IS (Srinivasan and Davis 1987), thereby making it more important to study the diffusion of IS in terms of its impact on organizational effectiveness and the organizational climate to use of IS for strategic purposes captured by the role of IS in the organization (Cash et al. 1988, Clemons and Weber 1990, Cooper and Zmud 1990).

Three other organizational variables that have recently been found to significantly influence the IS planning system are included in the model of Figure 1: the quality of strategic business planning, the quality of facilitation mechanisms, and the quality of implementation mechanisms. Since strategic business planning drives the functional planning process by providing the essential information inputs and guidelines (King 1978, Herderson and Sifonis 1988), the first variable was included. The pressing need for various mechanisms to facilitate the integration of organization's business goals and plans in IS planning (Lederer and Mendelow 1987), and significant problems in implementation of IS planning (Lederer and Sethi 1988, Goodhue et al. 1988) determined the choice of the other two variables.

## Research Hypotheses

Hypotheses were developed that relate the eight organizational variables with the two planning dimensions. The rationale for each hypothesis is discussed without repeating research results previously discussed. In some cases, the hypotheses are of fundamental importance to understanding what influences effective planning. In other instances, hypotheses are proposed because, while they reflect intuitively-appealing or long-standing beliefs, they have not previously been empirically tested. In some cases, hypotheses are proposed because they have been identified with serious problems in IS planning or impediments to effective planning. Any study which purports to accomplish the comprehensive objectives of this one must deal with all of these varieties of hypotheses.

After each hypothesis is presented, the manner in which each relevant construct is operationalized is discussed

## Hypotheses Related to Organizational Size

Organization theorists have long believed that firms will institute more planning, practice more formal planning and formalization and invest in information systems to manage growth in size (Galbraith 1973). Kimberly and Evanisko (1981) and Delone (1981) found that size was related to the use of IS and Lehman (1985) found that it was related to sophistication in the use of IS. Raymond (1985) found that for small business firms, size was significantly related to various measures of applications development, but not to user satisfaction. Despite these positive findings, a few researchers have found only a weak relationship between organizational size and IS characteristics (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, Gremillion 1984, Raymond 1990). Gremillion (1984) suggests that this weak relationship may be due to a discontinuity in the relationship, i.e., it is important below a certain threshold value of size, but above that value it is not an important factor.

In the context of IS planning, McFarlan et al. (1983), claim that as organizations increase in size, their complexity increases thereby creating the need to have more formal planning processes to ensure the development of an integrated vision for the IS function. This view is supported by much of the prescriptive literature on IS planning (Martin 1989, Cash et al. 1988, Ward et al. 1990).

Since this relationship is of such longstanding and basic importance, two fundamental hypotheses are addressed

HyPothEsis 1A. The larger the organization, the better will be the quality of the IS planning process.

## Hypotheses Related to Industry

Industries have varied requirements for information and information technology in their operations. Case studies that highlight strategic IS applications (Ives and Learmonth 1984, Buday 1986, Reich and Benbasat 1990), suggest that service contexts are more conducive to the use of IS for strategic purposes. The ten firms discussed by Buday (1986) were all in service industries. Similarly, more than 90% of the examples provided by Ives and Learmonth (1984) relate to firms in service industries, as were seven out of ten firms studied by Reich and Benbasat (1990). Jarvenpaa and Ives (1990) found that top management's interest in IS, an indirect measure of the importance of IS, was lower for firms in the petroleum industry compared to firms in banking. The level of investment in IT varies widely among industry groups, with banking and insurance among the top investors in IS. These results, along with those previously discussed, suggest an industry level effect on strategic use of IT.

At a point in time there are differences in the information content of different industry's products/services and strategic potential to utilize IT to warrant differences in the IS planning systems. Cash et al. (1988) indicated that differences in IT use among industries influences the IS planning practices in these industries. Johnston and Carrico (1988) found that strategic planning for, and deployment of, information technology varied widely among industries. Firms in service industries are more likely to depend more on IS for their operations and therefore might be expected to invest more resources in IS planning, have better quality planning processes, and have greater expectations on the performance of its planning systems. Firms in manufacturing industries are more likely to be focused on transaction processing and IS may have less strategic value. Hence, the quality of the planning process and the expectations of planning performance might be expected to be lower,

Despite the intuitive appeal of these relationships, they have not been subjected to empirical validation. Although not as rigorous as large-scale empirical studies, case studies highlighting the differences in IS among industries are used as adequate empirical evidence (Benbasat et al. 1987), and is used here as the basis for formulating these exploratory hypotheses

HypoTíEsis 2A. Organizations in service industries will have better quality IS planning processes than organizations in manufacturing industries.

HyPotHEsis 2B. Organizations in service industries will have greater IS planning effectiveness than organizations in manufacturing industries.

In this study, organizations were classified into eight industry groups, adapted from the Fortune 500 classification scheme, as shown in Table 2. Firms in banking, insurance, retailing, and information services/publishing were classified as belonging to the “service" group; and those in manufacturing, utility, or petroleum-related industries were classified as belonging to the broad “manufacturing” group.

## Hypotheses Related to Planning Time Horizon

The planning time horizon has often been used as a measure of the organization's time frame (Ein-Dor and Segev 1978, Rhyne 1986). It influences the scope of planning, the issues addressed, and planning priorities (Martin 1989, King and Cleland 1978, Digman 1990). Empirical studies in strategic business planning have found that firms with a longer business planning time horizon exhibit better performance (Rhyne 1986, Bracker and Pearson 1986, Bracker et al. 1988). Similarly, Raymond (1990) found that firms with longer planning time horizon were more successful in their use of computer systems.

In the context of IS planning, McFarlan (1971) highlighted the importance of identifying a proper planning time horizon. Some studies (McLean and Soden 1977, Martino 1983) have found that the IS planning time horizon matches better with the often-longer business planning time horizon in firms with good IS planning, but tends to be lower for firms where the emphasis is on budgeting and operational planning. Lederer and Sethi (1988) also found that firms with longer planning time horizon had lesser problems with their planning process. The nature of some of the activities of strategic IS planning, such as the design of IS architectures, suggest that a longer time horizon should lead to better planning.

Based on these prior findings, the following hypotheses are formulated:

HyPOTHEsis 3A. The longer the planning time horizon, the better is the quality of the IS planning process.

HypoTHEsis 3B. The longer the planning time horizon, the greater the IS planning effectiveness.

The planning time horizon is operationalized in terms of its duration in years

## Hypotheses Related to Resources

Researchers have emphasized the need for adequate resources for ensuring the quality of the planning process (King 1988a), but allocating adequate resources has been a significant practical problem in organizations. Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1988, 1989, 1990), found that planning resources were a very important factor in IS planning. It has also been found to be a major determinant of effectiveness of strategic business planning (Ramanujam and Venkatraman 1987).

Resources need to be adequate both in quantity and quality. A lack of resources could lead to reducing the scope of planning or failing to perform the later phases of planning (review, revision, etc.). Most empirical studies have found that, in particular, top management and user involvement is very important for IS planning success (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988, Johnston and Carrico 1988). In addition to participation, users and top management also must provıde high quality inputs since their inputs determine the alternatives that are considered, and therefore the quality of the planning process (Adrians and Hoogakker 1989, Lederer and Mendelow 1987). Also the quality of the planning staff and project leader has a significant effect on the planning process (IBM 1981, Lederer and Sethi 1988). Since these factors have been found to be an important issue in IS planning the following hypotheses are therefore proposed:

HyPOTHESis 4A. The greater the level of resources the better the quality of the IS planning process.

HypoTHEsis 4B. The greater the level of resources the better the IS planning effectiveness.

The construct “resources" was measured using seven items: three that assessed the quality of the inputs from top management, users, and planners respectively, three that assessed the adequacy of the involvement of these participant groups, and one that assessed the adequacy of financial resources for IS planning. The items are shown in Appendix 2.

## Hypotheses Related to the Role of IS in the Organization

Recent research indicates that IS plays different roles in various organizations and different planning approaches may be required to support the various roles (Cash et al. 1988). To comprehensively capture the meaning of the “role ofIS" in an organization, one needs to measure the impact of, and dependency on, IS for present and future business operations. The “strategic grid" proposed by McFarlan et al. (1983), provides a useful classificatory scheme to assess this.

The strategic grid classifies firms, based on the strategic impact of present and future IS applications, into four major groups: strategic, turnaround, factory, and support. Firms in the “strategic” group are critically dependent on the IS function for their present and future operations. Firms in the “turnaround" group, while not dependent on IS for their current operations, have plans for IS to support their future operations. Firms in the “factory” group are dependent on IS for their present operations, but not critically dependent on it to compete in the future. Firms in the “support" group are not dependent on IS for their present or future operations.

These differences in the current and future roles of IS are believed to significantly influence the IS planning process. McFarlan et al. (1983) predict that firms which plan to use IS for strategic purposes, i.e., those in the “strategic" or “turnaround" group, will be more committed to IS planning and therefore will have better quality planning processes compared to the other two groups. Zviran (1990b) describes a decision support systems for IS planning which uses the “strategic grid" as the primary framework for deciding on major IS planning parameters, thereby highlighting the impact of the “role of IS" on IS planning. Conceptual models of IS planning extensively use the strategic grid framework for describing various planning characteristics (Earl 1989, Cash et al. 1988, Ward et al. 1990). Two recent studies, (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1990, Premkumar and King 1992) found that of the two grid dimensions, the strategic impact of future IS applications has the greater influence on the IS planning system. Hence, two hypotheses are formulated to test this significant relationship and the efficacy of the strategic grid measure:

HyPOTHEsıs 5A. The quality of the IS planning process will be higher for organizations that plan to use IS for strategic purposes in their future operations compared to those that do not plan to do so.

HyPoTíEsis 5B. IS planning effectıveness will be higher for organizations that plan to use IS for strategic purposes in their future operations compared to those that do not plan to do so.

The guidelines for operationalizing the strategic grid (McFarlan et al. 1983) suggest both categorical and continuous-scale items. Since it is difficult to aggregate categorical items to obtain a single measure for each dimension, ten items from that study were converted to a continuous scale. The items for the two dimensions are shown in Appendix 2.

## Hypotheses Related to the Quality of Strategic Business Planning

Strategic business planning in an organization provides the long-term strategic direction for the functional elements of the organization. The link between business and “functional" IS planning has been considered to be of fundamental importance to effective IS planning for more than two decades. The inputs from business planning form the basis for subsequent IS planning in a top-down planning approach (King 1978, Vitale et al. 1986, Martin 1989) and therefore influence the quality of the IS planning process. Many exploratory studies have identified the link between business and IS planning as critical to the success of IS planning (Lederer and Mendelow 1986, McLean and Soden 1977, Martino 1983). Researchers have found significant correlation between the quality of the two planning systems (McFarlan 1971, Premkumar and King 1991). Martino (1983) found that firms that have tighter linkage between business and IS planning exhibited better IS planning and allocated resources more wisely. Lederer and Sethi (1988) found that the extent of problems faced by organizations in IS planning and implementation is inversely related to the sophistication of their strategic business planning. Hence, this fundamental relationship is formulated as hypotheses:

HyPoTHEsEs 6A. The better the quality of strategic business planning, the better will be the quality of the IS planning process.

HyPOTHESEs 6B. The better the quality of strategic business planning, the better will be IS planning effectiveness.

## Hypotheses Related to the Quality of Facilitation Mechanisms

The integration of inputs from business planning into the IS planning process has been identified as being a potential major problem in IS planning (Lederer and Mendelow 1986). In some firms, business strategies and plans exist only in the minds of a few senior managers and are not communicated to others (King 1978, Pyburn 1983); in other firms these information inputs are provided primarily through business plan documents that may not be sufficiently detailed to provide guidance for effective IS planning. Moreover, frequent changes in the business environment make a plan rapidly obsolete (Henderson and Sifonis 1988). The use of strategic IS and interorganizational systems for business joint ventures and value-added partnerships have amplified the criticality of constant interaction between business and IS planners so that changes in the business environment are quickly reflected in the IS planning process. Hence, many firms use an interactive environment and real-time coordination for information flow between business and IS planning systems. Indeed, a recent study by Ernst and Young on the effectiveness of total quality management (TQM) found that effective communication of the contents of the strategic plan throughout the organization was one of the “universal truths" of all TQM programs.

Various specific mechanisms have been used to facilitate the information flow between the two planning systems as discussed earlier. In addition, Johnston and Carrico (1988) found that firms that are successful in the strategic use of IS have joint task forces, involving business management and IT functions, that constantly evaluate the strategic potential of IT in their operations and Lederer and Sethi (1988) found that the level of problems in IS planning were lower for firms where the IS manager participated in strategic business planning. Thus, the presence of quality facilitative mechanisms is suggested as a necessity for IS planning success. Hence, the hypotheses:

HyPOTHEsEs 7A. The better the quality of facilitation mechanisms, the better will be the quality of the IS planning process.

HyPoTHEsEs 7B. The better the quality of facilitation mechanisms the better will be IS planning effectiveness.

Two items (Appendix 2) were used for measuring the quality of facilitation mechanisms.

Hypotheses Related to the Quality of Implementation Mechanisms

An excellent planning process may not provide the desired outcomes if it is not followed up with suitable implementation activities. Raghunathan and King (1988) found this category of management activities to be strongly related to planning success. Some of the common problems associated with implementation are: lack of implementation plans and migration strategies, lack of resources for implementation, lack of commitment to change, and the size of the development backlog (Lederer and Sethi 1988, Goodhue et al. 1988, Galliers 1991). Suitable mechanisms must be instituted to facilitate implementation. The mechanisms that are often used are: management control system for review and feedback, resource mobilization for the implementatior of plans, and top management and user involvement in the monitoring of implementation (Camillus 1975, Ein-Dor and Segev 1978, Lorange 1982, Doll 1985). These mechanisms help to provide flexibility in IS planning through timely feedback, the overcoming of the perennial problems of resource shortages, and greater control of implementation through monitoring systems. While the use of such mechanisms has great face validity and intuitive appeal, the significance of their use to effective IS planning has not been empirically demonstrated. Thus, the hypothesis:

HypOTHEsis 8A. The better the quality of implementation mechanisms the greater the IS planning effectiveness.

Four items (Appendix 2) were used to measure the quality of implementation mechanisms.

## Research Methodology

The selection of a methodology for an empirical study is dependent on the variables studied, the current state of knowledge, and the objectives of the study (Benbasat 1984, Galliers and Land 1987). Various practical limitations such as time, cost and availability of data also influence the choice.

Two methodologies that were considered to be appropriate for this study were survey and case study methodologies. Survey methods are useful for studying a large number of variables using a large sample size and rigorous statistical analysis; they provide greater external validity and easier generalization of results. Case studies are good for capturing the richness of the “process" dimensions and are appropriate when research and theory are in very early stages and smaller sample size and generalization of results are not major concerns. In IS planning, most of the prescriptive models are based on case studies of few organizations (McFarlan 1971, McFarlan et al. 1983, Pyburn 1983, Lederer and Mendelow 1987). Most of the prior large-scale empirical studies have been exploratory (Lederer and Mendelow 1986, Martino 1983, McLean and Soden 1977). These studies have provided the necessary background information on the planning process and identified the major issues and potential problems in IS planning. Research in this area is now at a stage where there is a need to verify and validate models and to generate research findings that are applicable across a wide variety of organizations. Thus, a field survey was considered to be the most appropriate methodology.

The head of the IS function responded to the survey. The use of a single respondent might be thought to create informant bias. However, since the target respondent was the top-level IS executive and since no other manager in an organization would have the necessary broad view to respond, other approaches were found to be infeasible to accomplish the study's objectives.

The data were collected and analyzed in 1989–1990 using a survey questionnaire that was dlesigned to measure the various constructs in the research model. Each construct was measured, wherever possible, with multi-item indicators aimed at tapping the underlying theoretical dimension. The items in the questionnaire were initially derived from an extensive literature review and were subject to a detailed analysis to identify the items measuring the various constructs. The questionnaire was pretested, initially with IS doctoral students and later with three IS faculty, for content validity, general format, and readability. Revisions were carried out and then the instrument was further tested with three senior IS executives in industry.

The target population included 720 companies, randomly chosen from the Corporate 1,000, a directory of the 1,000 largest manufacturing and service companies in the USA. The survey instrument was mailed to the head of the IS function. Two hundred and forty nine responses were received for a response rate of 35%.

## Discussion of Results

The analysis presented above has two important themes. One is the notion of a multidimensional perspective of IS planning success linking the two planning dimensions that capture the “means" and “ends" of the IS planning system. The second theme is the establishment of an organizational context for IS planning by the validation of a comprehensive model linking salient organizational factors with the planning dimensions. The results are discussed below in the context of these two broad themes.

Recent research, in strategic business planning, has highlighted the need for a multidimensional conceptualization of planning system success (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987). They identified two major dimensions: planning system capability, which measures the quality of the planning process or the “means,"and fulfillment of planning objectives, or the “ends" of the planning system. Although these concepts have been discussed to some extent in the prescriptive IS planning literature, very limited guidelines exist for operationalization of these constructs. With ever-increasing investments in IS and IS planning, there is an increasing recognition of the need to evaluate the benefits from these investments. Developing good evaluation measures, such as the ones developed in this study for the two planning dimensions, is therefore an important step toward the comprehensive evaluation of IS planning systems.

In this study, two dimensions were used to characterize the success of the IS planning system: the quality of the IS planning process and IS planning effectiveness. The results of canonical correlation analysis reveal that the multidimensional measure incorporates nearly equal loadings from each major dimension, thereby providing support for using such a conceptualization of IS planning success. The variance explained by each variable is about equal, emphasizing the need for not only an excellent planning process, but also for effective implementation, so that the planning objectives are realized. This conceptualization should provide a sound foundation and measurement instruments for future studies on IS planning.

The second objective of the study was to identify a set of organizational factors and determine their relationship with these two planning success dimensions. The results of canonical correlation analysis provide some insights to determining the strength of the relationships between the criterion and predictor sets of variables.

“Resources" is the most significant variable to be associated with the two planning dimensions, explaining about 28% of the variance. Since it was measured primarily as the efforts of, and inputs from, the principal participants (top management, users, and planning staff) in the planning process, this result highlights the importance of the involvement of these groups. They also confirm the findings of earlier studies that have stressed the importance of such involvement (King 1988a, Johnston and Carrico 1988, Lederer and Mendelow 1987, Goodhue et al. 1988).

The “future impact of IS," one of the dimensions of the role of IS, was found to be a significant factor that explained 24% of the variance. This confirms the findings of Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1990).

The combined effect of “resources” and “future role” can be inferred to be of great practical value. The guidelines provided by top management on the growth directions for the organization and the IS function, combined with users' estimates of their future opportunities and needs, are the most important information inputs for IS planning. These results are particularly significant in any organizational context where planning is considered as a resource drain and may be performed by diverting operations personnel to planning on a short-term and “when-required" basis. In such firms, one of the first targets for cut-back in times of a resource squeeze is planning, since the effects of the cut-back are not immediately fel.

Another important aspect with respect to resources is the quality of the resources. The mere involvement of top management and users may not provide the desired benefits if they do not understand the potential of IS technology in their respective areas. Johnston and Carrico (1988) found that a vision to see the opportunities for IT was an important internal factor for firms that were successful in the strategic deployment of IT. Hence, educating key members of senior management on the potential of IS technology becomes important to ensure that their inputs to IS planning are useful (Lederer and Mendelow 1988).

A closely related factor, the quality of facilitation mechanisms, is also significantly associated with the planning dimensions. The participation of the IS manager in business planning helps him/her to develop a broader business perspective, as well as provide an opportunity to explain the potential of IS technology to senior management.

Another important factor identified by the analysis is the quality of implementation mechanisms, which explains 24% of the variance. The implementation of plans has been a major bottleneck in IS planning and has left many firms dissatisfied with their IS planning (Lederer and Sethi 1988). Some of the common reasons cited for failure in irnplementation are resource shortages, the significant amount of efforts required from busy operations personnel, the substantial changes required in the existing systems, the significant investment in new systems to replace operational and effective existing systems, the lack of clear migration strategies to new information architectures, and the friction between IS and user personnel (Martin 1989, Goodhue et al. 1988, Brancheau et al. 1989).

These problems arise due to lack of attention to implementation strategies and the lack of organizational mechanisms to support implementation. This clearly suggests the need for, and value of, resource mobilization plans. close monitoring of plan implementation, the use of frequent and rigorous reviews and the feedback of implementation issues to subsequent planning processes so that realistic implementation constraints can be taken into account in formulating plans.

The canonical loading for the “quality of strategic business planning" is above the cut-off value (0.37) and therefore can be considered to be statistically significantly associated with the two planning dimensions. However, it explains only 5.77% of the variance in the canonical variate, suggesting that there are other variables with better explanatory power. There could be various reasons for this result. First, it could reflect the growing sophistication of IS planning. In some organizations, IS planning has been initiated prior to business planning, in contrast to the opposite sequence which is usually presumed. Second, the IS planning process often formally includes an initial phase in which top executives are interviewed and appropriate business-oriented guidance is received. Also, in an organization where there is adequate top management and user involvement in IS planning, a creative and conducive environment exists for incremental and informal inputs to IS planning, which may itself be sufficient to integrate the IS plans with organizational plans. A third reason could be that it is not the quality of business plans per se, but the level of alignment that is more relevant to success in IS planning. The fact that the quality of facilitation mechanisms, ensuring tighter linkages between the two planning systems, is a more significant variable provides additional evidence to support that premise. A well conceived business plan may not be useful if its plans and strategies are not incorporated in IS plans and therefore lack synergies in implementation. These reasons may reduce the importance of formal business plans, which has long been considered to be important in the conventional wisdom of IS planning. We can only speculate on these reasons in this study, but more research will be necessary to examine the important relationship between the IS planning dimensions and the integration or alignment of the two planning systems.

The canonical analysis also provides some other results that are somewhat different from the conventional wisdom of IS planning. Organizational size is not found to be related to the IS planning dimensions, which is contrary to the traditional expectations that large organizations will have better IS planning systems. IS researchers have had mixed findings in relating size to other IS variables and have alluded to the notion of threshold size, above which, size will not be a major determinant of IS characteristics. Perhaps, our sample population did not have adequate representation of firms below the threshold level to reflect the differences in planning characteristics. The results may also reflect the changing environment in which the growth in the power and versatility of PCs, combined with relatively low costs, make it possible for even small firms to integrate IS into their business operations and to have an effective IS planning system. Another possible explanation, provided by Goodhue et al. (1992a), is that large firms may have coordination difficulties in instituting a comprehensive IS planning system and be splintered by divergent interests, while smaller firms may be able to better coordinate their planning activities and thereby have a more effective planning system. A major issue in strategic IS planning is the “broad scope" of analysis resulting in intractable complexity and information overload, and it is conceivable that larger organizations may require a broader scope of analysis that could lead to more problems. Also, it has been found that “data integration," a major outcome of strategic IS planning, becomes more difficult when organizations become complex and there is significant heterogeneity among the subunits (Goodhue et al. 1992b), which is more common among large organizations than smaller ones. It seems that although large firms need to have better planning processes and stand to benefit significantly from IS planning they also encounter more implementation problems. Therefore, size seems to have both positive and negative effects on IS planning.

The lack of a significant relationship between “industry" and the planning dimensions may be attributed to the “coarse" classification of “service" and “manufacturing" which may have suppressed interindustry differences. However, it may well be that the increasing use of IT by all firms has outdated the time-honored distinction that some industries, such as banking and insurance, have more strategic potential for IS and therefore can be expected to have better IS planning

The fact that the “future role" planned for IS is important and “industry" is not important suggests that various firms in a given industry are selecting different roles, some choosing to use IS as a basis for competition and others in the same industry choosing to compete on other bases. For instance, 'Texaco considers IS to be important and does extensive IS planning while some other major oil firms do not (Richardson et al. 1990).

## Implications for Practice and Research

This study involves the identification of salient dimensions that may be used to characterize the efficacy of the IS planning system, the identification of a comprehensive set of organizational factors that relate to IS planning, and the empirical validation and assessment of these relationships.

The results of the analyses indicate that the two dimensions of planning system success—quality of the planning process and planning effectiveness—are valid and useful. Arnong the eight organizational factors that were considered, the most important ones were the resources provided for IS planning, the quality of implementation mechanisms, the future impact of IS, the quality of facilitation mechanisms, and the quality of strategic business planning.

The findings from this study have identified many new topics for further research. As well, the study has identified factors that conventional wisdom and intuition suggest as important which are, in fact, not empirically shown to be so. For instance, the significant variation in the quality of planning process and planning effectiveness within industry provides an opportunity for a study at a more detailed level to determine the nature of the differences in the deployment of IS between firms within the same industry and the factors influencing them. It would also be useful to examine how other strategies are used in lieu of, or in conjunction with IS to achieve the firm's business cbjectives.

Of course, this study did not exhaust the range of organizational factors that might influence IS planning. Rather it simultaneously assessed those that are most naturally derived from prior research. Other promising variables that might be of interest are: planning climate, organizational form in terms of bureaucracy/adhocracy (Mintzberg 1979), prior experience (success/failure) with IS planning, and stakeholder relations.

The study also provides some interesting findings concerning factors that can be controlled by management and which should therefore be of interest to practitioners. The findıng that organizational size is not significant clearly sends a message that even small firms can perform high-quality and effective IS planning. The significant intra-industry differences in the use of IS suggests that it is not essential for firms to use IS in a similar manner to others in their industry. Firms should develop IS plans which are implementable and synergistic with their strengths, rather than creating systems that could become strategic risks (Vitale 1986).

The importance of the participation of users and top management in IS planning and the use of various organizational mechanisms to facilitate their participation provides significant amplification of the importance of these pragmatic and controllable factors.

In addition to its research potential, the operationalization of the “means and ends" planning dimensions may be used to develop a diagnostic instrument to evaluate a firm's IS planning system and to assess potential problem areas in the planning process. More generally, the result that shows both to be of approximately equal importance can serve to heighten a manager's awareness of these two important aspects of IS planning.

This study uses cross-sectional data that has obvious limitations in explaining causality. Another limitation of the study is the use of a single respondent in an organization thereby creating some possibilities for informant bias. Future research could address some of these limitations by collecting longitudinal data from multiple respondents.

Thus, this study provides results that have significant implications for improving our understanding of the important relationships between organizational attributes and IS planning, for guiding future research, and for practitioners.\*
