The research reported here is an adaptation of a model developed to measure Planning Systems Success, to the information systems (IS) context. This study is motivated by the increasing importance given to IS planning in recent years, the lack of an empirical model in the IS literature to measure a construct as important as IS planning system success, and the usefulness of developing such a model to help guide future research efforts addressing the issue of IS planning effectiveness. An operational model for measuring IS planning system success is developed in terms of two interrelated dimensions: IS planning system capabilities and extent of fulfillment of key IS planning objectives. Due regard is paid to the call for greater attention to methodological issues in developing such measures. The model meets the measurement criteria suggested in the literature.

## Introduction

Ithough IS and IS planning in organizations have grown in importance in recent Lyears, there has been very little research directed towards developing a model to measure the IS planning system success construct. This research attempts to develop such a model with specific emphasis placed on methodological issues in model formulation and serves as a first step in bringing rigor in theory testing and modelling to an important area of IS research.

## The Importance of a Model to Measure IS Planning System Success

IS planning has been described as a managerial process for integrating IS considerations into the corporate planning process, linking application of IS to business goals, and determining information requirements to help meet an organization's short and long term goals (Earl 1989, Galliers 1987a, Wiseman 1988). Cash et al. (1992) have suggested that IS planning should be merged with the primary strategic thrusts of the firm, with the focus on developing an IS plan within the context of evolving organizational strategies. The growing importance of IS planning has been recognized in the literature (Brancheau and Wetherbe 1987, Earl 1983, Galliers 1987a, Wilson 1989). Earl's (1983) results indicate that planning was the dominant concern among IT managers surveyed. Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) found strategic planning for IS at the top of their list of ten key issues. Studies by Galliers (1987a) and Wilson (1989) both indicate that a significant majority of organizations undertake IS planning. The importance of IS planning continues to be recognized, as is evidenced by more recent surveys (Andersen Consulting 1990, Champy 1990, Niederman et al. 1991). Although IS planning has become an important organizational issue, empirical research in the IS area has not, to date, provided a model to operationalize IS planning system' success. In the absence of an acceptable operational measure of the construct of IS planning system success, any attempts to justify the importance of IS planning, and the resources devoted to it can, at best, be weak efforts lacking substantive support. The need for research on IS planning effectiveness has been stressed by IS researchers. Boynton and Zmud (1987) point out that an “important direction for future research is the development of a better understanding of those issues that have been least attended to within the IT planning literature . , . projects aimed at better understanding how to carry out an effective planning process are likely to be more significant than projects focused on identifying additional issues to be addressed in the planning process" [p. 67]. IS planning researchers are urged to define and develop IS planning effectiveness constructs appropriately, and with particular attention paid to measurement criteria such as content validity, convergent validity and discriminant validity (Venkatraman 1985–1986, p. 72). Sethi and King (1991) point out that research in the IS field is being criticized for making more progress in “identifying research questions than in answering them." This, they state, is true largely because of the methodological problems associated with IS research. The usefulness of results in many IS areas, for example, has been undermined by a lack of well-defined constructs and measures.

The research reported here is an attempt to develop a valid measure of an important IS construct, namely, IS planning system success. Due regard is paid to the call for greater attention to methodological issues in developing such measures. The next section describes the approach used in the present study to develop this measure.

## An Approach to Conceptualizing and Operationalizing IS Planning System Success

IS planning is a complex and multifaceted activity encompassing a broad range of managerial, system, and technological components. Various contingent frameworks have been proposed for IS planning (Cash et al. 1992, Earl 1989, Galliers 1991). These approaches have suggested that IS planning can be viewed from a number of different perspectives such as planning context, organizational importance of IS, methodological emphasis, and level of commitment and involvement of organizational members. Assessment of the success of IS planning is also complex because of the need to consider a variety of attributes, many of which are context-related (Galliers 1987b). For example, different stakeholders—IS managers versus user managers versus top executives—may have different views of success (Earl 1990). Success may be measured in terms of thebenefits of the planning process (such as, improved top management support and involvement, better understanding of the organization and its business), and/or in terms of the applications that are developed as a result of planning. Issues such as method, process, and implementation have been found to be related to the "success" or “unsuccess" of IS planning (Earl 1990, p. 272) and, hence, can be factors in evaluating IS planning success.

It has been suggested that IS researchers are likely to find the paradigms and research frameworks proposed to date in the strategic planning research arena to be useful for incorporating IS planning success concepts (Venkatraman 1985–1986). A number of close similarities have been noted in the research streams in the areas of strategic planning and IS planning. "Both streams began approaching their respective research objectives by focusing on selected case studies . . . later studies on strategic planning have moved toward comparative analysis using samples . . . there are increasing concerns that MIS planning research should (also) move away from generalizations based on isolated case studies toward a systematic program of comparative research" (Venkatraman 1985–1986, p. 69). Studies such as Pyburn's (1983) empirical study (of the IS strategic planning practices in eight organizations) took the important first steps in this direction. Comparative surveys of European and Australian organizations have been undertaken by Earl (1983, 1990), Galliers (1987a), Ruohonen (1991) and Wilson (1989).

Recognizing the similarities in the two research streams, the absence of a rigorous and empirically grounded model of IS planning success, and the methodological weaknesses in the IS planning area (Sethi and King 1991), the present study attempts to model IS planning systems success using proven methodology from the strategic planning area to guide the model formulation. The primary focus of the present study is on the development of a methodologically rigorous model. Because of this emphasis on methodological rigor in model-building, the scope of the success measure has been limited to two constructs. The development of the two constructs is described below.

Based on Cameron and Whetten's (1983) work on the measurement of effectiveness, a number of distinct approaches to study the success of IS planning can be identified. The goal-centered approach evaluates success in terms of fulfillment of planning goals (King 1988). The comparative approach evaluates the effectiveness of planning in one organization in relation to planning in comparable organizations (Pyburn 1983, Earl 1990, Galliers 1991). A third approach evaluates planning effectiveness against normative standards for an ideal system (Cash et al. 1992). Finally, an improvement-oriented approach evaluates the current planning system in terms of improvement over a period of time and/or after certain actions are taken to improve the system (King and Srinivasan 1983). The present study uses a combination of goal-centered and improvement-oriented approaches in modelling IS planning system success.

A study by Venkatraman and Ramanujam (1987) used these two approaches to develop a planning systems success model in terms of two distinct but interrelated planning dimensions: improvements in the capability of the planning system, and extent of fulfillment of key planning objectives. Theirs was the first systematic attempt, in the strategic planning research area, at operationalizing the complex constructs related to the design and success of strategic planning systems, and developing a valid measurement scheme for use in research settings. The present study is a first attempt to develop a similar measurement scheme for IS planning effectiveness following the rigor of the Venkatraman and Ramanujam model. Based on their framework, this study develops the dimensions of systems capability and fulfillment of objectives for evaluating IS planning success. The former represents the improvement-oriented approach referred to earlier, and the latter the goal-centered approach. The construct validity of these two dimensions of IS planning system success is evaluated. Results provide support for the conceptualization of IS planning system success in terms of these two dimensions as shown in Figure 1. These results can be useful to IS researchers interested in studying the relationships between IS planning effectiveness and various aspects of organizational processes.

## Dimensions of IS Planning System Success

## Fulfillment of IS Planning System Objectives Dimension

The fulfillment of objectives, i.e., the effectiveness of an organization's IS planning system, is a multifaceted concept (King 1988) and therefore needs to be viewed from different perspectives. Based on a review of the IS planning and strategic planning literature, the levels of achievement of each of several planning objectives are proposed as indicators of these different perspectives. The planning objectives considered in this study (along with literature support) are: Enhancing management development (Koory and Medley 1987); Predicting future trends (Galliers 1991); Improving short-term and long-term IS performance (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988); Improving decision-making (Ein-dor and Segev 1978); Avoiding problem areas (Ramanujam et al. 1986); Increasing user satisfaction (Baroudi and Orlikowski 1988); Improving systems integration (King and Srinivasan 1983); and Improving resource allocation (Sethi 1978).

## IS Planning System Capability Dimension

Planning system capability is conceptualized in terms of the ability of a system to foster control as well as creativity (Ramanujam et al. 1986). Nolan's (1979) growth model of controls and slacks in the development stages of data processing is an example of how both creativity and control can be applied at the appropriate stages to optimize IS effectiveness. IS planning should have the in-built flexibility to allow adaptation of IS processes to new opportunities as they present themselves, and thus foster creativity. At the same time, it should seek to balance this with adequate control mechanisms so that frequent adaptations on the grounds of creativity do not lead to loss of control. While the former should emphasize the ability to anticipate surprises and crises, identify new opportunities and adapt to unanticipated changes (Ansoff 1984), the latter should focus on control issues through mechanisms such as systems integration and coordinated decision-making (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1989). Appropriate attention given to these two aspects in the design of IS can be a key element in influencing its ultimate effectiveness. The items used to measure this dimension (along with literature support) are: Ability to anticipate surprises and crises (Ansoff 1984); Flexibility to adapt to unanticipated changes (Ansoff 1984); As a mechanism for identifying new opportunities (Ansoff 1965); Role in identifying key problem areas (Steiner 1979); As a tool for managerial motivation (Steiner 1979); As a tool for managerial control (King and Cleland 1978); As a mechanism for integrating diverse functions (Grant and King 1982); As a basis for enhancing innovation (Taylor and Hussey 1982); As a tool to improve cross-functional systems integration (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988); As a tool to improve coordination of decision making (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988); and As a tool to improve integration of IS into organization (Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1988).

## Research Methodology

## Data Collection

The data used in this research were obtained using a mail survey. A self-administered questionnaire instrument was mailed to 800 information systems executives chosen at random from a list of 3,000 IS executives. There were 202 responses, of which 192 were complete and usable as the sample for this study. The response rate of 25% is similar to that in comparable studies; due to lack of information on nonrespondents it was not possible to test if the responding group differed from the nonrespondents. Table 1 provides an overview of the general characteristics of the sample. The data are skewed in favor of manufacturing firms and bigger firms with sales of more than 50 million dollars. The use of the mail survey as a data-gathering technique restricts the scope for tapping additional information from individual organizations within the context of their specific situations. This factor should be considered when generalizing the results. A further limitation is the use of responses of IS executives and the resultant single respondent perspective that may have affected the results. IS executives were chosen as the respondents because they were considered to be the most appropriate individuals, within an organization, to respond to questions on systems capabilities.

## Analysis and Discussion of Results

The indicators of the two dimensions were measured using multi-item scales. Respondents were asked to indicate, on a one-to-five scale, the extent to which the various objectives of IS planning had been achieved, and the extent of improvement in the various capabilities of the IS planning system. The measurement properties of the two operational indicators of the dimensions of the IS planning system success construct were evaluated by assessing the four components of construct validity elucidated in the Venkatraman and Ramanujam paper (1987). These components are: Content validity; Unidimensionality (and convergent validity); Discriminant validity; and Predictive validity.

Content validity: To ensure content validity of the indicators of the two dimensions, the survey instrument was presented to two IS executives who commented on the appropriateness and completeness of the items and lack of ambiguity in interpreting the items.

Assessment of measurement properties of unidimensionality, discriminant validity and predictive ability was carried out by replicating the four different models reported in the Venkatraman and Ramanujam study. The tests were carried out using Joreskog's analysis of covariance structures. The first two models examined the unidimensionality of the two individual dimensions of systems capabilities and fulfillment of objectives. Joreskog's conventions of measurement model specifications were used. Assessment of the fit of each of these two models was based on the following set of statistics: the chi-square statistic and associated p-value; the parameter estimates and the associated level of statistical significance given by the t-statistic; the goodness of fit index. The probability level associated with the given chi-square statistic indicates the probability (p) of obtaining a large chi-square value given that the hypothesized model is supported. The higher the value of ${ \dot { p } } ,$ the better is the fit. A value of $p > 0 . 1 0$ is considered as an indication of satisfactory fit (Lawley and Maxwell 1971). Since exclusive reliance on the chi-square statistic is criticized (Fornell and Larcker 1981), Bentler and Bonett's (1980) incremental fit index—which is an indication of the practical significance of the model in explaining the data—is used to complement the chi-square statistic. The general rule of thumb for this statistic is a value of 0.90 or higher (Bentler and Bonett 1980). Models were estimated using LISREL VI.

The operational model of IS planning system success described in this study represents a first step in the direction of specifying a theoretically supportable model which pays specific attention to construct measurement issues. Future research aimed at refining this two-dimensional conceptualization of the IS planning system success construct (whether it is done through refinement of the items comprising the operational indicators, or through participation of more than one respondent from organizations to capture multiple perspectives, or through use of other statistical estimation procedures) can make a significant contribution to research in the IS arena. Such a contribution can open up opportunities for further research for IS researchers seeking empirical support for extant theories linking organizational characteristics to various degrees of IS planning success.

## Raghunathan • Raghunathan

In concluding, an important limitation of this study is addressed. The primary goal of this research has been to make a contribution to the IS planning literature by bringing rigor into an important area of IS research that has had little work in theory testing undertaken in this style. This focus on methodological measurement has limited the scope of the paper in that a more comprehensive inclusion of variables measuring the success construct has not been undertaken. Future studies in the direction of the line of research begun by this study can add to the literature by addressing the success construct from a number of different perspectives suggested in the literature.\*
