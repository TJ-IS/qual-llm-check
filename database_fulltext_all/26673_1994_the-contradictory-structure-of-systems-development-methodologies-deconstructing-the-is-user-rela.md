# The Contradictory Structure of Systems Development Methodologies: Deconstructing the IS-User Relationship in Information Engineering

Cynthia Mathis Beath

Management Information Sciences Department

Edwin L. Cox School of Business

Southern Methodist University

Dallas, Texas 75275-0333

Wanda J. Orlikowski

Information Technologies Group

Sloan School of Management

Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Cambridge, Massachusetts 02142-1347

In this paper we show that systems development methodologies may contain incompatible assumptions about the role of users and information systems (IS) personnel during systems development. Using deconstruction, we analyze and interpret a systems development methodology currently receiving considerable attention—Information Engineering. We find that this methodology's characterization of IS-user relations and, in particular, its recommended partitioning of responsibility between IS and users is inconsistent and contradictory. Despite a heavy emphasis on user involvement, users are given a relatively passive role to play during development. At the same time, users are expected to sign off on projects and take responsibility for project outcomes. We suggest that such prescriptions, when put into action during systems development, make the relationship between users and IS personnel problematic. Further, we argue that the contradictions we surface in the methodology reflect contradictions and ideologies in the context within which systems development occurs. Our analysis raises important questions about the relationship between the production and consumption of information technology in organizations.

## Introduction

Tn the information systems (IS) literature, the relationship between users and IS personnel is typically seen as central to the success of systems development projects. This is evident in the early work of Churchman (1968), Lucas (1975), Bostrom and Heinen (1977), and Ginzberg (1979, 1981), which stressed user involvement (see also the reviews by Ives and Olson (1984) and Melone (1990)). More recently others have called for a greater partnership between IS and the line (Rockart 1988, Henderson 1990). Despite these endorsements of user involvement, actual engagement of users in systems development appears to have been less than successful—being either symbolic and hence ineffective (Robey and Markus 1984, Hirschheim and Newman 1991, Beirne and Ramsay 1988), or problematic due to poor communication and conflicting interests (Franz and Robey 1984, Lyytinen 1987, Newman and Noble 1990, Newman and Robey 1992). These researchers have sought to explain such a lack of success in terms of the motivations and mindsets of users and analysts, as well as in organizational elements such as incentives and rewards, lack of adequate resources, and inappropriate coordination mechanisms. Their prescriptions for overcoming these difficulties have included enhanced user training, increased business exposure for analysts, more vigorous and rigorous use of structured methods, and a variety of mechanisms for facilitating closer interaction between users and IS, such as interdisciplinary project teams and liaison positions to support lateral coordination. Some researchers have proposed new development methodologies specifically designed to facilitate more significant user involvement, for example, ETHICS (Mumford and Weir 1979), Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland 1981), and Cooperative Design (Greenbaum and Kyng 1991).

The research reported in this paper began by asking whether the persistent difficulties in relations between IS and users were influenced by more than just a lack of motivation on the part of users and analysts, or weaknesses in IS development methodologies. In particular, we asked whether the difficulties might not also be rooted in more fundamental organizational design choices such as the division of labor between users and IS, the locus of control over information technology resources, and the allocation of investments in technical and work expertise. Questions about the distribution of authority over information technology in organizations have largely been glossed over or subsumed within debates about centralization and decentralization of information technology resources. IS practitioners tend to take for granted their role as technical experts and their control over technology resources (Beath 1991, 1993). The IS research community, in turn, has rarely challenged or scrutinized this way of distributing authority.

In this paper we attempt such a scrutiny by examining, in depth, the contents of a widely-applied systems development methodology. By understanding how this one development methodology lays out the relationship between users and IS personnel, and how it distributes tasks, resources and responsibilities among them, we can gain insight into how users and IS might interact in practice. This study builds on research suggesting that systems development methodologies significantly inform and shape the cognitions and actions of organizational members engaged in systems development (Boland 1979, Hirschheim and Klein 1989, Markus and Bjørn-Andersen 1987, Orlikowski 1992, Robey and Markus 1984).

As a basis for our investigation, we drew on an approach not yet commonly used in IS research—deconstruction—to facilitate an in-depth examination of the specific content of a written document. A deconstruction of à document reveals the dependence of that text on taken-for-granted assumptions that may suppress, distort, marginalize, or exclude certain ways of thinking (Kildruff 1993). While related to linguistic and hermeneutic interpretations (Boland 1985, 1991, Boland and Day 1989, Hirschheim and Newman 1991, Elkjær et al. 1991, Preston 1991), a deconstructive analysis goes beyond the text itself in revealing how contradictions and distortions present in the text are reflections of conditions in the world.

After a brief overview of the method of deconstruction, we describe the steps we followed in analyzing the Information Engineering (IE) systems development methodology.' We then present and discuss our analysis. We conclude by raising some questions that we believe are central to the research and practice of systems development.

## Deconstruction

Deconstruction² is often described as a tool for “de-constructing" or taking things apart, whether they be philosophical arguments, literary texts, historical narratives, conventions, canons, or value systems of any kind. As an analytic strategy, “to deconstruct" is to examine cultural artifacts with “an eye sharply trained to look for contradictions" (Norris 1991, p. 137) and ambivalences (Cooper 1989). Deconstruction's strengths lie in revealing implicit meanings and unacknowledged biases. It therefore seemed a good approach to draw on for closely examining the form and content of the IE methodology.

We were also drawn to deconstruction because it provided a strategy for challenging a text in its own right, as distinct from a strategy for challenging its author. In deconstructive analysis the author as producer of the text is “decentered," and the analytical focus is centered on the text and how it can be read and interpreted. Indeed, from a deconstructive perspective, since any text is separate from its author, multiple readings and interpretations of the text can be generated. Our particular reading of the IE methodology focused on the roles and interactions of users and IS personnel. Our interpretations are therefore concerned only with those relations; other deconstructions could examine this same methodology to study other questions.

How does deconstruction proceed? There are several possible approaches, of which two are described here. One is to identify the premise of the text and then to use that premise to analyze the text itself. Arrington and Francis (1989) use this technique in deconstructing Jensen's (1983) article "Organization Theory and Methodology," explicitly taking the text's criteria for scientific work and then using deconstruction to show how the text violates its own criteria. Kildruff (1993) follows a similar strategy in his examination of March and Simon's (1958) book, Organizations. He shows how the model of employee-as-laboring-machine criticized by the text persists and reappears in the alternative model of employee-as-computing-machine. A second approach is to search the text for rhetorical devices such as metaphors, contrasts, logical sequences, or marginalization (via quotation marks, footnotes or, as illustrated here, parentheses). These are then interpreted in order to surface hidden assumptions or unacknowledged implications. Joanne Martin employs this approach to expose ideological elements in a speech given by a corporate executive (1990a) and in Weber's (1946) treatise on bureaucracy (1990b). Our study followed a combination of these deconstructive approaches.

Before proceeding to our study, we introduce two ideas developed by deconstructionists, différance and supplement, as they form important components of our analysis.

(1) Différance. All of us understand concepts because we can compare them to different or similar concepts we already know something about. For example, we can distinguish colors such as red, blue, and yellow because of how different each is relative to the others. Sometimes we focus our conceptual distinctions by contrasting a concept with one that appears to be orthogonal to it, for example, life/death, white/ black, man/woman, speech/writing, cause/effect, and principal/agent. While the two elements placed in such a dichotomous relationship may seem to be mutually exclusive or in simple opposition, deconstruction suggests that it is often the case in practice that one element of the dichotomy is privileged over the other. The dichotomy— while appearing at first to be a contrast of equals—often masks a relationship of superiority. That is, one element is treated as the essential, primary, definitive member of the pair, while the other is treated as secondary and derivative, typically being undervalued or suppressed. Derrida coined the term différance to remind us that surface difference often implies deferring, both in the sense of submission and in the sense of postponement. For example, design and programming are distinctly different development phases, but, in addition to being different, programming defers to design, because it is dependent on it, and also because it (usually) does not begin until the design is complete, so that there is postponement as well. Dichotomies are not always specifically identified in a text. Rather, the différance between two things may be hidden in the metaphors, silences, double-entendres, or marginalized elements of a text. Différance is often upheld by a logical fallacy, as expressed in some form of tautology, ideology, inconsistency, law-of-nature claim, or convenient assumption. To deconstruct a dichotomy is to search for différance and, having found it, to challenge its validity, Iegitimacy and relevance in a particular context.

(2) Supplement. The notion of supplement builds on that of différance and refers to the supplementary relationship of the “deferred" element to the privileged one in a dichotomy. Dichotomies, by exaggerating the differences between two elements, lead us to ignore their common roots and to undermine what may be an inherent inseparability. In such cases, the privileging of one side conceals the often critically important consummating or compensating role of the other. To illustrate, in the design/ programming example above, privilege is given to design; but until the design is realized though programming, it generates few tangible benefits. The interdependence of two opposing elements of a dichotomy can often be seen in the privileged element's reliance on the supplemental one for meaning—death defines life, citizens grant authority to their elected officials, workers give managers something to manage, women give birth to men. Derrida's notion of supplementarity highlights the way in which the deferred element in a dichotomy necessarily compensates for the inherent inadequacy or incompleteness of the privileged element.

The use of deconstruction in the field of management studies is still relatively new. Like all analytic approaches, deconstruction has some weaknesses (Arrington and Francis 1989, Cooper 1989), and like all nontraditional approaches, it is relatively controversial (for example, see Ellis 1989). Deconstruction is sometimes criticized for its difficult concepts and arcane vocabulary. Beyond the introduction of two important ideas from deconstruction, we have refrained from using the convoluted vocabulary of deconstruction in our paper and have attempted to keep our analysis and discussion grounded in specific examples. A second concern is that deconstructive procedures can be used to dispute claims that are obscure or simply inconvenient. When a deconstruction focuses on extreme positions that hardly anyone holds, such criticism would seem warranted. In our work we have used deconstruction to examine and question positions that are fairly widely held in the information systems field. Another limitation of deconstruction is the lack of guidance on how to extend the textual analysis to specific conditions and social structures in the world (Kildruff 1993). We draw on the logic of structuration theory (Giddens 1984) to guide us in raising questions about the connection of systems development methodologies to their institutional contexts.

## The Study

Our research study focused on a single methodology that reflects widely-held norms and assumptions of systems development and that is characteristic of the systems development methodologies advocated by practitioners and taught in educational and corporate settings. We selected the IE methodology for three reasons. First, it is one of the most recent and most comprehensive methodologies to appear in the IS field, hence it should represent the “state-of-the-art" in thinking about the IS-user relationship. Second, it is sufficiently well known and widely used, so that an analysis of it would be relevant to a large component of the IS community. Third, it serves as the foundation for two prominent integrated Computer-Aided Software Engineering (CASE) tools—the Information Engineering Workbench™M (from KnowledgeWare) and Information Engineering Facility™M (from Texas Instruments)hence its influence is potentially longer-term and more far-reaching than other comparable methodologies. While James Martin has published a number of books on the Information Engineering methodology, we chose to analyze his most recent (1989, 1990a, 1990b) three volume set entitled Information Engineering (which we refer to collectively as "the text").

We began our analysis by selecting chapters from the text that we judged, based on an initial review of the whole, to be most likely to deal with the IS-user relationship. These chapters included discussions of the objectives of the IE methodology, characterizations of users or analysts, specifications of their roles and responsibilities, descriptions of the exchanges between users and analysts (such as analysis and design sessions, contracts, reviews, and approvals), and depictions of end user computing. Table 1 lists the titles of the 45 chapters in the three volume text and indicates those we selected. The 11 chapters we chose represent 25% of the text chapters, and 22% of the total number of pages in the text. We selected both introductory chapters and chapters dealing with the specific details of what analysts and users do. By doing so, we captured the methodology's justification as well as its prescribed operation. In our opinion, both are important aspects of the methodology, as those adopting it will be guided not only by the specific steps, but also by the rationale behind those steps.

Having selected the data, we proceeded with the analysis of the text. Initially, each author analyzed the data on her own. We used a mode of analysis referred to as "dialoging with the data" that is common in investigations that seek to discern the meaning of text or action. In such investigations “the researcher immerses her/himself in the data, reads and rereads, and dwells with the data, so s/he may achieve closeness to them and a sense of the whole" (Tesch 1990, p. 93). One author worked with a research assistant; the two first read the text separately, then a second time together and aloud. Eventually, these two extracted 221 passages. These passages were then assigned substantive labels such as "Portrayal of Users," "Objectives of Joint Application Design," and “Analysts' role and responsibilities." The other researcher worked alone, reading the text slowly and using the same deconstructive procedure recommended by Joanne Martin (1990a) to identify a set of 245 passages.

With the two sets of passages identified, we met to confirm or resolve our individual interpretations of the passages and arrive at a shared understanding. Because our objective was to reveal the text's meaning by laying open its rhetorical devices (rather than by coding the text into predefined categories as, for example, in a content analysis), we cast our analytic net widely—focusing initially on the union of passages we had individually identified rather than their intersection. We discussed each of the passages and jointly decided on the appropriateness of our individual interpretations. We discarded from our sample those passages where we did not agree, finishing with a set of 73 passages, 41 (56%) of which had been originally identified by both of us in our individual transcriptions. Having sorted these selected passages by the substantive themes assigned earlier, we then searched for similarities across these themes, collapsing them into broader ones where appropriate. We concluded with two primary motifs that appeared to reflect the underlying characterization of the IS-user relationship: "The Ideology of User Involvement," and “The False Dichotomy between Users and IS Analysts."³ Below, we discuss these motifs in detail and provide the supporting evidence for our interpretation.

Before proceeding, three comments are in order. First, as noted earlier, deconstruction inquires about a reader's interpretation of a text; it does not seek to uncover the intentions of the text's author (James Martin, in this case). From the viewpoint of a deconstruction any text represents a coming-together of knowledge from its field (Norris 1991). In many senses this text is the work of many people, mostly unidentified, whose intentions may be neither consistent nor knowable. Speculating on these intentions is viewed by deconstructionists as relatively pointless. They concentrate instead on identifying what a reader of the text could find there. We follow this approach. Second, while most deconstructions have focused on polished, accepted masterpieces of artistic or scholarly acclaim, we are concerned in this case with a text that makes no claim to being theoretical or literary. It is a practitioners' handbook. Consequently, our analysis ignores (where other deconstructions might not) poor writing style, naive or precritical reasoning, and unsupported claims of effectiveness. For example, the absence of empirical evidence to support many of the text's claims for the value of the IE methodology might subject it to considerable challenge in some deconstructions. Third, we acknowledge that deconstruction is an ongoing process, with no obvious end point. Our treatment only examines one aspect of the text—the IS-user relationship—so the text can also be deconstructed around other themes. Or, other authors might choose to continue to deconstruct the IS-user relationship in this text. Finally, our interpretation, because it is a text too, can itself be deconstructed in turn.

## Analysis and Discussion

In this section we draw on our sample of selected text passages to lay out and support our interpretation. Quoted passages from the three volume text (James Martin 1989, 1990a, and 1990b) are referenced by an index [offset in square brackets like this] employing a four-part coding scheme showing the volume number, chapter number, page number, and paragraph number where the passage appears. That is, [I, 5, 107, ¶6] refers to a passage in the sixth paragraph from the top of page 107 in the fifth chapter of the first volume. Fragments of paragraphs, pseudo code, quotes, lists, figures, and boxes are counted as whole paragraphs. The text uses italics for emphasis; where we wish to add emphasis, we use underlining.

As a first observation, we note that the text does not make explicit its intended audience. Where many books indicate—on the back, on the dust cover, or in the preface—the type of reader being addressed, this one does not. The text is written in a generally prescriptive style, although it remains silent about who should implement its recommendations. The text appears to be addressing IS practitioners in that many of the recommended tasks and responsibilities are technical ones, and these currently fall within the purview of IS personnel. We draw the implication that it is the IS analysts or project managers who are expected to execute the text's prescriptions.

As indicated above, our reading of the text yielded two primary motifs that characterize the IS-user relationship. We analyze each of these in turn, presenting the textual evidence and our discussion side by side, so that readers can assess our interpretations in context.

## The Ideology of User Involvement

We begin with an examination of the overall characterization of the relationship between IS and users. The text takes as a general conviction the importance ofinvolving users throughout the systems development process:

“A basic principle of information engineering is that end users should be involved at every stage" [I, 5, 120, ¶3].

The text makes frequent reference to “strong" user involvement or participation in the IE process. For example, on page two of the first volume the ten main characteristics of the IE methodology are listed. The eighth item is:

“IE involves end users strongly at each of the stages" [I, 1, 2, ¶4]

Similarly, in a later chapter, we find that the analysis stage (which IE refers to as Business Area Analysis or BAA) "requires intensive user involvement" [I, 5, 107, ¶6], while during system design it is

“Particularly important, [that] the end users should be as deeply involved as possible in the design process" [I, 5, 112, ¶2].

The IE methodology is premised on having users work with analysts to develop their information systems. This premise is also apparent in the attachment of the word “joint" to the names of some of the phases, as in “Joint Requirements Planning" [III, 7, 127, ¶1] and "Joint Application Design" [III, 8, 143, ¶1], where "joint" apparently refers to the users and IS personnel sharing in the activities of planning and design. Further evidence is available in the detailed description of IE phases. For example, the description of the analysis stage reads:

"[BAA], like all of information engineering, needs close cooperation between end users and I.S. professionals" [II, 11, 209, ¶4].

Later, in the description of Joint Application Design (JAD) sessions, the text notes that:

"The key players are the end users . . ." [III, 8, 145, 14],

that user participation is valued because;

“Well-conducted JAD sessions result in designs which meet the end users' needs better because the design largely comes from the users" [III, 7, 128, ¶7],

and that the session leader:

“. . . puts IS and end users on equal'terms and establishes them as partners" [III, 7, 132, 2].

Despite this stated commitment to the value and importance of user involvement, a closer examination of the procedures and roles specified suggests a contrary interpretation. Rather than recommending mechanisms to bring about joint problem solving, equal participation, or shared responsibility for the system, we find that the text's prescriptions actually limit the engagement of users in systems development.

Consider first the extent of user involvement. The text claims that:

“End-user participation is essential in BAA studies" [II, 11, 209, ¶7]

However, later in the same paragraph it declares that:

". . . one or two end users are allocated full time to the study. . . . Where full-time participation is not possible, users . . . should be allocated on a part-time basis with, perhaps, a quarter of their time committed" [II, 11, 209, ¶7].

The initial position is that user input is “essential" to the success of the analysis activity. But this claim is revealed as ideological when the extent of user input is specified and respecified. One or two users, some of whom may have quarter-time appointments, will be at a distinct disadvantage relative to the full-time analysts. Token participants commonly attempt to limit their visibility, are inhibited by their isolation, and adopt a variety of conservative and low-risk responses to their situation (Kanter 1977). In such situations, user participants will likely defer to the IS analysts. Further reinforcing the inconsistency around the extent of user involvement is the declaration in Box 11.2 of the BAA chapter that lays out the “steps in business area analysis." In describing the formation of the BAA project team, the text notes:

"There may be an end user on the team" [I, 11, 213, ¶1].

The limited engagement of users in IE is also evident in the nature of user involvement prescribed by the text. The text implies that user participation should be closely directed by the IS analysts:

“Analysts guide end users through joint application design sessions" [I, 1, 24, ¶2].

"The users are guided through the system design procedure and are encouraged to express their needs and concerns as a design emerges" [1, 5, 121, ¶2].

In these descriptions users are assigned a passive role, while the analysts dominate the action. Consider the following description of the JAD process:

“A short seminar should be given to those users who will assist in the project" [I, 11, 211, 13],

“The users should be given a brief training about the meaning of the diagrams prior to the JAD session. They should be given diagrams. . . . They should be shown relevant data models. . . . They should be asked to study these diagrams as preparation for the new design work" [III, 8, 147, ¶4]

"The relevant portion of [the previously developed models] should be explained to the participants” [II, 11, 212, ¶1].

The text recommends that during JAD, the users:

“. . . need to be familiarized with what [JAD] is and what they are expected to contribute" [III, 8, 155, 2].

Where users have little knowledge of the IE process and where their participation is structured and closely guided, they are less likely to feel like equal participants

An examination of the procedures specified for communicating with users during BAA indicates that the analysts are in charge of the interaction. For example, in end-user interviewing, the “appropriate users" are to be consulted “to validate the model as it evolves" [I, 14, 283, ¶1]. The suggestion is that analysts devise a model and then refine it with user input. Likewise, the next procedure reads:

“Extend the process decomposition using the analyst's own knowledge and then discuss

the model with users and managers to extend it and refine it further" [I, 14, 283, ¶1].

In cases “where in-depth expertise [of the business process] already exists" among the analysts, the text suggests that “Informal contact [with users] suffices" [II, 14, 283, ¶1]. The text is silent on who determines if the analysts have sufficient in-depth knowledge of the user domain, or how, if at all, this is established to the users' satisfaction.

The text occasionally refers to the user participants as “players," for example:

"The key players are the end users who want a system . . ." [III, 8, 145, ¶4].

This usage, together with the directing and guiding of users seen above, suggests a possible unconscious subtext or double-entendre in the text. At least two readings of the use of the term “players" are possible. One is that this is a sports metaphor, the other is that it is an acting metaphor. These metaphors are closely related, depicting users taking part in an organized performance with defined roles and game plans. Players participate on others' terms, in that the rules of the game or the script of the play are devised by and commanded by others (for example, game officials, umpires, coaches, playwrights, directors, and producers). Users, like these metaphorical players, are not autonomous agents within the systems development process and are unlikely to express their knowledge, interests and requirements in their own way or on their own terms. This interpretation is further reinforced by other elements in the text, such as the requirement that the players be groomed for their performance,

“Prepare the user participants" [II, 11, 211, ¶1],

the requirement that they learn their “plays":

"Give participants the preparatory material for them to study" [III, 8, 158, ¶5],

"Inform them that they must understand it well before the first workshop" [III, 8, 158, 5].

descriptions of stage props:

"Refreshments are available in the room, but there is no telephone" [III, 8, 155, ¶5]

and descriptions of settings

“The session may take place in a hotel to isolate the users from business distractions" [III, 8, 145, ¶11].

The text even presents detailed layouts of JAD rooms [III, 8, 156, ¶1]. Akin to ball players in a game or actors in a play, users seem to have little direct control over the nature and frequency of their contribution, the content and context of interaction, and the timing of events. Such players will have more difficulty being critical participants in systems development. This consequence is anticipated in the text, which describes the users' naive final reaction to a JAD session:

“End users often want the system immediately" [III, 8, 165, ¶2].

"It is necessary to manage the expectations of a JAD session" [III, 8, 165, ¶3]

To substantiate the degree to which users are assigned a relatively minor role in the development process, we scrutinized the specific procedures prescribed by IE during two of its phases. We selected those IE phases during which the interaction between IS analysts and users may be assumed to be most significant—BAA and JAD—and noted the distribution of action across the various participants. We searched the text for active verbs and categorized the action by the participant identified as performing the action. The results of our analysis are shown in Tables 3 and 4 (for the BAA and JAD phases respectively). As is evident from the tables, the amount of action assigned to users is quite minor (on average 2% of the actions taken—1.7% in BAA and 2.7% in JAD) compared to what is assigned to the "Unidentified Participants," presumably IS personnel, as we indicated in our discussion of the text's silent audience. Further, an examination of the nature of the actions also shows that the users perform largely passive actions rather than being proactive or directive, for example,

“Inform them [user participants] that they must understand it [preparatory material] well before the first workshop" [II, 11, 214, ¶1] and [III, 8, 150, ¶1].

“End users should list questions and suggestions ready for the second workshop"” [III, 8, 153, 11].

The passive role assigned to the users by the text can also be read as a submissive one:

"When systems are built by IS . . . professionals, it is important to harness the end users into the proceses of requirements planning and design" [III, 7, 127, ¶1].

In this sentence, the choice of the word “harness" when referring to how analysts should treat users may reflect a double-entendre and an unconscious conflict over the role of users. One interpretation is that users serve as an information source to analysts, and that akin to energy sources, they are drawn on in the service of the analysts' work. Another interpretation builds on the meaning of “harness," which according to the Oxford dictionary means “equipment of draught-horse or other animal by which it is fastened to a cart, etc. and controlled." and which has the additional meaning in the Chambers dictionary of“a baby's leading-strings."Both these meanings intimate that users need to be handled and controlled in a certain fashion. By implication, users are presented as incapable of proceeding alone or in parallel with others.

"Normally, there will be much argument about the definitions of data items and about which data items are standard. It is the task of the data administrator to resolve these arguments" [II, 13, 253, ¶2].

The implicit claim presented here is that users are territorial and unable to resolve their differences, while the data administrator has the ability and global perspective to mediate among them, and bring order and uniformity to the data design. The data administrator is presented as a sort of deus ex machina, conveniently descending to resolve the paradox of equally legitimate, but opposing, user views. The text, however, is silent on how exactly the data administrator accomplishes this. It is also not clear why he or she should be given this task or be expected to have the qualifications to do it. Indeed, some of the literature on information management suggests that such a technocratic strategy is not particularly effective (Davenport et al. 1992, Goodhue et al. 1992).

To summarize our analysis of the text thus far, we found a significant incompatibility between the rhetoric of “user involvement" and the prescribed procedures that engage users only marginally and passively in the systems development process. This incompatibility is evidence of a logical contradiction or paradox, and it indicates that the text is deeply ambivalent about the level of responsibility to be afforded users during the systems development process. This ambivalence feeds into and is reinforced by the dichotomy that the text establishes between analysts and users.

The False Dichotomy between Users and IS Analysts

The text creates and sustains—both implicitly and explicitly—a strict dichotomy between users and IS analysts, and in this it mirrors (and, we argue, reaffirms) the distinction and distance between the technical and social worlds that is evident in much IS literature.

First, Différance—Establishing the Distinction. In the previous section we saw the extent to which users were portrayed as passive relative to the IS analysts. The text is quite specific about the division of labor between users and analysts:

"There are, however, things which users should not do with computers. Some activities need professional IS skills" [III, 5, 101, ¶5].

“JAD recognizes that users cannot design complex procedures without professional help" [III, 7, 128, ¶2].

These statements sustain a dichotomy between users and IS analysts by suggesting that users require the support and guidance of IS personnel. The text is silent on why IS analysts should be better equipped to design procedures than users. This is puzzling in that the procedures being designed are concerned with business logic, work practices, and information flow, about which users typically know more than analysts.

The text reiterates frequently that IE diagrams must be simple and easy for the users to comprehend [I, 1, 22, ¶2; I, 5, 116, ¶6; II, 11, 205, ¶1; III, 8, 143, ¶2], for example,

“It is critical to select types of diagrams which users can learn to employ easily" [III, 5, 101, 16].

"End users can be taught to understand . . , diagrams . . . in a simple form . . . [U]sers [should be able to] see simple uncluttered structures and be able to understand and think about them" [III, 8, 147, ¶3].

"These four diagram types are a basis for clear thinking and can help users to structure their ideas about systems, and, where appropriate, build procedures . . . Experience has shown that end users can be taught how to read (but not necessarily create) these diagrams in a half-day training course" [III, 5, 102, ¶4].

The patronizing tone adopted toward the users in these passages is reinforced by the layouts of the JAD rooms shown in the text [III, 8, 156, ¶1]. The users are expected to sit in the middle of the room facing the JAD leader (who typically comes from IS [III, 7, 130, ¶5]). They resemble schoolchildren facing their teacher. Such practices signal, produce, and reproduce différance—a difference between users and analysts, and the deference of users towards IS analysts.

Thé text also reveals itself to be inconsistent in its portrayal of users. On the one hand, users are depicted as passive and technologically naive, while on the other, they are recognized as capable of developing their own systems. For example, in discussing the end-user computing “revolution" [I, 1, 24, ¶2], the text notes that:

“Increasing numbers of end users are learning how to use computerized tools. . . . [to] build complex programs for statistical analysis, financial modeling" [I, 5, 116, ¶1].

Glossing over the apparent contradiction, the text reverts to type, characterizing the users as nonetheless technologically ignorant in their use of technology.

“Sometimes, multiple copies are used [by end users] in different locations without much comprehension of how the program works" [I, 5, 116, ¶4].

With some paradox, the implication is drawn that users may be dangerous. For example, the text notes that end-user computing:

“. . . creates the danger that users build subsystems for their own purposes which do not link to the other systems in the enterprise" [III, 5, 101, ¶2],

and that without IE, user-oriented techniques will result in a mass of isolated user applications, usually with significant redundancy and chaotic data. For example,

"The danger of end-user computing is that many tools permit users to create their own data structures and design their own files. If this is not controlled, corporate data becomes a Tower of Babel. System integrity is lost, and management cannot extract and process the vital information needed for top-level planning" [I, 5, 1 16, ¶3].

"Some end users add to their programs a little at a time, until a large spaghetti-like mass exists, undocumented and unmaintainable" [I, 5, 116, ¶4].

While the problems of end-user computing may be accurate, the text is strangely silent about the many “spaghetti-like" systems produced by IS personnel before the adoption of structured design and programming approaches.

The Tower of Babel metaphor is used several times in the text, and it serves to reinforce the dichotomy between users and analysts:

"[End-user computing] can rapidly become a Tower of Babel with thousands of isolated noncommunicating systems. Many of these systems have been undocumented, unauditable, and unmaintainable" [III, 5, 101, ¶2].

"It has become clear that techniques are needed for guiding end-user computing to prevent a Tower of Babel from springing up as a result of randomly designed data and redundant procedures. Information engineering is perceived as a necessary guidance mechanism" [I, 1, 24, ¶2].

The Tower of Babel refers to a biblical story in which God's wrath destroyed the heaven-aspiring constructions of mere mortals, and future threats on God's power were thwarted by saddling the mortals with a diversity of languages, making it impossible for them to communicate, and thus also impossible to cooperate in such heady ventures. By (mis)applying this metaphor to describe users' aspirations and activities the text claims that users typically pursue local (departmental or unit) goals. By implication, the text suggests that analysts, not being users, can be presumed to pursue global or corporate goals.

In addition to being portrayed as insular, users are depicted as enmeshed in conflict:

“The end users in potential conflict resolve their differences in the JAD workshop" [III, 7, 129, ¶1].

"The end users or managers in question confront each other at the workshop under the guidance of a session leader trained in negotiating skills" [III, 7, 133, ¶2].

The suggestion is that the only or primary form of conflict that arises during systems design occurs among users. In particular, the text is silent on the need for negotiation between users and IS analysts. This omission obscures any conflict that may exist between them. The text creates the impression that IS analysts are detached, neutral, and above the fray, yet numerous commentators have shown that analysts may be a key source of tension, difficulty, and frustration during systems development (Markus and Bjørn-Andersen 1987, Newman and Noble 1990). The text further disregards the fact that IS analysts will also be “users" of the future system, as maintainers and operators, and as the organizational unit charged with responsibility for common systems and shared data. Addressing the concerns of analysts-as-users would make it difficult to assert their neutrality.

The text depicts users as engaged in turf battles, while it is silent on the power issues with which IS analysts may be concerned. User organizations are portrayed as fiefdoms, while analysts are not seen as protective of IS interests. Analysts, apparently, do not lobby for use of the existing technology infrastructure, negotiate for systems that are easily maintained, or advocate for the use of standard data, reusable code, the latest database technology, or new hardware. IS analysts, at least as represented in the text, have no parochial interests. Elkjær et al. (1991), in their examination of an in-house software development methodology, found a similar ideology in the representation of system developers as free from hidden agendas.

Reaffirming the distinction between users and IS analysts, the text lists criteria for selecting project participants. Compare:

"There should be one or more IS professionals who build the design and ensure that it is good technically" [III, 8, 145, ¶6],

with:

"End users should be chosen who are easy to work with, who know the business area well and who express their opinions easily" [III, 8, 145, ¶4].

Analysts' personal characteristics are not mentioned. The implication is that analysts' technical skills are a sufficient criterion for participation. Users, in contrast, need screening and careful selection on the basis of personality and knowledge. Note that the first user selection criterion is that users should be “easy to work with." The text does not define what “easy" means in this context, but we might speculate that given their presumed lack of technical knowledge and the passive role they are to play, users should be accommodating, cooperative, and not disagree with or challenge the intended system, the design models, or the process.

The distinctive characteristics of users and analysts are further highlighted in a particularly expressive passage, where the text suggests that in traditional systems development approaches, analysts “save" the users from the ignorance of their unenlightened ways:

"When the traditional systems analyst and potential end users first come face to face . . . It is rather like a Victorian missionary first entering an African village" {III, 5, 98. ¶3].

"The missionary is steeped in computer terminology and analysis methods. The villagers' culture is accounting, chemical engineering or production control—cultures with a complex folklore. . . . If the missionary is skilful at communicating and can offer the villagers a promise of better things, they can begin to learn each other's conceptual framework. However there is no way that either can understand the nuances and subtleties of the other's way of thinking” [III, 5, 98, 14].

With this metaphor, the text implies that information technology is a religion, that analysts are priests, and that systems development methodologies are scriptures that will lead the heathens (the users) to their salvation. Users are unsophisticated “villagers," and their views are “"folklore," hence, discountable. The analyst's job is to offer the users “a promise" of a better life. The user's role is unspecified; we can infer that the user listens passively to the analyst and accepts his or her doctrine and promises on faith, without negotiation. The text goes on to suggest that in contrast to traditional methodologies, IE overcomes the problem of incompatible conceptual frameworks; that it allows users and analysts to understand each other and speak the same language. In this claim the metaphor is quite accurate—although, one suspects, not in the way implied in the text-for missionaries' typical modus operandi is to convert the natives to their view of the world, rather than to try and accommodate the natives' view. In the referenced religious tradition, there is little modification of the faith by the converted.

In the IE paradigm, users are expected to learn the analysts' view of the world and to speak the analysts' language. Even though the text takes the position that:

"The language used throughout the session, and the language on the diagrams, should be the language of business, the language of the application, or the language of the user. not technical language" [III, 8, 147, ¶8],

we find that much of the documentation generated about the business and the application is expressed in terms of data flow diagrams, data models, process models, action diagrams, etc. This documentation, which may not be technical in the sense of including computer jargon, is technical in the sense of being expressed in relatively abstract concepts and terms with specific meanings that are foreign to the users. The alien nature of the diagrams has already been established in that users have to be "taught" to read them.

Finally, the dichotomy between users and analysts is evident in the text's reference to users as "users" rather than as, for example, "customers," “patrons," "clients,""colleagues,"“associates," "partners,""specialists,"“domain experts,"

"practitioners." or“professionals."The text employs the term “users"without definition, other than its being contrasted with "IS professionals."For example,

"The right balance needs to be achieved between user activities and professional IS activities" [III, 5, 101, ¶5].

It is interesting that the text takes for granted the “professional"status of the IS analyst.4 while never acknowledging that many of the so-called “users"may actually be professional accountants, lawyers, engineers, or physicians. Aggregating across occupational and hierarchical differences to create a homogeneous “user"category facilitates stereotyping, and emphasizes différance. Further, the term “user" connotes passivity, one who consumes or operates, rather than one who controls, creates, designs, develops, prescribes, or manages.

Then, Supplement—The Users Supplement the Analysts. We interpret the passages presented above to be saying that IS analysts and users are different. Users have to be given structure and guidance to avoid their messy and parochial inclinations, and they can be taught to read but not to create models. The impression is that users are not very sophisticated or knowledgeable about computers, and they must be protected from themselves. Focusing on these specific differences sets up the rationale for why users need to be carefully led through the process of systems development. The social construction of user dependency reaffirms the power of the IS analysts and legitimates the condescending treatment afforded the users. However, it also creates a logical contradiction in the systems development process, as we will show.

Despite the constructed dependency of users, on which the. différance between users and analysts is based, the text asserts that in the end it is the users who bear the most significant responsibility for the system—reviewing it, validating it, and signing off on it. Thus, while users are portrayed as playing a supplemental role in the development process, the text ultimately exhibits a deep confusion about the location of responsibility for the system.

Evidence of the user's supplemental role is present in both the analysis and design activities. Users are passive, yet responsible. Consider the BAA description:

“A technique that has proved highly successful is to form a team of users who have expert knowledge of their own data. The team is composed of selected users who meet periodically with the data administrator, who will think about the data structures needed in their area. The data administrator, with the help of systems analysts, feeds the user views of data into a data modeling process and into the I.E. encyclopedia. Appropriate printouts of the mòdels, from the encyclopedia, are then given to the user committee to check" [II, 13, 253, ¶2].

This passage is ambiguous about who is really in charge of systems analysis. Users have the knowledge, but IS personnel do the thinking and the designing. Users check the outcome, presumably using their superior knowledge about the business process. It might be argued that “checking" is more difficult than creating the original prototypes or models, in that it can be harder to find errors in an abstracted design than to create an erroneous one in the first place. Users are elsewhere described as “critiquing" or “reviewing" prototypes [I, 1, 24, ¶2; I, 5, 120, 7], and "checking" models or details [I, 5, 123, ¶4; II, 13, 250, 5]. The text recognizes the criticality of these final activities:

“Experience with much modeling has shown that a key to success is strong end-user involvement so that end users check the models to ensure that they reflect the real world correctly" [1, 5, 120, 15].

In such passages the most significant task in systems development appears to be carried out by the user.

Confusion about whether the user is a reference source for the analyst or the final arbiter of the system is apparent again in this description of JAD:

“. . . the users are guided through a series of structured steps which causes them to think about and describe the procedures they require. A JAD session makes the user interact with easy-to-understand design diagrams, screen layouts, printed reports, and simple prototypes. When the users are made responsible for the design, they take a strong interest in it, often creating imaginative new procedures, and the results are much more valuable" [III, 7, 128, [2].

First, the users have to be guided through structured steps, made to interact with easy-to-understand designs and simple prototypes. But the supplementary role of users can be seen when the text notes that “the users are made responsible for the design . . ." Such contradictory treatmet is similarly evident in the BAA prescriptions:

“Determine which end users will help in developing the model. Establish a committee of end users and managers who will be responsible for making the BAA models correct and complete" [II, 14, 282, ¶1].

Here the text captures the basic conundrum. Users are assistants, but they also bear all the responsibility for correct and complete project outcomes.

Who or what makes the users responsible? The text's use of passive voice makes it hard to know. We find a hint by returning to the metaphor of the missionary and the villagers:

“For his own protection the missionary needs the villagers to sign [the specifications]" [III, 5,98,15].

By obtaining a signoff, the missionary obtains release from the obligation to fulfill the villagers' real system needs, substituting instead an obligation to fulfill the letter of a contract. This passage suggests that user signoffs, along with user validation of system analysis and design, may be simply symbolic (as discussed by Robey and Markus 1984). This gives the reader one possible resolution to the paradox raised by the users' supplementing the analysts' work by reviewing and validating it: these activities are proforma and symbolic, not supplementary. But it is hard to see how a hollow signoff can effectively release the missionary (a.k.a. the analyst) from the obligation to fulfill the villagers' (a.k.a. the users') requirements.

The espoused dichotomy between users and analysts, if it is rooted in the users' lack of knowledge or parochial interests, breaks down. Analysts are dependent on users in two ways, As noted earlier, analysts need users to go along with IS management's architecture and standards objectives, to agree to build on existing technological and data infrastructures, to fund the design and construction of reusable elements, to replace obsolete technology, and to cooperate in dozens of ways if budget and schedule commitments are to be met. In addition, analysts are dependent on users to validate and legitimate their contributions to the firm. Because these IS interests are not acknowledged, they have no organizational legitimacy. Hence, analysts pursue their interests indirectly, by driving the systems development process, leading and coaching the users, and invoking “directives" prescribed by the methodology. In the end, analysts and users both defer to each other. Their relationship is one of unacknowledged interdependence.

In this section, we have examined passages that exhibit significant confusion about whether and how responsibility can be distributed between analysts and users as long as they are jointly accountable for jointly-developed products. This is not a problem that is unique to analysts and users; it is one that confronts most expert-client interdependencies. But in this text the problem is swept under the table, leaving analysts and users without mechanisms for engaging with each other as equals or for sharing authority over information technology.

## Conclusion

Our deconstruction analyzed a reader's interpretation of the IE systems development methodology, as represented by one text.⁵ We found evidence of contradiction and logical inconsistency surrounding the IS-user relationship. In spite of a heavy emphasis on the importance of user involvement and the value placed on “joint development," we found that the methodology's prescriptions for the distribution of tasks, expertise, and accountability between users and analysts in systems development are ambivalent about the degree to which users can be expected to be true co-agents with the IS staff.

We found that the text begins by explicitly advocating “strong user involvement" in the development process. This prescription, however, is contradicted by the actual procedures recommended for engaging users on the project. We showed that the text is ambivalent in its commitment to user involvement, an ambivalence reinforced by the dichotomy set up between users and analysts. This dichotomy was expressed by means of both différance and supplement, and it was revealed in the metaphors, silences, and double-entendres of the text. In terms of différance, users are portrayed as naive, technically unsophisticated, and parochial, while the IS analysts are presented as more knowledgeable, more professional, and more corporate-minded. Despite the rhetoric of user involvement, the text has analysts exercising nearly complete control over the development process and users playing a passive role. The dichotomy between users and analysts is thus a privileged one, with analysts in command and users submissive. Then, using the concept of supplement, we realize that this privileged dichotomy is not sustainable, in that the users, in the end, are expected to be responsible for the outcomes of the development process. This contradiction about authority over the development process and its outcomes reveals a deep confusion about the relationship between analysts and users, and particularly about the appropriate distribution of power, control, responsibility, and accountability between analysts and users in the execution of a shared task. The contradiction leaves both users and analysts in an untenable position: users submissive during the development process are expected to take charge at the end, while analysts in charge throughout the process are expected to yield to the users at implementation. We suggest that these characterizations—when enacted—are likely to undermine interactions between users and IS analysts, and, in an interesting twist, disable both parties, leaving them ill-equipped to negotiate with each other, to share responsibility, and to cooperate effectively. These findings may shed light on the recurring conflict between analysts and users during systems development, which has been well documented in the literature and which persists through use and maintenance (Franz and Robey 1984, Hirschheim and Newman 1991. Markus and Bjørn-Andersen 1987, Newman and Noble 1990, Newman and Robey 1992, Swanson and Beath 1989).

A deconstructive analysis such as the one conducted here is valuable in exposing contradictory or ideological elements in systems development methodologies. Because the ambivalences are embedded in methodology—the means through which the analysts and users work—they are usually undetected and hence tend to remain undiscussed and unexamined. Deconstruction also leads us to consider how the contradictions and ideological elements in texts mirror those in the world. With the exception of explicitly frame-breaking work, texts largely subsume assumptions, meanings, and expectations present in the contexts in which they are produced and consumed. A text is neither written nor read in a vacuum, and the conditions of a text's creation and appropriation shape its form, content, and interpretation, as they are also reaffirmed by it. Texts such as systems development methodologies thus are likely derivative of their institutional contexts, which include industry-wide structures of competence. historical patterns of resource allocation, assumptions about who has control, as well as norms about how and where work gets done.

All organizations embody relations ofinterdependence between different people or interest groups. Because such interdependencies are often complex and variable, they tend to be managed by being defined as relations of dominance/dependence. Such relations of dominance/dependence are then sustained by the particular structures of meaning, power, and norms that constitute an organization's institutional context (Giddens 1984). One interest group tends to be privileged over another by being assigned greater authority, legitimacy, or knowledge—resources, which in turn are the means through which the dominance/dependence is perpetuated. For example, Perin (1991, p. 260) shows that the inherent interdependence of employer and employee is expressed in contemporary human resource models as “management's giving permission for more employee participation' and greater latitude for ‘selfmanagement.'" In this case, the idea of employee participation relies on such elements as management's authority to structure employee action, employee compliance with management directives, and their shared definitions of“participation" and “management."

Similarly, in systems development, the interdependence between users and analysts is often rendered as a relationship of dominance/dependence. The very notion of“user involvement" portrays IS as naturally in charge and having the authority to decree the participation of users in the development of their own work support systems. The systems development context thus enacted is one in which some people are designated technical experts and others are designated consumers of technical expertise. Technical experts deploy information technology resources to build systems that users depend on in their work. Users rely on the technical experts to shape applications and hence the meanings the users will be able to ascribe to them. Together, the technical experts and the users enact—unintendedly—a relationship of dominance/dependence that masks and distorts their essential interdependence. This shared social construction of analyst dominance and user dependence and the suppression of analyst-user interdependence is inherently contradictory but it is nevertheless reproduced in many features of the IS-user relationship. It is thus not surprising that a text such as Information Engineering should embody this contradictory stance.

In our paper we have only examined one systems development text, and thus we cannot generalize beyond it to others. However, we would expect that texts on systems development methodologies such as De Marco (1978), Gane and Sarson (1979), Weinberg (1980), Yourdon (1982), and Jackson (1983) would exhibit many of the same contradictions and ambivalence towards the IS-user relationship as does IE, as they mirror the same institutional context of systems development. Because control over technical resources is tied to structures of power, meaning, and norms (Orlikowski and Robey 1991), the conflict between analysts and users is likely to persist across contexts and over time. We suspect that resolution of difficulties tied to this conflict will not be possible through such means as improved user training or revised systems development methodologies. Nevertheless, textual analysis of the sort done here could be conducted on these other methodologies, to determine if they systematically embody the same or similar contradictions, hidden conflicts, and ambiguities. Ideological elements of the institutional context that we expect to find reflected in these methodologies include the characterization of users as technologically naive, the portrayal of the methodology as inherently correct and hence nonnegotiable, and the presentation of analysts as guided exclusively by corporate goals.

It would also be instructive to examine methodology texts that explicitly claim to focus on users, such as the participatory design approaches (Mumford and Hensall 1979, Kuhn and Muller 1993). These methodologies might be seen as attempts to shift the dominance/dependence roles between users and analysts—that is, to make the users dominant and the analysts dependent—and thus to change the modes of interaction that typically define systems development. But if these approaches do not challenge the institutional context within which the IS-user interdependence is embedded, it seems unlikely that they will be particularly effective. Without connecting changes in systems development methodologies to changes in structural elements such as the division of labor, forms of control, locus of technological and work expertise, and the allocation of resources and responsibility, these methodologies will embody their own contradictions. When institutional contexts are shaped by other forces, such as in Scandinavia where codetermination legislation could result in a more interdependent IS-user relationship, participatory methodologies might be more powerful.

More generally, the deconstructive approach we adopted in this paper leads us to suggest for the study of systems development what deconstruction proposes for the study of written texts—that the primacy of the producer should be displaced in favor of an emphasis on the cultural artifact itself. We have shown that the ideology and rhetoric in the text we examined is centered primarily on analysts as the primary actors in the development of systems. Because the analysts are “authors" of information systems, it is their understandings, intentions, actions, and responsibilities that are at the center of attention. The "text and its readers"—the information system in use—are suppressed and deferred. For us this raises a critical question—how and why is this privileging of analysts taken for granted and sustained?

Taking such a question seriously would require investigating the conditions and rationales in the institutional context that reinforce the primacy of analysts in contemporary systems development texts and practices. We would need to examine the professional authority and power afforded systems development experts in organizations, the assumptions and knowledge they do and do not share with users, and the consequences of such an asymmetric distribution of material, authoritative, cognitive and symbolic resources. We would need to inquire into the circumstances within which users require, understand, and appropriate information systems, and we would need to examine the conditions that leave users with little control over the development of the technological resources they use in their work. Such examinations cannot be attempted in this paper, but inquiry into these institutional elements are critical areas of research for the field.

We believe that the findings discussed in this paper raise a central challenge for the field of IS. In general, both the research and practice of systems development have tended to take for granted rather than question the structural distribution of power, authority, knowledge, control, and resources that constitute the institutional context of systems development. Given this context for the production and consumption of information technology, it is not surprising that some methodologies construe the interdependent relationship between IS analysts and users as a unilateral one. Ignoring this contradiction prevents both researchers and practitioners from understanding, challenging, and changing the contradictions inherent in the context and methodologies of systems development. Our work suggests that without deconstructing the ideologies, contradictions, and pieties present in the discourse of systems development, we will not be able to reconstruct an effective user-IS relationship in the practice of systems development.\*
