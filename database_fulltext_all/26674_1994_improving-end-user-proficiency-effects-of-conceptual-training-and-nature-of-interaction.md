Users of information technology form mental models that reflect their understanding and knowledge of an information system. These models affect the proficiency with which they use these systems. In this paper, we draw upon assimilation theory of learning to propose and test a two-stage model of mental model development. We examined the effects of two types of training method, namely conceptual model and procedural, and two levels of nature of interaction, namely novel and simple tasks, on end-users' proficiency in forming accurate mental models of an electronic mail system. Our results indicate that the actual mental models of the system formed by the users predict learning success instead of the type of training provided. Subjects who formed mental models that were conceptual in nature performed significantly better than those who formed mental models that were procedural. Main effects for nature of interaction were not significant. However, we observed a significant interaction effect between the models formed by the users and the nature of their interaction with the system. Our findings suggest that end-user performance is enhanced through training methods that provide good conceptual models but only if users form conceptual mental models and retain them.

## 1. Introduction

mpirical studies demonstrate that the ability of end-users to understand computer systems affects the acceptance and utilization of computers in organizations (Nelson and Cheney 1987, Thompson et al. 1991). A user can be considered proficient if he/she has the requisite skills and understanding to enable him/her to use a system to successfully accomplish tasks. With the large growth in investments in information technology, it has become critically important to devise ways to improve end-user proficiency.

One accepted approach to improving user proficiency is through training (Cheney et al. 1986, Sein et al. 1987a. Rivard and Huff 1988, Nelson 1991, Robey and Zmud 1992). Canon-Bowers et al. (1991) emphasized that the effectiveness of such training in improving the skills of end-users depends upon the type of training provided. However, only a few studies in the literature on information systems have examined the efficacy of specific training methods (Bostrom et al. 1990, Davis and Davis 1990, Olfman and Bostrom 1991, Webster and Martocchio 1992).

An effective training method should help novice users develop an accurate mental model of the system. The concept of méntal models has been used by researchers in cognitive psychology (Mayer 1981, Norman 1983), information systems (Galletta 1984, Bostrom et al. 1990) and human-computer interaction (Borgman 1986), to define a user's internal representation of the system that allows him/her to reason and make sense of system behavior (Gentner and Stevens 1983, Owen 1986, Payne 1991). Individuals with an accurate mental model of a system have been found to be more proficient in using a system (Moran 1981, Douglas and Moran 1983, Borgman 1986, Sein and Bostrom 1989). The question, then, is how can training facilitate the formation of accurate mental models? Further, once training is over, how can users be helped in enhancing and expanding their mental models?

The objective of this paper is to propose and test a two-stage model of mental model development. We examined two determinants of mental model formation: training methods and nature of interaction by measuring subjects' performance in using an electronic mail system immediately after training and after a three week period. We investigated the effectiveness of two training methods frequently used in end-user training and in the preparation of help manuals, namely conceptual models and procedural. In the conceptual model method, the user is provided with a basic description of the components of the computer system which serves as a knowledge structure that can be used to assimilate new information. This approach differs from the procedural method where system operations are described as à series of“how-to" action sequence steps.

Our second factor, nature of interaction, is a determinant because users can enhance their mental models by interacting with the system (Mack et al. 1983, Carroll and Rosson 1987). After subiects form an initial mental model through training, they may interact with the system at one of two levels. They may have a low level of interaction by performing simple routine tasks that they already know how to accomplish. Alternatively, they may have a high level ofinteraction by performing new and complex tasks and experimenting with new procedures and commands. Norman (1983) and Brown and Newman (1985) suggest that such a high level of interaction, involving innovative use of the system, may be required to enhance users' mental models.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In §2, we discuss assimilation theory which explains the relationship between training methods and the formation of users' mental models (Ausubel 1968). In §3, we describe the experimental method employed to test hypotheses derived from the theoretical model. We report the results in 84 and discuss our findings in §5. We conclude the paper by suggesting future research directions and practical implications in §6.

## 2. Research Framework

## Mental Models

A term attributed to Johnson-Laird (1980), a mental model is defined as a knowledge structure that describes the relationship of the various components of a system (Rouse and Morris 1986). It permits users to mentally try out actions before choosing a particular action. The process of mental model formation has been described by yarious schema induction theories that explain knowledge acquisition (Sein 1988). One such theory, assimilation theory, has been specifically applied to the learning of new concepts such as computer systems (Mayer 1981, Davis and Bostrom 1993).

Assimilation theory proposes that meaningful learning is a three-step process through which the learner connects new materials to knowledge already existing in memory. At the reception step, the learner pays attention to the incoming information so that it reaches short-term memory. The learner must have appropriate prerequisite concepts available in long-term memory that he/she can use in assimilating the new information. Finally, the learner must activate these concepts during learning to connect new material

Assimilation theory requires the presence of preexisting concepts in a novice user's memory which can be used to form an initial mental model. Novices learning a new domain may not possess prerequisite concepts. Hence, Ausubel (1968) proposed that external aids must be provided. These aids, which he termed advance organizers, provide “ideational scaffoldings" needed to construct new knowledge structures. Later researchers in human-computer interaction have adopted the term conceptual model to describe external aids provided to novice learners of computer systems (Mayer 1983). The difference between a “mental model" and a "conceptual model" is that mental models are users' internal representations of a system. Conceptual models are external training aids that provide a basis for forming mental models. They highlight the major components in the system and their interrelationship. Researchers have argued that mental models can improve only if training provides an explicit conceptual model of the system (Du Bouley et al. 1981, Mayer 1981, Bayman and Mayer 1984). This is known as mapping via training.

Some researchers propose that novices form mental models based on prior experiences with similar systems (Young 1983). This is known as mapping via analogy. Other researchers propose that novices use both prior experience and their own experience from interacting with the system to form mental models (mapping via usage) but these tend to be inaccurate (Nickerson 1981, Norman 1983, Carroll et al. 1987). Inaccurate models are likely to contribute negatively to the interaction process leading to errors and frustration. Since the assimilation of knowledge based purely on experience often produces inaccurate mental models, we believe that the formation of mental models should be explicitly facilitated through training.

## Mental Model Formation via Training

Mental Model Formation via Conceptual Model. Two types of conceptual models have been used in training research: analogical and abstract (Sein and Bostrom 1989). An analogical, or metaphorical, model represents the system on which the user is being trained, called the target system, in terms of another system, called the base system, with which the user is familiar. For example, the VAX electronic mail system (target system) can be described in terms of an office filing cabinet (base system). The relationship, "file cabinet contains drawers," can be mapped to “VAX directory contains files". Providing analogies is considered a powerful instructional tool because users can map their existing knowledge structures from a known domain onto a new domain and concretize the new information (Rumelhart and Norman 1978, Lakoff and Johnson 1980).

In an abstract conceptual model, the target system is depicted as a synthetic construction, using schematic diagrams, hierarchical charts, or mathematical expressions. For example, the hierarchical structure of the VAX directory can be represented as an inverted tree, with the root directory at the top, the subdirectories at levels below that and the files at the lowest level. According to Gentner (1983), abstract and analogical models are really two ends of the same continuum. In comparing these two types of conceptual models, Sein and Bostrom (1989) found that factors such as a user's cognitive ability and learning style determined which type was more suitable. To control for any model type effect, we used both types of conceptual models in this study.

Research on conceptual model training has found them to be generally useful but has not provided conclusive evidence of their superiority over other training methods (Kieras and Bovair 1984). This can be attributed to drawbacks associated with conceptual models. First, since they do not emphasize procedures, users often use computers inefficiently through suboptimal commands (Carroll and Rosson 1987). Second, analogical conceptual models are susceptible to over-mapping; novices often map shortcomings of the model to the target system, fail to use the correct metaphors, or use them inappropriately (Halasz and Moran 1982, Borgman 1986). For example, Carroll and Mack (1985) observed that people were using the desktop metaphor of an Apple computer to get a piece of paper from a paper pad icon by sweeping the cursor across the icon in a tearing fashion. While abstract models are relatively immune from over-mapping, they provide less concrete information and often require novices to first learn the abstract model itself. For example, Sein et al. (1987b) found that many novices had difficulty understanding the mathematical expressions used as the abstract conceptual model of a modeling software. Finally, users often integrate existing knowledge about computer systems with new models to form defective mental models of the system (Mack et al. 1983).

Mental Model Formation via Procedural Training. An alternative to conceptual model training is the widely used procedural method. In this method, a novice is instructed on step-by-step action sequences to accomplish tasks. No specific information about the system structure and its components are given. In the absence of a conceptual model, users understand the system in terms of essentially isolated concepts. The users may still form mental models but these models would consist of a set of procedures. This is in accordance with the knowledge assembly theory (Hayes-Roth 1977) which postulates that learning begins with the development and strengthening of representations of lower order components of a knowledge structure. The components are subsequently linked by associations that are strengthened as learning progresses resulting in a higher-order unitized structure. Eventually, 'an entire knowledge structure may be unitized into a single memory representation, i.e., a mental model albeit in a procedural form.

While the value of a procedural training method is not clear, there is evidence that users form mental models that are procedural in nature (Sein and Bostrom 1990, Santhanam and Wiedenbeck 1993). Procedural models have also been used to represent users' interaction with computer systems. The well-known GOMS (Goals Operators, Methods, Selection rules) theory (Card et al. 1983) models user's knowledge about a system in terms of system procedures and operations. According to GOMS, a user has a specific goal in using the system. To accomplish this goal, he/she decides on a method and chooses a set of operators which leads him/her to different system states. Similarly, other theories of user system interaction such as Generalized Transition Network (Kieras and Polson 1985), Backus Normal Form (Reisner 1981) and Production Rule System (Bovair et al. 1990) describe user behavior and the mental events that occur in terms of procedures. These illustrate the fact that a major portion of user system interaction is made possible through a rich procedural knowledge. Hence, training people on system procedures may enhance their performance to a considerable degree. However, researchers have postulated that procedural training will be more useful when the system is simple to operate (Kieras 1988). Catrambone (1990) found that users provided with specific task instructions in a manual performed significantly better than those provided with general instructions. However, they performed poorly on novel tasks.

In summary, users may form mental models after being trained with both conceptual model and procedural method. Where the methods diverge is in the strategy employed. Conceptual model training has the express objective of providing a structure on which a user can build a mental model through assimilating new information. Procedural training provides users with a series of instructions to help them interact with a system. However, these procedures may ultimately be unitized into a coherent mental model. This requires the user to abstract rules and concepts from the procedures. Conceptual models, on the other hand, relieve the user from some of the burdens of the abstraction process. Training with the conceptual model method requires users to deduce procedures from a coherent mental model, whereas training with the procedural method requires users to induce a mental model from the procedures. Hence, conceptual model training is likely to provide a better opportunity for a user to form a coherent mental model compared to procedural training.

## Mental Model Enhancement via Interaction

Novice users may form mental models through prior experience and through interacting with the system (Nickerson 1981, Norman 1983, Young 1983). The problem is that models thus formed are often “incomplete, inconsistent, oversimple and rife with superstition" (Carroll et al. 1987, p. 26). Therefore, training is the preferred route of mental model formation. However, interaction can play an important role in enhancing users' mental models after they have been trained.

Research on expert models and nature of expertise indicates that expert knowledge has an experiential component. Users' mental models become more refined and expanded with experience because a large proportion of users' knowledge is obtained through interaction with the system (Gilfoil 1982, Adelson and Soloway 1985, Carroll and Mack 1985). Carroll and Mack (1985) also found that users engage in active learning; they try to create, explore, and thus add to their knowledge.

While interaction generally improves a user's mental model, using the system in simplistic ways or executing routine tasks may not be enough. Assimilation theory suggests that active use of internal knowledge structures is required for the acquisition of new knowledge. Active use implies tasks that require users to reason and understand the functioning of the system. This suggests that users need to have a high level of interaction with the system to perform novel tasks that necessitate inference bevond training tasks. Through nonroutine system use, users may be able to enhance their mental models (Norman 1983, Brown and Newman 1985, Owen 1986).

## Effects of Mental Models

Two methods have been used in the human-computer interaction literature to measure the effects of a mental model. The first method, which is based on assimilation theory, is observing behavior that can be theoretically predicted to have resulted from an accurate model. One approach in this method requires users to reason about system states. This can be done through paper and pencil tests. A second approach requires users to demonstrate their proficiency in performing specific hands-on tasks on the target system. The process of assimilating new knowledge may result in the loss of technical details such as syntax or commands. Hence, the effect of an accurate mental model can be demonstrated by superior performance in creative and fartransfer tasks. These tasks require users to devise strategies by making inferences beyond their training tasks. This can be achieved consistently only if a user has assimilated a coherent and accurate mental model of the target system. Near-transfer tasks are similar to the training tasks and can be accomplished through simple commands acquired through rote learning. A number of studies have shown that accurate mental models do not lead to superior performance in near-transfer tasks, but do lead to better performance in far-transfer tasks (Mayer 1981, Bayman and Mayer 1984, Bennett 1984. Halasz 1985. Borgman 1986, Sein and Bostrom 1989). We used this method in our study.

The second method of examining mental models is directly explicating them through verbal and/or written protocols. Researchers have used protocols to infer users' mental models (Adelson and Soloway 1985) and to classify them into analogical, procedural, and abstract models (Sein and Bostrom 1990). In simple domains, such as electronic mail, this method can be used to assess the accuracy and completeness of a user's mental model. In this study, we employed this method to classify the nature of the subjects' mental models and used the classification in our data analyses.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Our research model, shown in Figure 1, extends the computer learning process model proposed by Bostrom et al. (1990). While their model depicts mental model formation as an outcome of a training session, our model views this as a multistage process. In the training stage, a novice user forms an initial mental model via training. Bostrom et al. (1990) include several other classes of variables (e.g., individual differences and interface type) in their model. While these variables are relevant, we treat them as contextual variables in our study and control their effects through randomization. In the interaction stage, users interact with the system and further enhance their mental models. The process of mental model enhancement is predicated upon meaningful interaction that requires users to constantly make inferences beyond the tasks on which they were trained. Since this is a continuous process, the second stage may be repeated several times. Thus, the full measure of the effects of the initial training method and the nature of interaction can only be tested after time has elapsed, i.e., in a delayed session.

## 3. Method

## Overview

A two-stage laboratory experiment using VAX electronic mail as the target system was conducted to test the hypotheses. At stage 1, the training method variable was manipulated by training subjects using either conceptual models or a procedural method. After undergoing descriptive and hands-on training, subjects were asked to complete experimental tasks where their performance was measured. In a three week period following this stage, subjects were asked to execute homework tasks in order to manipulate the nature of the interaction variable. Following this, stage 2 of the experiment was held where subjects were asked to complete another set of experimental tasks and their performance was measured again. The entire interaction of the subjects with the system was captured through session logs. The sequence of steps in this experiment is shown in Figure 2. A description of the variables, experimental tasks, and measurement procedures follow.

## Subjects

The subjects were 77 volunteers recruited from 120 junior level students enrolled in an introductory iņformation systems course at a state university. They received extra credit for participating in the experiment. As added motivation, bonus points were awarded for better performance. They were randomly assigned to two training groups. Each treatment group was further randomly divided into two levels of interaction groups.

## Variables in the Study

Independent Variables. We examined two independent variables, training method and nature of interaction, each with two levels. The two levels of training method were conceptual model and procedural. In the conceptual model method, the components and functioning of the VAX mail system were described in terms of both analogical and abstract conceptual models. In the analogical model, the mail directory was said to be similar to an office filing cabinet. The drawers in the cabinet were said to be similar to mail files that contain folders with messages. One could use the drawers and the folders, and read or send messages by giving orders to a messenger, which was the computer. The abstract model showed the VAX mail system as a hierarchy with the directory at the top level, files at the next level, folders below that and messages at the lowest level. In the procedural method the system components were not explicitly described. Instead, procedures for such common tasks as sending and receiving mail were described in detail. Subjects in both training groups received a list of VAX commands and common error messages. Care was taken to ensure that both groups received the same amount of information about the system.

The two levels of the nature of interaction variable were high and low. Subjects in the low interaction group were asked to perform simple tasks such as moving and deleting messages. These tasks, on which the subjects had been trained, required either one or a simple sequence of two commands. Subjects in the high interaction group were assigned these tasks plus additional tasks on which they were not trained. For example, one task was to rename a folder. This cannot be achieved through a single command. To be successful, subjects had to experiment, figure out how to use the commands they knew, or learn new commands, and devise the method.

We examined the system log files of every subject to determine whether experimentation took place. Based on this examination, we reclassified some subjects into high or low level of interaction groups. A subject assigned to the high interaction group who executed the assigned tasks successfully, used several commands, and used a novel command or procedure not taught to him/her was classified into the high level of interaction group. Otherwise, he/she was reclassified into the low interaction group. A subject assigned to the low interaction group who experimented and tried out several new commands was reclassified into the high level of interaction group. A total of eight subjects was reclassified from their assigned groups.

Dependent Variables. We measured users' task performance accuracy in neartransfer and far-transfer tasks. We measured the subjects' ability to reason about system states through a comprehension test. In addition, we asked users to describe the system prior to stage 1 experimental tasks, after stage 1 experimental tasks, and after stage 2 experimental tasks. These descriptions were analyzed and classified into a model type and graded for accuracy.

Covariates. We collected data on subjects' prior exposure to similar electronic mail systems through a background questionnaire. This was used as a covariate.

Training and Experimental Tasks. The training tasks involved the use of the system to send, read, and move messages. The experimental tasks for stage 1 consisted of four near-transfer and two far-transfer tasks. The near-transfer tasks were either the same ones or very similar to the training tasks. The far-transfer tasks required subjects to make inferences beyond training tasks. For example, one task called for the deletion of a message and its subsequent retrieval. To be successful, subjects had to understand the notion of a wastebasket (which is a folder) and figure out a method to move the deleted message from the wastebasket back to a folder. Subjects had not been explicitly taught how to accomplish this task, although they knew how to move a message and delete a message. Stage 2 experimental tasks were set up in a similar manner. Subjects had to execute three near-transfer and one far-transfer tasks. The system comprehension test at both stages consisted of five conceptual questions. The questions for the two stages were essentially the same except for slight changes in the names of folders. Each of these questions described a scenario of a user interacting with the VAX mail system. Subjects had to predict how the system would respond to specific sequence of commands.

## Experimental Procedures

Subjects were asked to report to one of four sessions for stage 1 (initial training) of the experiment. Using a common script, the same researcher, with the help of the second researcher and an assistant, conducted every training session. Each session, comprising between 18 and 21 subjects, was held in a room equipped with terminals connected directly to the VAX system. It was ensured that subjects could not see what other subjects were doing. They were allowed to talk to only the researchers and the assistant.

At te outset, subjects completed a background questionnaire that assessed their prior computer experience. In a brief overview, the trainer told the subjects that the objective of the study was to determine better ways of designing user manuals. The training materials were then handed out and subjects were given 15 minutes to study them thoroughly. They were then asked to write a description of what they knew of the VAX mail system. The purpose was two-fold. First, we wanted to do a manipulation check. Second, we wanted to get an assessment of their initial mental models. The hands-on training phase was then held.

At the end of this phase, the subjects were asked to perform the experimental tasks. No help of any sort was provided. A subject taking longer than 15 minutes on a particular task was instructed to stop working on it and continue on to the next task. The purpose of this was to ensure that subjects did not get into difficult unrecoverable problem tangles. Based on pilot tests, 15 minutes was considered sufficient to accomplish each of these tasks. At the end of the experimental phase, subjects completed the system comprehension test. Subjects were then asked to report to the stage 2 experimental session three weeks later. In between the two stages, 'subjects were given homework assignments. Stage 2 sessions, held in the same room as stage 1, required subjects to perform experimental tasks and take the system comprehension test.

Measurement and Scoring

The subjects' session logs provided the raw data to measure task accuracy. The dependent variables were measured as follows:

Task Performance Accuracy. We developed the following grading scheme to score tasks:

10 points—Correct answer with exactly needed command sequence.

8 points—Correct answer; Up to two “innocent" extra commands (e.g., DIREC-TORY), followed by the correct sequence.

6 points—Correct answer; specified extra commands followed by correct sequence or evidence of systematic error recovery.

4 points—Incorrect or partially correct answer; accidentally discovered answer; use of nonfatal commands

0 points-Totally incorrect and/or incoherent answer.

A research assistant, who had no knowledge about the study, was trained on this method and graded the session logs for task accuracy.

System Comprehension. We developed a grading scheme along the lines described above to score each question. Scores ranged from 5 to 10 points based on the question. Another research assistant who was trained on the scheme graded the comprehension tests.

Model Accuracy. Each researcher separately scored the completeness of the subjects' three descriptions (initial, end of stage 1, and end of stage 2) on a scale of 1 to 5 where 5 indicated a complete well-formed model. A model was scored 5 if it contained an accurate description of the hierarchical structure of the mail filing system, had a complete list of all the key commands, described key commands, and explained procedures to retrieve deleted messages. By contrast, a description that mentions only one or two features was scored a 1. After completing the scoring individually, the two researchers met to compare scores. In 85% of the cases, both researchers assigned the same score. In most of the remaining cases, where the scores differed by either 0.5 or 1 point (e.g., 4 vs. 3.5 or 3.0), we took the average score. In the other cases, the disagreements were settled through discussion.

Mental Model Type. The procedure to classify a description into mental model types was similar to that followed to score their accuracy. Descriptions were classified either as a conceptual mental model or a procedural mental model. Conceptual mental models included both analogical and abstract descriptions. A descriptions was classified as an analogical mental model if it described the system in terms of another system or object. Almost all the descriptions of this type followed either the conceptual model used in the experiment or variations of it. An example of a different analogical model is as follows: "The filing system is like a huge mailing room. The room itself is the mail file. There are several mail boxes in this room. These boxes are the folders. Messages are stored inside the folder by the postman (computer)".

A description was classified as an abstract mental model if the description had no analogies and if it contained some form of abstract representation such as diagrams, tree-like structures or mathematical notations. Most of the abstract models used the hierarchical diagram provided in the conceptual model.

## 4. Results

Due to technical problems, such as terminal malfunction, or failing to generate session logs, 11 out of the original 77 subjects were excluded from the study leaving us with an initial usable sample size of 66. Of them, 32 were in the conceptual model group and 34 were in the procedural method group. There were 27 males and 39 females and the average age was 23.

We expected all the dependent variables (accuracy scores in near-transfer tasks, accuracy scores in far-transfer tasks, and scores in the system comprehension test) to be correlated as they were really different manifestations of understanding or the strength of their mental models. As expected, Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ : hence, we used multivariate procedures to analyze our data. We expected prior computer experience to affect the dependent variables. Although this did not prove to be the case $( p < 0 . 4 0 )$ , we ran Multivariate Analysis of Co-Variance (MANCOVA) using prior experience as a covariate in order to “obtain a more precise estimate of treatment effects" (Keppel 1982, p. 491). ## 5. Discussion

The results of our study show that novices who are trained with conceptual models will not necessarily perform better than those who are trained with procedural methods. They must form conceptual mental models (i.e., either analogical or abstract) in order to perform better. Twenty five percent of the subjects trained with the conceptual model method formed procedural mental models of the system even before they started to work on the experimental tasks. A smaller proportion of subjects trained with the procedural method (9%) developed conceptual mental models of the system. When we analyzed our subjects' performance based on the mental models they formed instead of the methods on which they were trained, we found a significant model type effect.

It is generally believed that people are adept at forming their own mental models about physical systems (Gentner and Gentner 1983, Carroll et al. 1987, Payne 1991). It was intriguing to find that users explicitly trained with a conceptual model, change their mental models immediately after training. This perhaps explains many of the conflicting results obtained about the value of conceptual model training. In some cases, it was found to be better than other forms of training (Bayman and Mayer 1984, Borgman 1986). In other cases, it was not so (Kieras and Bovair 1984). Since the actual mental models formed by the subjects were not assessed in these studies, it is highly likely that users did not form the appropriate mental models.

A user's reception of new input from the environment may be biased depending on the contents of an existing mental model. He/she may well filter out new information contained in the provided conceptual model. The tuning of mental models occurs because relevant new information from the environment cannot be constantly filtered out. The user must realize that instead of attenuating new information that does not agree with his/her mental model, a reassessment of the old model is in order. Incoming stimuli are then received, and assimilated with the existing mental model which is restructured into a more accurate one.

It therefore becomes critical to reinforce the correct conceptual model. One method to accomplish this during training is to explain errors with the help of the provided conceptual model. This will help users in the assimilation process by activating their mental models. Another method is to provide users with immediate feedback through an appropriate interface. Computer systems that utilize Direct Manipulation Interfaces (Hutchins et al. 1985, Teeni 1991) continuously present conceptual models of the system at the interface level. This may explain why endusers have generally exhibited superior performance when interacting with these systems (Santhanam and Wiedenbeck 1991, Davis and Bostrom 1993).

Our findings indicate that, in the absence of such reinforcement, subjects tend to develop procedural mental models, irrespective of the training provided. While almost half of the subjects were trained with conceptual models, at the end of stage 2, only about 31% of them were classified as having a conceptual mental model of the system. This closely parallels the findings of prior researchers who observed that many optional users of technology tend to form procedural mental models of the system (Carroll and Rosson 1987. Sein and Bostrom 1990, Santhanam and Wiedenbeck 1993). One explanation for this is provided by the GOMS model which proposes that a user has a goal-oriented approach when using the computer and views the interaction task as choosing the right commands/operators to complete the task. A second explanation can be seen in the system environment under which most users operate. Most of the help commands, documentation and user manuals organize information around commands' establishing a procedural mental model of the system in the user's mind. For example, the help facility in VAX electronic mail is in the form of commands or procedures (to do x you have to first do y and then do z).

While knowing the correct procedures and commands is important for task accomplishment, having a predominantly procedural mental model of the system may not help users to execute tasks effectively and efficiently. The lack of a conceptual mental model becomes a drawback when users face nonroutine tasks or when they have to interpret and recover from errors (Catrambone 1990, Santhanam and Wiedenbeck 1993).

The significant interaction effect between the actual mental models formed by subjects and the level of interaction variable on far-transfer task accuracy scores suggests that only those with a conceptual mental model benefit from performing complex tasks on the system. Assimilation theory provides an explanation for this finding. Individuals with an integrated conceptual mental model can assimilate new information better than those whose knowledge structure consists of a set of procedures. The former enhanced their mental models through experimenting (and thus, actively learning) while the latter may have added a few extra commands without integrating new knowledge to the old.

One explanation for not finding any significant main effect for our second independent variable, level of interaction, is provided by the interaction effect between the two independent variables. Another possible explanation may lie in the simplistic nature of the target system where few complex tasks were available. We intentionally chose VAX mail because (a) we did not want a very complicated system that would frustrate our subjects' learning process, (b) it is a system used extensively, and (c) prior literature on learning behavior was available to compare our results. A more complex system may have provided a better target system.

## 6. Conclusions

We undertook this study to test our two-stage model of mental model development, Our findings indicate that it is a useful research framework. We found support for the effect of conçeptual model training at the initial stage and the combined effect for the initial model and the level of interaction at the later stage. Users who develop conceptual mental models, either through training or usage, achieve better proficiency than those who do not. However, users also change their mental models of the system. It therefore becomes a challenge to help them retain a good conceptual understanding of the system. Our findings also suggest that a high level of interaction with the system alone is not sufficient to develop a good understanding of the system. Rather, a high level of interaction supplemented with good initial mental models of the system leads to better performance.

Our findings are in accordance with the predictions of assimilation theory which has been used extensively by researchers in human-computer interaction and cognitive science. Since this theory has been adopted and used by MIS researchers (Davis and Bostrom 1993), our study makes a contribution towards developing a cumulative tradition in MIS research in the area of end-user training.

## Implications for Practice

From a practical perspective, it is clear that appropriate training improves users' mental models which in turn improves their performance. To be effective, training programs should not only provide hands-on training, but also inculcate good mental models of the system. It will be worthwhile to provide additional training to users where models of the system are repeated so that they develop more than a procedural view of computer systems. System documentation and help manuals should also be written in a manner that provides explanation on how to use commands along with a good explanation of the structure of the system. In curriculum design, introductory computer literacy programs should emphasize conceptual models rather. than commands.

However, neither conceptual training nor practice will accomplish much by themselves. A caveat needs to be added to the adage that “practice makes perfect". Instead "meaningful practice makes perfect". Novice users must be provided with an appropriate environment to actively experiment with the system and execute complex tasks. This has been proposed by other researchers as well (Brown and Newman 1985, Sein et al. 1987). Finally, trainers must assess delayed effects of training. Too often, there is little follow-up to assess whether a training program was effective in making users productive with end-user tools.

## Implications for Research

Important research implications emerge from this study. Since mental models are dynamic, it is important to develop a process theory of mental model development. A longitudinal study that will take snapshots of users' mental models over several stages will provide insights into how and why users change their mental models. This study will also provide clues on how users change their models based on the errors they make. Although great strides have been made based on the works of Mayer (1981), Norman (1983), Young (1983), Owen (1986), Carroll et al. (1987), Kieras (1988), Payne (1991), and Kitajima and Polson (1992), a process theory of mental model formation is yet to emerge. We also need to manipulate the nature of the interaction variable more effectively and observe its effects. For this a semantically richer domain such as Windows could be used. The effect of constantly experimenting and thus stretching one's mental model in order to enrich and enhance it can be observed.

Some specific research topics can be proposed:

(1) The effect of combined treatment: We can expect that subjects trained with both conceptual models and procedural method will perform better in the long term. Initially, this group may not perform as well as a “pure" group (conceptual model or procedural method).

(2) The effect of a direct manipulation interface—such as the iconic model (Apple) —on the efficacy of a conceptual model: Since an iconic interface constantly presents a conceptual model to the user and continually reinforces it, we can expect subjects using such an interface to form an accurate and well-developed conceptual mental model. However, the mental model thus formed will retain any disadvantages that are associated with the interface.

(3) The combined effect of nature of interaction and a direct manipulation interface: Does a DMI motivate users to experiment more with a system? Does this experimentation involve complex and far-transfer tasks?

(4) Mental model formation in a multitasking environment: In a multitasking system, a user confronts a variety of interfaces that may not be consistent (Satzinger and Olfman 1993). Hence, conflicting mental models may result. What is an appropriate and efficacious training approach in this environment?

The examination of these research questions and the research direction outlined earlier will further enhance the development of the theory of mental models and in turn the theory oflearning. It will also enable us to reach the goal of making end-users more proficient in productive use of information technology.\*
