# RESEARCH REPORT

# Modeling Interpersonal Processes During System Development: Further Thoughts and Suggestions

Daniel Robey

Department of Decision Sciences and

Information Systems

Florida International University

Miami, Florida 33199

This article offers commentary on the paper by Barki and Hartwick (1994), to replicate and extend the model of conflict during systems development reported in previous research by Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993). Because of differences in the approach to measurement and data analysis, Barki and Hartwick's contribution is more properly considered as an extension of the model rather than a replication. Barki and Hartwick's strategy of model fitting is appropriate for such an extension, but it is not clear what role their “hypothesized model" plays in this exploratory work. A more careful distinction between hypothesis testing and data exploration is suggested. Finally, all of the studies using the original model or its variants are limited in their ability to support theoretical reasoning about the process of system development. The direct use of process research strategies is encouraged as a means of overcoming this limitation.

This subject has been addressed by many previous researchers, who have focused on a variety of social behaviors that occur during the development and implementation of complex technological innovations. Conflict, communication, learning, decision making, and other interpersonal processes need to be understood as they occur within systems development projects. Equipped with a greater understanding of these social processes, project managers and team members may experience greater satisfaction while producing information systems of greater value.

Barki and Hartwick have positioned their article to “replicate and extend"a theoretical model used by Robey and his colleagues in their research on conflict during systems development (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993). Barki and Hartwick offer several refinements to the theoretical model because they disagree with some of the arguments raised in these earlier papers. The purpose of the present article is to provide commentary on their article and to offer further thoughts and suggestions for research on the social process of system development and implementation.

It is not the purpose of this article to defend the earlier model against the criticisms presented by Barki and Hartwick. Their paper stimulates the discussion of these issues, but this is not a “counterattack" designed to rescue the earlier model from their criticism. The exchange occurring in these pages is properly understood as part of a dialogue within the wider IS research community, rather than an academic duel. Through such dialogues our collective understanding is advanced.

Within this frame of understanding, the extent to which Barki and Hartwick have met their stated objectives of replication and extension is examined. Their strategy of modifying a hypothesized model to provide a better fit with their data is evaluated in light of these objectives. Their efforts to improve the measures of the model's constructs are then noted. Finally, comments are offered concerning the ability of any model of this general type to support conclusions about the social process of systems development.

## Replication or Extension?

It is important to replicate the findings of empirical studies so that more general support for proposed theoretical relationships can be established. The practice of “normal science" requires researchers to state their methods clearly so that studies can be replicated with different samples. Theories have wider application only if their hypothetical relationships can withstand tests in different samples. Yet, researchers typically view replication as an inglorious pursuit and understand (correctly) that the editors of leading journals, including Information Systems Research, are interested in publishing “new information" (King 1993). Thus, intended replications often become “extensions," designed to modify prior findings rather than to replicate them exactly.

Judging from their research method, Barki and Hartwick's (1994) interest is extension beyond prior research on the interpersonal process in systems development, not replication of the model employed by Robey and his colleagues. Barki and Hartwick designed modified measures, derived new dimensions and constructs, hypothesized a "modified Robey et al. model," and employed different techniques for analysis. These decisions actually prevent them from being able to replicate the findings of Robey and his colleagues. While the relationship to prior work is clearly apparent, direct comparisons are impossible to interpret due to the substantial differences in method. In their conclusion, Barki and Hartwick refer to the limited comparability between their work and that of Robey and his colleagues.

Had a true replication strategy been employed, it is conceivable that the model employed by Robey and his colleagues would have been replicated with the sample drawn by Barki and Hartwick. Their modified Robey et al. model did provide a "reasonably good fit" with their data. All paths were significantly different from zero except for one path involving Barki and Hartwick's new construct, Disagreement. Thus, the earlier model would likely have been upheld, even with modified measures and analytic procedures. At the very least, Barki and Hartwick's results are generally confirmatory of Robey and his colleagues' three earlier studies.

Barki and Hartwick's extension is based on a model-fitting strategy, executed with the techniques of linear structural equation modeling. They share with the reader the iterative process of model-fitting, producing their “best-fitting" model as an end result. Importantly, this model departs from all previous models in interesting ways. Most dramatic is the strong negative path between Influence and Conflict, an effect hypothesized neither by Robey and his colleagues nor by Barki and Hartwick. The other striking result is the direct link between User Participation and Conflict, a path omitted in the Robey et al. model.

Given their objective of extending our knowledge, and their method of model fitting, it is not clear why Barki and Hartwick have used the term "hypothesized research model" to begin their exploration. If we assume that hypotheses are stated for the purpose of testing, then their hypothesized model could have simply been tested by using path coefficients as test statistics. However, the Chi-square difference tests employed do not test their hypothesized model; they simply compare the fit achieved by alternative models with the data. Their final, “empirically supported" model is different from their hypothesized research model, leading readers to conclude that their a priori hypotheses were not supported by their data. By their own account, their hypothesized research model fares little better than the modified Robey et al. model.

Having abandoned their hypothesized model in search of one with a better fit Barki and Hartwick later return to a discussion of hypothesized relations in their discussion. Specifically, they claim that the empirical relationships between User Participation and Conflict, and between Disagreement and Conflict, "confirm the main study hypothesis, that User Participation leads to Conflict, both directly and indirectly." However, the negative path between Influence and Conflict disconfirms all expectations, and speculations on this relationship are offered in their following paragraph. Thus, it appears that Barki and Hartwick are mixing their interest in testing theoretical relationships with their interest in exploring new theory.

It is premature to assert the theoretical superiority of a model that results from model-fitting exercises. Any model produced simply fits a unique set of data better than alternative models. Axiomatically, there is always one model that fits any set of data better than alternative models. By corollary, models that best fit one set of data may not fit later sets as well. To survive a traditional test of hypotheses, the resultant model should be posed a priori and tested with new data. Such a test would constitute the first real test of the model, so replications would be important to establish the model's generality more firmly. This is how normal science proceeds, and this is how we build confidence in theories that purport to explain social phenomena. Model fitting plays a valuable role in exploring the patterns in sets of data, but we should be aware of the difference between exploring new relationships and testing established theory.

This conservative stance should not be taken to mean that normal science is the only, or necessarily best, method of conducting research.¹ The community of researchers in information systems have moved beyond the narrow confines of normal science to employ a diversity of disciplinary perspectives and research methods (King

1993). High-powered techniques for exploring the underlying structure in measurement instruments and sets of data are valuable tools for research, as are interpretive research methods that allow novel theoretical insights to be induced from both qualitative and quantitative data. Because of this increasing diversity of perspectives and methods in research on information systems, we must carefully observe the distinction between theoretical testing and pattern searching and be clear about the goals of any particular research project.

## The Measurement of Participation and Conflict

In previous work, Barki and Hartwick have substantially advanced our ability to conceive and measure constructs relevant to the study of user participation in systems development (Barki and Hartwick 1989, 1994, Hartwick and Barki 1994). By clarifying the difference between participation (activities) and involvement (a psychological state), and by deriving multiple dimensions of User Participation, they have ensured that future research will produce more meaningful results. It is particularly important for researchers to understand how the activities that users experience during systems projects are related to their subsequent affective and cognitive reactions. The specification of responsibilities, hands-on activities, and relationships with users must be understood, and Barki and Hartwick have given these concepts greater meaning through their research,

Similarly valid and reliable measures should be developed for the remaining constructs in the models pertaining to conflict during system development. In particular, the meaning and measurement of Conflict requires greater attention. While abstract academic definitions of conflict may emphasize its constructive consequences (e.g., Robey and Farrow 1982), the word "conflict" is inadmissible in many corporate settings. “Disagreement" is a more acceptable term, which may account for its separation from the items measuring Conflict in Barki and Hartwick's measurement model. These and other refinements in measurement can improve our ability to conduct research on these crucial social processes.

In exploring the dimensionality of theoretical constructs, it is essential to maintain the integrity of the basic construct. User Participation, for example, is definable in terms of Responsibility, Hands-on Activities, and User-IS Relationships. Each of these dimensions is a form of Participation, although each dimension differs from the others. Thus, Barki and Hartwick have a strong basis for establishing User Participation as a multidimensional construct.

They have a much weaker basis for establishing the multidimensional nature of Conflict. In their assessment of the measurement model, Barki and Hartwick identified Conflict and Disagreement as separate dimensions of Conflict. However, they argue that these two “dimensions" bear a causal relationship with each other, with Disagreement causing Conflict. This argument more accurately supports the conclusion that Disagreement and Conflict are separate constructs, not dimensions of the same construct. In their support for the multidimensional nature of conflict, Barki and Hartwick identify three “facets" of the concept found in the literature on conflict: divergence of interests, interdependence, and interference with another's goals. However, divergence of interests (differentiation) and interdependence are more accurately conceived as antecedents of conflict, not dimensions of conflict (Pondy 1967, Schmidt and Kochan 1972, Walton and Dutton 1969). Little effort has been made in prior research on systems development to test the effects of these antecedents on manifest conflict. Barki and Hartwick may have uncovered a useful antecedent of conflict in their exploration of the measurement model. Since their single-item measure of Disagreement, or divergence of interests, is drawn from an existing measure of Conflict, it is premature to assert much confidence in the measure or the construct. However. future research on the antecedents of conflict during system development is very much needed.

## Understanding Social Processes

Barki and Hartwick have followed Robey's lead in specifying interpersonal processes as variables in a research model. All of the constructs in the model are conceived and operationalized as variables that range from low to high, and the model is evaluated in terms of the variance in outcome variables that is explained by predictor variables. This is the strategy of variance theory (Mohr 1982), which is employed in the vast majority of studies in information systems and most other areas of social science. Variance theories receive support when the associations among variables exceed critical levels, permitting the conclusion that such associations are not due to chance.

Mohr (1982) and others (e.g., Markus and Robey 1988, Van de Ven and Poole 1990) have noted the limitations of variance theories in explaining social processes. By conceiving of processes as systems of variables, the variance strategy affords little insight into the dynamics of the social processes it purports to explain. While some percentage of variance in one variable may be “explained" by the variation in another, little “explanation" of how and why social events occur is possible.

The difficulty in inferring information about interpersonal processes from variance research is evident both in the studies conducted by Robey and in Barki and Hartwick's (1994) article. For example, Barki and Hartwick offer plausible reasons in their discussion to explain the determinants of disagreement:

The mediated path, from Participation through Influence to Disagreement, suggests an active role for the user in the development of disagreements. As users participate they exert influence aimed at supporting their own interests, opinions, or goals. As they exert this influence, disagreements could then arise with other parties who have different interests, opinions, or goals.

Similar reasoning supports the other causal relationships identified in their research.

Comparable passages could easily be drawn from Robey and Farrow (1992), Robey et al. (1989), and Robey et al. (1993). Such arguments are well reasoned, but ultimately they are guesswork because the data afford no direct insight into the social events that comprise the processes being studied. The magnitude of a coefficient reveals little about the sequence of actions or the actors who performed those actions. The variance research strategy is limited to providing educated guesses about how conflict actually occurred or how it was resolved.

To compensate for this weakness, process theories have been prescribed as a means of treating social processes as sequences of events that occur over time. Instead of regarding conflict and other behaviors as variables, a process theory regards behavior as a sequence of social events. The process research strategy can remove much of the guesswork inherent in variance research by assessing social events directly. Process research on systems development activities permits a different type of interpretation of interpersonal processes because sequences of action are studied directly. Examples of process research in information systems include Robey and Newman's (in press)

application of a social process model to study system development over a 15-year period, Orlikowski's (1993) research on the adoption of CASE tools, and Sabherwal and Robey's (1993) derivation of a taxonomy of implementation processes from the sequences of events in 53 cases. In these studies, the interpersonal processes that comprise systems development can be understood as sequences of social action rather than as levels of variance measures.

Process research strategies also have their limitations and their critics, and it is not the purpose of this article to assert the superiority of the process strategy for research on the systems development process. Nonetheless, the advantages of process research for understanding interpersonal processes should be considered, even though such an approach poses difficult requirements on the researcher. Variables must be reconceived as events, and existing static theory must be converted to dynamic form. To facilitate the construction of new theory, detailed descriptive studies of systems development (e.g., Walz et al. 1993) can be quite useful. The contribution made by empirically supported theories about the social events that comprise system development is likely to be large. Even an elementary understanding of how effective teams operate can suggest methods for radical improvements in the practice of systems development.

Process and variance strategies can be used together. While Mohr (1982) cautions against combining the form of variance and process models within the same theory, he does regard the two types of theories to be “mutually informative." For example, data on the occurrence of social events might be used to enhance our interpretation of measures of association produced by variance research. For example, the joint application of process and variance strategies was applied by Robey et al. (1989) to augment the interpretation of conflict within a 22-month longitudinal study. More recently, Sabherwal and Robey (1994) applied measures drawn from both the process and variance strategies to produce à deeper understanding of systems development projects. Efforts such as these are aimed at reconciling the differences between process and variance theories and using them together productively.

None of these suggestions diminish the contribution of Barki and Hartwick's (1994) research. Rather, they are made in the hope that other researchers will embrace the alternative approaches that are available to study the social processes of system development. If we ultimately want to convey results of practical value, we should depend less on guesswork about how system development occurs and more on empirical observations of the development process. By measuring systems development as sequences of action over time, we may complement the insights produced by variance research.
