Thus, we agree that researchers should use multiple research methods, including both variance and process research strategies, to investigate interpersonal processes. We agree that researchers should give strong attention to the meaning and measurement of their theoretical constructs, thereby facilitating an understanding of research results which ensue. We agree that it is important to replicate the findings of empirical studies so that more general support for theoretical relationships can be established. And we agree that hypothesis testing and hypothesis generation represent two distinct research objectives, and that investigators need to be clear about their objectives when they conduct and report the results of research studies. On the other hand, there are a few points we would like to make which differ slightly with what Robey has said. As we make these points, we urge the reader to keep in mind the much greater areas of agreement between Robey and ourselves.

## Understanding Social Processes

We agree with Robey when he suggests that researchers should use multiple research methods, including both variance and process research strategies, when they investigate interpersonal processes. However, Robey goes on to suggest that a process research strategy is superior for the development of an understanding of such processes. As Robey notes, many of the processes which are suggested in our paper (Barki and Hartwick 1994), as well as those which have been suggested in his previous papers (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993), must be considered “educated guesses." We agree with Robey that studies which employ a process research strategy, where sequences of social events are assessed, can provide a fruitful avenue for future research endeavors. Such studies will help us narrow the range of feasible “guesses"and further our understanding of the interpersonal processes under investigation. That is not to say, however, that a process research strategy represents the only such avenue for future research. A variance research strategy which incorporates hypothesized mediator variables, or which includes antecedents and consequences that are implied by some, but not other, processes can also be used to help pin down the number of feasible “guesses." Thus, we believe that both variance and process research strategies may be used fruitfully to foster a deeper understanding of the interpersonal processes which occur during system development.

## The Importance of Replication

As Robey notes, it is important to replicate the findings of empirical studies in order to establish more general support for proposed theoretical relationships. However, it is important to recognize that there are different forms of replication, including exact replications (where samples, organizations, systems, measures, and analyses are as identical as possible to the original study) and conceptual replications (where an attempt is made to replicate research results using different samples, organizations, systems, measures, or analyses). Moreover, to the extent that this latter form of replication (using conceptually similar, but procedurally different operations) yields similar findings, our confidence in the theory being tested increases to a much greater degree. Of course, failure to replicate research results with such a design opens the door to numerous possible explanations (the theory is wrong, the measures are faulty or conçeptually different, contingencies concerning the sample, organization, or system need to be considered, etc.). This, however, points the way to future research possibilities, and ultimately, richer theories.

## Hypothesis Testing Versus Hypothesis Generating Research

We also agree with Robey when he states that hypothesis testing and hypothesis generation represent two distinct research objectives. In hypothesis testing research, the researcher specifies one or more a priori hypotheses, based on existing theory and/or data, and then puts these hypotheses to an empirical test with a new set of data. In hypothesis generating research, the researcher explores a set of data searching for relationships and patterns, and then proposes hypotheses which may then be tested in some subsequent study. However, while these objectives remain distinct, we see no reason not to pursue both of them in a single research project. In particular, we believe that, following a failure to support a priori specified hypotheses in a project designed to test some theory, further explorations of the data in an attempt to generate new hypotheses frequently yield interesting and useful results.

## A New Look at Barki and Hartwick (1994)

Several of these points can be illustrated by looking, once again, at Barki and Hartwick (1994). Robey suggests that, judging from our research methodology, our primary interest must have been to extend, not replicate his theory. That is not true, at least not at the beginning of the project. As we began, our primary goal was to embed our new measure of user participation into an existing, and supported, theory of information systems development. By replicating the work of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993), we believed that we would establish some nomological validity for our new measure. In conducting this replication, we replaced Robey's measure of participation with our own, reworded slightly his questions for the constructs of influence, conflict, and satisfactory conflict resolution, and employed a sample of users drawn from a diverse population of organizations and systems. As such, our study must be considered a conceptual replication.

Two sets of hypotheses were established for the replication. Robey's theory proposed links between participation and influence, between influence and conflict, between influence and satisfactory conflict resolution, and between conflict and satisfactory conflict resolution. This became our hypothesized Model 1. On the other hand, Robey's data frequently supported an additional direct link between participation and conflict. While Robey and his colleagues have dismissed this link because they felt there was little theoretical justification for it, we believed there was such a justification. The model containing this link became hypothesized Model 2.

Tests of these two models (not presented in Barki and Hartwick (1994) showed little support for either of the two hypothesized models (for hypothesized Model 1: Chi-square = 115.95, df = 40, p < 0.001; Chi-square/df = 2.90; NNFI = 0.890; CFI = 0.917; AASR = 0.053; for hypothesized Model 2: Chi-square = 109.79, df = 39, p < 0.001; Chi-square/df = 2.80; NNFI = 0.891; CFI = 0.923; AASR = 0.052). Thus, we failed to replicate the empirical results obtained by Robey and his colleagues. In part, our failure to replicate the results of Robey and his colleagues seemed to stem from the poor measure of conflict that was being employed. This measure appeared to combine two conceptually separate things, disagreement and conflict. In Barki and Hartwick (1994), we labelled these as two "dimensions" of conflict. However, as Robey correctly notes, they would be better labelled as two separate “constructs." At this point, we were faced with two options. On the one hand, we could re-test the two hypothesized models, this time using the single conflict item (hypothesis testing). On the other hand, we could explore the relationships of the two constructs, conflict and disagreement, in a model we believed to be consistent with the writings of Robey and his colleagues. We chose the latter option, believing that not only could we assess the hypothesized relationships between participation, influence, conflict, and satisfactory conflict resolution (hypothesis testing), but that we could also explore interesting new relationships between the two constructs of disagreement and conflict (hypothesis generation). The search for a “best-fitting" model continued this latter objective.

In Barki and Hartwick (1994), several interesting new relationships were suggested concerning the two constructs of disagreement and conflict. As Robey points out, these hypotheses now need to be subjected to additional hypothesis testing research. Here, we wish to focus on the attempt made to replicate the previous findings of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993). In Barki and Hartwick (1994), three specific aspects of Robey's theory failed to replicate. First, the predicted relationship between conflict and satisfactory conflict resolution failed to materialize. Second, a negative path between influence and conflict was obtained. And third, the direct path from participation to conflict (consistent with Robey's data, but not his theory) was again found.

Had we restricted ourselves to a replication of Robey's theory, by using the single conflict item and investigating only the construct of conflict, the same three results would have been reported. This analysis (again, not reported in Barki and Hartwick (1994)) yields little support for hypothesized Model 1, based on Robey's theory (Chisquare = 67.02, df = 24, p < 0.001; Chi-square/df = 2.80; NNFI = 0.917; CFI = 0.945; AASR = 0.040). This lack of support stems from nonsignificant paths between conflict and satisfactory conflict resolution, and between influence and conflict. However, it also stems in part from a need to include a path from participation to conflict. Thus, hypothesized Model 2 appears to do better (Chi-square = 46.81, df $= 2 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 ; { \mathrm { C h i - s q u a r e } } / { \mathrm { d f } } = 2 . 0 3 ; { \mathrm { N N F I } } = 0 . 9 5 2 ; { \mathrm { C F I } } = 0 . 9 6 9 ; { \mathrm { A A S R } } = 0 . 0 3 3 )$ Unfortunately, the apparent overall superiority of this model is in part due to the inclusion of the nonpredicted negative path from influence to conflict. On the other hand, the predicted path from participation to conflict was significant (with a path coefficient of $0 . 8 5 ; t = 3 . 2 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Moreover, the hypothesized path from conflict to satisfactory conflict resolution was found to be nonsignificant. These results are similar to those reported in Barki and Hartwick (1994).

The results, then, fail to replicate those obtained by Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993). Future research will be needed to determine the reasons for this failure. However, it must be kept in mind that this failure occurs at the level of overall theory. There are several specific relationships which do replicate, and in which we feel considerable confidence can be placed. We would like to conclude with a brief summary of the relationships that have been consistently found in Robey's studies and our own.

First, the research finds a strong positive relationship between user participation and influence. Users who participate in system development efforts consistently report having had greater influence on the systems that result. Second, a positive relationship is consistently found between user participation and conflict. Users who participate in system development efforts repeatedly report having been engaged in a greater degree or number of conflicts. Third, the research finds a consistent and strong relationship between influence and satisfactory conflict resolution. Individuals with influence achieve a more satisfactory resolution of the conflicts they find themselves in. To the practitioner, then, we may say the following. Have users participate in system development efforts. More conflict will probably occur. However, with participation, users will gain influence and with this influence, achieve a satisfactory resolution of this conflict. As such, participation and conflict are likely to play a constructive role in the development of new information systems.
