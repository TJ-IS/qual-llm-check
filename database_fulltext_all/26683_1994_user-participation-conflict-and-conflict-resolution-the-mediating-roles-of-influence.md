User participation has long been considered a key ingredient in information system development (ISD). However, research has generally failed to clearly demonstrate the benefits of user participation. A better description of interpersonal processes which occur during system development could be used to help explain the weak results. The present study builds upon the work of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, Robey et al. 1993), who examined user participation, influence, conflict, and conflict resolution during ISD. Results obtained in a field study of 74 IS projects suggest the following four conclusions: (i) conflict is best represented and measured as a multidimensional construct; (ii) the relationship between user participation and conflict is more complex than previously believed; (iii) influence has a dual role in the emergence of conflict; and (iv) influence plays a key role in the satisfactory resolution of conflict.

User participation—Conflict—Influence—System development

Iser participation has long been considered a key ingredient in the development of information systems (Ives and 'Olson 1984). Two streams of participation research can be identified. The first stream of research has investigated the impact of user participation on system development outcomes; for example, system quality, attitude, use, and satisfaction (e.g., Hartwick and Barki 1994, Ives and Olson 1984, Pettingell et al. 1989). The results from this research have generally been considered weak, and inconclusive. For example, Pettingell et al. (1989) in a meta-analytic review, found average correlations ranging from 0.23 to 0.34 between participation and system attitudes, and an average correlation of 0.12 between participation and system use. Several explanations have been provided for the weak results. Ives and Olson (1984) suggested methodological and measurement problems attenuated the relationships which were observed in past research. Doll and Torkzadeh (1989) have found that the effect of user participation on satisfaction can depend upon users' expectations concerning their participation. Hartwick and Barki (1994) have noted a number of psychological variables (e.g., involvement, attitudes, and subjective norm), which intervene and mediate the relationship between user participation and system use. All of these explanations have merit.

The second stream of research has looked at interpersonal processes, such as communication. influence. and conflict, which occur during system development; (e.g., Bostrom 1989, Franz and Robey 1984, Hirscheim 1985, Robey et al. 1989). These processes can also be considered as intervening and mediating the relationship between user participation and system outcomes. Because such processes can have positive or negative effects on the outcomes of development efforts, their understanding can also help explain the weak results of past research. The present study continues these efforts. In doing so, it builds upon the work of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993), who have examined the relationship between user participation, and the three constructs of influence, conflict, and conflict resolution.

## User Participation

Barki and Hartwick (1989) define user participation as the behaviors, assignments, and activities that users or their representatives perform during information system development (ISD). Barki and Hartwick (1994) identified three dimensions of user participation: Responsibility, Hands-on Activities, and User-IS Relationship. Responsibility refers to user activities and assignments reflecting overall leadership or accountability for the system development project. Examples include being the leader of the project team, having responsibility for the overall success of the system, and being responsible for selecting hardware or software, estimating costs, requesting funds, etc. Hands-on Activities refers to specific physical design and implementation tasks performed by users. Examples include defining screen layouts and report formats, creating a user procedures manual, and designing a user training program. User-IS Relationship refers to the communication, evaluation, and approval activities which take place between users and IS staff. Examples include the initial evaluation and approval of a formal agreement of work to be done by the IS staff, being kept informed by the IS staff during various stages of ISD, and the evaluation and approval of work done by the IS staff. A measure of user participation encompassing these three dimensions was proposed and validated by Barki and Hartwick (1994) This measure is employed in the present study.

## Conflict

The topics of conflict and conflict resolution have received considerable attention in the communication and management literatures. In early work, conflict was thought to be bad for the organization, a destructive situation to be eliminated. In recent vears, conflict has come to be seen as a dynamic process, something to be managed (Kolb and Sheppard 1985). Now, conflict is generally considered to be neither bad nor good (Deutsch 1973). Instead, it is seen as an opportunity for growth and change. If properly managed, the divergence of interests and opinions inherent in conflict situations can stimulate the development of new and creative solutions to the problems facing an organization. That is not to say that all conflicts will result in positive outcomes. If poorly managed, conflict can produce uncomfortable and destructive results (Filley 1975, Pruitt and Rubin 1986).

At the present time, multiple definitions of conflict exist. For example, Putnam and Wilson (1982) define conflict as “content-oriented differences of opinion that occur in interdependent relationships and can develop into incompatible goals and interests" (p. 633). Hocker and Wilmot (1985) define conflict as “an expressed struggle between at least two interdependent parties who perceive incompatible goals scarce rewards, and interference from the other party in achieving their goals" (p. 23). An examination of various definitions reveals three key facets of conflicts. First. conflicts occur among interdependent parties. Second, there is a divergence of interests, opinions, or goals among these parties. Third, these differences appear incompatible to the parties, with one party's actions interfering with the attainment of the other party's goals, thus leading to expressed struggles.

These conditions can frequently be found during ISD. In most cases, ISD involves multiple interdependent parties (e.g., users, IS staff, management, and vendors). Further, the interests and goals of such parties often differ (DeBrabander and Thiers 1984, Dos Santos and Hawk 1988, Kaiser and Bostrom 1982, Kumar and Welke 1984, Smith and McKeen 1992). For example, IS staff may take a systems view of tasks and operations, focusing upon the technical aspects of a system; upper management may take an organizational perspective, focusing upon system goals; users, on the other hand, may take an operational perspective, focusing upon how the system will be used. During ISD, each party is likely to behave in a manner which furthers their own personal goals. To the extent that these actions interfere with other parties' goals, conflicts arise.

Conflict is evidenced by the existence of a number of symptôms: intergroup hostility and jealousy, interpersonal friction, poor lateral and vertical communication, a proliferation of rules and regulations, appeals to higher authority, and low morale (Handy 1986). Numerous examples of these symptoms have been noted in the IS literature (Barki and Saunders 1990)—hostility and jealousy (e.g., Orlikowski 1989, Smith and McKeen 1992), negative perceptions of the other parties (e. g., Gingras and Mclean 1982, Smith and McKeen 1992), poor communication (e.g., Bostrom 1989, Franz and Robey 1984), a proliferation of technical rules, norms, and regulations (e.g., Franz and Robey 1984), and frustration and low morale (e.g., Glasser 1981).

It is also important to note that conflict, as well as other interpersonal processes such as participation, influence, and conflict resolution, can be studied at either an individual or group level of analysis. At the group level, one can then study the amount that a group participates, the extent of the group's influence, conflict both within the group and between the group and other groups, and the resolution of these conflicts. To assess such variables, researchers may either directly observe the group and its process, or employ aggregates of individual group members' perceptions of such phenomena (e.g., Robey et al. 1989). Conflict, however, can also be studied at the individual level. As noted by Robey et al. (1989), “The individual level of analysis is appropriate for examining the psychological experiences of participation in a group process, influence over others, and engagement in conflict and its resolution" (p. 1175). Consistent with the work of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993), the present study employs the individual level of analysis.¹

## Participation and Conflict

Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993) have investigated the relationship between user participation and conflict. Influence plays a central role in the formulation of their model. According to Robey et al., influence is defined as the extent to which users have affected the final design of an IS. It is through participation that users attain and exercise their influence. Thus, influence is seen as an important consequence of user participation.

Influence plays two additional roles in the Robey model. First, influence is said to mediate the participation-conflict relationship. Second, influence is necessary for the satisfactory resolution of conflicts. "User participation should lead to conflicts, which should then be satisfactorily resolved. However, conflict and its resolution are more likely to occur when users can exercise their influence in the development process" (Robey and Farrow 1982, pp. 74–75). Figure 1 depicts the Robey et al. model.

While Robey and his colleagues found empirical support for a direct path from user participation to conflict in all three of their studies, they dropped this link from the model in both their 1989 and 1993 papers, stating "Participation is necessary for one to exert influence, but it seems illogical to expect that participation without influence can produce conflict" (Robey et al. 1989, p. 1180). We disagree with this argument. User participation can lead to conflict for at least three different reasons. First, as Robey et al. (1989) note, users who participate in ISD voice opinions or perform behaviors (thereby exerting influence) which can lead to conflicts with other users, upper management, or IS staff (when such opinions and behaviors differ from those of the other parties involved). Thus, by exerting their own influence, users may engender conflicts with other parties. The Participation → Influence → Conflict path of the Robey et al. model depicts this process.

However, as users participate in ISD, there is also greater interpersonal contact between users and other parties. Increased contact is likely to heighten the awareness and importance of differences among the parties. Thus, when others (other users, upper management, or IS staff) voice their opinions or perform behaviors in pursuit of their goals (thereby exerting their influence), conflicts can also occur (when others opinions or behaviors differ from those of the user). Further, user participation is likely to provide an occasion for the expression of conflicts of a more personal nature (for example, conflicts arising from jealousies, personality differences, and past actions of the parties). Both of these mechanisms, stemming from increased contact during participation, can be represented with a direct Participation → Conflict path. This path (along with the indirect path noted above) is therefore needed to fully capture the relationship between User Participation and Conflict.

## Conflict Resolution

The model proposed by Robey and his colleagues suggests two determinants of Satisfactory Conflict Resolution: Conflict and Influence. In the Robey et al. model, Conflict can be conceptualized as the number of conflicts that a user is engaged in, or as the intensity of such conflicts. In each case, a negative relationship between Conflict and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution can be expected. A “numbers" interpretation suggests that the more numerous the conflicts that a user is engaged in, the less likely it is that he/she will resolve each and every one of them to his/her satisfaction (possibly due to insufficient time or energy). An “intensity" interpretation suggests that the greater the intensity of a conflict that a user is engaged in, the less likely it is that he/she will resolve it to his/her satisfaction (possibly due to the greater importance of the issue or greater differences between involved parties).

Robey et al. also suggest a positive path from Influence to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. Without influence, ISD conflicts may not be resolved to a user's satisfaction. Influence can facilitate satisfactory conflict resolution in a number of ways. As Ury et al. (1988) note, there are at least three ways of resolving conflicts: (i) reconciling interests and goals, typically through negotiation; (ii) applying independent standards, to determine what is right or fair; and, (iii) power tactics. In ISD, users can employ all three to satisfactorily resolve the conflicts which have arisen.

## Goals of the Study

The present study was conducted to replicate and extend the Robey et al. model. Our hypothesized research model includes a path linking User Participation directly to Conflict. As noted above, this path was empirically supported in all three studies conducted by Robey and his colleagues, but dropped from their model on conceptual grounds. Given our disagreement with their justification, the present study aimed to once again empirically support the presence of this link, and then to include it in a theoretical model of participation and conflict. In doing so, we used a diverse sample of organizations and systems, an expanded 20-item measure of user participation and structural equation modeling techniques.

## Method

In an earlier study, Barki and Hartwick (1994) identified 130 organizations who were planning to start the development of a new business-oriented IS application. Applications selected for inclusion in the sample were new systems (not enhancements of existing systems) which were to be developed in-house. One thousand and fifty-nine potential users of these systems were identified. A user was defined as a person who, as part of his or her regular job, would either use the system hands-on or make use of system outputs. Each user was mailed a pre-development questionnaire assessing expectations concerning the new system and its development process. Two hundred and ninety-three questionnaires were returned.

Three months after each system was put into use, users were mailed a post-implementation questionnaire. Responses to this questionnaire provide the data which are studied in this paper. The following constructs were assessed: User Participation during ISD (with items measuring the three participation dimensions of Responsibility, Hands-on Activities and User-IS Relationship), Influence (assessed with three items), Conflict (assessed with three items), and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution (assessed with two items). This questionnaire was mailed to 212 users (the remaining 81 respondents of the predevelopment questionnaire were not contacted because system development had either been abandoned or delayed). One hundred and twenty-seven usable questionnaires were returned, representing 74 different IS projects in 60 organizations, and forms the present study's sample.

## Sample Characteristics

Thirty-nine percent of the respondents were male, 61 percent female. Their average age was 37 years, ranging from 21 to 64 years. Thirty-one percent of the respondents identified themselves as having elementary or high school education, 24 percent as having some university education, 30 percent as having a university degree, and the remaining 16 percent as having some post-graduate education. On average, respondents stated that they had 16 years of work experience, ranging from 2 to 44 years. They had been in their current organization an average of 10 years, with a range of 0 to 24 years. Thus, a diverse and representative set of respondents form the study sample.

## Operationalization of Constructs

User Participation. Three dimensions of User Participation identified by Barki and Hartwick (1994). Responsibility, Hands-on Activities, and User-IS Relationship, were assessed in the study. These three dimensions were operationalized with six-, six-, and eight-item scales, respectively, developed by Barki and Hartwick (1994). The 20 participation items are presented in the Appendix.

For each of the 20 items, respondents answered yes or no to indicate whether or not they had performed the activity. Responses were scored 1 (yes) or 0 (no). Scores for the three participation dimensions were obtained by averaging scale items. The means and standard deviations for each scale are presented in Table 1.

Internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach alpha) for the three participation scales were 0.84, 0.77, and 0.85, respectively. Further evidence for the reliability and validity of the scales used to assess each dimension is presented in Barki and Hartwick (1994).

Influence, Conflict, and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. The present study may be viewed as an extension of the three studies conducted by Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993). Questions adapted from these three studies were used to assess the constructs of Influence, Conflict, and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. Three items were used to measure Influence: (1) How much influence did you have in decisions made about this system during its development? (2) To what extent were your opinions about this system actually considered by others? and (3) Overall, how much personal influence did you have on this system? Answers to each question were made on a 7-point unipolar scale ranging from "none" (1) to “very much" (7).

Conflict was measured with three items: (1) Was there much conflict concerning this system between yourself and others? (2) To what extent were you directly involved in disagreements about this system? and (3) Was there much debate about the issues concerning this system between yourself and others? Answers were again made on 7-point unipolar scales ranging from “none" (1) to “very much" (7)

Satisfactory Conflict Resolution was measured with two items: (1) To what extent were opinion differences about this system resolved to the mutual satisfaction of conflicting parties? and (2) To what extent were conflicts concerning this system resolved to your own personal satisfaction? Answers were made on 7-point unipolar scales ranging from “not at all" (1) to “very much" (7).

## Analytical Procedures

As recommended by Joreskog (1993), data analysis proceeded in two steps. In step one, a measurement model for the latent constructs was first specified and tested. The model was then refined to create a “best" measurement model. In the measurement model, all latent constructs are allowed to correlate with one another. The measurement model then examines the indicators of each latent construct. In the present study, four latent constructs were assessed: Participation (with the three participation subscales as indicators), Influence (with the three influence items as indicators), Conflict (with the three conflict items as indicators), and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution (with the two satisfactory conflict resolution items as indicators). This analysis indicated that one of the constructs, Conflict, could best be modeled by splitting it into two constructs, Conflict and Disagreement. The "best" measurement model reflects this change.

In a causal model, correlations between latent constructs are replaced with specific causal relationships. The causal model, however, should be based on theory (Joreskog 1993). In the second step of our analysis, two theoretical causal models were analyzed and compared. Both of these models incorporate modifications based on the results of the measurement model analysis. Thus, the single Conflict construct was replaced by the two constructs of Disagreement and Conflict, with Diságreement hypothesized to lead to Conflict. This link follows from the conflict literature where disagreement, or divergence of opinions and goals, is seen as a key cause of conflict (Handy 1986). In addition, the antecedents of Conflict were now hypothesized as antecedents of both Disagreement and Conflict. Similarly, the consequences of Conflict were now hypothesized as consequences of both Disagreement and Conflict. The modified Robey et al. model is presented in Figure 2A; our hypothesized model, following modification, is presented in Figure 2B.

The study data was analyzed using EQS, a structural equation modelling program developed by Bentler (1989). EQS implements a general mathematical and statistical approach to the analysis of linear structural equations, incorporating both latent and manifest variables. With structural equation modelling, one is able to do two things: (1) check the overall goodness of fit of a proposed model, and (2) compare the relative goodness of fit of competing models, thereby assessing the need for, and strength of different model paths.

Overall Goodness of Fit. Currently, there is no one accepted measure of overall goodness of fit. Thus, researchers recommend the use of multiple fit criteria (Bollen and Long 1993, Breckler 1990, Tanaka 1993). The present study uses a set of five goodness of fit indices in order to assess model fit.

The first, a chi-square statistic, tests the proposed model against the general alternative where all variables are correlated. With this index, significant values indicate poor model fit, whereas nonsignificant values indicate good fit. While the most popular index, the chi-square has its problems. Being a direct function of sample size, the chi-square statistic will almost always be significant in large samples. On the other hand, the statistic may not be chi-square distributed in small samples, leading to inaccurate probability values (Bentler and Bonnett 1980, Joreskog 1993).

Chi-square/degrees of freedom is the second index employed in this study. This index provides an indication of model ft per degree of freedom used, with smaller values indicating better fit. Two thresholds for reasonable fit have been proposed in the literature: 3 or less (Carmines and MacIver 1981), and 5 or less (Wheaton et al. 1977).

The third index, the Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI), was proposed by Bentler and Bonnett (1980). This index, a transformation of chi-square, takes into account the number of model parameters used to fit the data, penalizing more complex (i.e., less parsimonious) models. It typically ranges from 0 to 1, with larger values indicating better fit. Values greater than 0.9 are considered to reflect good model fit.

The fourth index, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), was proposed by Bentler (1990) as an index that would fit well at all sample sizes, and provide a more stable estimate than NNFI. CFI also ranges from 0 to 1, with larger values indicating better fit. Again, values greater than 0.9 are considered to reflect good model fit.

The fifth index, the Average Absolute Standardized Residual (AASR), provides an indication of the proportion of variance not explained by the model. Consequently, smaller values indicate better fit.

Relative Goodness of Fit. An accepted index for testing the comparative fit of hierarchical or nested models does exist. The Chi-square difference test (Bentler and Bonett 1980) assesses the statistical significance of parameters differentiating two nested models. A statistically significant difference in chi-square indicates that the general model (the one with additional parameters) has a significantly better fit, and thus, the additional parameters are needed. In addition, the statistical significance of individual model parameters can be tested with a t-test.

## Results

Analysis of the Measurement Model

As the first step in the analysis, the measurement model for the four latent constructs of User Participation (with the three participation subscales as indicators), Influence (with the three influence items as indicators), Conflict (with the three conflict items as indicators), and Satisfactory Conflict Resolution (with the two satisfactory conflict resolution items as indicators) was assessed (Measurement Model 1).

Goodness of fit indices for Measurement Model 1 indicate a poor fit. Not only was the chi-square index found to be significant (chi-square = 106.26, df = 38, p < 0.001), the remaining four fit indices also suggested a poor fit for this model (chi-square/df = 2.80; NNFI = 0.892; CFI = 0.925; AASR = 0.048). An examination of the standardized residual matrix indicated that the poor fit was in large part due to the measurement of the Conflict construct. Specifically, large covariance residuals remained between the three Conflict indicators and other model parameters.

These results suggested fit could be improved by modeling Conflict as two separate constructs: Conflict (indicated with the single item—Conflict item 1) and Disagreement (indicated with the single item—Conflict item 2), were suggested. This split is also consistent with various conflict definitions which view disagreement as a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for defining conflict. The third Conflict item was weakly related to each of the other two and, because of its ambiguity (i.e., the presence of debates does not necessarily indicate the presence of conflict), was discarded. The chi-square index was found to be significant (Chi-square = 112.73, df = 32, ${ \pmb p } < { \pmb 0 } . 0 0 1 )$ ). Thus, model fit significantly deteriorates. Further, the remaining fit indices for Causal Model 3 were all inferior to those of Causal Model 2 (chi-square $\mathbf { \nabla } / d f = 3 . 5 2 ;$ NNFI = 0.869; CFI = 0.907; AASR $\mathbf { \delta } = 0 . 1 1 8 )$ . Confirming this, a chi-square difference test, comparing Causal Models 2 and 3, was significant (chi-square difference = 58.90, df = 4, $\begin{array} { r } { p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; } \end{array}$ ). Thus, deleting all nonsignificant paths from Causal Model 2 worsens model fit.

Next, to explore which of the four paths need to be included in a “best-fitting" model, each path was individually added to Causal Model 3, and the four resulting models assessed. Two points can be made concerning these analyses. First, neither the path from Conflict to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution, nor the path from Disagreement to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution, was significant (both $t ^ { \mathrm { * } } < 1 . 3 0 ,$ , ns.), nor did they improve model fit. Thus, both can be excluded. Second, the two paths, from User Participation to Disagreement, and from Influence to Disagreement, were significant when individually added to the model $\mathbf { ( } t ^ { * } s = 5 . 3 1$ and 7.85, both ${ \pmb p } ^ { \bullet }$ $< 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and improved model fit. These paths, then, would seem to be needed.

Based on these results, a "best-fitting" model, Causal Model 4, was developed and assessed. This model kept the two paths found to be significant in the above analyses, but excluded the two nonsignificant paths. Goodness of fit indices for Causal Model 4 are presented in the last column of Table 2. The chi-square index was found to be significant (chi-square = 57.54, df = 30, p < 0.01). The remaining fit indices for this model were chi-square/df = 1.92, NNFI = 0.952, CFI = 0.968, and $\mathbf { A A S R } = \mathbf { 0 . 0 3 6 }$ To ensure that this model represented an improvement over previous models, Chisquare difference tests were performed, comparing Causal Model 4 with Causal Models 2 and 3. The test comparing Causal Models 3 and 4 was significant (chi-square difference = 55.19, df = 2, p < 0.001), indicating that the addition of the two significant paths (from User Participation to Disagreement, and from Influence to Disagreement), improved model fit. On the other hand, the test comparing Causal Models 2 and 4 was nonsignificant (chi-square difference = 3.71, df = 2, ns.), indicating that the deletion of the two nonsignificant paths from our hypothesized model did not worsen fit. Thus, Causal Model 4 would seem to represent a "best-fitting" model for the study data.³

The path coefficients for Causal Model 4 are depicted in Figure 3. Several observations can be made concerning this model. Consistent with both our hypothesized model, and the model of Robey et al., significant paths from User Participation to Influence, and from Influence to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution were observed. These paths suggest that users, through their participation in system development, will influence the system that is developed. Further, through this influence, they will satisfactorily resolve the conflicts which arise during system development.

Not all paths, however, are consistent with the Robey et al. model. Robey et al. have suggested that the relationship between User Participation and Conflict is mediated by Influence. However, our data indicate a positive direct path from User Participation to Conflict. In addition, a weak path from User Participation to Disagreement is also observed. Thus, User Participation is found to have an effect on Disagreement and Conflict not mediated by Influence. Robey et al. also suggest a positive relationship between Influence and Conflict. Consistent with this, we have found a weak positive relationship between Influence and Disagreement. Taken together with the positive path between Disagreement and Conflict, a weak positive effect of Influence on Conflict (mediated by Disagreement), is confirmed. On the other hand, a strong negative direct path between Influence and Conflict is also observed. Influence has, therefore, both positive and negative effects on Conflict. Users with influence are apt to have more disagreement, which in turn, leads to greater conflict. However, independent of disagreement, users with influence will have less conflict.

## Discussion

## The Measurement of Conflict

In the analysis of the measurement models, it quickly became apparent that the three-item measure of conflict used by Robey et al. was multidimensional in nature. In particular, Conflict item 1 (Was there much conflict concerning this system between yourself and others?) and Conflict item 2 (To what extent were you directly involved in disagreements about this system?) were not highly related, leading to large covariance residuals between these and other items assessed in the study. This finding suggested a two-dimensional representation of conflict: Conflict (assessed with item 1) and Disagreement (assessed with item 2). Empirically supporting this representation, a measurement model employing the two constructs provided a much better fit of the study data.

The two-dimensional representation of conflict also makes sense from a conceptual standpoint. Our examination of various conflict definitions suggested three key facets of the concept. The first is a divergence of interests, opinions, or goals among the parties. This facet seems to parallel our empirically-derived construct of Disagreement. Not all disagreements, however, will develop into conflicts. The other two facets seem relevant here. For conflict to arise, the parties must be interdependent. Further, the parties' actions must interfere with the attainment of one another's goals. It is these two conditions, interdependence and interference, that can escalate disagreements into full-blown conflicts. This reasoning would suggest a causal link from Disagreement to Conflict. Such a path was included, and empirically supported, in the causal analysis of the study data.

## The Determinants of Disagreement, Conflict, and Conflict Resolution

Disagreement. As various parties participate in ISD, differences in their interests, opinions, or goals are apt to prompt disagreements among them. Consistent with this, User Participation was found to be positively related to Disagreement. Three causal representations linking User Participation to Disagreement were equally predictive of the study data: a model including the single path from Participation to Disagreement, a model including the single path from Participation through Influence to Disagreement, and a model including both of these paths. Given that there was no clear reason for selecting one or the other of the two paths, we decided to retain the third model, containing both paths. This model suggests two different ways in which User Participation can lead to Disagreement. The mediated path, from Participation through Influence to Disagreement, suggests an active role for the user in the development of disagreements. As users participate they exert influence aimed at supporting their own interests, opinions, or goals. As they exert this influence, disagreements could then arise with other parties who have different interests, opinions, or goals. The direct path, from Participation to Disagreement, suggests a more passive role for the user. As users participate in ISD, they are more likely to come into contact with other parties. When these other parties exert influence aimed at supporting their interests, opinions, or goals, disagreements could also occur.

Conflict. Three causal determinants of Conflict were observed, User Participation (positive path), Disagreement (positive path), and Influence (negative path). The two positive paths confirm the main study hypothesis, that User Participation leads to Conflict, both directly and indirectly. As noted above, users who participate in ISD are more likely to experience disagreements with other users, upper management, or IS staff. Such disagreements may then escalate into conflicts. This is represented by the indirect path from User Participation to Conflict through Disagreement. In addition, conflicts of a more personal nature may also arise as one participates in ISD (for example, conflicts stemming from jealousies, personality differences, or past actions of the parties). This is represented by the direct path from User Participation to Conflict, and differs from the Robey et al. model.

A negative path from Influence to Conflict was also observed. This finding, while unanticipated by Robey et al. and ourselves, is significant. The existence of this path suggests a further role for influence in the prevention of conflicts. Specifically, it suggests that conflicts are least likely when users have influence, and most likely when they have no influence. This could occur as follows. When users have influence, they will generally get what they want, and consequently experience little conflict. On the other hand, users without influence are less likely to get what they want. As they view the situation, they may feel disenfranchised and frustrated, experiencing conflict with others.

Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. All three studies conducted by Robey and his colleagues hypothesized and found some support for a negative path from Conflict to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. However, this relationship was not observed in the present study. Conflict Resolution was found to be solely determined by Influence. Thus, individuals with greater influence were more likely to resolve conflicts to their satisfaction. Further, the lack of a direct path from Conflict to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution indicates that this satisfactory resolution occurs regardless of the number or intensity of conflicts.

## Summary and Implications

The study reported here was designed to extend the works of Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993). There are three areas of disagreement between the observed results and their work. First, Robey et al. conceptualized Conflict as a unidimensional construct. However, our results suggest the presence of at least two dimensions, Disagreement and Conflict. In the present study, these two constructs were each operationalized with single-item measures. Future work will need to develop a new and better measure of conflict, reflecting all of its facets. Our review of the conflict literature suggests three such facets, divergence of interests or opinions, interdependence among the parties, and interference in the attainment of goals.

Second, the determinants of conflict were found to be more complex than theorized by Robey et al. In addition to an indirect path from User Participation through Influence to Conflict, the present paper argued and found empirical support for the presence of a direct link between User Participation and Conflict. Thus, several different mechanisms seem to underlie the relationship between participation and conflict. Further, both positive (Influence → Disagreement → Conflict) and negative links (Influence → Conflict) between Influence and Conflict were observed. Influence would therefore seem to have multiple mediating roles between participation and conflict. Future research will need to take this complexity into account, clearly specifying and testing the various underlying mechanisms which have been suggested. It is through such efforts that a better understanding of conflict processes in ISD can be achieved.

Third, Robey and his colleagues (Robey and Farrow 1982, Robey et al. 1989, 1993) hypothesized and found partial support for a negative causal path from Conflict to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution. This relationship was not observed in our data. Future research needs to clarify this relationship, explaining when, where, and why such a negative link exists. A direct link from Conflict to Satisfactory Conflict Resolution fails to take into account the specific actions performed by conflicting parties in order to resolve conflicts. Thus, one key variable that needs to be considered is the approach adopted for managing conflict.

In comparing the results of this study with those of Robey and his colleagues some caution needs to be exercised. The model analyses reported here and those reported in the three Robey et al. papers differ in one key respect: the Robey et al. construct of Conflict was revised and analyzed as the two separate constructs of Disagreement and Conflict. This differentiation alone is likely to influence the magnitudes of various path coefficients, thereby making difficult a direct comparison of our model with that of Robey et al. In addition, differences in the measurement of user participation and the samples studied may also have contributed to the differences observed between the studies.\*
