An important management question today is whether the anticipated economic benefits of Information Technology (IT) are being realized. In this paper, we consider this problem to be measurement related, and propose and test a new process-oriented methodology for ex post measurement to audit IT impacts on a strategic business unit (SBU) or profit center's performance. The IT impacts on a given SBU are measured relative to a group of SBUs in the industry. The methodology involves a two-stage analysis of intermediate and higher level output variables that also accounts for industry and economy wide exogenous variables for tracing and measuring IT contributions. The data for testing the proposed model were obtained from SBUs in the manufacturing sector. Our results show significant positive impacts of IT at the intermediate level. The theoretical contribution of the study is a methodology that attempts to circumvent some of the measurement problems in this domain. It also provides a practical management tool to address the question of why (or why not) certain IT impacts occur. Additionally, through its process orientation, the suggested approach highlights key variables that may require managerial attention and subsequent action.

## 1. Introduction

he annual investment in information technologies (computers, office and communication equipment) by U.S. industries is hundreds of billions of dollars. This staggering, figure does not include money spent on software and systems development. For example, according to S. S. Roach of Morgan Stanley (1987) and P. A. Strassman (Berger 1988, p. 22)

• Information technology “hardware" accounted for 32.5% of all business capitalequipment expenses in 1986.

• These categories were 90.4% of business capital investment in “high tech" in 1986.

• The “information sector" accounts for 83.6% of all “high tech" capital stock in the U.S.

Furthermore, the performance/price gain in “computers" for the 30 year period 1954–1984 has been estimated to be at an annual rate of 19% (Gordon 1987). Today, the senior executive responsible for most of the investment decisions in IT is the Chief Information Officer (or CIO). Surprisingly,

“About 60 percent of the CFOs, CEOs, COOs and other top officials use gut instinct' as a criteria for evaluating the CIO, accordıng to a 1991 survey of 200 executives by Coopers & Lybrand" (Marion 1992, p. 29).

The rapid growth of IT investments puts pressure on management to make more informed decisions. At the same time, executives are confronted with conflicting information for major investment decisions. On the one hand, academics and professionals argue that information technology (IT) is tomorrow's key to competitive advantage and bolster their arguments with (now popular) anecdotal case histories of success (e.g., Cash and Konsynski 1985; McFarlan 1984; Porter and Millar 1985; Wiseman 1985, 1988; Kramer and King 1986; Laudon and Turner 1989). However the investment commitment requires an even larger commitment of faith. On the other hand, there is a literature of empirical research which suggests that (among other things) the “productivity gains" from investments in IT over the past decades are neutral or negative (e.g., Salerno 1985, Roach 1987). Who is right? Or, what is wrong?

At the national and industry sector levels of analysis, part of the problem may be due to measurement or mismeasurement issues (e.g., see Baily and Gordon 1989) That is, most of the macro studies rely on the Federal Government (Dept. of Commerce or Bureau of Economic Analysis/Bureau of Labor Statistics) as the primary data source, and the quality of these data has been questioned (Gordon 1989) on various grounds. But what about the level of the individual enterprise? Are the anticipated results being achieved and the business value of IT realized? If the answer is “yes", the corollary question is: Why or how? If the answer is “no", then: Why not?

We do not believe that management (in general) is or has been irrational in its IT investment decisions, despite the noise at times in the information it has received as advice. Economic survival precludes that. We believe there are deeper answers to the questions raised casually above which subsequently carry normative implications for investments in IT. In particular, our research addresses the following questions:

• What type of economic impacts exist and what is the nature of the contribution (if any) from IT?

• How can the IT impacts on the performance of organizational units (the enterprise, profit centers, etc.) in the private sector be quantified and measured?

• What theory (economics, management, etc.) can explain the phenomena involved?

• Does empirical evidence support this theory?

A key to developing answers to these questions is also, in part, a measurement issue, But it differs from the macro level problems alluded to above. IT benefits and the processes through which they are created in the firm are often elusive and difficult to quantify and measure. What is needed is a process-oriented model of the enterprise (incorporating exogenous competitive influences) to understand the creation of IT impacts, and a scientific approach to measuring the economic consequences of IT investments. Through a process model at an appropriate level in the firm, it is possible to identify key performance variables (the impacts), their coefficients or parameters (the contribution), and possibly to derive normative implications for information systems (IS) and general management regarding IT investments and IS usage. We elaborate on these concepts and our approach to developing answers in the balance of this paper.

In the next section we review some of the relevant research on these issues and the potentially conflicting conclusions reported. These results lead us to conjecture reasons for the conflicting results and the rationale for a conceptual model. Next we outline a research strategy and approach, propose a methodology, present a specific model, and estimate it with data from the manufacturing sector. Our results confirm our hypotheses regarding the low level impacts of IT related factors. IT inputs were found to have a significant positive effect on the intermediate level output variables. The contribution to one high-level performance variable was positive, while it was insignificant for another. While our results are restricted to a part of the manufacturing sector and our dataset is limited, our approach to measuring the contribution of IT related factors to higher level output performance variables via intermediate variables can be applied to a variety of settings. Therefore, we believe the contribution of this study is the development and initial testing of a generalized process-oriented modeling approach to IT impacts measurement.

## 2. Motivation

In recent years there have been numerous studies that attempt to estimate the economic impacts of IT investments. In a critical review of this literature, Brynjolfsson (1993) identifies at least four reasons for this controversy including measurement problems. possible lags between IT investments and impacts, redistribution of outputs within an industry and mismanagement. Brooke (1991) hypothesizes that the primary impact of IT is greater product variety leading to lower economies of scale and lower profitability. Alpar and Kim (1990) call for theory based research and propose a microeconomic cost model for IT impact analysis. Our goal here is not to review this growing literature, but to discuss a subset of the recent studies to motivate our thesis.

The results of past studies are inconclusive. Some of these studies have found little or negative impacts of IT on firm productivity, while others have identified significant positive impacts. The studies in the first category include Roach (1987), Baily and Chakrabarti (1988), and Loveman (1994).

Roach (1987) compares the productivity of information and production workers. He concludes that information worker productivity has either declined in some sectors or not kept in pace with production worker productivity in other sectors. Similarly, Baily and Chakrabarti (1988) conclude that current evidence does not indicate any significant productivity gain from IT. They argue that the failure of markets to value IT possibly leads to misallocated resources and lower productivity growth.

Loveman (1994) examines the productivity impact of IT in the manufacturing industry using data for the period 1978–1984. He does not find evidence of any significant positive impact from IT. His results indicate that “the marginal dollar would best have been spent on non-IT inputs into production, such as non-IT capital".

A notable exception in the IT impacts research is Bresnahan (1986) who estimates the welfare gains (“consumer surplus") from the use of mainframe computers in the financial services sector (FSS) from 1958 to 1972. He assumes that the nonregulated portions of the FSS are competitive so that firms act purely as agents for final consumers. He finds that the gains to consumers were five or more times the expenditures by FSS firms on computer “rentals" during the time period studied.

Gordon (1989) and others have observed that one possible reason for the seemingly lackluster impact of IT on the services sectors is the output measures employed by the Department of Commerce, i.e., mismeasurement. Consider, for example, the finance and insurance sectors that account for roughly 17% of computer/communication capital and about 7% of total capital. Gordon (1989) notes: “All our data on GNP by industry measure the output of the financial sector by labor input. That is, productivity growth is computed to be zero by definition." He cites numerous instances where mismeasurement may account for misstatements of productivity figures.

Another potential reason for the lack of positive findings for IT impact is also measurement related, but at the enterprise unit of analysis. This concerns the type and availability of data within the firm and the organizational level as the focus for the analysis. That is, typically, IT impacts studies have focused on an aggregate level of analysis (see Baily and Chakrabarti 1988, Jonscher 1983, Roach 1987, and Stabel 1982). By attempting to relate IT expenditures directly to output variables at the level of the firm (such as market share) through a microeconomic production function, the intermediate processes through which IT impacts arise are ignored. There has been a growing concern (Crowston and Treacy 1986; Kauffman and Kriebel 1988a, b; Mukhopadhyay and Cooper 1992, 1993) that the effects of IT on enterprise level performance can be identified only through “a web of intermediate level contributions."A similar approach in the form of“value-added analysis" has been suggested by Porter (1985). In fact, there is some evidence (Banker and Kauffman 1988, Kauffman and Kriebel 1988b, Banker et al. 1990, Banker and Johnston 1994) that IT impacts exist, and that they can be detected when the analysis is executed at a lower level in the firm (i.e., at the strategic business unit (SBU) stage, or within the SBU rather than at the corporate level) (see also Weill and Olson 1989). The lower level impacts, in turn, are expected to affect higher level performance measures (e.g., see King and Kramer 1989).

As illustrations, Banker and Kauffman (1988) investigate the impacts of automated teller machines (ATMs) on the local deposit market share of a bank. Kauffman and Kriebel (1988b) use a new approach called “Business Value Linkage (BVL)" to measure the impacts of treasury management workstations on demand balances from customers. Kauffman et al. (1988) use the BVL approach for point-ofsale technology. Banker and Johnston (1994) study the market share impact of computerized airline reservation systems. These studies indicate the need for more process oriented models instead of traditional “black-box" approaches. Our basic thesis is that primary economic impacts or contributions (to performance) of information technologies (if any) can be measured at lower operational levels in an enterprise, at or near the site where the technology is implemented. To capture these impacts, measurements should be taken in the organization where the potential for first-order effects exists. These effects may then be traced through a chain of relationships within the organizational hierarchy to reveal higher order impacts (if any) on enterprise performance. For example, in a manufacturing environment, investment in material requirement planning (MRP) systems may improve overall capacity utilization (firstorder effect), which, in turn, may lead to a higher return on investment (second-order effect). We suspect that as the distance between a first-order effect and higher levels increases, the ability to detect and measure an impact decreases (perhaps rapidly). For this reason, we believe prior research based on conventional microeconomic production theory (attempting to relate variables such as MIS budgets and market share directly) does not have the power to reveal an association with high statistical significance.

In summary, our review leads us to several informal conjectures at this point which we investigate on a more formal basis in the following sections:

1. The identification and isolation of the economic impacts of IT require a process oriented, industry, or company specific model.

2. The level of analysis identifying IT impacts is at the SBU and (preferably) lower responsibility unit in an organization.

3. IT contributions (if any) occur at the level of application systems and aggregate up the organization's hierarchy to the enterprise level.

4. Measurement problems have made it difficult to draw consistent conclusions from past empirical studies in this area.

5. It is viable to develop normative implications for management practice and IT investment decisions from empirical research employing value-added process models of past experiences; that is, the research can lead to prescriptive guidelines or provide insights into how to obtain them.

## 3. Proposed Methodology

To describe production processes, microeconomics employs the paradigm of a production function which relates physical input resources (factors) with output products or services (actually “maximum outputs"). This theory yields the concepts of marginal product (of an input) and marginal (revenue) product, where the latter constitutes the gross value-added of an input resource (process) to the firm's output. By isolating economically and technologically distinct activities within a business one may identify the value added of an input to individual products (e.g., Kauffman and Kriebel 1988b). The "value chain" framework of Porter (1985). on the other hand, takes a closer look at how IT may affect particular activities, and can provide a starting point for detailed IT impacts analysis. In particular, Porter (1985) has classified activities into primary (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service) and support activities. For our analysis, we are interested in finding key variables (corresponding to these activities) where IT may have significant impacts.

We conjecture that IT is expected to have first-order effects on operational level variables (such as capacity utilization and inventory turnover for a manufacturing firm). Each intermediate variable measure relates to the performance of one or more activities, e.g., production or marketing management. The intermediate variables, in turn, affect higher level variables such as the market share or profitability of the business unit as a whole. Our approach is summarized in Exhibit 1. We also include appropriate exogenous variables to reflect the industry and macroeconomic conditions during the study period.

We consider two alternatives for measuring the economic impact of IT: at the 1) firm level, and 2) application level. Any medium or large organization is expected to have multiple applications in each primary or supporting activity. Thus a firm level analysis attempts to aggregate the impact of IT over many applications from all activities. We believe the effectiveness of IT applications is not uniform across al activities (e.g., production, marketing, and innovation). Thus a firm level analysis may not provide meaningful guidelines to management.

The application level IT impact analysis does not suffer from the aggregation problem. But unlike the firm level analysis it may require a great deal of data for an organization because the number of applications may be fairly large. Moreover, the allocation of resources consumed to specific applications may not often be accurate. Thus we perform the analysis at the activity or function level. The applications are expected to be similar within an activity but different across activities. In addition, IT budgets in many organizations are set at the functional level. Thus, analysis at the functional level is also likely to be useful for management control purposes.

We also suggest that elements of the dataset should be grouped by industry type. This ensures that comparable linkages exist between IT investments and economic performance for each SBU in a given group. For example, consider two SBUs, one in the retail industry and the other in the banking and finance sector. Intuitively, the IT impacts on the retail SBU would be different from those on the B&F SBU.' Therefore, it may be misleading to compare the impacts of IT in these two SBUs on the basis of a common set of parameters. We now describe our dataset, the model specification and our conjectures about the IT impacts on the intermediate variables.

## 3.1. Description of the Dataset

The data we employ were collected by the Strategic Planning Institute (SPI), Cambridge, MA, as part of a program called “Management Productivity and Information

Technology" (MPIT). SPI administers a more well-known data analysis program called PIMS (Profit Impact of Marketing Strategies) which facilitates ongoing access by corporate subscribers to a large (300–400 time series variables) database. The MPIT dataset was patterned after the PIMS database and maintained by the same SPI staff, but it contains additional information on IT investment and expenses. SPI discontinued the MPIT program in the mid-1980s; the PIMS program continues operation.

The MPIT data was collected on approximately 60 strategıc business units (SBU) or profit centers operating in the U.S. and Western Europe from about 20 large corporations. Nearly all the SBUs are in the manufacturing sector and most (about 70%) serve industrial or commercial customers. Thc data collection employed a well-structured survey instrument patterned after the PIMS program. The instrument identified 130 variables for annual data from the years 1979 to 1983, including some observations for 1978 and 1984. Balance sheet and income statement data were also gathered. Typically, a designated person in each SBU was responsible for completing the survey instrument. For a fee each SBU could obtain summary statistics generated from the dataset. The dataset has been maintained and administered by the PIMS staff at SPI, although there are no new entries since 1984.² The distinguishing feature of the MPIT dataset is the separation of capital, labor, and purchases into IT and non-IT components (see also Strassman 1985, Loveman 1994).

The MPIT dataset is classified into three major functions: (1) production; (2) marketing, sales and distribution; and (3) innovation. In the dataset, production encompasses “inbound and outbound logistics, and operations" from the Porter framework, i.e., production represents the core of the manufacturing SBUs. Marketing, sales and distribution corresponds to Porter's “marketing and sales, and services." Finally, innovation is equivalent to Porter's “technology development" support activity.

We do not have separate data on firm infrastructure and human resource management. Instead, we have total IT and non-IT capital data. For each of the three areas, however, we have IT and non-IT purchase data as well as employee cost data. In addition, we use publicly available data for some of the exogenous variables used in our model. The definition of input, intermediate, and final performance variables appears in Exhibit 2. The list of the variables used and their summary statistics appear in Exhibit 3.3

Relative Price (REL-PRC).In our dataset, all SBUs are in the manufacturing sector, and approximately 70 percent of the SBUs are engaged in manufacturing raw or semifinished materials, or components for incorporation into finished products. In these product markets, relative price is likely to be determined both by competitive pressure and the SBU's internal cost structure. Thus the relative price of products is an important determinant of both the profitability and market share of an SBU. While pricing is primarily a marketing decision, a consideration of manufacturing cost is also important for SBUs in this sector. In this model we include all marketing variables plus labor and capital (IT and non-IT) figures for all functional areas. The two relevant exogenous variables for this model are new products of competitors and typical purchase amounts:

$$
\text { REL - PRC } = f _ {4} (\text { IT - CAP }, \text { NIT - CAP }, \text { PEMP - CST }, \text { MIT - PUR }, \text { MNIT - PUR },
$$

$$
\text { M   E   M   P   -   C   S   T }, \text { I   E   M   P   -   C   S   T }, \text { N   E   W   -   C   P   R   D }, \text { P   U   R   -   A   M   T }).\tag{4}
$$

New Products (NEW-PROD).Our fifth and final intermediate variable captures the rate of new product introduction. Obviously this performance measure is primarily driven by the innovation function. Thus we include the innovation variables in addition to the IT and non-IT capital variables in this model. The following equation also contains three exogenous variables:

$$
\text { NEW - PROD } = f _ {5} (\text { IT - CAP }, \text { NIT - CAP }, \text { IIT - PUR },
$$

$$
\text { INIT - PUR,   IEMP - CST,   MKT - GRTH,   NEW - CPRD,   RD - CST). } \tag {5}
$$

We note that in a JIT environment, as inventory is reduced, quality problems become easier to detect and correct. Thus lower level of inventory can lead to higher level of quality. During the time period under consideration (1979–1983), however, JIT was at its infancy in the U.S. and Europe (Voss and Clutterbuck 1989).ª Since JIT was not prevalent during the study period, we specify inventory turnover and inferior quality independent of each other.

Stage II. The dataset enables us to compute return on assets (ROA), return on sales, market share and value-added as the economic output variables. However, ROA is highly correlated with return on sales and value-added in this dataset.⁵ Therefore, we dropped the last two variables and retained ROA and market share as the economic performance variables of interest. The correlation between ROA and market share was 0.43, implying that both the variables should be considered in the analysis. A third measure of the economic performance of the business units is shareholder value, but it was not available in the MPIT dataset.

The marketing literature includes many studies that deal with profitability and market share. The general result of this stream of research indicates that higher market share results in superior profitability due to increased market power (Kohli et al. 1990). We draw from this literature and attempt to develop reasonable models for the two performance variables based on our dataset.

Market Share (MKT-SHR).Of the five intermediate variables, we believe that market share of an SBU is driven by relative price, relative quality, and the rate of new product introduction. In addition, we include the macroeconomic variable, prime rate change, that affects the financing cost of business transactions in the intermediate industrial goods sector from which most of our SBUs are drawn:

$$
\mathrm{MKT-SHR} = g _ {1} (\text { INF - QTY }, \text { REL - PRC }, \text { NEW - PROD }, \text { PRM - CHNG }).\tag{6}
$$

Return on Assets (ROA).Based on the marketing literature, we specify ROA as a function of market share. The intermediate variables we include in this model are capacity utilization, inventory turnover, and relative price. The general condition of the economy is captured by GNP change for the manufacturing sector:

$$
\mathrm{ROA} = g _ {2} (\text { MKT - SHR }, \text { CAP - UTL }, \text { INV - TRN }. \text { REL - PRC }, \text { GNP - CHNG }).\tag{7}
$$

We note certain aspects of the above specification. First, no equation in the system is intended to imply a microeconomic production function, in any technical sense. For example, the model suggests that capacity utilization is related to expenditures on manufacturing support (such as manufacturing IT and non-IT capita), labor, etc.) in the period, plus the rate of growth of the industry segment. Similarly, return on assets (ROA) may depend on market share, capacity utilization, and inventory turnover, etc. That s, we hypothesize that a correlational relationship may exist between the dependent and independent variables in the model, and for simplicity we specify the relationship as linear.

Second, we have not attempted (or intended) to write the model as a system of accounting identities or definitions. One might consider such an approach, applying it recursively from end-point measures; however, it rapidly degenerates due to a lack of the relevant data not captured by conventional (or even “managerial’) accounting systems. We are merely seeking to establish the strength of association, given strong a priori beliefs that such an association should exist.

Finally, we recognize that a model reflecting a series of equations is, in one sense, an “ideal," although multiple (i.e., more than two) levels might convey an enhanced sense of realism. That is, even at this simple level of abstraction, one can envision many specific designates for the variables. As an example, corresponding to “IT capital costs," it would be desirable to have finer partitions of the data which track details to the activity level, say, for production, marketing and innovation functions. Similarly, for aggregate economic outputs, it may be constructive to be able to trace to “shareholder's equity/value." Unfortunately, we do not have data to pursue this line of analysis.

Estimation of our models and the statistical tests of significance on coefficients of interest provide preliminary evidence in support (or refutation) of the conceptual model conjectures regarding intermediate level impacts of IT. Through our analysis, we can determine the impacts (positive, negative or neutral) of IT on a group of SBUs, thus providing an answer to whether or not the IT benefits have been realized. Further, we can study differences in IT contribution to intermediate performance levels, and the extent to which these differences affect performance. This helps in answering the second set of questions raised in the introduction: Why or why not?

## 3.3. IT Related Hypotheses or Conjectures

The focus of this paper is on the impact of IT on intermediate variables. The effects of non-IT factors on intermediate variables are of secondary interest. In the following paragraphs, we postulate certain hypotheses or conjectures regarding the impacts and contributions of IT related factors.

HypoTHEsis 1. IT capital and IT services purchased for the production activity have a positive association with capacity utilization.

Investment in production IT, such as for materials requirements planning scheduling systems, are expected to result in more accurate and timely information on capacity, shop floor control, production and vendor lead times, and production schedules. These should lead to increased capacity utilization through improved operations schedules and feasible capacity plans.

HyPoTHEsıs 2. IT capital, production IT purchases and marketing IT purchases have a positive association with inventory turnover.

Investment in production related IT is expected to result in better monitoring and control of raw material, work-in-process and finished goods inventory levels, accurate information on vendor lead times and timely order release. Similarly investment in marketing (sales, marketing and distribution) IT should lead to better forecasting and distribution of end items. This should lead to higher inventory turnover. According to a study by Anderson et al. (1982), inventory turnover increased by 34% on average due to the use of computer-based MRP systems. They report 3.2, 4.3 and 5.3 as the “pre-MRP estimate”, “current estimate" and “future estimate" of inventory turnover respectively.

HYPoτíEsis 3. IT capital, production IT purchases and innovation (R&D) IT purchases have a negative association with relative inferior quality

Investment in innovation (R&D) IT should lead to improvements in intrinsic product quality and design features. Similarly, investment in production related IT should lead to improved production, quality control, more accurate delivery lead times and percentage of on-time shipments. These, in turn, are expected to improve the quality of the firm's products.

HYPoτíEsıs 4. IT capital and marketing IT purchases have a negative association with relative price.

Paraphrasing, the hypothesis states that increasing I'Γ investment and purchased services can facilitate relative price reductions. Why is this logical or rational for the firm in this case? The impact of IT factors on relative price is expected to be primarily dependent on the type of products and markets involved. In this study we are dealing mostly with semifinished industrial goods and component markets where price is likely to be an important and major decision criterion for customers. We presume that long term profits are positively associated with market share and under these circumstances market share is price elastic. IT investments and purchases can provide better information on price elasticities and market phenomena. Therefore, improving IT resources can facilitate more informed price reductions to achieve market share gains. Thus our hypothesis is based on two assumptions. First, we assume that SBUs in this sector would try to achieve low relative price due to the competitive pressures, ceteris paribus. Second, pricing is primarily a marketing decision. IT capital in general and marketing IT purchases in market research, databases, software and communications services would allow an SBU to achieve the goal of lower relative price. These two IT related factors are thus expected to be negatively associated with relative price.

HYPoTíEsis 5. IT capital and innovation (R&D) IT' purchases have a positive association with new products

IT has the capability to aid in the introduction of new products by reducing the product development time (McFarlan 1984) and by adding more features through applications like CAD and CAM. Accordingly, IT inputs related to the R&D function are expected to have a positive impact on new products.

## 4. Results

The empirical analysis is carried out in two steps: 1) intermediate variables validation and 2) contribution measurement for stage I (input variables and intermediate variables). Our analysis strategy is to pool time series and cross-sectional data using a panel format for linear regressions. For each regression, we tested for multicollinearity using Belsley, Kuh and Welsch (BKW) condition indices. We report the value whenever it exceeds 30.

## 4.2. Contribution Measurement for Stage I

We again tested for heteroskedasticity in our data set for the five stage I models. The Goldfeld-Quandt test revealed the presence of heteroskedasticity (significant at 1% level) only for new products (NEW-PROD). The Breusch-Pagan test, on the other hand, indicated heteroskedasticity in inventory turnover (INV-TRN) (5% level) and relative price (REL-PRC) (1% level). However, all the time-corrected estimates for intermediate variables were made homoskedasticity-consistent by applying White's correction (1980). The results of our intermediate level analysis are shown in Table 2.

Since the independent variables for Equations (1)–(5) are not identical, contemporaneous correlation between the residuals for each SBU can not be ruled out due to interrelationships between activities within firms. However, the efficiency gain from seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) estimates is likely to be small in this case for three reasons (Judge et al. 1985, Doran and Griffiths 1983): 1) the five equations contain similar explanatory variables, 2) the estimation is based on trend data, and 3) the correlation coefficients among the explanatory variables, by year, often are greater than 0.50, and sometimes higher. Nonetheless, we obtained SUR estimates for these equations. The results were similar to the estimates presented in Table 2. We also performed a series of Spencer and Berk (1981) tests to check if any of the intermediate variable specifications should contain another intermediate variable. None of these tests violated our original specification.

Hypothesis 1 is supported by our estimation results. IT capital (IT-CAP) and purchased IT services for the production function (PIT-PUR) had significant positive impact on capacity utilization (CAP-UTL) in our data set. Also, as expected, market growth (MKT-GRTH), an exogenous variable, had a positive association with capacity utilization. The negative sign for the non-IT capital (NIT-CAP) is explained by the fact that investment in plant and equipment (non-IT capital) increases the available capacity. The sample size for this analysis was limited by the number of observations on capacity utilization in the dataset. We can summarize by saying that IT related factors had a favorable impact on capacity utilization.

Hypothesis 2 (inventory turnover) is partially supported by the data. IT and non-IT capital (IT-CAP and NIT-CAP) had highly significant positive impacts on inventory turnover (INV-TRN) in this data set. Contrary to our intuition, production IT purchases (PIT-PUR) had negative impacts on inventory turnover. Production employee costs (PEMP-CST) also had a significant negative impact. Production employee cost is calculated as the ratio of total production employee dollars to the total number of employees. Thus, it is a form of relative wage costs of production employees. One explanation for the negative impact of the variable may be the fact that SBUs with higher relative wage costs for production employees had higher levels of production (and hence inventory) to minimize costly idle time. The negative impacts of marketing variables (MIT-PUR, MNIT-PUR and MEMP-CST), although only one of the coefficients in this group is significant, are not surprising. A common tendency of the marketing function is to increase finished goods inventory to reduce the probability of stock out and improve customer satisfaction.

Hypothesis 3 (inferior quality) is supported in part by the results. IT capital (IT-CAP) was a significant factor in reducing the relative inferior quality (INF-QTY) for this sample. Similarly, production IT purchases (PIT-PUR) had a negative impact (although statistically not significant) on percentage of inferior products. Note that new products of the competitors (NEW-CPRD) had a significant positive impact on inferior quality. This could be due to the fact that new products introduced by competitors are likely to be superior to existing products. Interestingly, although not significant at the 0.10 level, innovation IT purchases had a positive impact on inferior quality. In short, IT capital was a significant factor in improving product quality, while IT services for production had a negative but insignificant impact on inferior quality.

As mentioned earlier, the products of the SBUs are primarily industrial semifinished materials or components. It is likelv that relative price is a significant factor in market share and ROA determination, and that the business units try to reduce their relative prices. IT capital (IT-CAP) and marketing IT purchases (MIT-PUR) had significant negative impacts on relative price (REL-PRC), thus supporting Hypothesis 4 regarding the impact of IT on relative price.

At this point, it is useful to discuss the study by Loveman (1994) involving the MPIT dataset, and compare his results with the ones we have obtained. Loveman uses the microeconomic production function approach with the MPIT dataset to calculate the productivity impact of IT. Output is measured as sales less inventory changes, deflated by an appropriate price index. The inputs are material expenditure, non-IT purchased services expenditures, labor compensation, non-IT capital, and IT capital. Loveman finds the output elasticity for IT to be slightly negative, and concludes that firms would have done better by investing their marginal dollar in non-IT factors of production. In our study, however, IT was found to have (mostly) favorable impacts on the five intermediate variables which in turn were significant drivers of the high-level economic output variables (market share and ROA). Unlike the production function approach (used by Loveman), our two-stage model enables us to open up the “black box" of IT usage, and detect and measure IT impacts where they occur.

Our study was motivated by the lack of a process-oriented model to trace IT contributions through low/intermediate impact variables to the higher level performance variables. The results highlight our basic thesis that the most significant contributions of IT investments occur at low organizational levels where they are implemented, and that the intermediate level contributions, in turn, affect output measures. Our model focuses on the areas within the business units where IT impacts are expected to be found. Measurement reveals the actual presence or absence of such impacts, and thereby highlights variables which require further investigation and possible managerial action. A second contribution of the proposed analysis is the inclusion of industry-specific and economy wide environmental variables in the model. It explicitly recognizes the fact that the intermediate and higher level output variables are affected by industry-specific exogenous variables and general business conditions.

## 4.3. Limitations

We have developed and tested a model of IT contribution measurement. There are certain limitations of this study, both data and methodology related. First, we have a short time series of five years. We did not include IT lag terms because the high correlation between year to year figures (between 0.92 and 0.99) would introduce severe multicollinearity besides reducing the already modest sample size. Thus we are unable to account for the fact that IT impacts may take a considerable period of time (three or more years) to have an impact on performance variables.

Second, no information on the type of IT (e.g., scheduling vs. inventory control systems, etc.), manufacturing processes (e.g., assembly vs. jobshop environments, etc.), and managerial strategies (e.g., price reduction for market share, etc.) was available. Inclusion of these variables in the model would have led to a better understanding of the impacts creation processes, and improved the predictive power of the model

## 5. Conclusion

Investments in Information Technology (IT) constitute more than 50% of all new capital investments by major firms in the U.S.A., and much has been speculated about the potential benefits of these investments (Kriebel 1989). A growing empirical literature suggests that the productivity gains from IT investments have been neutral or negative. We have argued that the answer as to whether the anticipated benefits have been realized depends on the ability to measure the economic impacts of IT investments, and that various measurement problems have contributed to the mixed results reported in the literature. Measuring the economic contribution of IT investments is a key activity that can shape the very nature of business through its influence on corporate strategies and future investments in technology.

In this paper, we have proposed and empirically tested a new approach to assessing the impacts of IT investments. By focusing on intermediate levels of analysis and by incorporating industry-specific and economy-wide exogenous variables, we have empirically demonstrated that many of the significant IT impacts occur at low levels in the organization, and that they can be traced and measured. Similarly, we have provided an approach to measure the contribution of IT related factors to high level performance variables like ROA and market share through an intermediate level analysis. While some parts of the analysis presented here are limited by the availability of data. we have outlined a generalized process oriented approach which can be applied in other settings. In the empirical analysis, IT related factors showed a significant positive effect on intermediate level variables. The methodology provides a way to address the question “Why or why not?" by pointing to the absence or presence of IT contributions at intermediate levels

The contribution of this paper to IS literature is a methodology that attempts to circumvent some of the measurement related problems in this area. Kekre and Mukhopadhyay (1992), for example, use our two-stage method in a field study to assess the impact of EDI technology on inventory and quality (intermediate variables) and performance (final output variable). For the manager, this method of analysis is expected to provide insights for better management of IT resources by highlighting the critical variables. Future research should investigate the application of our approach to data in other industry sectors. This may require primary data collection from private sources.\*
