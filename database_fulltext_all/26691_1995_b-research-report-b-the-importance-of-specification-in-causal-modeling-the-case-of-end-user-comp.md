## Introduction

ne indicator of maturity in a discipline, as in the case of the Information Systems (IS) community, is the amount of attention directed toward the methodological issues of measurement. It is argued that valid and reliable measures of concepts or constructs are a prerequisite to model testing or theory building (Zmud and Boynton

1991, Straub 1989, Klenke 1992). Without appropriate measurement, results from any empirical research can be called into question. In a survey of IS instruments spanning the years 1973 to 1988 (Zmud and Boynton 1991). Doll and Torkzadeh's (1988) 12-item End-User Computing Satisfaction instrument was reported as one of three IS instruments that met conditions to qualify as “well developed." Further evidence of the instrument's stability and internal consistency were shown in a subsequent test-retest study (Doll and Torkzadeh 199la). Recently, Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand (1991) questioned the instrument's validity over various measurement issues. Part of Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand's critique revolved around their use of LISREL (Jöreskog and Sörbom 1988) to perform a confirmatory factor analysis test on this final 12-item model. Although detailed results were not given, Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand did state that various tests and techniques indicated that the overall fit of their confirmatory model based on an orthogonal factor pattern was inadequate. They suggested that this lack of fit may be due to inappropriate number of factors, inappropriate position of item loadings to factors, as well as nonorthogonality of the five factors. In response to this result, Doll and Torkzadeh (1991b, p. 9) said they were not surprised. Rather than questioning the particular model examined in Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand's LISREL analysis, Doll and Torkzadeh seemingly agreed that their five-factor model may be misspecified since they had forced a five-factor solution in their study.

In this report, we demonstrate that the results obtained by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand can be attributed primarily to their specification of an orthogonal pattern for the five factors as opposed to an inappropriate number of factors or positioning of items. It is not our intent to debate the other potentially valid points raised by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand. Rather, in our re-analyses of Doll and Torkzadeh's original covariance measures, we show how model fit is extremely dependent on model specification. While still maintaining the same number of constructs and positioning of items to factors, we demonstrate how two alternatives to the original model analyzed by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand can result in models with acceptable fits, thus suggesting that Doll and Torkzadeh's five-factor model may still be an acceptable means of measuring End-User Computing Satisfaction.

## Reanalyses of Doll and Torkzadeh's Correlation Matrix

In Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand's analysıs of Doll and Torkzadeh's End-User Computing Satisfaction model, they modeled the five factors as orthogonal (i.e., not related to one another). Although not explicitly depicted, we conjecture the analysis they performed used the model as shown in Figure 1.' Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand reported that the overall fit of this model was poor and correctly indicated that nonorthogonality among the five factors may be a contributing reason. The orthogonal model was analyzed possibly because of the fact that Doll and Torkzadeh used a varimax rotation in their principal components analysıs of their measures (which strives toward orthogonal simple structures).

What Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand did not consider in their re-analysis, however, was the full model implied by Doll and Torkzadeh. Doll and Torkzadeh depicted their five factors as reflecting an overall End-User Computing Satisfaction factor (the top-most oval in Figure 2). This model hypothesized both first and second order factors. The second order factor labelled End-User Computing Satisfaction represents a higher level of abstraction that drives the first order factors (i.e., Content, Accuracy, Format, Ease of Use, and Timeliness). This molecular relationship (Chin and Gopal 1993) suggests that each of the five first order factors are correlated due to a single common higher order factor. Such molecular models assume that the second order factor actually exists but cannot be directly measured bv manifest variables. Instead. the second order factor can only be inferred from first order factors that in turn are partially measured by their respective manifest variables. In our opinion, it seems that Doll and Torkzadeh implicitly presented a second order factor model. If this is true, the orthogonal model is not the model that Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand should have submitted to re-analysis. Furthermore, we note this second order perspective is consistent with work done in the adjacent research area dealing with Satisfaction with Life. For example, Harlow and Newcomb (1990) went even further by conceptualizing a third order factor model.

An alternative model to the second order molecular model would be a correlated first order factors model. Compared to the second order model that put a particular structure to the pattern of correlations among the first order factors, the correlated first order factors model (as shown in Figure 3) allows for five more degrees of freedom. This model still considers the five factors as distinct constructs, but they are assumed to correlate because they represent different aspects of the concept of End-User Computing Satisfaction.

Using Doll and Torkzadeh's original correlation matrix, we performed Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation runs. Since there is currently no one agreed upon measure for model fit, Table 1 provides a representative set obtained from either EzPath 1.0 (Steiger 1989) or EQS 3.0b (Bentler 1989). In addition to the chi-square test statistic for each model analyzed (Bollen 1989, pp. 263–269), we include the same test statistic for the baseline independence model (i.e., uncorrelated variables). These two statistics are needed in calculating other relative (i.e., comparative) goodness-offit indices not reported here. Not surprising is the fact that the chi-square goodnessof-fit measure suggests a poor fit for each of the three models. Because of the extraordinary power (i.e., low probability of type II error) due to the large sample size of 618, it would be extremely difficult to find a model that accounts for the sample correlation matrix such that we can say that all leftover residuals are not different from zero (Hayduk 1987, pp. 167–169; Jöreskog and Sörbom 1988, p. 43).

In the case of the other goodness of fit indices (which are alternative measures of how well the variances and covariances are jointly accounted for by the model), both the second order factor model and the correlated first order model performed quite well (with the correlated first order model performing slightly better overall on all measures). Conversely, the orthogonal model 1 performed poorly on all counts. For the nontechnical user, Steiger (1989) suggests that a good model would have an adjusted RMS index of below 0.10 and an adjusted population gamma index above 0.90. Excellent fit models should be below 0.05 and above 0.95, respectiv ely. Alternatively, Bentler and Bonnet (1980) have stated that models that have a fit below 0.90 for either of their normed or nonnormed comparative indices would imply room for substantial improvement.²

Whether model 3 is substantially better than model 2 can be further tested using a sequential chi-square difference test (Steiger. Shapiro, and Browne (985). The better goodness-of-fit indices obtained for model 3 (correlated first order factors) relative to model 2 (second order factor model) is as expected since model 2 is nested within model 3. In other words, model 2 is a special case of modcl 3. Here, the second order factor in model 2 places restrictions on the correlation pattern among the first order factors in model 3. The sequential chi-square difference test helps us decide whether to stay with the more parsimonious model 2 or the better fitting model 3. A large change in the chi-square between the two models compared to the difference in degrees of freedom means that freeing the restrictions of model 2 to obtain model 3 does constitute a real improvement. In our case, the chi-square difference of 47.35 with 5 degrees of freedom was significant $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . Thus, based solely on this test, we would conclude that constraints imposed in model 2 should be rejected in favor of model 3.

The appropriateness of the association of items to factors can be assessed by examining the factor loadings (i.e., the paths linking items to factors). Table 2 presents these and other parameter estimates obtained via EQS. All parameters were significantly different from zero at the 0.001 level assuming the data are multivariate normally distributed. The factor loadings for all items are quite reliable (e.g., above the 0.6 level suggested by Bagozzi and Yi, 1988). For the orthogonal model, four of the five factors are not identified.3 This implies that the estimates associated with these factor are not unique and that an infinite set of equallv good values can be obtained, Therefore, only the loadings for the identified factor are presented. The correlations among factors for model 3 suggests that the factors are distinct. Each of the correlations is substantial, but none is close enough to unity to suggest that two factors are equivalent and can be combined. Likewise, the paths from the second order factor to first order factor in model 2 also demonstrate the distinctiveness of each factor. Again, the paths are substantial, but not close to unity.

## Conclusion

What we have attempted to demonstrate by the three models is that results obtained via programs that perform covariance based structural equation analysis are very dependent on the specification of the model. The model analyzed by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand resulted in a poor overall fit. Yet. it would be premature to suggest, based on that analysis, that the End-User Computing Satisfaction model is without merit. Additional analyses as performed here can help to disentangle the underlying reasons. In particular, we have determined that the poor fit obtained by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand had more do with the structural specification than the number of factors or placement of items to their respective factors. This was accomplished by analyzing two additional models that not only maintained the same factor-to-item structure, but are also consistent with those depicted by the original authors and other studies of satisfaction.

Our intent was not to critique the End-User Computing measures on grounds beyond that of structural equations analysis. To answer whether any of the three models presented are appropriate would require the inclusion of both theory and substantive knowledge. Rather, we showed that with minor structural changes (such as correlating the five factors), other plausible models can be found to fit the data. All three models represent five-factor models. The primary difference is that in models 2 and 3 these factors were allowed to correlate either among themselves or via a higher order factor.

Two cautions should be noted before leaping to the opposite conclusion that the End-User Computing Satisfaction measures have been proven valid. The first is that the assumption of multivariate normality implicit in the ML estimation procedure was not reported by Doll and Torkzadeh. Therefore, the significance of the estimates as indicated by the analyses are not conclusive, nor are the fit measures presented. Second, as noted by Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand, the final 12 manifest variables in Doll and Torkzadeh's model was a product of exploratory factor analysis. Therefore, there is the possibility that the good fits obtained in the analysis of the two new models are due to capitalization on chance. An additional set of analyses performed on a new data set is needed.

We conclude by stating our opinion that covariance-based structural equations analysis is often taken as truth. This is likely due to its reputation as a quantitatively rigorous statistical approach. Yet, the truth is contingent on the interpretation of the model specified. While one interpretation of the bad fit obtained for model 1 is that the five factor solution is incorrect, an alternative is that the assumption of orthogonality is overly restrictive and artificial. When the orthogonality assumption is relaxed to allow for correlations among factors, the model improved dramatically. Thus, any statements in IS research studies that claim confirmation or disconfirmation of a model using this technique should be interpreted with a bit of caution by examining the actual model analyzed. Whether one knows the technique or not, an individual can logically examine the causal diagrams implicit in these analyses. Questions regarding the appropriateness of linking certain measured items to factors and the relationships among factors can be considered. The use of comparative fit indices and chi-square difference tests for nested models as provided in this report can assist in the process. In the final analysis, if the model does not seem reasonable based either on theory or substantive knowledge, the results of the analysis are questionable no matter how well the model may fit statistically.\*
