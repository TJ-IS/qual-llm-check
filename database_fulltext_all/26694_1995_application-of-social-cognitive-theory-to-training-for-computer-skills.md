# Application of Social Cognitive Theory to Training for Computer Skills

Deborah R. Compeau

School of Business

Carleton University

Ottawa, Ontario, Canada K1S 5B6

Christopher A. Higgins

School of Business

The University of Western Ontario

London, Ontario, Canada N6A 5B9

While computer training is widely recognized as an essential contributor to the productive use of computers in organizations, very little research has focused on identifying the processes through which training operates, and the relative effectiveness of different methods for such training. This research examined the training process, and compared a behavior modeling training program, based on Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 1977, 1978, 1982, 1986), to a more traditional, lecture-based program. According to Social Cognitive Theory, watching others performing a behavior, in this case interacting with a computer system, influences the observers' perceptions of their own ability to perform the behavior, or self-efficacy, and the expected outcomes that they perceive, as well as providing strategies for effective performance. The findings provide only partial support for the research model Self-efficacy exerted a strong influence on performance in both models. In addition, behavior modeling was found to be more effective than the traditional method for training in Lotus 1-2-3, resulting in higher self-efficacy and higher performance. For WordPerfect, however, modeling did not significantly influence performance. This finding was unexpected, and several possible explanations are explored in the discussion. Of particular surprise were the negative relationships found between outcome expectations and performance. Outcome expectations were expected to positively influence performance, but the results indicated a strong negative effect. Measurement limitations are presented as the most plausible explanation for this result, but further research is necessary to provide conclusive explanations.

While a few authors have reported contrary findings (Delone 1988, Schewe 1976, Zmud and Lind 1985), the evidence in support of training is more convincing, both in terms of number and quality of studies.

The practitioner literature also supports the view that training is essential to effective computer use. Testimonials to the importance of training are common features in the popular press. Sarah Pennefather (1989) hailed training as a “Key to Productivity." Warner and Smith (1990) stated that computer training is a "necessity not luxury." Similarly, Guidice (1990), Lewis (1990), Monsalve and Triplett (1990), Oberle (1990), Redkey (1990), Shulman (1990), and Tannenbaum (1990) all claimed that training is an essential contributor to the success of organizational computing.

In spite of the acknowledged importance of training, relatively little research has been conducted concerning the types of training that are most effective or the process issues involved in training (Martocchio and Webster 1992). The relative merits of various training programs are certainly debated in the literature. For example, proponents of classroom training claim that it is superior, since it is a cost effective way to train groups of people. Proponents of individual training methods, such as self-paced tutorials or computer-assisted instruction (CAI), claim that these methods are superior since they allow individuals to progress at their own pace. However, empirical evidence to support either of these claims is lacking. Recent studies by Bostrom et al. (1990), Davis and Bostrom (1990), Olfman and Bostrom (1990), Sein and Bostrom (1989). Martocchio and Webster (1992), Webster and Martocchio (1993, forthcoming) and Webster et al. (1990) have started to address this issue. Their findings suggest that the choice of training method has consequences for the degree of learning as well as the motivation of the trained individuals to continue to use the technology.

The aim of this research is to provide further insight into the questions of training method effectiveness and learning processes. This is accomplished by the application of Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 1986), a widely accepted and empirically validated model of individual behavior. Two specific dimensions of Social Cognitive Theory were particularly relevant for this research. The first relates to Bandura's conceptualization of the cognitive influence on behaviour. Two forces are held to be the primary cognitive determinants of individual behavior. The first set relates to outcomes. Individuals are more likely to undertake behaviors they believe will result in valued outcomes than those which they do not see as having favorable consequences. The second set of expectations encompasses what Bandura calls “self-efficacy," or beliefs about one's ability to perform a particular behavior. Self-efficacy affects choices about which behaviors to undertake, the effort and persistence exerted in the face of obstacles to the performance of those behaviors, and ultimately, the mastery of the behaviors.

While outcome expectations have been considered by many Information Systems researchers (e.g., Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989, Desanctis 1983,' Lucas 1978, Robey 1979. Schewe 1976), the role of self-efficacy has received little attention in studies of computing behavior. Only a handful of researchers have studied computer self-efficacy (Burkhardt and Brass 1990, Compeau and Higgins 1991, Gist et al. 1989, Hill, et al. 1987, Martocchio and Webster 1992, Webster and Martocchio 1992, 1993, forthcoming). Of these, only Gist et al. (1989) and the studies by Webster and Martocchio examine the influence of self-efficacy in the context of computer training.

The second dimension of Social Cognitive Theory to be applied in this research was the technique of behavior modeling. Research within the Social Cognitive Theory paradigm has demonstrated that observing others performing a behavior (behavior modeling) is a powerful means of learning. Modeling has been used to teach new behaviors such as assertiveness (e.g., Mann and Decker 1984). It has also been used to teach skills such as mathematics (Schunk 1981), selling skills (Meyer and Raich 1983), supervisory skills (Latham and Saari 1979) and leadership (Manz and Sims 1986) and to overcome destructive phobias (Bandura 1982). Moreover, a meta-analysis of the managerial training literature (Burke and Day 1986) demonstrated the effectiveness of behavior modeling training on both learning and subsequent behavior on the job. In spite of its application in other areas, only one study (Gist et al. 1989) has examined the role of behavior modeling in end-user software training.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In §2, the research model and study hypotheses are presented, supported by both theoretical and empirical rationale. Then the research methodology is outlined, and specific information about research procedures and measures are given (§3). Section 4 outlines the data analysis technique and presents the results. The implications of the findings are discussed in Section 5, as are the limitations, and the paper concludes by outlining the opportunities and issues for future research.

## 2. Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 1 presents the research model which guided the study. This model was formulated based on Social Cognitive Theory. Five constructs are included in the model. Prior performance of the behavior and behavior modeling are each held to directly impact self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and performance. Self-efficacy is posited to influence outcome expectations and performance, and outcome expectations also influence performance. The following sections describe each element of the model in detail, and present the hypotheses relating them.

## Behavior Modeling

According to Social Cognitive Theory, one of the principal mechanisms through which behavior modeling operates is self-efficacy (Bandura 1986). Observing someone else performing the target behavior raises the subjects' perceptions of their own ability to perform it successfully. This effect has been shown in several studies, across a diverse range of behavioral domains (Bandura et al. 1977, Bandura 1982, Brown and Inouye 1978, Schunk 1981).

Only one study has examined the impact of behavior modeling on the development of self-efficacy with respect to computers. Gist et al. (1989) compared computer-assisted instruction (CAI) to behavior modeling in a spreadsheet training workshop. Subjects in the behavior modeling condition were found to develop higher self-efficacy than those in the CAI condition. Thus, the first hypothesis of this study was as follows:

H1. Subjects who receive behavior modeling training will develop higher perceptions of computer self-efficacy than subjects who receive nonmodeling training.

Modeling also influences outcome expectations. Bandura (1971) demonstrated that mòdeled behavior that is rewarded is adopted by the observers. He argued that this behavior was the result of a vicariously learned outcome expectation. Subjects learned from the experience of the models that the behavior would be rewarded, and thus adopted the behavior in order to achieve similar outcomes.

This effect has not been studied in an information systems context. It seems reasonable to assert, however, that subjects should alter their outcome expectations when exposed to models who demonstrate a sense of personal accomplishment, or express their beliefs that their new computer skills will enhance their effectiveness or productivity. Thus, hypothesis two was as follows:

H2. Subjects who receive behavior modeling training will develop higher outcome expectations than subjects who receive nonmodeling training.

In addition to its influence on self-efficacy and outcome expectations, modeling can directly influence performance by demonstrating appropriate and effective strategies for dealing with challenging or threatening situations (Bandura 1978, 1982). Schunk (1981) used modeling to teach children mathematics skills. Adult models performed division tasks, describing the cognitive operations involved as they went along. This cognitive modeling treatment had a significant impact on subsequent performance, indicating that subjects had acquired new skills by observing the models' performances. Within the context of computer training, the models might be observed using screen prompts and the help facility to solve problems. Subjects then have the opportunity to learn effective tactics for dealing with similar situations in their own efforts.

H3. Subjects who receive behavior modeling training will score higher than those in nonmodeling training on measures of performance.

## Self-Efficacy

Social Cognitive Theory argues that self-efficacy perceptions influence an individual's outcome expectations (Bandura 1978). Self-efficacy judgments are purported to influence outcome expectations since “the outcomes one expects derive largely from judgments as to how well one can execute the requisite behavior" (Bandura 1978, p. 241). Compeau and Higgins (1991) found a significant positive relationship between computer self-efficacy and outcome expectations in a survey of 940 Canadian managers. Thus, the next hypothesis was as follows:

H4. Individuals with high computer self-efficacy will demonstrate higher outcome expectations regarding computer use than individuals with low self-efficacy.

Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura 1986) also predicts that self-efficacy expectations will influence individuals' actual ability to perform the behavior. Studies in both clinical and organizational settings have demonstrated this effect on a wide variety of behaviors, including smoking cessation (Condiotte and Lichenstein 1981, DiClemente 1981), sales performance (Barling and Beattie 1983), mathematics achievement (Schunk 1981), and attendance (Frayne 1986). Gist et al. (1989) also found a relationship between self-efficacy and performance in a computer training course. Accordingly, hypothesis five was as follows:

H5. Individuals with high computer self-efficacy will score higher than those with low computer self-efficacy on measures of performance.

## Outcome Expectations

Social Cognitive Theory holds that expectations about the consequences of behavior are a strong force guiding individuals’ actions. Individuals are more likely to undertake behaviors they believe will result in valued outcomes than those which they do not see as having favorable consequences. The influence of outcome expectations on computing behavior has been demonstrated by Compeau and Higgins (1991), Hill et al. (1987), Moore (1989), Pavri (1988), and Thompson et al. (1991). These studies dealt with choice behavior, however, and thus do not address the relationship between outcome expectations and performance. However, Social Cognitive Theory suggests that outcome expectations influence performance as well as choice (Bandura 1986). Individuals who expect positive benefits from using computers would be expected to be more highly motivated than those who do not expect positive benefits, and to persist more in their attempts to learn. Bostrom et al. (1990) found that trainee motivation, specifically the perceived usefulness of the package (a similar construct to outcome expectations) and learning performance were related. Thus, the next hypothesis was:

H6. Individuals who expect positive outcomes from their use of computers will exhibit higher performance than those who do not expect positive outcomes.

## Prior Performance

Three additional hypotheses were formulated to reflect the influence of prior performance on self-efficacy, outcome expectations and performance. Enactive mastery, or successful execution of a behavior, is held to be the strongest source of information about self-efficacy (Bandura 1986). Prior success would be expected to increase self-efficacy, while prior difficulties might be expected to result in decreases in self-efficacy. Wood and Bandura (1989) demonstrated this effect in studies of a series of simulated organizations. Bandura and Schunk (1981) found that successful performance in mathematics builds self-efficacy. Similarly, Locke et al. (1984) found that performance in a brainstorming task predicted future self-efficacy with respect to the task.

In the context of computer training research, prior performance could encompass many things. One would expect the most influential experience to be actual experience using the same software package. However, where novice users of software are being studied (as is the case in this research), this approach is not feasible. There are, however, other perspectives on prior performance. For example, self-efficacy perceptions may be generalizable across different domains of computing. Thus, performance experience with one type of software may influence self-efficacy with respect to another package. In order to capture this effect, this study examines the influence of performance scores for one software package on self-efficacy for another software package. This approach also permits the use of an objective measure. Often prior performance (or prior experience) is captured by questionnaire items asking respondents how familiar they are with certain types of software or how they rate their level of experience (novice to expert). While these types of questions undoubtedly tap some elements of prior experience they may also reflect self-efficacy, since they are self-reports of skill. Thus, Hypothesis 7 was formulated as follows:

H7. Subjects with higher prior computer performance scores will develop higher perceptions of computer self-efficacy than subjects with lower prior performance scores.

In addition to its impact on self-efficacy, prior performance using computers can also contribute to the formation of outcome expectations. Bandura (1986) noted that “response outcomes influence behavior antecedently by creating expectations of similar outcomes on future occasions" (p. 229). Thus, in terms of computing behavior, individuals whose past use of computers has been successful and rewarding are more likely to expect positive outcomes in the future. Hypothesis 8 describes this relationship:

H8. Subjects with higher prior computer performance scores will develop higher outcome expectations than subjects with lower prior performance scores.

Finally, prior performance can be expected to directly influence performance. Individuals who perform well on one type of computing task perform well partly because they possess particular skill in the task domain. These individuals are likely, other things being equal, to perform well in related tasks. This approach was taken by Wood and Bandura (1989) in assessing performance in a simulated organization. Prior performance was found to be a significant predictor of current performance. The last hypothesis of the study, then, was as follows:

H9. Subjects with higher prior computer performance scores will exhibit higher performance than subjects with lower prior performance scores.

This research, then, assesses a comprehensive model of the training process. It includes two of the key sources of self-efficacy information (prior performance and behavior modeling) and assesses the influence of these factors on self-efficacy as well as outcome expectations and performance. The inclusion of self-efficacy as a mediator between training method and performance is one of the key differences between this study and other studies of end user software training (e.g., Bostrom et al. 1990, Martocchio and Webster 1992, Webster and Martocchio 1993, forthcoming).

Martocchio'and Webster (1992) and Webster and Martocchio (1993) consider self-efficacy as a control variable, rather than a mediator. Gist et al. (1989) conducted one of the first studies of the influence of behavior modeling on the development of computer self-efficacy. However, their study did not assess the multiple linkages between behavior modeling, self-efficacy, outcome expectations and performance. Moreover, the comparison group in Gist et al. was computer-aided instruction. While this comparison is interesting, instructor led, lecture plus hands on practice training is still the dominant form of formal training in North American organizations. Thus, it represents an important comparison group to be examined in studies of behavior modeling training.

## 3. Research Design

## Overview

Eighty-eight research subjects completed a two day training course covering Lotus 1-2-3 (hereafter referred to as Lotus) and WordPerfect 5.1 (WordPerfect). They were randomly assigned to one of eight experimental groups. The eight groups differed in terms of the order in which the software packages were taught and in terms of the use of different instructional techniques. Two groups received modeling training for both packages, two received exclusively nonmodeling training, and the remainder received modeling training for one package and nonmodeling training for the other. Because this design is not a completely crossed factorial design (i.e., none of the subjects received modeling training for Lotus on day one and nonmodeling training for Lotus on day two, for example), some collapsing across groups was necessary for data analysis. Thus, the different combinations of modeling and nonmodeling were not investigated. In order to conduct analysis, the data were assessed through four separate models, based on day of training and software package. Training method (behavior modeling vs. nonmodeling) is incorporated into the models as a construct. This approach to analysis is discussed further in §4.

All subjects received substantial training in the use of the software package. This is in direct contrast to previous studies of modeling, in which the control group was untreated. The decision to adopt this approach was also based on the need for a stricter test of behavior modeling. Prior studies of modeling, relying on comparison with an untreated control group, can, at best, conclude that modeling training is an effective way to promote behavior change. They cannot, however, speak to its relative effectiveness compared with other instructional techniques.

## Subjects

Ninety-five subjects were recruited to participate in the main study. The target population was managers and professionals with little prior computer experience who wanted to learn both WordPerfect and Lotus 1-2-3. Potential participants were first screened to ensure that they had little prior experience with computers. This screening took place during a telephone interview, and relied on the judgments of the potential subjects. While some had used computers before, they all considered themselves to be novices. More importantly, they had no significant experience with WordPerfect or Lotus 1-2-3. As a check on the effectiveness of the screening, three questions from the background questionnaire were examined. The first asked the respondents to indicate their overall level of familiarity with computers on a five point scale, where 1 = not at all familiar and 5 = Totally familiar. The average score on this measure was 2.3, indicating a general lack of familiarity with computers. Familiarity with WordPerfect and LOtus was also assessed. Only 5 of the subjects indicated a level of familiarity with Lotus of more than 2 on the 5 point scale, and only 8 of the subjects indicated a level of familiarity with WordPerfect of more than 2 on the 5 point scale. Further conversations with these individuals indicated that they had used the software before, but only once or twice, and both they and the researcher still considered themselves to be novices.

Of the 95 subjects who started the training program, seven did not complete the training due to demands at work or illness. Thus, the final sample included 88 subjects. The subjects, 45 women and 43 men, ranged in age from 20 to 67, with a mean of 39.5 years. They came from a variety of educational backgrounds. Seventy percent were employed in organizations with less than 200 employees, though the mean size of organization was 409. The subjects represented all levels of managers and professionals, from the executive level (21.5%), to first line management (16%), and professional (17%) positions. Three subjects were unemployed, and seeking managerial or professional positions. The subjects represented a variety of functional areas, including accounting and finance (17%); engineering, design, R&D (12.5%); marketing and sales (15%); human resources (3%); and general management (31%).

As assessment of the similarity of the groups before training was undertaken in order to ensure that there were no significant differences between the groups on any of the demographic measures or in terms of their familiarity with either of the software packages. ## Procedures

Subjects were recruited through advertisements in the local daily newspaper, offering a two day training course, as part of a research program. Individuals who were interested contacted the researcher to register for the course. At this point, initial screening was conducted to ensure that subjects met the criteria for inclusion in the study (manager/professional, little prior experience with computers).

An experienced professional trainer was hired to conduct the training sessions. The individual who was hired had several years of experience in designing and conducting training in a large Canadian bank. While it was not possible for the trainer to be completely blind to the hypotheses, since she showed the videotapes to the subjects, she was unaware of the specific hypotheses being tested.

The order of events during the training program is shown in Figure 2. Each day included a 40 minute lecture on computing concepts. On the first day, this lecture provided very basic information about the computer equipment and about the operating system being used. Then, a 90 minute lecture/demonstration was conducted. Subjects were shown how the software was used and what features were available. The trainer followed a fixed outline but questions were answered as they arose.

Following the software demonstration, the manipulation was introduced. Participants either saw the modeling videotape or had a brief lecture followed by 30 minutes to review their notes. The purpose of the lecture and review was to equalize the training time for the two groups. Since the modeling group received 30 minutes of instruction beyond the software demonstration (the videotape), the control group had to receive 30 minutes of additional instruction in order to equalize the training time. Had the control group gone immediately to the practice session, it would not be possible to separate the influence of the videotape from the influence of additional training time. Following the manipulation, the post-training questionnaire was administered.

After the training (or after the videotape for the modeling groups), subjects were given two hours to practice what they had learned. A set of practice exercises were provided, which reinforced most of the concepts learned in the lecture/demonstration. During the practice session, questions were answered by referring the subjects to the appropriate section of their training notes, and stepping through the instructions with them, if necessary. Following the practice session, the performance tests were administered.

On the second day of the course, another short computing concepts lecture was held first. This lecture focused on issues such as computer viruses, backup and security. The software demonstration was repeated for the second package, followed by the manipulation, and the second post-training measure. Subjects were again given two hours to practice with the software package, and at the end of the day, the performance tests were administered.

## Manipulation

The modeling intervention was implemented through a 20 minute videotape. The videotaped model performed introductory exercises on the computer, expressing frustration initially, but eventually achieving some success. This approach to modeling was based on studies by Meichenbaum (1971) and Dillon et al. (1972), who suggested that models who demonstrate initial difficulty, but who then gain in confidence and skills (or coping models) are more effective than those who demonstrate too facile a performance (mastery models).

The actual scripts for the modeling videotapes were developed based on a pilot study, in which 13 subjects were videotaped learning to use either WordPerfect or Lotus. The types of problems that they encountered, and the specific comments that they made were incorporated into the videotapes.2 Appendix 1 shows an excerpt from the modeling script for the Lotus videotape.

## Measures

Outcome Expectations. Eleven items were used to measure outcome expectations. This measure was developed by Compeau and Higgins (1991) based on a review of existing measures in the IS literature. The measure presented a variety of outcomes which might be associated with computer use, including increased productivity, decreased reliance on clerical support, enhanced quality of work output, feelings of accomplishment, and enhanced status. Respondents were asked to indicate, on a 5 point scale, how likely they thought it was that each of these outcomes would result from their use of computers. In addition to this general measure, respondents were asked to consider the outcomes of using Lotus 1-2-3 and WordPerfect specifically. These specific measures of outcome expectations permit testing of the relationships between self-efficacy, outcome expectations and behavior at a more specific level of abstraction, consistent with the recommendations of Bandura (1986). Compeau and Higgins (1991) found the outcome expectations construct to be multidimensional, incorporating both performance-related and personal outcome expectations. Thus, for the purposes of the data analyses, outcome expectations were separated into Performance Outcome Expectations (items 1, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11) and Personal Outcome

Expectations (items 2, 3, 4, 6, 7). Appendix 2 contains the 11-item measure (shown for general outcome expectations).

Self-efficacy. Self-efficacy was measured by eight items which asked the respondents to rate their expected ability to accomplish a task using an unfamiliar software package with different levels of assistance. For example, the respondents were asked whether they could accomplish the task using the computer “if no one was around to tell them what to do as they went" or “if they had a lot of time to complete the job." This measure was developed based on an extensive review of the literature on self-efficacy (Compeau and Higgins 1991).³ As with the outcome expectations construct, both general and specific (Lotus 1-2-3 and WordPerfect) self-efficacy was measured. Appendix 2 contains the 8-item measure of general self-efficacy.

Performance. Two hands-on exercises were used to assess subjects' knowledge of the software packages. The first exercise was designed to test basic concepts that had already been used in the practice exercises. The second exercise contained less explicit instruction, and required the subjects to do things that were somewhat different from the practice session, though not beyond their capability. Scoring was conducted according to a detailed key by an independent grader who was blind to the conditions of the experiment.4

Prior Performance. Prior performance was measured in this study only for the second day of training. The measure used was the individual's performance score on the previous day of training. Thus, performance in Lotus training was used to indicate prior performance for WordPerfect training and performance in WordPerfect training was used as an indicator of prior performance for Lotus training. This approach yields an objective measure of prior success and also permits assessment of the generalization of experience across different software domains.

## 4. Data Analysis

The research model was analyzed using Partial Least Squares (PLS), a relatively new, extremely powerful technique for analyzing structural equations with latent variables. PLS is similar to LISREL, which is probably the best known of the second generation statistical techniques, in the sense that the measurement and structural (or theoretical) models are analyzed simultaneously. However, unlike LISREL, PLS relies on ordinary least squares estimation techniques to solve the equations. Thus, the interpretation of results is somewhat different. Loadings of measures on each construct can be interpreted as loadings in a principal components factor analysis. Paths are interpreted as standardized beta weights in a regression analysis. R² values are used to assess the proportion of variance in the endogenous constructs which can be accounted for by the antecedent constructs (for a more comprehensive discussion see Barclay et al., in press).

Separate models were needed for each of the software packages, since the measures of self-efficacy, outcome expectations and performance were all specific to the particular package. In addition, prior performance data were only available for the second day of training (Day 1 performance was used as the measure), so the full model could only be tested for the Day 2 training data. A subset of the model (including all of the relationships except those pertaining to prior experience) was tested using the data from Day 1 training.

The analysis proceeded in two phases. First, it was necessary to establish a common measurement base for the four models and to assess the properties of the measurement models. Establishing a common measurement base was important since the meaning (in a theoretical sense) of constructs in a structural equațions model is, in part, determined (empirically) by the measures associated with them. Since different models may result in very different constructs (by virtue of different loadings of measures on the constructs) and since these variations might have serious consequences for the conclusions of the study, it was necessary to find a set of measures which would be adequate for all four of the models.

In the initial test of the models, the self-efficacy and performance constructs displayed no problems in measurement. The outcome expectations constructs, however. did display measurement weaknesses. In order to address these weaknesses and to ensure a common measure base, items 1, 10, 11 were dropped from the performance outcome expectations construct and item 3 was dropped from personal outcome expectations construct.

## Tests of the Revised Models

## Influence of Behavior Modeling

Overall, the analyses demonstrated that behavior modeling can have an important influence on self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and performance, under some conditions. Subjects in the modeling condition for spreadsheet training developed higher self-efficacy (H1) and exhibited higher training performance (H3) than those in the nonmodeling condition. Modeling also influenced outcome expectations in two of the four models tested (H2).

There are a number of interesting differences in the findings across the four research models, which suggest aspects of the modeling process that require further study. First, as noted above, the effect of modeling was only significant for Lotus training. Two possible explanations for this finding were considered. First, the differences may be related to differences in the modeling tapes themselves. Different actors were used for the two tapes, and the tasks performed were different. While every attempt was made, in developing the tapes, to achieve similarity in the level of functioning for the different software packages, it was not always possible to translate concepts across the packages. Thus, differences in either the individual characteristics of the behavior models or in their actual interaction with the software may have contributed to the inconsistencies in findings between Lotus and WordPerfect.

The second potential explanation for the differences in. results across different models relates to the subjects' level of familiarity with the subject matter being learned. While all of the subjects were computer novices, they appeared to be more familiar with the concepts of word processing than they were with the concepts underlying spreadsheets (e.g., they were more familiar with writing tasks than with computational tasks). This familiarity with the conceptual underpinnings of the software may have moderated the impact of modeling on self-efficacy. Gist and Mitchell (1992) suggest that there are differences in the formation of self-efficacy judgments for familiar and unfamiliar tasks. When subjects are very familiar with a particular behavior, their judgments of self-efficacy are more automatic, and more directly determined by prior performance. When faced with unfamiliar tasks, subjects rely more on an in-depth assessment of what is required for performance and whether they have the appropriate skills (Gist and Mitchell 1992, p. 191). Gist and Mitchell's : assertion suggests, effectively, that self-efficacy judgments are more malleable for unfamiliar tasks than for familiar ones. Thus, since Lotus was essentially less familiar to the subjects than WordPerfect, subjects' Lotus self-efficacy could be influenced by modeling, while their WordPerfect self-efficacy could not.

## Influence of Self-efficacy

The hypotheses related to the impact of self-efficacy on both outcome expectations and performance were largely supported by the data. Individuals who had confidence in their ability to use the computer had higher expectations of the outcomes of using computers (H4a and H4b), and scored higher on measures of performance than those who lacked this confidence (H5).

Interestingly, a stronger relationship was found between self-efficacy and performance for WordPerfect than for Lotus. This may also be related to the issue of familiarity. Since subjects were more familiar with writing tasks, their self-efficacy judgments would be more fully formed based on prior experiences. As a result, they might be expected to exert a higher degree of influence on performance. Additional research is needed to examine this possibility.

## Influence of Outcome Expectations

The findings with respect to outcome expectations were not consistent with the hypothesized relationships (H6a and H6b). Only two of the paths were in the direction predicted by the hypotheses, and only one of these was significant (Performance Outcome Expectations → Performance, Lotus Day 1, β = 0.116, p < 0.05). Most of the paths were negative, suggesting that individuals with higher outcome expectations exhibited lower performance.

One possible explanation for these unexpected results relates to the time horizon over which outcome expectations and performance were measured. That is, since outcome expectations focus more on long term effects, and since the performance measure considers only short-term performance, it may be that the relationship has been artificially reduced (or negated). Consider an individual (Pat) who has never used computers before, but has a strong belief that using computers will be ultimately beneficial both from a professional and personal standpoint. Pat attends a training class, and by observing the instructor and others in the class, becomes even more convinced that using the computer will be beneficial (increase in outcome expectations). Pat is very motivated to do well on the performance test and to continue to use computers after the training. However, being very new to computers, Pat struggles greatly with the performance tasks at the end of the day, and is able to accomplish very little (low performance score), though as much as might be expected for a novice. This example shows how high outcome expectations (from a longer term perspective) might be accompanied by low performance (from à short term perspective). Since the average performance score was somewhat low (34% for Lotus; 55% for WordPerfect) it is not unreasonable to assume that this type of phenomena is partly influencing the results.

## Influence of Prior Performance

The two models which included the influence of prior performance (measured as performance on the previous day) on self-efficacy and performance provide some support for the Hypotheses 7 and 9. Prior performance had a significant impact on self-efficacy for Lotus training (H7), and on performance for both software packages (H9). The fact that prior performance only influenced self-efficacy for Lotus training is consistent with the explanation that self-efficacy judgments are formed more automatically for familiar packages, such as WordPerfect, on the basis of similar past experiences (e.g., perhaps using a typewriter). On the other hand, since using Lotus was a completely novel experience to the participants, the subjects had to draw upon related experiences (such as using WordPerfect) or vicarious experiences (such as the modeling videotape) in order to develop a sense of their own ability.

These findings suggest that experiences with computers differ in their generalizability to other domains. Given the pace at which information technology is changing in organizations, users are constantly forced to adapt to new systems and technologies. Depending on their familiarity with the area, they may rely on their past experiences with other technology to form initial judgments of their capabilities with a new technology. Thus, past disasters may foster an ongoing sense of inefficacy which will endanger further learning. On the other hand, past success may help to build an enduring sense of confidence that new skills can be learned. This study provides preliminary evidence to support this notion, but additional research is necessary in order to better understand the conditions under which experiences generalize.

In terms of outcome expectations, again, the negative relationships between prior performance and outcome expectations were unexpected (H8a and H8b). However the notion that differences in the time horizon of measurement for performance and outcome expectations resulted in systematic differences in the variances of each construct seems plausible.

## Limitations

The limitations of the study relate to both internal and external validity. With respect to internal validity, two limitations should be noted. First, the differences in the influence of modeling for the two software packages may have been due to differences in the nature of the behavior models. Different actors were used for the two software packages, and they may have differed in their influence, either in terms of their personal characteristics or in terms of their interaction with the software. The results of the study do not provide insights into the extent to which this may have occurred.

Second, the practice session, which was included in the sessions in order to maximize the realism of the training course, may have influenced the self-efficacy of the participants. Actual experience with a behavior is one way in which self-efficacy judgments are formed. Thus, subjects' self-efficacy may have changed following the post measure but before the performance test. Since these possible changes were not measured, the relationship between self-efficacy immediately prior to performance and actual performance may be somewhat different than that shown by the data.

On the other hand, examination of the changes in self-efficacy scores through the course of the training provided evidence against this possibility. Software specific self-efficacy scores were found to increase at each measurement until training in the software was received, but then to stay constant for the duration of the program. Thus, the self-efficacy possessed by an individual one day after training was the same as that possessed immediately after training but before the performance test. Since these measures are the same, it is unlikely that self-efficacy increased or decreased markedly at any points between the two measures.

With respect to external validity, the findings of the experiment are limited in terms of the characteristics of the sample. The subjects were primarily from smal businesses (less than 200 employees), and may thus have distinguishing personal characteristics which would limit the ability to generalize to employees in larger firms. While this seems somewhat unlikely, it is possible that factors which encourage people to self-select into smaller organizations may be related to how they approach different aspects of their jobs (including the use of computers).

Of more importance than the organizational background of the subjects is their educational background. Most of the subjects had at least some college or university background, and a large percentage had a degree (or even a graduate degree). Thus, the findings are limited to individuals with similar backgrounds. Differences in educational background may indicate differences in approaches to learning or in ability to learn, and these differences may have particular relevance for how training influences the development of computing skills.

Lastly, the relatively small sample size (n = 88) must also be acknowledged as a limitation. While this number of subjects is typical of experimental studies, additional research with larger samples is necessary to further examine the generalizability of the findings. In particular, it would be helpful to test the hypotheses in an organizational setting, where individuals may be required to attend training. This would reduce the potential of a selection bias in the findings.

## 6. Conclusions and Implications

Several general conclusions can be drawn from this study. First, self-efficacy does indeed influence computing performance. Individuals who see themselves as capable of using WordPerfect or Lotus 1-2-3 end up achieving higher performance than those who doubt their capabilities. From a practical perspective, this finding suggests that in order to assist individuals in developing their skills with respect to information technology, it may be necessary to assist them in developing a positive perception of their ability to use them. Much of IS research has focused on developing positive outcome expectations towards computers (or positive attitudes), and the role of selfperceptions has been largely ignored. The findings of this research suggest that self-efficacy represents a unique and important contribution to the development of computing skills. Thus, one of the key objectives for organizational training and support programs should be the promotion of high self-efficacy. Without attention to self-efficacy, many people will simply not develop the computer skills they require, and both the individuals and their organizations will suffer the consequences in terms of productivity.

Second, modeling can be used successfully to alter self-efficacy and performance in some instances. Subjects in the Lotus training, who viewed a videotaped model demonstrating some of the typical problems encountered by novice users, developed a stronger sense of their own capability, and ultimately demonstrated higher performance on a test of software proficiency. Thus, based on these findings, it appears that one way to design training programs to promote high self-efficacy is to incorporate learning by observation. This suggestion must be tempered, of course, by the difference in the success of modeling for Lotus and WordPerfect. This difference may indicate one (or more) of three things, which suggest some of the limiting conditions on the use of modeling. One possibility is that some software packages are more amenable to modeling (e.g., it is easier to model effective use of Lotus than WordPerfect). The features of the software itself and how it is used may in fact influence the degree to which behavior modeling can be used. Alternatively, very specific aspects of the modeling (such as individual characteristics of the models, level of performance demonstrated or types of activities demonstrated) may influence the way in which the modeling was perceived. If this is so, then a great deal of attention to the way the modeling is conducted would be necessary in order to implement modeling in a practical sense. A third possibility is that the impact of modeling is moderated by the learners'familiarity with the particular software being modeled. When individuals are more familiar with the software (either in general or specific terms) their self-efficacy perceptions appear to be more resistant to change. Thus, the opportunity to influence their self-efficacy through modeling is not as strong as when individuals have little or no familiarity with the software.

Understanding the relative contribution, if any, of these explanations to differences in the success of modeling represents an important opportunity for future research. If modeling is to be used successfully within an organization, it will be necessary to provide answers to the questions raised above.

Other opportunities for future research involve examining other means of influencing self-efficacy. This research focuses on the use of modeling to alter self-efficacy efficacy perceptions. However, Social Cognitive Theory provides insight into other ways in which self-efficacy can be promoted as well. Bandura (1986) identifies four sources of information on which self-efficacy judgments are based: actual experience (enactive mastery), vicarious experience (including behavior modeling), social persuasion, and physiological states. These sources of information have implications for training as well as end user support.

From a training standpoint, it has already been suggested that modeling is one way to enhance self-efficacy through training. Guided mastery is another means. In a guided mastery process, learners are assisted through increasingly challenging exercises so that they may experience repeated success as they are learning. Thus, a training program must incórporate opportunities for practice, and in particular, success!

Social persuasion is also a part of training. One of the roles of the trainer is to provide encouragement to learners that they are capable of learning the new skills required. However, while it is important, the impact of social persuasion is not entirely clear, as the following example demonstrates. Most trainers have repeatedly experienced a situation where a learner, frustrated with an exercise, says “I must be the dumbest person you've ever seen . . . I just can't get this." The trainer, of course, responds that the individual is not dumb, that frustration is normal, and learning to use new software is a time consuming process. At this point the learner shrugs, as if to say "Well, I know you don't mean that; you're just being nice, but I won't argue." The learner in this case is filtering the information received from the trainer and discounting it. Since social persuasion can only influence self-efficacy if it is believed and accepted by the learner, the technique is not always successful. In behavior modeling, on the other hand, the learner sees someone else experiencing that sense of frustration before ever encountering the software, and thus find it easier to believe that they are not unique in their struggles.

Physiological states have also been found to influence training performance. Webster et al. (1990) reported on training studies which suggest that labelling of training as play, rather than work, promotes a condition referred to as flow. In the flow experience the individual is actively engaged in the task, and feels in control. This state of flow promoted through training may also serve to enhance the development of self-efficacy.

Understanding self-efficacy is also important for end user support personnel. Viewed from this perspective, providing end-user support involves two different dimensions. First. support personnel must be concerned with fixing a technical problem in order to permit the user to continue his or her work. But end-user support also involves helping users become confident in their own skills. Compeau and Higgins (1991), however, found a negative relationship between organizational support for end users and self-efficacy. While there are several explanations for this counter-intuitive finding, one explanation is that support as currently practiced in organizations, focuses too much on technical help and not enough on self-efficacy. That is, the “expert" comes to the user's rescue, fixes the problem quickly and efficiently, but without taking time to explain the situation and how it is resolved, and leaves. The user is left feeling inadequate, especially if it was easy for the expert to fix the problem, and thus lowers his or her estimation of his capability, based on the observation of an expert at work. If this occurs (and the authors’ experience suggests that it does at least to some extent), then self-efficacy may indeed be negatively affected by one of the critical organizational mechanisms for promoting high self-efficacy.

One key area in which future research is necessary relates to the third general finding of this study. The lack of support for relationships between outcome expectations and performance seems to suggest that, when comparing outcome expectations and performance, the time horizon of measurement needs to be considered. In the present study, outcome expectations were not specifically measured in terms of short term outcomes, while the performance test reflected only short-term performance attainments. Over time, outcome expectations and performance attainments might be expected to relate positively, but in the short term they do not appear to be related. Since most of the research which examines outcome expectations has, to date, been cross-sectional, the time horizon problem has not previously been an issue. This finding, then, offers a new consideration for measuring individual expectations with respect to computers.

This study represents one of the first attempts to apply Social Cognitive Theory, and in particular the concept of self-efficacy, to the study of end-user training. It provides evidence of the mediating role of self-efficacy in explaining the influence of behavior modeling on performance. It also highlights the potential for applying modeling in organizations, and some of the issues which must be addressed if modeling is to be implemented in an organizational context. More work is needed to understand some aspects of the model that were not supported, especially the role of outcome expectations in the context of training, and the moderating influence of software differences. Overall, however, the results suggest that Social Cognitive theory (with some refinements) is applicable to the training context.
