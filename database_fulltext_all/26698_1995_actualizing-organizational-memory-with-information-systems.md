# Actualizing Organizational Memory with Information Systems

Eric W. Stein\*

Pennsylvania State University

Great Valley Campus

Management Program

30 East Swedesford Road

Vladimir Zwass

Malvern, Pennsylvania 19355

Fairleigh Dickinson University

Teaneck, New Jersey 07666

Preservation of organizational memory becomes increasingly important to organizations as it is recognized that experiential knowledge is a key to competitiveness. With the development and widespread availability of advanced information technologies (IT), information systems become a vital part of this memory. We analyze existing conceptualizations and task-specific instances of IT-supported organizational memory. We then develop a model for an organizational memory information system (OMIS) that is rooted in the construct of organizational effectiveness. The framework offers four subsystems that support activities leading to organizational effectiveness. These subsystems rest on the foundation of five mnemonic functions that provide for acquisition, retention, maintenance, search, and retrieval of information. We then identify the factors that will limit the success of OMIS implementation, although full treatment of this issue is outside the scope of the paper. To initiate a research agenda on OMIS, we propose an initial contingency framework for OMIS development depending on the organization's environment and its life-cycle stage, and discuss the relationships between an OMIS and organizational learning and decision making.

## 1. Introduction

The purpose of this study is to examine the role of information system (IS) based L on advanced information technologies (IT) in actualizing organizational memory, and to provide a model for an organizational memory information system. Organizational memory is the means by which knowledge from the past exerts influence on present organizational activities. Organizational memory is considered distinct from individual memory just as organizational learning is distinct from individual learning (Argyris and Schon 1978). Indeed, it has been recognized that the means by which organizational memories are retained are not limited to the individual memories of the agents within the organization (see the extensive review in Walsh and Ungson (1991). Organizational memory provides information from the past that reduces transaction costs, contributes to effective and efficient decision making, and is a basis for power within organizations (Walsh and Ungson 1991). Argyris and Schon (1978) argue that memory is necessary to organizational learning. Information systems can be a vital component of this memory, although today this occurs largely by happenstance.

The need to recognize and develop the information-systems aspect of organizational memory is growing ever stronger with the increasing weight of knowledge work, as a consequence of environmental demands imposed on organizations, and because of the capabilities offered by advanced information technologies, which are increasingly used by organizations. Drucker (1992) argues that the purpose of every organization is to integrate “specialized knowledges" into a common set of tasks. Knowledge of an organization's past dispersed through a variety of retention facilities (such as individuals and administrative structures) can render an organization more effective if made accessible via information systems.

The environments of post-industrial organizations present specific challenges to the effective functioning of organizations. Huber (1984) finds that three organizational processes have to be enhanced to meet these environmental demands: decision making, innovation, and information acquisition and distribution. Two of these processes relate directly to the organizational capability for processing information, and the third, innovation, depends on it to a large degree. Information systems have been increasingly deployed in these processes. Aside from the vastly lowered costs and enhanced processing and storage capabilities, IT offers rich and multiple informational media, and the possibility to interrelate various informational items and thus situate them in context. It is equally important that the increasing use of information systems as communication and coordination media can leave an extensive record of processes (“through what sequence of 'events?"), rationale (“why?"), context (“under what circumstances?"), outcomes (“how well did it work?"), along with mere results (e.g., a set of plan figures) or artifacts (e.g., a final design). Thus, the need to contribute to the effective functioning of organizations, combined with the opportunities furnished by IT, motivates us to focus on information-systems support for the functions of organizational memory.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In §2, we review the distinctions between individual and collective memory, articulate a definition of organizational memory as grounded in organizational effectiveness, and justify the need to explicitly recognize IT support for organizational memory. In §3, we discuss current characterizations of IT-supported organizational memory, and enumerate several existing taskspecific implementations. We then articulate a two-layer model for an organizational memory information system (OMIS) in §4. We proceed to discuss the meta-requirements and meta-design features of the ÖMIS subsystems designed to support four aspects of organizational effectiveness that we identify.¹ Our analysis reveals that significant elements of the proposed framework are emerging from leading-edge practices, prototype designs, and information systems research. We also discuss the processes and enabling technologies required to support the foundation layer of the model, which include the mnemonic functions of an OMIS qua memory: acquisition, retention, maintenance, search, and retrieval. Section 5 identifies the major factors that will limit the success of an OMIS implementation; i.e., those that recognize the human and social aspects of the system in development and use. In §6, we discuss an incipient contingency model for organizational memory information systems, proposing that the type of system actually implemented will depend on the environment and the stage of an organization in its life cycle. We also discuss the possible relationships between an OMIS and organizational learning and decision making. We thus lay out an initial agenda for research regarding the impact of an OMIS on the organization and the effect of organizational context on the development and use of organizational memory information systems.

## 2. The Construct of Organizational Memory

Although it is outside the scope of this paper to resolve the inherent tension between individual and collective memory, we provide a brief introduction to the ideas concerning these constructs in order to make explicit the concepts and assumptions that form the basis for this work. We proceed to explain the need to support organizational memory, which is an instance of collective memory, with information systems designed expressly for this purpose.

2.1. A Brief Review of Ideas Concerning Individual and Collective Memory An individual's memory is “. . . the faculty of retaining and recalling past experience . . ." as well as “. . . all that a person can remember" (The Illustrated Heritage Dictionary 1992). Thus, the term applies both to the functional processes of memory and to its contents. Both are known still in largely hypothetical form (Estes 1982, Schacter 1989, Squire 1986). Individual memory is considered as consisting of semantic and episodic memories, as well as a skill-based memory. Semantic memory refers to generalized knowledge independent of specific events, which is believed to be stored as a network of concepts. Episodic memory contains information about specific individual experiences, including time of event occurrence and context (Tulving 1983). These two types of memory are known jointly as declarative memory. Thus, the contents of individual memory reflect individual experience as well as general knowledge. Individual memory also includes skill-based memory. Sometimes called procedural memory, this type of memory refers to the implicit knowledge of how to do things, which may be accessible only through performance (e.g., touch typing or riding a bicycle).2 The contents of memory are subject to the processes of encoding (acquisition), storage (retention), and retrieval.

Functionally, individual memory is a means by which information about the past leads to behaviors that are more or less adaptive. However, because memory can not be seen or experienced by an observer, some have argued that it is a construct that serves to explain current behaviors in terms of past conditions and experiences (Ashby 1956).

Collective memory is a construct that evolved from the work of the Durkheim school of sociology at the end of the last century. The idea has also served to explain current social activities in terms of the past. Durkheim's notion of collective memory inhered in his notion of collective mind: “The collective mind is only a composite of individual minds. But the latter are not mechanically juxtaposed and closed off from another. They are in perpetual interaction through the exchange of symbols; they interpenetrate one another."3 Thus, the concept of collective memory, later analyzed by Halbwachs (1950/1980), refers to the social process of articulating, exchanging, and sharing information leading to shared interpretations. Symbols as representations of reality are the tokens that circulate, are reinterpreted, and stored through communication processes (see also Krippendorff (1975)). Social memory involves both the creation of these tokens through representation (itself a social process) as well as the interpretation of the tokens created. It is no wonder that the construct of collective memory has caused confusion among the disciplines, since it forces various schools of thought to confront one another.

Organizational memory is an instance of collective memory. The notion of organizational memory was introduced in its earliest form as a function required by a systems-theoretic analysis of organizations (e.g., Churchman et al. 1957) in order to explain and analyze the influence of the retained past on present organizational behaviors. Organizational memory relies on knowledge that is spatially distributed throughout the processes, individuals, and artifacts of the organization and beyond its boundaries. Later works adopted one or another extremes, viewing organizational memory simply as a function of people and artifacts (e.g., Miller 1978) or as a metaphor (e.g., Argyris and Schon 1978). As organizations have come to be seen as interpretative systems (Daft and Weick 1984), organizational memory has emerged as a construct more in line with Halbwachs' analysis of collective memory indicated above. For instance, Walsh and Ungson's (1991) comprehensive study views organizational memory as a construct (rather than a simple concept) that helps observers explain and interpret organizational proçesses and structures since organizational memory cannot be directly experienced (see Quinn and Rohrbaugh (1983) for the concept vs. construct discussion). Their work is explicitly grounded in the notion of organizations as systems that process information from the environment through enacted processes of interpretation.

Among the advantages of cultivating or expressly maintaining organizational memories are the honing of core competence, increased organizational learning (Hedberg 1981), increased autonomy (Churchman et al. 1957), integration of organizational actors, and lower transaction costs (Walsh and Ungson 1991). One of the dangers of retrieval from memory is encased learning, i.e., invoking rote responses to situations that do not meet current environmental demands. Maintenance of memory has its own costs, and maintenance failures can lead to decisions based on incorrect information. We shall return to the problematic aspects of organizational memory, as augmented by information systems, later in this paper.

A major threat to the organizational memory is personnel turnover, since much of this memory is situated today in the minds of individuals. Indeed, Carley (1992) finds, within the context of her simulation model where there is no repository for knowledge other than personnel, that turnover leads to a reduction in institutional (organizational) memory. March (1991) offers a similar result in these circumstances with respect to the organizational code, a concept akin to organizational memory. March also identifies the salutary effects of the growth in this code at relatively low turnover levels and high socialization rates, owing to the contribution of new members to the code; we shall return to this when discussing the dependencies between higher-level learning and organizational memory in §6.

## 2.2. Toward a Definition and Understanding of Organizational Memory

Our working definition of organizational memory reflects the merger of two streams of thought. Organizational memory has been defined elsewhere as “stored information from an organization's history that can be brought to bear on present decisions" (Walsh and Ungson 1991). This definition is reasonable, but insufficient. Schatz (1991–1992) suggests that organizational memory can provide “. . . knowledge that enables the organization to continue to function effectively." Following Schatz, we view organizational effectiveness as axial to our definition of organizational memory. However, we do not assume that increased effectiveness is inevitable, but only possible, with organizational memory. Organizational memory can also lead to lower levels of effectiveness. Therefore, we consider organizational memory to be the means by which knowledge from the past is brought to bear on present activities, thus resulting in higher or lower levels of organizational effectiveness.4 We view effectiveness in terms of a competing values model that considers organizational adaptiveness, goal attainment, information coordination, and human resource development as key criteria. Further justification for our linking of memory and effectiveness is provided in §4.

Our understanding of the contents of organizational memory parallels treatment at the individual level. Organizational memory appears to contain both semantic (general) and episodic (context-specific) information (El Sawy et al. 1986). Semantic information includes organizational practices contained in handbooks, manuals, and standard operating procedures, as well as the techno-scientific knowledge assimilated by the organization. Episodic memory includes engineering work-arounds and contextually situated decisions and their outcomes. Much of the semantic information is rather broadly available to the world in which a corporation competes and the advantages that may be gained from a superior retention and subsequent handling of this information derive from technique and efficiencies. However, “. . . (t)he real sources of advantage are to be found in management's ability to consolidate corporate-wide technologies and production skills into competencies that empower individual businesses to adapt quickly to changing opportunities" (Prahalad and Hamel 1990, p. 81). We, therefore, believe that the core competence of an organization derives from episodic memory and from an evolving (through organizational specialization) semantic base of knowledge. In other words, if a published technical paper related to a firm's specialty is “known to the world," and is entered into an organization's IT-based semantic memory, it may be helpful to the firm's knowledge workers but contributes little to its core competence. However, if that paper is annotated by company engineers with comments based on corporate experience and related to other pertinent and similarly “internalized"information items, it does indeed contribute to core competence.

Our structural understanding of organizational memory begins with Walsh and Ungson's (1991) comprehensive framework, which proposes that organizational memory is structured into five retention facilities. These organizational “bins" are: individuals, culture, transformations (e.g., production processes or personnel life-cycle processes), structures (casting individuals into roles), and ecology (physical setting of the workplaces). However, this framework allows for the role of information systems only by noting that “individuals and organizations keep records and files as a memory aid" (Walsh and Ungson 1991, p. 63). We find this framework unsatisfactory in its failure to recognize the role of the information systems constituent in organizational memory. In the present study, we attempt to bring about such recognition and offer a framework for an organizational memory information system as a component of organizational memory.

## 2.3. The Need to Recognize IS Support for Organizational Memory

Growing environmental complexity and increased managerial and professional turnover make it necessary to turn to information systems for support of organizational memory. Technological advances “in the world"and advances diffused throughout individual corporations make it possible. For instance, Huber (1990) postulates that the availability of advanced information technologies and related procedures will lead to the development and use of databases and expert systems as components of organizational memory. We emphatically agree with this proposition and wish to go further. In our view, organizational memory today and in the future will rely on expressly structured information systems support. Indeed, it is unclear how contemporary organizations can make decisions or engage in knowledge-intensive work without gaining access to past design solutions or plans (together with their outcomes) through their information systems. For example, environmental changes result with increasing frequency in organizational crises. Under the law of requisite variety, the combined human-IT control subsystem must provide responses to these disturbances. One response to environmental crisis is found in the use of the crisismanagement system, a component of electronic meeting systems (see, for example, Nunamaker et al. (1989)). The possibility of immediate recourse to such a system is an important asset in an environment of increasing turbulence (Huber 1984). The contents of organizational memory have to be brought to bear in a rapid manner in the complex context of new projects, changing intra-organizational configurations and myriad inter-organizational strategic alliances.

Broadening the repertoire of the information-systems support for organizational memory helps human actors cope with a possible information overload and supports their roles as information processors. For instance, American Express' Authorizer's Assistant expert system handles approximately 80 percent of authorizations for credit purchase automatically (Feigenbaum et al. 1988). With its response repertoire constantly replenished from the arising cases, the system is a part of the company's organizational memory. The system is also able to identify the need for human expert judgment and to present the facts of the case to the experts who handle exceptional situations.

Another reason to develop IT support for organizational memory is that this encourages us to impoşe certain requirements on the handling of the core knowledge of the organization. To result in activities that lead to higher levels of organizational effectiveness and learning, organizational knowledge must be explicit, communicable, and integrated (Duncan and Weiss 1979). If properly used, an IT-supported memory has advantages in fulfilling these requirements, since the contents that are stored in IS are explicit, can be modified promptly, shared as necessary, and changes . may be propagated through the use of IT. At the same time, we wish to stress that IS can only realize a portion of organizational memory. The successful task-specific memory information systems that we disçuss in §3 are expressly designed to augment the interaction between a knowledge seeker and human organizational experts rather than to supplant these experts.

## 3. Characterizations of IT Support for Organizational Memory and Examples of Task-Specific Designs

## 3.1. Čharacterizations of IT-Supported Organizational Memory

Literature that expressly addresses information systems support for organizational memory has emerged only recently. The work attempting to conceptualize IT-supported organizational memory is represented by (i) El Sawy et al. (1986), Paradice (1988), Konda et al. (1991), Sandoe et al. (1991), and Sandoe and Olfman (1992), who offer general characterizations of such systems; (ii) Ackerman and Malone (1990), Ackerman (1992), and Schatz (1991–92), who suggest specific systems that may serve as nuclei of such memory, and (iii) other contributions, such as Hall (1984), which lend themselves to adaptation to this stream of research. The principal categories (which may be found in or can be inferred from these works) for characterizing components of organizational memory are summarized in Table 1 and further discussed here.

Since time plays a vital role in memory, chronology furnishes an important distinction in an IT-supported organizational memory. El Sawy et al. (1986) offer a framework for a strategic information system, consisting of a “historian's information system" answering the question "Where have we been?"; a management information system telling us “Where are we now"; and a “futures research information system," responding to: "Where might we be going?" They see organizational memory as a part of the first of these three subsystems. In the light of the conceptualizations of organizational memory provided by Huber (1990) and others, we consider answers to all three questions accessible through an organizational memory information system. The argument for an anticipative memory, which considers the influence of the anticipated or hypothesized future states on present behavior, was first suggested by Tapiero (1977). A conceptual framework of a temporally-oriented planning and control system with such memory is contained in Ariav (1992). Paradice (1988) also stresses the role of organizational memory in anticipation of future environmental states. Krippendorff (1975) notes that the temporal aspects of processes are generally neglected by researchers, which partially explains why information-systems support for organizational memory is in its infancy.

As discussed in §2.1, the contents of memory range from general knowledge to specific instances. À strong claim that “human memory is story-based," or episodic has been made by Schank (1990, p. 12). Organizational memory has both semantic and episodic contents (El Sawy et al. 1986). Stein (1995) discriminates between declarative contents (e.g., schema's or frames) and the procedural ones (e.g., scripts). We note that information systems such as the Answer Garden (Ackerman 1992, Ackerman and Malone 1990) mix text with procedural code (“hot buttons"). Indeed, with the advent of object-oriented design and hypermedia (see, for example, Minch (1990)), we expect a proliferation of such mixing, which will lend power to IS support for organizational memory.

Another important distinction is made concerning the underlying epistemology and ontology of organizational memory. For example, Sandoe et al. (1991) epistemologically classify the approaches to organizational memory as representational or interpretational, i.e., some view organizational memory simply as representing knowledge, while others see the shared values and understandings that evolve among members of an organization as illustrative of organizational memory. Ontologically, organizational memory can also pertain to concrete knowledge embodied, for example, in the data- or knowledge-base contents, or to abstract knowledge. We note that some abstract knowledge such as cognitive maps (Argyris and Schon 1978), influence diagrams (Roos and Hall 1980), or cause maps (Hall 1984) increasingly lend themselves to “concretization" with information systems.

IS support for organizational memory will likely vary across the organization. Specific aspects of memory support are required by people who already share a common language and set of interpretations, such as a community of automobile designers. Konda et al. (1991) designate systems that support the work of tightly knit work communities as vertical systems. On the other hand, horizontal systems are interdisciplinary, i.e., systems that are used by members of different functional departments. Horizontal memory information systems are much more difficult to develop, because they have to assist members in developing a shared set of interpretations. Total quality management assigns high importance to evolving shared languages and interpretations, and IT-supported organizational memory will likely assist in this activity.

Finally, IT support for organizational memory raises the issue of accessibility, Price Waterhouse provides broad organizational access to its nascent organizational memory system (Zwass 1992), and similar policies are instituted in other firms. The contents and processes of organizational memory are, by their nature, distributed throughout the organization. Consequently, information system support for organizational memory also must be distributed. Various degrees of access may be specified both through corporate policies and through IS controls.

## 3.2. Task-specific IT Support for Organizational Memory

Several task-specific memory information systems have been either implemented or proposed. Their objectives, which overlap to a certain degree, are specified in Table 2.

Systems that are called team or group memory are oriented toward the support of a business team working on a set of projects and involving a large number of meetings with participants who are possibly dispersed in space. Such memory has been implemented as a component of the GroupSystems electronic meeting system (Nunamaker et al. 1991), with a prototype of a more elaborate system described in Morrison (1993).

Design rationale memory aims to preserve the intent and the evolution of product design (Reddy 1993). Several such memories have been implemented, including n-Dim (Konda et al. 1991) and DRCS (Klein 1993). Design rationales are captured by archiving ongoing argumentation into model-based frameworks. Closely related (and sometimes called discussion memory) is gIBIS (graphical Issue-Based Information System), which captures the evolving rationale behind policy decisions (Conklin and Begelman 1988). This hypertext-based system is marketed as CM/1 by Corporate Memory Systems.

Project memory has been implemented to support groups of researchers with extensive shared knowledge (Lynch and Chen 1992). Related to project memory, electronic-community memory has been proposed by Schatz (1991-92) to support a large and dispersed community of researchers. The major difference between project memory and design rationale memory is epistemological. Whereas project memory is primarily representational, design rationale memory is interpretive.

A meeting memory that supports the continuity of face-to-face meetings has been prototyped by Sandoe et al. (1991). Topical memory, which offers answers to questions about a range of topics, has been implemented under the name of Answer Garden (Ackerman 1992, Ackerman and Malone 1990). The memory grows as answers by experts to new questions (via electronic mail) are added. The Answer Garden is related to sophisticated help systems (e.g., Coppeto (1989)). Document memory, a system to access richly described documents, is described by Huhns et al. (1989). Much of the value of such a system as a memory component is delivered through the description or annotation, which may rely on multiple media.

A person-machine system known as the Knowledge Cache (Elofson and Konsynski 1991) that is used for environmental scanning and interpretation may be thought of as an environmental memory information system. It assists in the sensemaking process as new events occur in the organization's environment. A memory of interorganizational linkage patterns derived from richly described electronic data interchange (EDI) messages has been suggested by Mascarenhas (1989).

Organizational experience with task-oriented, IT-supported memory is being accumulated by firms such as Tandem (Sproull and Kiesler 1991), Price Waterhouse (Kiely 1993), Cypress Semiconductor (Govoni 1992), and Southern California Edison (Spin-off Provides Corporate Memory 1992). Ives et al. (1993) describe a database of previous designs maintained by an engineering firm. Accessible internationally throughout the company, this design memory supports the management vision of being able to engineer and manufacture equipment in any part of the world where a sales deal is concluded. McKinsey & Company consultants increasingly rely on its Firm Practice Information System, which stores project information (Peters 1992). The system contains the original problematique of the project, early diagnoses and solutions proposed by the consultants, and progress reports, i.e., anything that can help other members of the firm in the future. The firm also supports the Practice Development Network, which places on-line the core project documentation for the purposes of promoting organizational memory and learning. Peters (1992) lists over 20 other companies that have implemented a systemic scheme for capturing organizational knowledge.

As.emerging technologies (multimedia, object-oriented design and programming, very high capacity storage, and very high bandwidth communications) decrease in cost and become assimilated by organizations, richly indexed knowledge of past experiences will become increasingly available to corporate decision makers. However, the purpose, functions, and processes of an organizational memory information system have not been articulated in a model; what we have at present is simply a list of independent projects. In the next section, we propose a comprehensive framework for organizational memory information systems. The proposed model is intended to encourage designers to seek multiple ways to support organizational memory with IS, rather than cast such support into a single mold.

## 4. A Framework for Organizational Memory Information Systems

Organizational memory is the means by which organizational knowledge is transferred from the past to the present. While organizational memory does not by itself necessarily lead to organizational effectiveness, it can by design. We define an organizational memory information system (OMIS) as a system that functions to provide a means by which knowledge from the past is brought to bear on present activities, thus resulting in increased levels of effectivéness for the organization. As conceptualized and implemented at present, information systems have not generally been designed to transfer knowledge from the past to the present. The OMIS framework we propose explicitly addresses this need by supporting activities leading to effectiveness. We are not advocating one and only one OMIS, but a framework for a range of possible information systems designed to support memory at the organizational level. It is premature to offer specific implementations at this time.

## 4.1. Towards an Effectiveness-based Framework for an OMIS

We evolve a framework for an OMIS based on the following premises. First, the system we envision is meant to serve the organization, not the individual. Thus, our framework is grounded in theory at the organizational level of analysis. Second, we were compelled to build a framework using a central construct from organizational theory. We consider organizational effectiveness to be one of the chief concerns of organizations and agree with Cameron (1986a) who writes: “Despite the ambiguity and confusion surrounding it, the construct of organizational effectiveness is central to organizational science . . ." (p. 540). One of the inherent difficulties, yet strengths, of effectiveness theory is that no single measure of organizational effectiveness has been adopted. Criteria of effectiveness are a function of organizational values and as many as eight different models of effectiveness have proved useful in different research contexts (Cameron 1986a).

We chose to structure the IT possibilities for organizational memory using the competing values model of organizational effectiveness proposed by Quinn and Rohrbaugh (1983). This model was selected for several reasons: (i) the model was developed using prior research and a carefully applied methodology; (ii) the final model shows agreement with Parsons' (1959) landmark work in social systems theory; (iii) Lewin and Minton (1986) demonstrate that several earlier works can be deduced from the model; (iv) the model has been used in subsequent empirical investigations of effectiveness (Cameron 1986b, Cameron and Tschirhart 1992, Reagan and Rohrbaugh 1990, for example). A principal benefit of this effectiveness model is that it is comprehensive and even contains conflicting elements. This allows us to lay out our framework'as a range of possible OMIS models without having to specify what design is appropriate to a particular organizational context, which will be based on contingencies (discussed in §6).

An implicit assumption of this work is that effectiveness is related to organizational memory. The links between the two concepts are best illustrated in the context of the model we have chosen. Quinn and Rohrbaugh (1983) derived the model from a list of 30 organizational effectiveness criteria identified by Campbell (1977). Four functional clusters of effectiveness criteria were obtained and found to be consistent with the four effectiveness functions identified by Parsons (1959) as applicable to any system of action:

The first set of criteria, grouped under the integrative function, views effectiveness in terms of the organizational coordination and management of information across the organization. The second set of criteria views effectiveness in terms of the ability of the organization to adapt to changes in its environment. The third set of criteria views effectiveness in terms of the ability of the organization to set goals and evaluate the degree of their fulfillment. The fourth set of criteria views effectiveness in terms of the ability of the organization to maintain the cohesion and the morale of the workforce. Memory is directly relevant to each of these functions. For instance, integration of information over time is a chief function of memory. In order to adapt to its environment, an organization must know what has worked in the past in the context of the retained environmental information. Organizations must retain memories of goals in order to measure progress toward their achievement. Culture, morale, and cohesion represent memories of prior attitudes, values, and behaviors that develop and change over time. These links between effectiveness and memory (and the advent of task-specific information systems already operating in organizational settings) led us to frame IT support for organizational memory in terms of effectiveness. Our position is consistent with the earlier work by Galbraith (1977), who proposed that the design of effective organizations is based on two types of strategic choices: those that reduce the need for information processing by actions that buffer the organization from environmental influences and those that increase the organization's capacity for information processing. According to Galbraith, investment in vertical information systems is one of two methods for increasing this capacity.

We thus proceed under the assumption that an OMIS will lead to higher levels of organizational effectiveness while recognizing that it can be used for other purposes and that there is no simple cause-and-effect link between an OMIS and organizational effectiveness (as further discussed in §5). For example, Feldman and March (1981) argue that information collection is not tightly coupled to rational models of decision making and problem solving. Information that is collected may be irrelevant, used for surveillance rather than decision making, may be misrepresented, or used for symbolic effect. These authors do not suggest, however, that all uses of information are subject to these qualifications. There is mounting evidence that information systems do indeed increase decision-making and problem-solving effectiveness. For example, Sainfort et al. (1990) were able to show experimentally that communication media and decision support system technologies improved effectiveness as measured in terms of the quality of decision-making processes and problemsolving outcomes. This result lends support to Huber's (1990) contenton that the use of computer-assisted communication and decision-support technologies leads to higher quality decisions and reduces the time required to make the decisions.

In summary, the very rich framework provided by organizational effectiveness allows us to identify specific IT support for organizational memory. The competing values model is especially useful to our task because different OMIS designs can be constructed with this framework for different organizations, or even the same organization in different circumstances. A start-up firm that must place high emphasis on its ability to adapt to changes in its environment (Quinn and Cameron 1983) wil develop a very different OMIS than a mature organization. We further discuss such contingencies in §6.

## 4.2. Overview of the OMIS Design Framework

In keeping with our definition of organizational memory, we seek to conceptualize the organizational memory information system (OMIS) as the IT base for organizational memory. We are not seeking an automated system, but, to use the coinage of Zuboff (1988), we are seeking an “informated" organization, where other systems of the information system framework (Zwass 1992) and organizational actors would both rely on and contribute to the OMIS. The model we offer can be described using the distinctions provided by information systems design theory (Walls et al. 1992).

We propose two layers to the OMIS framework, each based on an appropriate kernel theory⁵ (see Figure 1). The kernel theory for the first layer is the competing values model of effectiveness. This top layer includes a set of subsystems that directly support the four functions of organizational effectiveness. An organization can choose to develop support for each of these functions to a greater or lesser degree. The kernel theory for the second layer is the information processing model of memory. This lower layer includes a set of mnemonic functions that enable the processes of memory and support the first layer. All OMIS implementations will require the second layer of mnemonic functions.

Using the terminology of information systems design theory, we describe the metarequirements (goals) of each layer, the meta-designs of each layer (attributes of the artifacts) and the progress achieved toward their implementation with current and evolving technologies in the following sections. We note the unevenness of the exposition that results from the gaps in current theory and practice.

## 4.3. Effectiveness-Oriented Functions

4.3.1. Integrative Subsystem. The IT capability for providing multilocal and instantaneous access to shared information is increasingly vital to organizational integration and coordination. We conceive the fulfillment of this function in terms close to those of Huber (1990) as enabling pervasive intraorganizational knowledge sharing to achieve productive functions at all levels of the organization. This understanding may be broadened to include knowledge sharing among collaborating organizations. The integrative element of OMIS is therefore designed to ensure that the internal knowledge of the organization regarding technical issues, past decisions, projects, designs, and so on is made explicit and available for future use, complete with contexts, rationales, and outcomes. Support for this function is also found in Lee and Leifer (1992), who offer an analysis of information system structures based on the contingency of information (or knowledge) sharing in the firm, and in Scott Morton (1991), who highlights the coordination aspect of IT-supported organizational memory.

Integration in toto involves the sharing of organizational knowledge across both space and time. Unfortunately, there has been a lack of explicit recognition of

Temporally integrated organizational memories exist where transfers of knowledge between past, present and future members are ongoing. Covington (1985) observes that college fraternities and sororities are representative of integrative memory organizations because tradition is passed from past to future members. In contrast temporally nonintegrated organizational memories exist only in the present moment. Organizations at this extreme continually recreate themselves and are incapable of learning from experience. Freshman dormitories are representative of this type of organizational memory because the membership and thus the rules and norms change every year. Changes in government administrations produce a similar effect on institutional memory. As seen in Figure 2, the ideal organizational memory is both connected and retentive. Partially integrated organizational memories include those that are fragmented but retentive, and ones that are highly connected but forgetful (amnesic). In the worst case, an organization's memory may lack any sort of integration, being both fragmented and forgetful. The integrative function of an OMIS addresses the need to integrate across both time and space.

Features of the meta-design for spatial integration include shared databases and knowledge bases, and shared distributed work templates delivered over communication networks increasingly available in groupware and workflow systems. Currently, spatial integration is achieved primarily through the sharing of information in company databases. Prototypes of more supportive designs are starting to emerge. The incipient organizational memory built around Lotus Notes system begins to play an integrative role at Price Waterhouse. The system integrates the knowledge of professional accountants located across the world into a unity that may be brought to bear on a client's problem. While at this time most of the knowledge is contributed by the human participants, it is technologically feasible to embed much of it within a larger information system. As noted in §3, a system with similar objectives has been implemented at McKinsey & Company. Interorganizational sharing is also possible and desirable for firms involved in collaboration networks, such as can be found in the burgeoning digital media industry. While following Astley and Brahm (1988), we consider the “full disclosure information systems" of Miles and Snow (1986) to be unrealistic, we see the sharing of specific knowledge caches (as described by Elofson and Konsynski (1991)) among organizations to be possible. By being able to furnish their own expert analysts or knowledge caches, the collaborating firms can bring immediate assets to the table.

Features of the meta-design for temporal integration include temporally indexed databases of events, problems, and designs, enriched by multimedia. One example of such as system is a design rationale system, which explicitly stores the evolution of envisaged artifacts. Another example of a temporally integrated system is the Answer Garden described earlier (Ackerman 1992, Ackerman and Malone 1990). Organized as a human-machine system, it “organically" grows the system-resident knowledge about a recurrent package of problems over time. To facilitate the sharing among organizational newcomers and veterans, Zabloudil and Decker (1991) offer a system (TAXOPS, for Tax Opportunity Identification Tool) to deliver expert advice (rather than an answer) to younger tax professionals. The system analyzes a company's situation and presents video clips of the experts discussing their experiences from an indexed library. The system selects the most relevant clips for the user based on initial queries. Minior et al. (1991) address the problem of redundant engineering with a system that maintains design solutions that have been tried in the past or at other locations, and have been found to be successful. The system recognizes and retrieves cases that match the current problem, and information from a previous case is adapted to the case at hand. Patino-Siliceo and Bourne (1989) describe the key functionalities of an experiential knowledge library that maintains a growing collection of designs for concurrent engineering processes. The system explicitly uses a memory model.

4.3.2. Adaptive Subsystem. The meta-requirements of the adaptive subsystem include boundary spanning activities to recognize, capture, organize, and distribute knowledge about the environment to the appropriate organizational actors. The requirements of this system are similar to those ascribed to organizational intelligence systems (Wilensky 1967), which scan market data for opportunities. However, an organizational intelligence system is designed to primarily acquire information about the environment for its users in the present, and is not designed to store and maintain histories of competitors, vendors, clients and other organizational stakeholders over time. The adaptive subsystem we envision would be built to explicit design criteria and would be integrated with other functions of an OMIS where possible.

Features of the meta-design for the adaptive function include representations for the retention of cross-linked historical information on stakeholders, pattern recognition and matching, knowledge bases of user preferences, and links to external sources of information. One prototype that partially supports boundary spanning is the Knowledge Cache (Elofson and Konsynski 1991), which is conceptualized by its designers in terms of a delegation technology rather than organizational memory. The system proposed by Mascarenhas (1989), which captures a memory of organizational linkage patterns and assists in winnowing out unpromising business associations by analyzing the histories of stakeholder behavior, is another example of an adaptive memory system.

4.3.3. Goal Attainment Subsystem. The goal attainment function assists organizational actors in the traditional functions of planning and control, which recent work (Ariav 1992) explicitly approaches as memory processes. The meta-requirements of this subsystem are to help the organizational actors frame and identify goal states in the context of the organizational past, store goal states, formulate strategies for achieving goal states, evaluate progress in the direction of goal states, suggest alternatives based on the evaluations, update goal states based on new information, and store annotated histories. Features of the meta-design include templates of the contextplan-result nature, expert planning knowledge, evaluation models, company performance data, past and current performance forecasts with the variances from these expectations, temporally labeled snapshots of past and current commitments and the circumstances under which the commitments were made.

Several partial implementations of systems that support goal attainment over time exist. For example, groupware such as The Coordinator (Winograd and Flores 1986) helps track speech acts that contain commitments, and serves as a limited form of organizational memory that spans the life of a project or an entire organizational life cycle. Meeting memory, prototyped and discussed by Sandoe et al. (1991), is also based on the speech act theory. Groupware is used in a similar role in the practice of Cypress Semiconductor Corporation (Govoni 1992). Employees of the 11,000 member firm enter objectives, priorities, and estimated project completion dates four to six weeks in the future into the corporate goal management system. They also enter data on project progress. Managers review the goals, attainments, and make evaluations. In another instance, Deugo and Oppacher (1989) describe a system that supports planning through the application of historical cases. An interesting feature of this system is that it can modify and repair old plans. Several firms now archive data from their transaction processing and operations systems into “data warehouses," which serve to preserve numerical data for planning and control. This service expands the myopic view offered by most transaction processing systems, which maintain data for no longer than 30–60 days.

4.3.4. Pattern Maintenance Subsystem. The emphasis of this function is on the human resources of the firm. In the original conception, “patterns" pertain to the attitudes, values, and norms of members of the organization. Our conception of patterns also includes personal routines (Nelson and Winter 1982) and personal knowledge. Effective organizations “maintain," i.e., promote the continuance of, values, attitudes, and norms that contribute to corporate cohesion and morale (Quinn and Cameron 1983). Our notion of “maintenance" is somewhat broader than this. While “to maintain" means “to keep in existence or continuance; preserve; retain," a second meaning of the word is “to keep in due condition, operation or force; keep unimpaired" (American College Dictionary 1970). This second meaning allows for dynamic change and is the meaning ascribed to the term as it is used in software maintenance, whose function is to modify and improve software over time. We therefore interpret pattern maintenance as pertaining to the means by which human resources are preserved and developed over time at both the individual and organizational levels.

The meta-requirements at the individual level of support are envisioned in a fashion similar to Huber (1991b), who stresses the need to go beyond demographics in a human resource information system. Huber sees the future human resource information system as containing the work history of individuals, with emphasis on project descriptions, capabilities, skills, and aspirations. Training is another aspect of human resource development that could be supported by this subsystem. The meta-requirements at the organizational level are to support the preservation of organizational protocols and the values implicit in them (Schatz 1991–92): This would be accomplished partially via procedures for interacting with an OMIS. Contributions by organizational actors to the system and the use of these contributions by other actors would be tracked with the aim of rewarding those who add real value, which should result in the building up of organizational knowledge. This element would also retain attitudes regarding job satisfaction, fairness and equity, levels of stress, and so on, which contribute to organizational climate and the values and norms of the organization (reliable instruments exist for this purpose and the collection of this data is routinely carried out by human resource consulting firms). Earlier measurements would serve as a baseline to detect variances in organizational variables in the future.

Features of the meta-design include rich and partially structured descriptions of cases, projects, procedures, personnel attributes and contributions, group level and organizational indicators of climate and culture, all of which could be enhanced with multimedia capabilities. One of the most extensive human resource information systems in use today that includes memory-based features is PRISM, which was implemented for Federal Express (Palvia et al. 1992). The system maintains histories of employee training and development, safety, benefits use, and structural changes to the organization due to employee turnover and transfers. Peters (1992) describes the use of a system to track the value of individual contributions to an incipient OMIS at McKinsey & Company. At the individual level of support, firms are increasingly using hypermedia systems to create rich profiles of their members, such as the video indexing feature implemented in TAXOPS (Zabloudil and Decker 1991).

## 4.4. Mnemonic Functions

Since an OMIS is a memory-based system, the attendant processes of memory also need support. Memory may be parsed into the processes of acquisition, retention, maintenance, search, and retrieval of information (Krippendorff 1975, Stein 1989, Walsh and Ungson 1991). The non-IT-based processes by which organizations retain and retrieve knowledge have been treated elsewhere (Stein 1989, Stein 1995, Walsh and Ungson 1991) and will not be discussed here. However, IT support for the mnemonic processes of organizational memory requires elaboration. These processes are shown in Figure 3.

As indicated earlier in Figure 2, we envision these mnemonic functions as constituting the foundation layer of an OMIS that directly supports the effectiveness-oriented functions of the top layer. In the following sections, after a brief review of the available knowledge-handling technologies, we discuss the meta-requirements and meta-designs of each mnemonic function, and the role of current and emerging information technologies in realizing support for these processes. Greater emphasis will be placed on mnemonic processes related to the “soft" components of information (e.g., knowledge as opposed to data), since the processing of data has been treated extensively in the available literature.

4.4.1. Enabling Information Technologies. A number of information technologies, both mature and advanced, will contribute to the development of an OMIS. It needs to be stressed that an OMIS implementation will involve multiple technologies and that its viability is not predicated on the development of new information technologies. One of the major justifications for the present work is that all of the necessary technologies have been developed and many have achieved widespread acceptance in industry. Further technological developments may be expected to lead to more effective OMIS designs.

In this brief review, we shall only mention the knowledge-handling technologies that have already proven themselves in implemented systems. Hypertext and hypermedia technologies can assist in richly embedding knowledge in context. Expert systems support the transfer of individual expertise (or, frequently, competence) from past to future members of an organization. When the available knowledge does not lend itself to being cast into rules, semantic nets, frames, or other knowledgebased representations, other systems are available. In many cases it is more natural to represent knowledge as an organized library of cases, which have also been recognized as a form of corporate memory (Kolodner and Mark 1992). Case-based reasoning systems contain a base of cases already encountered together with their context, solution (action taken), and outcome. These systems learn by memorizing new cases and by constructing new knowledge (cases) by analogy using past cases. Learning-bybeing-told is a form of memorization that has much in common with information retrieval systems (Salton and McGill 1983). Both approaches provide means to add new facts to' an existing store of knowledge, means to index facts, and means to retrieve knowledge based on probabilistic best-match (as opposed to simple exact match) techniques. The learning that takes place is a function of the newly indexed facts.

Acquiring knowledge, organizing that knowledge, and retrieving knowledge for improved task performance are part of the processes commonly associated with learning. Future OMIS designs can be enhanced by progress in machine learning (see Liang (1993) for a review of efforts to integrate learning capabilities into information systems). However, systems utilizing inductive or deductive reasoning, while desirable, will have to wait for consideration in future generations of organizational memory information systems.

4.4.2. Knowledge Acquisition. The meta-requirements for the knowledge acquișition function are to provide means to transfer knowledge to the memory informatión system. Features of the meta-design include links to internal and external data sources, information filtering, limited natural language processing, and intelligent summarizing capabilities. Ackoff (1967, 1981) furnishes an early design of a management information system that collects data from both the organization and its environment, and processes the data, relying on memory and learning for decision making. A key element of the design is an information filter, which selectively targets the acquisition. The works of Elofson and Konsynski (1991) and Ackerman and Malone (1990) show how an effective person-machine system for knowledge acquisition can be implemented with contemporary IT. Work in artificial intelligence over the past decade has attempted to actualize automated systems that read and summarize text. For example, a system called FRUMP (DeJong 1979a, 1979b) read newswire stories and produced summaries without human intervention. A promising approach to automatic (or largely automatic) growing of knowledge bases is knowledge discovery in textual databases, also known as “knowledge mining" (Lynch and Chen 1992). Notably, this approach resulted in creating an organizational memory for an international research community. An OMIS will require a combination of such intelligent acquisition capabilities.

4.4.3. Knowledge Retention. The meta-requirements for knowledge retention are to provide means by which knowledge is perpetuated over time for the organization. Knowledge must have certain characteristics for it to be retained by the organization. Duncan and Weiss (1979) argue that organizational knowledge (as distinguished from individual knowledge) must be communicable (understood by others) and consensual (accepted by others for its validity and utility), as well as integrated. We accept the first criterion as a minimal standard for inclusion in an OMIS. The more stringent requirement that knowledge be consensual may be another design consideration for organizations that value participation and team building.

Features of the meta-design include structures for encoding different types of knowledge, temporal languages for indexing knowledge according to time and context, and models for organizing past and present knowledge. Encoding organizational knowledge poses several problems. First, it presumes knowledge can be explicitly represented. Mechanisms for encoding have been developed in artificial intelligence including: scripts (representing sequences of events), frames (representing attributes of objects and contexts), and production rules, which may be used to represent causal knowledge. Conceptual graphs (also known as semantic networks) allow for the representation of interrelated knowledge elements in a domain. Hypertext and hypermedia perform a similar function. However, explicit representation of some forms of knowledge needed by the system, especially the temporal aspects of information tokens, requires further research. For example, a description of the context in which an important decision was formulated usually is not included in the documentation of a decision. Recording time and context requires development of new languages. Languages fulfilling some of these requirements have been developed for design rationale memories (Klein 1993). An examination by McKenzie and Snodgrass (1991) of 12 temporal algebras that may be used to query databases for time-dependent objects in relational databases concluded that while none of them could satisfy all desirable criteria, several adequate algebras were available.

The choice of representation has important consequences for search and retrieval. Information-retrieval systems permit efficient search and retrieval through extensive indexing (Salton and McGill 1983). However, it needs to be stressed that full-text retrieval systems (as opposed to index-based retrieval) do not perform as yet in a fully satisfactory fashion (Blair and Maron 1990). Extensive hand-indexing is usually done today in systems relying on the information-retrieval technology. Far richer indexing and organization of information will be critical to efficient retrieval both in the systems based on the information retrieval technology and on the artificial intelligence paradigms. Research on human cognition and memory models offers useful points of departure in the latter respect. Schank (1980) suggests that human memories are arranged in Memory Organization Packets (MOPs), whereby objects or scenes related by context are indexed together. For example, related design situations may be linked by the similarity of the representational structures. Kolodner (1984) defines structures that organize related scripts as Episodic Memory Organization Packets (EMOPs). For example, an EMOP-based design script would include a cost specification script, a material selection script, a coloring script, and so on. Indexing is based both on the similarities and differences evident in the scripts. A system deployed to gather corporate experience to enhance software engineering products (Foley and Orttung 1991) uses traditional MOPs. Another case-library system (Patino-Siliceo and Bourne 1989) extends traditional MOP indexing to include an evaluation dimension. TAXOPS (Zabloudil and Decker 1991) indexes by similar themes evident in the video clips encoded in the system. We conclude that far richer organizing structures than currently employed are technologically feasible and could be made available for application to IT-supported memory systems.

4.4.4. Knowledge Maintenance. The meta-requirements for knowledge maintenance are to provide means by which the integrity of retained knowledge is maintained by the system over time. Two problems are evident. The simpler one is longterm storage of the contents of memory. The integrity of storage media is not an appreciable problem. However, determining the way the system responds to new knowledge (acquired from external sources or through retrieval processes), the way it integrates knowledge with existing knowledge, and the way it selectively forgets knowledge, are more significant issues. In essence, these are the problems posed by learning.

Features of the meta-design include mechanisms for long-term storage, methods for integrating old and new knowledge, and decision rules for selectively eliminating parts of memory (unlearning). These are the same problems currently addressed by knowledge engineers, who must manually execute truth maintenance on knowledge bases as new rules or facts are added (McCaffrey 1992). Truth maintenance usually involves elimination of older rules in favor of new ones. Patino-Siliceo and Bourne (1989) note that changes to their system's case library may be performed destructively or incrementally following retrieval and modification of a case by the user. For example, if a design is retrieved and modified, then the system can either eliminate the previous design from memory or retain both designs (we consider the latter more appropriate for an OMIS). The only well-known system that automatically performs some of these functions without human intervention is CYRUS (Kolodner 1984) which automatically reorganizes indexing categories as new information is added.

4.4.5. Search and Retrieval. It is fruitful to distinguish, after Krippendorff (1975), between search and retrieval. Search is a process by which retained information is selected as relevant to a particular problem or goal of the user. Retrieval, on the other hand, is the reconstruction of the selected information to satisfy the user's request. Both are problematic, although search appears to be the more “wicked" problem and is the one discussed more extensively here. The meta-requirements for the search and retrieval functions are to provide means by which the retained knowledge can be brought to bear on a decision-maker's problem context or goals. Automated search is a process that can be broken up into three tasks (Kolodner 1984). First, what is being sought must be described along with the context and the user's goals. Second, the users goals and context must be elaborated because it is unlikely that the user will specify a full context without a priori knowledge of the system's structure. Developing the query may be done interactively. Based on a query formulation, the system looks for matches that meet the specified demands. If no matches are found, then a new query is chosen for search. An OMIS will be most useful if there is sufficient similarity between the current context and prior contexts. Under these conditions, past experience can be used to select current courses of action. During the retrieval stage, the information selected can be presented according to the user's preferences and the requirements of the specific query.

Information retrieval systems have been traditionally oriented toward the retrieval of unstructured data (such as general text) and semistructured data (such as E-mail messages) using exact-match Boolean logic. We envision an OMIS going considerably bevond this approach. Features of a meta-design for the search and retrieval functions include pattern matching and recognition, knowledge of particular domain areas, limited natural language processing, knowledge of user goals and preferences, and the availability of alternative forms of knowledge representation. The necessary technology is already available, albeit not in an integrated fashion. For example, some information retrieval and filtering systems utilize best-match models (Loeb and Terry 1992). These models recognize a lack of congruence between matches and nonmatches, use measures of similarity between the query and the source material, and make retrieval selection using vector space models (Salton and McGill 1983). Other systems utilize fuzzy logic and probabilistic matching. For example, an information retrieval system named TOPIC built by Verity, Inc. relies on a text engine that searches using fuzzy logic. Each topic is represented as a probability function. Topics related to the query are retrieved based on the probability of correspondence. Smith et al. (1989) describe several other knowledge-based search strategies for information search and retrieval. Within the artificial intelligence domain, a hybrid caseand rule-based system known as CABARET has been implemented to retrieve legal cases best matching the selected line of defense argument (Skalak and Rissland 1992). An OMIS can also operate in a proactive way by monitoring and evaluating previous problem contexts, providing advice to users based on previous outcomes, and learning by storing new patterns and modifying existing generalizations.

## 5. Organizational Contingencies of OMIS Implementation

In evolving a design framework for a new type of organizational information system such as an OMIS, one must beware of making unwarranted assumptions about linkages between technological artifacts and organizational consequences. As Boguslaw (1965) warns us: "In the contemporary world, the utopian plan' has become known as the process of ‘system design'"(p. 2). An OMIS is recognized as only one component of organizational memory and is by no means its embodiment. We are aware that the implementation of an OMIS will occur in the context of organizational problems surfaced by a broader theory of organizational IS implementation and in light of experience accumulated in the use of the task-specific memory information systems, such as those discussed in §3.2.

The following analysis frames the major contingencies that will limit the implementation and use of an OMIS. It should be noted that a full analysis of these contingencies in terms of organizational behavior, motivation theory, leadership, and political power is beyond the scope of this paper, but should be made the basis for future research. We also do not address the negative consequences of OMIS implementation for the human social system, although some of these issues are raised in the last section, which deals with the interaction of the OMIS and the organization.

1. Once recognized as contributing to organizational effectiveness, an OMIS implementation may not necessarily be initiated

It has been recognized that information systems redistribute organizational power (Markus 1984, for example). A system that accumulates and makes visible the history of organizational decisions has strong political consequences. Such visibility or even availability of information may be deemed undesirable from a competitive or other standpoint. In this regard, it should be recognized that an OMIS represents a design framework within which various specific designs are possible. Some of these solutions, which target subsets of organizational knowledge, will be more politically feasible in the context of a given organization than others.

2. Once the implementation of OMIS is initiated, it will not necessarily be successfully concluded.

Aside from the technological issues of information systems development, successful IS implementation is conditioned by a number of factors (synthesized in Zwass (1992)). These include aligning the design to organizational values, obtaining senior management support, using appropriate change management strategies, motivating participation during implementation, and selecting appropriate training methods for intended system users. Keen (1981) analyzes most vividly how an IS implementation may be derailed through user resistance in the form of “counter-implementation."

3. Once an OMIS is implemented, it does not necessarily follow that the system and its contents will be maintained and used.

A transformational organizational system such OMIS is never complete; unless it is institutionalized and evolves, it falls into disuse. While this problem applies to the IT base of the system, the principal concern here is with regard to the contents of the system. A user's intrinsic motivation to contribute information to the system differs with the degree to which the contributed information is instrumental to the user's goals. The motivation to use and contribute to the system is a function of whether the system is used in a contributory or consultative mode. A meeting participant who uses a meeting memory may be expected to be intrinsically more motivated to enter the minutes than a consultant who is expected to contribute her own knowledge for the benefit of others. Nevertheless, participation can be encouraged through appropriate incentives. McKinsey & Company, for example, has introduced effective motivational processes to induce its employees to contribute knowledge to be shared (Kiely 1993, Peters 1992), as did Arthur Andersen & Company (Quinn 1992). Motivational measures reflect the realization that such contributions are an aspect of an organizational actor's work. These measures include promotion and award considerations based at least in part on the volume and quality (as reflected in the use by others) of an individual's contribution to an organizational memory IS. The likelihood that an OMIS is used in a consultative mode depends on the degree to which members of the organization contribute to the system. It is also based on the routine of making present decisions in light of a critically appraised past. This habit of “treat(ing) memory as a pest" (Weick 1979, p. 221), of using the past to make decisions in the present through a critical examination of what the past has to offer, takes time to develop.

4. Once used. it is not certain that an OMIS will be used properly.

A memory-support system such as an OMIS may be misused or abused in a variety of ways. The improper use of organizational memory in general is discussed in Walsh and Úngson (1991) and includes misuse of the past (e.g., evoking routine responses in nonroutine contexts) and the abuse of organizational memory resulting in autocratic entrenchment. Similarly, an OMIS may be used to in such as way as to lead to the domination of certain ideas or values. Improper use of an OMIS can include the introduction of incorrect or incomplete information (either unintentionally or intentionally) to whitewash or to support individual agendas

5. Once used properly, it is not certain that the system will fulfill its organizational potential.

If a system permits a user to redefine his or her job for greater organizational effectiveness, then a system that does not lead to such a redefinition is not adequate (Ginzberg 1978). For instance, Orlikowski (1992) shows that, in a given corporate context. Lotus Notes groupware may be used simply as a communication tool instead of a tool for collaboration, a result that can be attributed to the culture of the firm and misaligned incentives. Kiely (1993) contrasts this case with the way the same system was used at McKinsey & Company to much greater effect.

Clearly, the organizational aspects of OMIS deserve separate and much fuller treatment. However, the above review of contingencies indicates that a transformational system such as an OMIS is not likely to succeed without evolving a design that is aligned with the corporate culture, without enacting appropriate organizational processes and, ultimately, without evolving organizational norms that push the organization to improve its effectiveness.

## 6. Future Research on the Interaction Between the Organization and an OMIS

An extensive research agenda is generated by the advent of IT-supported organizational memory systems in general, and by the OMIS framework in particular. Although elaboration of a full agenda falls outside the scope of this paper, we proceed to identify the areas we consider particularly important. These areas include the impact of contingencies such as environmental and organizational context on OMIS development, and the consequences of an OMIS for organizational learning and decision making.

## 6.1. Contingencies Affecting the Development of an OMIS

The development and use of an OMIS is expected to relate to the characteristics of an organization's environment and to the organization's position within its life cycle. We expect both factors to affect the implicit or explicit choice of effectiveness criteria. which will in turn affect the development and use of an OMIS, rooted as its framework is in the competing values model of effectiveness.

The general properties of postindustrial society include a draratic increase in available and relevant knowledge, growth in environmental complexity, and increased turbulence (Emery and Trist 1965, Huber 1984), all of which affect organizational decision making (Huber and Daft 1987). High environmental complexity is characterized by a large number of entities an organization needs to interact with (e.g., competitors or other external stakeholders), their diversity due to increasing specialization, and increased dependence on the actions of these entities. Organizations operating in complex environments are expected to grow the adaptive function of their OMIS, since this subsystem is responsible for boundary spanning. On the other hand, environmental turbulence (i.e., an increase in the rate of change and unpredictability of that change) would appear to make much of the past irrelevant. Indeed, March (1991) demonstrates the salutary effects of moderate personnel turnover in a turbulent environment. These conditions would call for extensive planning and replanning, and there would be greater need for the scenarios stored in the goal attainment subsystem of an OMIS. Organizations operating in complex and turbulent environments may be expected to develop and use both the adaptive and goal attainment subsystems.

Furthermore, we postulate that the particular form of an OMIS developed by an organization will depend on where the organization is in its life cycle. According to Quinn and Cameron (1983), organizations satisfy different measures of effectiveness (as specified by the competing values model) throughout their life cycles. We expect that different OMIS designs, will be developed according to these needs. The cited authors hypothesize that young, entrepreneurial organizations emphasize attaining effectiveness through adaptation based on flexibility. We therefore expect these organizations to develop and use the adaptive subsystem to a greater degree than other subsystems of an OMIS. In the next stage, emphasis is placed on adaptation, as well as the development of human resources and cohesion. We expect that the organizations in this second, “collectivity" stage will develop and use the adaptive and pattern maintenance subsystems to a greater degree than other OMIS subsystems. In the third stage, Quinn and Cameron see a shift to emphasis on integration and processes leading to the attainment of specific goals. We expect that such maturing, or “formal," organizations, will develop and use integrative and goal attainment subsystems to a greater degree than other OMIS subsystems. The final stage sees a return to adaptation as the mature organization seeks to renew itself. Consequently, we expect a mature organization to lay renewed stress on the development and use of the adaptive subsystem of an OMIS.

## 6.2. OMIS and Organizational Learning

An organization learns if the range of its potential behaviors is changed through its information processing (Huber 1991a). Organizational memory perpetuates and reflects what has been learned and is a point of departure for further learning. Argyris and Schon (1978) note that “. , . for organizational learning to occur, learning agents' discoveries, inventions, and evaluations must be embedded in organizational memory" (p. 19). Indeed, organizational learning is sometimes defined in terms of memory: "Organizations are seen as learning by encoding inferences from history into routines that guide behavior" (Levitt and March 1988, p. 320); or“. . . organizational learning occurs through shared insights, knowledge and mental models . . (and) builds on past knowledge and experience—that is, on memory" (Stata 1989, p. 64). Garvin (1993) ascribes five skills to “learning organizations" (a term that denotes organizations whose learning processes and mechanisms enhance their performance—although such a consequence is not true as a general proposition). One of these, learning from their own experiences and past history, inheres in the enactment of the processes of organizational memory. Another skill, learning from the experiences and practices of others, relies in part on a type of environmental memory as discussed in §3.2. A third, transferring knowledge quickly and efficiently throughout the organization, is one of the mnemonic functions specified for an OMIS. Based on these interrelationships, the development and use of an OMIS is expected to have significant consequences for organizational learning. At this point ofinquiry into OMIS, only some of the many consequences of an OMIS for organizational learning may be hypothesized.

There are two commonly recognized types of organizational learning. We adopt the language of Fiol and Lyles (1985) to distinguish between lower- and higher-level learning, categories that are roughly equivalent to the single- and double-loop learning types identified by Argyris and Schon (1978). Lower-level learning takes place within a given set of rules; members of an organization learn to detect deviations from standards and to solve problems by reducing variance between operating variables and norms. Higher-level learning takes place as members of the organization question underlying assumptions, procedures, and values, and attempt to substitute new paradigms for old ones. The contrast between the lower-level and higher-level learning can be also seen, after March (1991), as the exploitation of old certainties versus exploration of new possibilities. Both types of learning rely on organizational memory; however, this relationship is more tightly bound in the case of lower-level learning.

Lower-level learning best takes place if the standards of performance are a part of the memory of the organization so that its members can detect and correct variances. We propose that an OMIS directly supports lower-level learning by helping users find solutions based on the memory of control variables, solutions that worked before, and decision-making processes that led to acceptable problem solutions. For example, Stata (1989) showed how defect histories retained for years in an information system supported a quality improvement process by mitigating the effects of turnover. An OMIS is expected to enhance lower-level learning in all types of organizations, although we expect these benefits to be more pronounced in organizations that favor loosely structured teams. This expectation is based on recent work by Carley (1992), who examined the effects of social structure on experiential learning. She found that turnover had greater negative effect (in terms of learning) on teams than hierarchies. Presumably, hierarchical structure serves as a memory mechanism that buffers the system from personnel losses, although the price paid is slower learning rates. Carley recognizes that “. . . mechanisms that go beyond the personal memories of the decision makers . . . standard operating procedures, information sharing, institution-wide data bases . $\therefore ( 0 . 4 3 )$ provide means to buffer the social system from turnover and to preserve the ability to learn. By extension, an OMIS wili have the same effect on lower-level learning in groups.

On the other hand, higher-level learning can only take place if social processes are instituted by which members of the organization continually question governing operating assumptions. The relationship between this type of learning and an OMIS is expected to be more complex. Higher-level learning is to a large degree exploratory and is enhanced by modest personnel turnover through the acquisition of new knowledge (March 1991). An OMIS will facilitate or impede higher-level learning depending on the existence or absence of enacted awareness of organizational change and learning processes. An OMIS can support higher-level learning by (i) providing a partial record of existing norms and standards, which are thus made explicit and subject to review and modification, and (ii) providing a means to encode the outcomes of higher-level learning for future use. Huber (1991b) notes that demonstrability and usability of learning depend on the effectiveness of organizational memory Since Huber defines demonstrability as the capacity to store what has been learned in memory and then bring it forth, that aspect of learning may be expected to be enhanced by a properly implemented OMIS. However, higher-level learning requires enactment of organizational processes directed at exploratory learning. These processes include organizational experiments and the acquisition of experience from outside through the grafting of new members and assimilating the experience of other organizations. Reliance on an OMIS that does not evolve through such processes may be expected to atrophy higher-level organizational learning.

## 6.3. The Impact of an OMIS on Decision Making

An OMIS is expected to have an effect on the performance of individual decision makers. The “Minnesota experiments" showed that the use of horizontally disaggregated data led to a greater confidence by managers in the decisions they made based on the data (Dickson et al. 1977). More recent anecdotal evidence indicates that a similar effect is registered by exercising the drill-down capability of an executive information system or the explanation facility of an expert system. We can draw an analogy between the availability of historical data (i.e., vertically disaggregated data) versus snapshots of current data, and the availability of raw transaction data (i.e., horizontally disaggregated data) versus that of summarized data. One may therefore hypothesize that the “depth" offered by an organizational memory information system will have a similar confidence-raising effect on the decision makers.

Additionally, Walsh and Ungson (1991) propose that (i) decisions made in consideration of organizational history (embedded partly in an OMIS) will be more effective; (ii) decision choices arrived at in the context of history are less likely to meet with resistance because of the legitimating weight of that history; and (iii) the transaction costs associated with the implementation of a decision will be lowered owing to the "hows" and the “whats" stored in the memory (which should be particularly enhanced by OMIS).

These prior results and contentions offer the nucleus of a far more extensive agenda for research on the influence of an OMIS on decision-making process and outcomes in organizations. Research methods and instruments are available to examine propositions regarding individual and group decision making. For example, as noted earlier in the paper, Sainfort et al. (1990) offer an experimental design for evaluating the impact of information technologies on decision-making processes and problem-solving outcomes in terms of effectiveness. A similar experimental design may be used to evaluate the nature and the degree of OMIS impact on individual decision making. Reagan and Rohrbaugh (1990) developed and applied a multicriteria instrument to evaluate the outcomes of group decision-making efforts in terms of efficiency, participation, legitimacy, support for final decision, and other relevant factors. The instrument, based on the competing values model of effectiveness, can be used to assess the degree to which an OMIS affects group decision making.

Based on the cited evidence, we expect that along most dimensions, the appropriate use of an OMIS will improve decision making and lead to positive outcomes. However, as mentioned in the previous section, an OMIS may be expected under certain circumstances to impede higher-level learning and thus lead to an improper narrowing of the repertoire of alternatives considered in the decision-making process. Research on the contingencies that are expected to lead to negative outcomes should be conducted as well.

## Conclusions

Preservation of organizational memory is a significant concern for organizations. The core competence of an organization is rooted in the experiential knowledge of its members, which increasingly is being used to secure competitive advantage. Until now, organizational information systems have not been expressly targeted to support organizational memory. This situation is clearly changing, albeit in a piecemeal way,

We have offered a framework for an organizational memory information system (OMIS) rooted in a recognized model of organizational effectiveness. We demonstrated that such systems could be built now using existing information technologies. Our OMIS framework consists of four subsystems that directly support the four Parsonian aspects of organizational effectiveness, and a foundation layer of five mnemonic functions that provide the means to acquire, retain, maintain, search, and retrieve information. We identified several existing task-specific, IT-supported components of organizational memory, which can be incorporated into an implementation based on the proposed framework. We also presented the beginnings of a theory regarding the relationships between an OMIS and other factors of organizations.

The conceptualizations offered here provide ample opportunitięs for further work. Further theoretical development is needed to refine the OMIS framework and move towards specific implementations. Developmental research needs to continue to evolve prototype systems that implement the functions proposed here in forms appropriate for specific organizations. The impact of emerging systems, as well as of the existing task-specific IT-supported designs on organizations needs to be studied as well. Together, the research streams should lead to a contingency framework for OMIS designs suitable for various organizational contexts.

The explicit recognition of the role of IT-supported organizational memory is expected to bring significant benefits to organizational practice. Having integrated the many components of an OMIS into a coherent framework, and having identified the enabling technologies, we hope to contribute to the development of integrated organizational memory information systems. Once implemented, specific OMIS designs will change the ways organizations compete, reward employees, make decisions, and adapt to changing conditions.\*
