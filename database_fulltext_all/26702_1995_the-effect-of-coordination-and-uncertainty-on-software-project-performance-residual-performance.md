# The Effect of Coordination and Uncertainty on Software Project Performance: Residual Performance Risk as an Intervening Variable

Sarma Nidumolu

MIS Department

College of Busıness and Publıc Administration

University of Arizona

Tucson, AZ 85721

In this research, a study of the effects of coordination mechanisms and risk drivers such as project uncertainty on the performance of software development projects was conducted. Two types of coordination mechanisms were considered: vertical and horizontal. The former refers to the extent to which coordination between users and IS staff is undertaken by authorized entities such as project managers or steering committees. The latter refers to the extent to which coordination is undertaken through mutual adjustments and communications between users and IS staff. A new research model was developed by synthesizing research using the structural contingency perspective from Organization Theory and the risk-based perspective in Software Engineering. The model suggests that residual performance risk, i.e., the difficulty in estimating performance-related outcomes during the later stages of the project, can clarify the relationship between project uncertainty, coordination mechanisms and performance. Eight hypotheses were derived from the model for empirical testing. Data were collected from 64 software development projects in the banking and other industries. The results provide considerable support for a revised research model. As expected, project uncertainty increases residual performance risk. Both in turn have a direct negative effect on performance. Vertical coordination significantly reduces both project uncertainty and residual performance risk. However, horizontal coordination does not have any significant effect on residual performance risk. Instead, it has a direct positive effect on project performance. Moreover, higher levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination lead to higher levels of overall performance. Their differential impacts on residual performance risk are interesting areas of future research.

These projects came in years behind schedule, exceeded their budget by millions, and failed to meet their users' needs even if completed eventually.

What does academic research have to say about the important practical problem of managing software projects effectively? A key question, according to researchers, is how managers can best cope with the uncertainties of software development (McFarlan 1981). Uncertainty is broadly defined as the absence of complete information about the organizational phenomenon being studied (Argote 1982), which in turn leads to an inability to predict it accurately (Milliken 1987). The concept of uncertainty has been central to research in Organization Theory. Indeed, Thompson (1967) argues that it is the fundamental problem that senior managers have to cope with. In the Information Systems literature, the role of uncertainty in software projects is summarized by Zmud (1980, p. 46): “Most difficulties can be traced to the uncertainty that pervades software development. Software development is an information-intensive activity, and decision points are continually reached where the decision maker possesses inadequate information."

In dealing with project uncertainty, both the Information Systems and Organization Theory literature stress the importance of coordination, which refers to “the linking together [of] different parts of an organization to accomplish a collective set of tasks" (Van de Ven et al. 1976, p. 322). In this paper, a new theoretical model is derived and tested, based on prior research in Organization Theory and Software Engineering, to answer the following research question: How do project uncertainty and the mechanisms for coordinating project activities affect the performance of software development projects?

## 2. Theory

## 2.1 Structural Contingency Perspective

The structural contingency perspective, long dominant in Organization Theory and Information Systems research, provides a useful starting point for this study. Since the early '60s, researchers have studied the relationship between technology and organizational structure. In Organization Theory, technology has typically been defined as the processes or tasks involved in transforming inputs into outputs (Fry 1982), in contrast to many practitioners who take a more “tool-oriented" view of it. The seminal contributions of the Aston group (e.g., Pugh et al. 1969), Fry (1982), Perrow (1967), Thompson (1967), Van de Ven and his colleagues (e.g., Drazin and Van de Ven 1985; Van de Ven et al. 1976), Woodward (1965) and others have led to the general conclusion that the most appropriate structure (i.e., the structure that maximizes organizational performance) is contingent on the uncertainty confronted by the organization.

Underlying the structural contingeny perspective is an information processing viewpoint of organizations. The approach, pioneered by Galbraith (1973, 1977), holds that performance is determined by the match (or “fit") between the uncertainty in the unit's tasks and the ability of the unit's structure to process the information required to cope with the uncertainty (Daft and Macintonsh 1981, Tushman and Nadler 1978). The better the match, the higher the performance. Consequently, the organizational structure needs to provide for this information processing, or else performance is adversely affected (Daft and Macintosh 1981, Galbraith 1973, 1977; Tushman and Nadler 1978). Because structures differ in their information processing capabilities (Galbraith 1973, 1977), the research problem is to identify the structure that maximizes performance for a given level of uncertainty.

Since structure is a complex concept, researchers have focused in practice on specific aspects of it for studying contingency relationships. In particular. the coordination structure, i.e., the mechanisms used to coordinate activities, has long been a focal point of study in Organization Theory (e.g., Argote 1982, Georgopoulos 1972, March and Simon 1958; Thompson 1967, Van de Ven et al. 1976) and more recently in Information Systems research (e.g., Kim 1988, Kim and Umanath 1993, Malone and Crowston 1994, Zmud 1984).

However, the structural contingeny perspective has a number of shortcomings. The concept of fit is ambiguous and difficult to distinguish from performance (Gutek 1989), and empirical results have been conflicting and disputed even after decades of research in Organization Theory (Miller et al. 199 1) and Information Systems (Weil and Olson 1989). Moreover, technology has traditionally been considered deterministic and a given (Scott 1990), and performance has often been described in general terms without being specific to the context being studied (Keller 1994).

## 2.2. Risk-based Software Engineering Perspective

Software engineering research provides a rich source of concepts specific to the context of software development which supplement the insights provided by the structural contingeny perspective. Along with structural contingency researchers, software engineering researchers argue that uncertainties in software development are a key problem affecting performance. The purpose of software engineering is therefore to control the software development process, particularly the sources of project uncertainty, and produce quality software for the user (Pressman 1992). This control is sought to be accomplished using methods, tools and procedures that bring more structure and formalism to the complex and uncertain task of developing software.

An important recent stream of research in software engineering, the risk-based perspective (e.g., Barki et al. 1993; Boehm 1989; Charette 1989), describes why project uncertainties have an adverse impact on performance. According to this perspective, project uncertainties can be viewed as risk drivers which increase the performance risk of the project. Performance risk refers to the difficulty in estimating what the performance of the project is likely to be. It therefore stems from a lack of information about outcomes, whereas risk drivers such as project uncertainties stem from lack ofinformation about the inputs to the project such as requirements specifications and technologies. Clearly, the presence of risk drivers increases the performance risk of the project. Risk-based researchers argue that performance risk is an important intervening variable that links risk drivers to the actual performance achieved by the software development project.

However, risk-based approaches also have a number of shortcomings. Performance risk has been conceptualized in a variety of ways (cf. Anderson and Narasimhan 1988, Barki et al. 1993, Boehm 1989, Charette 1989), often blurring the distinction between performance risk and its sources. Moreover, research has largely been conceptual and prescriptive in nature, resulting in the need for theory that relates risk, management practices and project performance (Barki et al. 1993).

## 2.3. Definition of Constructs

The research model developed in this study (Figure 1) synthesizes the insights from both the structural contingency and risk-based perspectives but addresses certain of their limitations in the context of software development. The key concepts used in the model are described below.

Performance. It is described in terms of two key aspects emphasized in the IS literature on software project performance (e.g., see Agresti 1986, Beath 1986, Cooprider and Henderson 1991, Mookerjee 1988): (1) process performance, which describes how well the software development process has been undertaken, and (2) product performance, which describes the performance of the system actually delivered to users. It is important to study both aspects because even though the software delivered by the project may be of high quality, the project itself may have significantly exceeded time and cost projections. On the other hand, well-managed projects which come in below time and cost budgets may deliver poor products.

Coordination Mechanisms. Although a variety of individuals and groups are involved in a software development project, the Information Systems literature suggests that the relationship between the Information Systems staff and users is the most critical. Indeed, its importance has been considered almost axiomatic (Beath 1986).

Vertical coordination is the extent to which coordination between users and Information Systems staffis undertaken through vertical means such as authorized entities (e.g., Van de Ven et al. 1976). In software development projects, vertical coordination through a strong project manager has long been emphasized in Information Systems and Software Engineering research (e.g., see Berkeley et al. 1990, Pressman 1992, Thamhain and Wilemon 1986, Thayer et al. 1981), as has the role of steering committees (e.g., see Davis and Olson 1985, Drury 1984).

Horizontal coordination is the extent to which coordination between users and Information Systems staff is undertaken through mutual adjustments and communications, whether through personal or group means (e.g., Van de Ven et al. 1976). The importance of such lateral communications through meetings and one-on-one discussions between users and IS staff has also been emphasized by Information Systems researchers (e.g., see Kydd 1989, Zmud 1980).

Project Uncertainty. The rısk drivers considered in this study include two key types of uncertainties in a software development project: requirements uncertainty, which refers to the uncertainty about what the user requires, and technological uncertainty. which refers to the uncertainty concerning technologies such as tools and techniques needed to convert requirements to a software system.

Requirements uncertainty• has been widely studied in Information Systems research because of the difficulty in eliciting requirements from users. Requirements analysis is the most important stage in the software development process, because it has the greatest impact on future stages (Turner 1992). At the beginning of many projects, users and analysts do not have a clear sense of what is needed (Lucas 1982), leading to an ambiguous and incomplete set of requirements (Rowen 1990). This is especially true for applications that are new or involve unstructured tasks (Zmud 1980, 1984).

Technological uncertainty is another critical risk driver in software development projects. Zmud (1980, p. 46) identifies the use of “complex or state-of-the-art technologies" and technological changes that alter system design as key sources of project uncertainty. Boehm (1989, p. 121) describes how many projects venture into advanced technologies such as distributed data processing or Artificial Intelligence, or otherwise push the boundaries of current technological capabilities, thus increasing the risk of failure. McFarlan (1981) views the organization's experience with technology (e.g., hardware, operating systems, databases, application languages) as a key source of uncertainty.

Residual Performance Risk. Performance risk can be defined as the extent of difficulty in estimating the performance-related outcomes of a project, regardless of the specific estimation technique used. These include outcomes such as the actual project cost, project completion time, system benefits, the system's compatibility with its environment, and the technical performance of the resulting system (McFarlan 1981). The amount of performance risk remaining in the project changes over time, gradually decreasing as performance becomes more evident. The risk that is specifically considered in this study is residual performance risk, which refers to the extent of performance risk present in the later stages of the project after project planning and requirements analysis have been completed (i.e.. during design, coding, testing and installation).

## 2.4. Research Hypotheses

Software Engineering and Information Systems researchers have identified a number of risk drivers that lead to difficulties in estimating project performance (see Barki et al. 1993 for a recent summary). For example, incomplete, ambiguous or inconsistent requirements (Thayer and Lehman 1987) or frequent changes in them (Berkeley et al. 1990) make it difficult to predict performance outcomes, because they often necessitate redoing requirements analysis (Jenkins and Wetherbe 1984). Lack of information about requirements therefore make it difficult to predict the time and effort

that the project will consume, thus increasing its performance risk. Similarly, the technological complexity of the project increases its performance risk (Beath 1983, Davis 1992, Zmud 1980) as does technological change or newness of the technologies (Beath 1983, Zmud 1980). These arguments suggest the following hypothesis:

H1. Higher levels of project uncertainty will lead to higher levels of residual performance risk.

Aside from its role as a risk driver, project uncertainty could also have a direct effect on performance which is unrelated to performance risk. For example, fluctuating requirements could vitiate the climate of the project and lead to mistrust between the users and IS staff. This in turn could lead to conflicts between users and IS staff that take considerable effort and time to resolve, therefore decreasing process performance, or the use of unfamiliar technologies in developing the system could lead to software problems that reduce the performance of the software product. The following hypothesis can be derived:

H2. Higher levels of project uncertainty will directly lead to lower levels of project performance.

The failure to consider the performance risk of projects and to take appropriate steps to reduce it are argued to be primary reasons why many projects are unsuccessful (McFarlan 1981). Efforts at reducing performance risk help avoid disasters, rework and other unnecessary effort (Boehm 1989). It could even lead to over 50 percent gains in productivity, often because 40–50% of the cost of developing software is spent on fixing problems with it (Charette 1989, p. 39). Risk-based research suggests an explanation for this negative relationship between performance risk and actual performance: software development projects whose risks are under control enable management to estimate resources more accurately and undertake better planning and implementation (Boehm 1989, Shumskas 1992). Moreover, this ability to “read" and understand the process facilitates sustained efforts at improving it, therefore leading to more mature software development processes (Humphrey 1989, Krasner 1992). These arguments suggest that:

H3. Higher levels of residual performance risk will lead to lower levels of project performance.

Software engineering research has identified a variety of ways in which project management can reduce the performance risk generated by risk drivers such as project uncertainties. Project management can initiate and direct activities such as management reviews, verification and validation of software quality, cost-benefit analysis, careful monitoring of ongoing performance, comparison of actual and planned performance baselines, and revision of cost and performance estimates (Boehm 1989). Through such vertical coordination, whether by project managers or steering committees, an enhanced understanding of the progress of the project can be obtained, leading to better estimates of project performance. More generally, vertical coordination through managerial control could help establish goals and to clarify the roles of the individual team members (Locke et al. 1980). Such control can be exercised either by monitoring behaviors or outcomes (Ouchi and Maguire 1975, Eisenhardt 1985), which provides the necessary feedback to managers in assessing the likely performance outcomes from the project. These arguments suggest the following:

H4. Higher levels of vertical coordination will lead to lower levels of residual performance risk.

Project management can also directly reduce the level of risk drivers such as project uncertainties, therefore reducing the need for processing information. For example, project management can proactively anticipate risk drivers and avoid them through obtaining signoffs from users and preventing requirements creep, reducing “gold plating" of requirements by negotiating to fulfill only the essential requirements (i.e., requirements scrubbing), and instituting change control and configuration management mechanisms to ensure that only the most necessary changes to the requirements are made (Boehm 1989, Shumskas 1992). Also, project management can obtain slack in the system by descoping performance objectives to a more achievable level, negotiating less stringent deadlines, and obtaining additional resources for undertaking the project. Such project management activities, typically performed by the project manager, buffer the project from risk drivers and ensure that they are not significant to begin with. Buffering mechanisms such as slack resources for coping with uncertainty have also been identified by structural contingency research (Galbraith 1977 Thompson 1967). These arguments suggest the following hypothesis:

H5. Higher levels of vertical coordination will lead to lower levels of project uncertainty.

When project uncertainty is high, research has advocated an arrangement where participants communicate with each other on a frequent and informal basis (Zmud 1980). Structural contingency research provides an explanation for why this may be so. Horizontal channels of communication provide large capacity for information processing which allow information to flow freely between the groups involved in performing the work (Scott 1981). Especially during requirements analysis, extensive horizontal communication between IS staff and users leads to better identification of the users' needs and the functionalities expected of the system. Meetings between IS staff and users in particular are critical at ascertaining a set of requirements for the system (Kydd 1989). In turn, this information can be expected to reduce the difficulty in estimating what the project's costs, schedules and other performance consequences are likely to be. This suggests that:

H6. Higher levels of horizontal coordination will lead to lower levels of residual performance risk

Horizontal coordination through direct communication between users and IS staff can also have a direct positive effect on project performance, apart from reducing the difficulty in estimating performance outcomes. Extensive horizontal coordination between users and IS staff suggests a high degree of user participation in the project. Such participation could promote a sense of involvement in the process by the users (Hartwick and Barki 1994) and help tailor the system's attributes to the users' needs (Robey and Farrow 1982). Open channels of communication among team members could therefore lead to superior software and high job satisfaction (Weinberg 1971). Moreover, software development groups that have high levels of communication among their members could be expected to have high levels of morale and job satisfaction, resulting in improved group performance (Mantei 198 1). In the Organization Theory literature, Sammons (1988) found evidence for such a direct effect: high levels

of participation and interaction among staff in a hospital unit were associated with high unit performance even when the tasks were more certain. These arguments suggest that:

H7. Higher levels of horizontal coordination will directly lead to higher levels of project performance

Organizational theorists have argued that lateral channels of communication often supplement and complement existing hierarchical channels, as for example in a matrix organization (Hrebiniak and Joyce 1984, Joyce 1986). Conversely, even participative organizations with high levels of lateral communication need to have a system of control to integrate and organize the diverse sets of interests and actions found in the group (Tannenbaum 1968). For example, lateral channels of communication and control in self-managed teams become supplemented by formal vertical arrangements such as coordinators. These coordinators are responsible for tasks such as scheduling, tracking of errors, and for serving as the interface between the project team and management (Barker 1993). Without such integration, project teams take too much time to complete their tasks, and could even head off in the wrong direction (Hackman et al. 1990)

Henderson and Lee (1992) have demonstrated recently that high levels of both managerial and team-member control and coordination have a positive effect on the performance of software development projects. While horizontal coordination leads to fuller utilization of information, vertical coordination helps assign accountability and ensures that the team does not succumb to protracted discussion (Nicholas 1990). For example, Constantine (1993) has espoused the use of a “structured open" approach to managing software projects which combines hierarchical elements of project coordination with collaboration through negotiation and discussion. Both forms of coordination should therefore be present in order to improve project performance, especially when the project is complex and uncertain, but time and cost objectives are critical (Nicholas 1990). These arguments suggest that:

H8. Higher levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination will lead to higher levels of project performance.

## 4. Research Methodology

A survey design was selected for testing the research model because it lends itself readily to operationalizing and measuring the key constructs in this study, is suitable for testing the theoretical formulation of the research question in the form of research hypotheses, and facilitates generalizability of findings to the population of software development projects.

## 4.1. Respondents

The survey data was comprised of 64 projects, half of which were collected from an Industry Partners group at the author's university (50% organizational response rate), and the other half from the top 250 banks in the banking industry (7.6% organizational response rate) where Information Technology is a critical resource (Hanley et al. 1985). For each firm contacted by mail, the liaison (typically, the VP of Data Processing) was asked to identify an equal number of successful and unsuccessful projects that were recently completed, which represented a strategy of deliberate sampling for heterogeneity (Cook and Campbell 1979). The liaison was requested to pass the questionnaire on to the DP manager of each project, or in his/her absence to a senior DP staff member involved with the project

Nonresponse bias is a potential threat to the external validity of this study, given the low responses for banks. This could happen if the nonrespondents differed systematically from the respondents on some important characteristics (Fowler 1993). For the banking industry in particular, firm size is a critical determinant of a bank's approach to information technology (Hanley et al. 1985). A common measure of bank size is the dollar amount of deposits (American Banker 1990). In this study, the mean deposits at the responding banks were \$6.4 Billion, which was comparable to the mean size of the population (\$5.3 billion). The t-test comparison between responding and nonresponding banks did not suggest any significant difference in size (t = -0.42, p = 0.68). This suggested the lack of a nonresponse bias, at least with regard to a critical variable such as firm size.

The two samples were also checked for similarity in the distributions of the study's variables, which would permit pooling of the two samples and generalization of the findings to a meaningful population (i.e., the population of software development projects). A t-test comparison of means showed that there was no significant difference in any of them, suggesting the lack of an industry-specific effect. Moreover, an F-test comparison of means of variables also suggested that there was no significant difference between projects drawn from different firms, suggesting the lack of a firmspecific effect. Overall, the two samples did not seem to have any systematic differences between them, suggesting that they could be pooled together for subsequent analysis.

Another threat to external validity was if the pooled sample itself was systematically biased with regard to key characteristics such as project size (e.g., the responses were generally for small, or large, projects). Overall, the 64 projects showed good dispersion on these size-related characteristics, suggesting that a project size-based bias was unlikely. Generalizability of findings would also be threatened if the pooled sample was systematically biased with regard to performance (e.g., if there was a response from only relatively successful projects). The findings suggested that the projects had good distribution on the performance dimensions, since the mean and median were similar, skewness was less than two, and kurtosis was less than five (Ghiselli et al. 1981). Overall, this suggested that a performance-related bias was unlikely.

Overall, although the response rates were somewhat low, the series of tests conducted above did not identify any significant threats to external validity. They therefore enhanced confidence in the generalizability of the indings to the population of software projects. ## 4.2. Questionnaire Development

The questionnaire developed for the study was subject to both a pretest and a pilot test prior to its usage. In the pretest stage, structured interviews were held with eight software project managers from four companies in the author's local area. The purpose of this stage was to assess whether the construct measures derived from prior research made sense to the practitioner, and whether items needed to be added/deleted or modified. The questionnaire developed as a result of the pretest was then subject to a pilot test consisting of a convenience sample of six doctoral students, three faculty members and 12 project managers from nine companies in the local area. The purpose of this stage was to refine the draft questionnaire developed earlier. The final questionnaire differed from the original, theory-derived draft in several important respects: 1) it contained more background information on the project and company (e.g., information on the purpose of undertaking the system, the calendar duration, project initiators and authorizers); 2) a few items were added to the original list, and 3) others were deleted.

## 4.3. Tests for Single-respondent Bias

Because a single respondent (i.e., the DP manager) was used to assess both the independent and dependent variables, there was a likelihood that the items actually represented a subjective “mental model" of performance as assessed by the DP manager, rather than a more objective measure for project performance. The potential threat to the construct validity of the instrument due to this single-respondent bias was assessed and corrected for in this study, using a subsample of 19 projects which included performance assessments by their user managers (i.e., a double-informant sample).

An MTMM test on the double-informant sample using the DP manager and user manager assessments as the two methods suggested that two of six dimensions used to measure performance—learning acquired during the project, and operational efficiency of the software—did not have good convergent or discriminant validity. A test for common method variance undertaken by comparing the monomethod correlations with their heteromethod counterparts (Straub 1989) also suggested that the learning and efficiency dimensions had considerable method variance. These two dimensions were therefore dropped from subsequent analysis because of a likely single-respondent bias.

In many projects, an escalating sequence of dispute resolution is often invoked, i.e., issues not resolved by the project manager are directed to higher levels such as a steering committee or senior DP and user management. Increased use of either one of these sources can therefore be considered as leading to increased reliance on vertical coordination. However, an increase in vertical coordination does not imply that all three sources of authority were increasingly relied upon (e.g., the project manager may have made all the decisions). The three sources of vertical coordination are therefore the causes, the vertical coordination the effect. Principal component analysis, rather than factor analysis, was therefore used to compute the composite score for vertical coordination (cf. Bollen 1989).

However, the first principal component gave negligible weight to reliance on the project manager, and considerably more to reliance on senior management above the level of the project. This suggests that the component may have measured coordination of exceptional cases that could not be handled by the project manager. The second component, which explained 34% of the variance in the three items (Table 6), gave predominant weight (0.97) to reliance on the project manager, which was the focus of the study. This component was therefore continued with as a measure for vertical coordination in subsequent analysis.

Horizontal Coordination. This was measured by the extent to which each of the following was used for coordinating activities between users and IS staff in the earlier phases of the project: 1) oral communication (e.g., face-to-face, telephone) between individuals from users and inhouse DP group, 2) written communication (e.g. memos, notes) between individuals from users and inhouse DP group, 3) scheduled group meetings between users and inhouse DP group, and 4) unscheduled group meetings between users and inhouse DP group to discuss issues. The first two items were suggested from field interviews. The last two items were derived from Van de Ven et al. (1976).

The factor analysis showed that all items loaded above 0.5 as predicted on the dimension, suggesting good construct validity. Moreover, the Cronbach alpha score of 0.72 (Table 6) also exceeded Nunnally's (1978) recommendations. The four-item scale for horizontal coordination therefore had adequate validity and reliability.

Residual Performance Risk. This variable was measured by the extent of difficulty (1—Very Easy, 5—Very Difficult) in estimating five items during the later phases of the project: (1) the costs of the project, (2) the project completion time, (3) the benefits of the software, (4) whether the software would be compatible with its environment, (5) whether the software would meet user needs. The items were derived from McFarlan (1981). McFarlan used a sixth item—technical performance—which was considered ambiguous during field testing, e.g., it was not clear whether it referred to response time, reliability of system, etc.

The factor analysis showed that all items loaded above 0.5 on the predicted dimension, suggesting good construct validity. Moreover, the Cronbach alpha of the five-item scale (Table 6) was high (0.81), suggesting good reliability.

## 4.5. Analytical Procedures

Since the theoretical model hypothesizes a set of effects simultaneously, Path Analysis is a suitable technique for assessing its validity. In this study, Path Analysis was undertaken using EQS, a structural equations modelling package which is also used to study a simultaneous set of regression equations (Bentler 1989). Four aspects of the model were considered: the probability value of the chi-square, the normed fit index, the Lagrange Multiplier (LM) test results, and the Wald test results. Corroboration among these four aspects would enhance our confidence in the statistical conclusion validity of the study.

The probability value for the theorized model's chi-square statistic should exceed a standard cutoff of 0.05 if the model's fit with the observed data is to be considered acceptable (Bentler 1989). In addition, the goodness of fit of the theorized model is also assessed by a normed fit index such as that of Bentler and Bonett (1980), for which values greater than 0.9 are desirable (Bentler 1989). Moreover, as Anderson and Gerbing (1988) indicate, the theorized model should be compared with alternative models: a) a more constrained model, where some of the effects are fixed, and b) a less constrained model, where some of the effects that were previously fixed are now free to be estimated.

The Wald and Lagrange Multiplier (LM) tests (Buse 1982) implemented in EQS were used to make comparisons among alternative models. The Wald test assesses whether the decrease in chi-square is significant if some previously free effects are now fixed. The LM test identifies whether the decrease is significant if some previously fixed effects are now freed. Support for the theorized model is enhanced if both the Wald and LM tests do not suggest any modifications.

In this study, the sample size to parameters ratio for the model was approximately 5:1, which conforms to Bentler's (1989) recommendations. Moreover, given five composite variables in the research model and a sample size of 64, the sample size to variables ratio of approximately 13:1 compares favorably with prior research. For example, Tait and Vessey (1988) had a ratio of 7:1 (N = 42), and Kim (1988) had a ratio of 4:1 (N = 28).

For undertaking path analysis, another important issue to consider was the direction of causality. Experimental designs are usually more effective than surveys for studying causal effects (Cook and Campbell 1979). Although the data for this study was derived from respondent recall, some additional precautions were taken in this study design for facilitating causal interpretations, apart from their basis in the theory described earlier. First, both vertical and horizontal coordination were measured specifically for the initial (i.e., project planning and requirements analysis) stages of the project, which ensured that they preceded residual performance risk and project performance. In addition, performance risk was measured for both the initial and the later stages, to verify whether it indeed typically decreased during the project, as theory (e.g., McFarlan 1981) would predict.

## 5.2. Tests for Internal Validity

The internal validity of the study was an important aspect to assess; it considered whether the independent variables did indeed have the theorized effect on the dependent variables (Cook and Campbell 1979). Internal validity is enhanced if alternative explanations for the observed relationships can be ruled out (Cook and Campbel 1979, Mitchell 1985). Project size was a potential alternative explanation for the effects in this study. For example, large projects may have higher uncertainty and performance risk (McFarlan 1981), be more difficult to coordinate, and therefore perform less well than smaller projects. The logarithm of size (Dewar and Hage 1978)—specifically, the natural logarithm of the effort (in person months) required to undertake the project—was used as the measure for project size. However, when size was introduced explicitly in the revised research model, the Wald test suggested that its effects on residual performance risk and project performance were insignificant and could be dropped.

Because the data was collected based on respondents' recollections of the project, it was important to test whether their recall could distinguish between the various stages of the project. In particular, given the importance of performance risk to this study, it was necessary to test whether respondents indeed recalled that it decreased over the duration of the project, as theory would predict. A t-test suggested that respondents did perceive a significantly decreased performance risk between the initial and later stages $( t = 5 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ based on their recall. Moreover, the correlation between these two performance risks was considerably less than one $( r = 0 . 5 2 )$ ), and the initial performance risk was less strongly correlated with overall project performance than was residual performance risk (-0.26 vs. -0.53).

Overall, the lack of support for project size as an alternative explanatory variable, and the support for a causal direction to the effects enhanced our confidence in the internal validity of the study. However, additional studies focusing more closely on issues of causality, e.g., using longitudinal designs, are needed.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Contributions of the Research

In developing and testing the research model, this paper drew extensively from structural contingency approaches in Organization Theory and risk-based approaches in Software Engineering research. In doing so, the study provides a number of new insights into the theory and practice of software project management.

The Role of Residual Performance Risk. The introduction of residual performance risk, i.e., the difficulty in estimating performance consequences during the later stages of the project, makes two important theoretical contributions to research. First, it helps distinguish between project uncertainties (such as requirements uncertainty and technological uncertainty) and performance risks. While project uncertainties can be viewed as risk drivers or causes of risk, residual performance risk is the effect i.e., performance outcomes become difficult to estimate. In distinguishing conceptually between causes and effects, the research model can be subsequently expanded by incorporating other risk drivers such as uncertainties concerning resources (e.g., personnel and equipment). Moreover, the residual performance risk construct has broad applicability because it is defined generally, not in terms of any particular esti mation technique.

Second, the findings show that residual performance risk is an intervening variable which explains the effect of vertical coordination on project performance. Rather than have a direct effect on performance, vertical coordination affects residual performance risk which in turn affects project performance. Moreover, the strong negative effect of residual performance risk on project performance provides considerable support for the arguments advanced by risk-based researchers, i.e., lack of information about expected performance outcomes is detrimental to the actual performance itself. Conversely, the ability to estimate outcomes enables project management to better plan and execute the project's activities and assemble the needed resources. As suggested by risk-based research, an important goal of project management should therefore be to gain control over project risk by improving the ability to estimate project outcomes.

The Role of Vertical Coordination. Traditional structural contingency approaches view technology as deterministic, and emphasize only the coordination mechanism's capacity to process the information needed to cope with the project's uncertainties. By contrast, the research model in this study drew from risk-based approaches in also emphasizing the coordination mechanism's ability to reduce the amount of information to be processed, by reducing or eliminating some of the risk drivers. In particular, vertical coordination (at least through a project manager) had a significant role in reducing risk drivers such as requirements and technological uncertainties.

The findings reinforce risk researchers' arguments that a primary role for project management is to manage the performance risks in the project. This can be done either by risk avoidance steps that reduce information processing needs (e.g., by making requirements specifications more difficult to change, or by negotiating slack in the project) or by generating more information to resolve the risks that do get through. However, vertical coordination did not have a direct effect on project performance, which implies that it is important only to the extent that there is residual performance risk in the system. If such risk is already low, merely increasing vertical coordination does not significantly increase the performance of the project.

The findings support risk-based arguments that the role of a project manager becomes more important as the risk drivers increase. By contrast, structural contingency researchers would assert the opposite: vertical coordination through a project manager is more important for projects that are routine, more certain and therefore less risky; it should be less relied upon as the project becomes more complex, uncertain and risky. Overall, the results support the arguments made in risk-based research: because of schedule pressures that typically characterize software development projects, project coordination and control are of paramount importance. Vertical coordination ensures such control, particularly when the risk drivers are significant. A post-hoc correlation analysis provides some additional support: among the dimensions of project performance, vertical coordination was most highly correlated with process control $( r = 0 . 2 8 , p = 0 . 0 2 4 )$

The Role of Horizontal Coordination. The lack of support for a direct effect of horizontal coordination on residual performance risk is interesting. Research on user involvement and conflict in software development projects provides a likely explanation. Newman and Noble (1990) develop a two-stage model of user involvement, where the first stage is characterized by conflict development, and the second stage by its resolution. Perhaps horizontal coordination between users and IS staff is as much a source of conflict as it is of its resolution, and increased interactions could increase the range of options (e.g., user requirements) that need to be considered. As such, they may not make the task of estimating performance outcomes any easier. In any case, research needs to be undertaken to identify why increased interactions did not reduce the performance risk in the project

The direct positive effect of horizontal coordination on performance is in keeping with organizational (e.g., Sammons 1988) and IS research (e.g., Mantei 1981, Weinberg 1971). In particular, a post hoc correlation showed that horizontal coordination was significantly and positively correlated with the quality of user-IS interactions (r = 0.31, p < 0.05). However, horizontal coordination was unrelated to process control, which suggests that increased interactions between users and IS staff need not lead to a project that converges well. Horizontal coordination does seem to increase

the quality of the system that is eventually developed, as suggested by its significant and positive correlation with software flexibility $( r = 0 . 3 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Perhaps this is because increased interactions between users and IS staff lead to a wider and better range of options that are considered, rather than focussing only on those that meet short-term needs.

The Joint Effects of Vertical and Horizontal Coordination. The results also suggest that projects perform best if high levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination are present in a project, and that the two mechanisms of coordination are not alternative approaches. A significant positive correlation $( r = 0 . 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ between them reinforces this conclusion, i.e., high levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination should be present in the same project in order for it to be high-performing.

The two mechanisms of coordination also appear to affect performance differently. Vertical coordination increases performance indirectly by decreasing residual performance risk and its drivers such as project uncertainty, thus ensuring greater control over the process. On the other hand, horizontal coordination does not have any significant effect on residual performance risk; instead, it directly increases project performance by improving the quality of the user-IS interactions and the flexibility of the developed software. The lack of a hypothesized effect on residual performance risk suggests that much more research needs to be undertaken to understand how exactly horizontal coordination works. The findings suggest that its influence on uncertainty reduction, particular concerning requirements uncertainty, is much less than commonly believed.

These arguments suggest tentatively that vertical and horizontal coordination may perhaps be fulfilling the roles of ensuring convergence and exploration respectively. The subgroup analysis further suggests that perhaps high-performance information systems development corresponds to a cell where the demands for exploration and for convergence are both high. Because software development is typically an unfamiliar activity, there is need to sufficiently explore requirements and design alternatives by relying on increased user-IS interactions. At the same time, because projects typically have limited time and resources and are subject to many risk drivers, there is need to bring closure to the project by relying on a strong project manager or steering committee to avoid or resolve performance risks. The simultaneous need for such exploration and convergence could therefore increase with higher levels of project uncertainty.

## 6.2. Limitations

A potential limitation of this study is that respondents had to reconstruct their experience in order to complete the questionnaire. Such reconstruction could be subject to biases, e.g., the respondent may not remember accurately key aspects of the project, or may not have been able to discriminate between different stages of it— an issue of importance for measuring residual performance risk. However, a survey involving recall was the only viable way to collect and analyze large-sample data, short of collecting longitudinal data on a large number of projects.

The potential problems in recall were mitigated to some extent in this study because: (1) the DP manager of the project, selected as the respondent, could be expected to have a better overall understanding of the project than other project members; (2) the company liaison was requested to identify only recently completed projects for the survey; (3) user manager responses were also collected for a sample of projects in order to cross-validate the responses from the DP manager, particularly for critical aspects such as project performance; and (4) respondents clearly distinguished between performance risk during the earlier and later stages of the project, as discussed previously. Nevertheless, the causal links hypothesized in this study need to be further tested through more elaborate research designs such as experiments or longitudinal studies.

Another potential limitation of this study is that perceptions of both independent and dependent variables were obtained primarily from a single respondent, i.e., the IS manager for the project. Consequently, a series of tests based on a double-informant sample were conducted to reduce the likelihood of a method or single-respondent bias, including dropping two of the performance dimensions that had inadequate construct validity. An alternative approach would be to collect performance data from user managers also. While such access is typically difficult to obtain in a largesample survey, it would nevertheless reduce the need to undertake extensive preliminary analysis to assess and eliminate single-respondent bias.

## 6.3. Future Research

The goal of this study was to stimulate renewed interest in a problem that remains central to the Information Systems field, i.e., how can software development projects be coordinated more effectively in the presence of uncertainty? This preliminary investigation provides some interesting avenues for further research by considering the role of residual performance risk. However, a note of caution needs to be sounded: there is significant unexplained variance in performance, which suggests that factors other than performance risk may need to be considered in order to explain project performance.

The differential impact of the two coordination mechanisms on residual performance risk and project performance is an interesting area for further research. For example, the findings that vertical coordination is primarily linked to process control whereas horizontal coordination is linked to the quality of interactions and software flexibility needs to be explored further. Such research is promising because it is based not merely on information processing capacity, but also on reducing the organization's need to process information

The temporary nature of software development projects is an area of considerable theoretical interest. This suggests the importance of studying variations in structure, uncertainty and performance risk over the duration of the project. The seminal work by Goodman (1981) on the coordination, professional development, performance and innovation of different kinds of temporary systems (e.g., auditing teams, theatrical groups, R&D units) is an interesting area of exploration in this context. For example, Goodman recommends that a “blurred-role" management style emphasizing group participation be pursued initially when the task is being understood and terms of agreement are being formed, whereas a “role-clarity” management style emphasizing centralized control should be followed subsequently when tasks are being executed.

Also, we can expect that not all temporary organizations are the same (Bryman et al. 1987). For example, IS researchers have suggested the importance of time pressure (Alter 1978, Beath 1983, Charette 1989) in software projects. An interesting research issue is whether the degree of reliance on the different mechanisms of coordination varies with the extent of time pressure (or need for convergence) faced by project participants. Another interesting time-related research direction is the midpoint effect identified by Gersick (1988), i.e., project teams undergo a important transition in their work at the midpoint of the project when team members begin to pace themselves. The implications of this transition for project structure, uncertainty and performance risk should be interesting

The lack of theory-based and empirically validated constructs has generally been a problem with IS research on software development projects. This study developed and validated a variety of construct measurements derived from structural contingency and risk-based theory for the context of software development projects. Further exploration of these constructs would be interesting. For example, it is quite likely that performance risk is multidimensional. Are there performance risk dimensions that are currently not being tapped by this study? For example, an interesting area of further research would be to develop dimensions of performance risk that relate to project convergence and exploration.\*

## 6.4. Summary

In this study, a research model was developed to explain the effects of coordination mechanisms and project uncertainty on software project performance. The model was derived from structural contingency research in Organization Theory and riskbased research in Software Engineering. Residual performance risk was argued to clarify the relationships between vertical and horizontal coordination, project uncertainty and performance. The findings suggested that a revised research model had considerable support: while vertical coordination significantly reduced both project uncertainty and residual performance risk and thereby increased performance, horizontal coordination did not have any significant effect on residual performance risk. Instead, it had a direct, positive effect on project performance. The findings also suggested that high performing projects are characterized by high levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination. The research model provides interesting avenues for future research.
