# Reconciling Variance and Process Strategies for Studying Information System Development

Rajiv Sabherwal

Department of Decision Sciences and Information Systems

Florida International University

University Park

Miami, Florida 33199

Daniel Robey

Georgia State University

Atlanta, Georgia 30303

Information systems researchers commonly describe variance and process strategies for studying information system development (ISD) as alternatives that may be difficult to reconcile. In this paper, we argue that it is possible to reconcile these two strategies, despite the clear differences that exist between them. Some possible methods of combining variance and process strategies are examined, the most powerful of which jointly applies these strategies while maintaining their distinct forms. This method is used in this paper, with variance strategy being implemented using levels of participation of key actors and process strategy being implemented using sequences of actions. Based on empirical analysis of 50 ISD projects, five clusters of ISD processes are examined. Results show that projects that are similar based on levels of participation are also similar based on event sequences, thus indicating that variance and process strategies can be reconciled. The insights that variance strategy, process strategy, and joint application provide into each cluster are examined.

Systems often fail to meet their potential, and some result in catastrophic failures (Hirschheim and Newman 1988, Lyytinen and Hirschheim 1987, Robey and Newman, in press).

Behavioral research on ISD has focused broadly on the design, construction, and implementation of IS. This research has followed two paths. The more popular variance strategy concentrates on identifying the predictors of successful implementation. Prescriptions for user involvement, top management support, design quality, resource adequacy, and so on have been generated from this line of research (Swanson 1988). Variance research supports predictive models capable of explaining the

Institute for Operations Research and the Management Sciences variation in such outcome measures as project success, system success, system use, and user satisfaction (Barki and Hartwick 1994, Lucas et al. 1990, Robey et al. 1993, Tait and Vessey 1988).

By contrast, the process strategy examines sequences of actions occurring during ISD. The outcomes of ISD are explained as consequences of the preceding actions. Process research requires data about the dynamics of ISD, which may be obtained through longitudinal or retrospective research methods. Rather than “explaining variation" in outcome variables by identifying significant predictor variables, process research seeks to “explain" outcome states as the result of a preceding sequence of actions. Such an explanation may be obtained through small or large-sample studies that employ either qualitative or quantitative analyses. The results of process research contribute to the construction and testing of theories about the dynamics of ISD. These theories can focus upon social processes like conflict, communication, or learning, or upon alternative sequences of events during ISD. Outcomes, like successful implementation, can be explained with reference to the preceding sequence of events in these processes.

Mohr (1982) argues that variance and process strategies may be mutually informative if used together, but we are not aware of any studies on ISD that have formally investigated this issue. Even though many studies of ISD use a combination of research methods to obtain data, the combination of variance and process strategies has not been made explicit. Our dual objectives in this paper are to show that the variance and process strategies can be used jointly and to suggest the additional insights that may be obtained through their joint application.

## Variance and Process Strategies

## The Variance Strategy

Using the variance strategy, a researcher typically identifies independent variables such as user participation, top-management support, and the system's technical quality. The primary theoretical requirement is that such variables do, indeed, vary along some range of values. Independent variables are then causally linked to the dependent variables—such measures of success as system use and user satisfaction. Causal links between variables are examined using the amount of variation in one or more dependent variables that can be explained by a set of independent variables. The amount of explained variation is evaluated with measures of statistical association, such as the coefficient of determination (R²). Large samples ensure greater confidence in these estimates.

To illustrate the variance strategy, any number of the variables mentioned above could be related to outcome variables. We will focus on one set of independent variables, namely the levels of participation of users, top managers, IS personnel, vendors/consultants, and joint actors (including steering committees, task forces, etc.). Interest in the level of user participation during ISD originated in the literature on problems of implementing management science techniques (Churchman and Schainblatt 1965, Huysmans 1970, Radnor et al. 1970, Schultz and Slevin 1975). The effect of “client receptivity" on the success of implementation was evident in early case studies of management science groups (Rubenstein et al. 1967). As largescale IS became more common, the logic of client receptivity was extended to include the role of users in ISD (Ives and Olson 1984, Lucas 1974). Interest in user participation was sustained by the growth of end-user development during the 1980s (Munro et al. 1988). More recently, top managers, vendors, and consultants have become frequent participants in ISD. As champions of change, top managers signal the importance of particular projects, allocate resources, and help to quell resistance (Howell and Higgins 1990, Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991, Leonard-Barton and Deschamps 1988, Manz et al. 1989, Pennings and Buitendam 1987, Swanson 1988). The trend toward cooperative external relationships in ISD has also made vendors and consultants more active participants (Dearden 1987, Lacity and Hirschheim 1993, Markus 1984). Recent literature has also examined other external actors (Lederer and Mendelow 1990). For example, Scandinavian companies frequently include trade union members in IS development (Ehn and Kyng 1987, Mumford 1981). The participation of external regulatory bodies will also increase as safety-critical computing applications become more widespread (Jacky 1989).

The levels of multiple actors' participation may be logically connected with dependent variables of interest, typically some notion of system or project success. The relationship between the level of participation by each of the five types of actors and a dependent outcome variable can be formally posited as a functional relationship:

## The Process Strategy

In contrast to the variance strategy, the process strategy views ISD as a sequence of events that occur over time (Hirschheim et al. 1991). The interest in research on the ISD process was foreshadowed by work that distinguished among the activities in different development stages, such as initiation, design, and implementation. For example, Edström (1977) found the influence of several key actors (users, functional managers, system-development managers, and project leaders) to vary across six stages of the development process. Other research reporting results by stages includes the work of Ginzberg (1981a, 1981b), Olson and Ives (1981), Robey and Farrow (1982), McKeen (1983), Franz and Robey (1986), and Newman and Noble (1990).

Stage models are actually an advanced form of process model because they specify a definite order to the sequence of activities. For example, the Lewin-Schein model of change includes three stages—unfreezing, moving, and refreezing—occurring in that order (Lewin 1947, Schein 1961). The Kolb-Frohman consulting model identifies seven stages that are related sequentially (Kolb and Frohman 1970). As innocent as they seem, stage models place strict demands on a social process by hypothesizing that the stages follow a specific temporal order. Some writers have regarded such models as inappropriate, given rapid rates of environmental and technological change, and the changing roles of end users in the development process (Srinivasan and Davis 1987). Mohr (1987, p. 15) questioned the validity of stage models in innovation theory because: “. . . the innovation process has a frequent way of proceeding in some fashion other than that specified in any given model." In addition, by dividing action into temporal periods (stages), stage models mask the sequences of events that occur within each stage. Thus, stage models sacrifice detailed and potentially valuable information about events comprising the ISD process.

More recent process models circumvent these problems by adopting a more elementary form. They operate as "descriptive quasi-theories" (Mohr 1987, p. 29) which avoid strong assumptions about temporal sequences of events. Researchers define events as instances of social action that occur during the process being studied (Hirschheim et al. 1991, Sabherwal and Robey 1993). For example, Newman and Robey (1992) described ISD as a social process comprised of brief encounters, such as meetings and signoffs, and longer episodes during which the relationship between users and analysts remains unchanged. Their approach facilitates detection of interesting and nonobvious patterns of events in ISD (Robey and Newman, in press). Such process models do not divide a process into a priori stages, which enables the preservation of detailed information about specific events and their temporal order (Van de Ven 1992, Van de Ven and Poole 1990).

In contrast with the functional formulation of the research question described for the variance strategy, research questions for the process strategy are posed in terms of sequences of events. Instead of predicting outcomes conceived as variables (e.g., greater or lesser system success), process models consider outcomes as events that occur toward the end of a sequence. The use of the process strategy can be illustrated by a representative research question that links a particular sequence of events during system development to an outcome of interest such as system success. The traditional lifecycle model of ISD (Swanson 1988), for example, is prescribed because of its expected positive effects on project outcomes. A research question to illustrate the process strategy, therefore, is to state that system success is predicted to occur when the events comprising the ISD process occur in the order prescribed by the lifecycle model. This general question can be refined to focus on particular events, such as defining the scope of the ISD project. It could be hypothesized, for example, that the definition of project scope leads to project success only if it occurs relatively early in the sequence of events. Unlike the research question used to illustrate the variance strategy, questions involving the sequence of events in ISD are not posed frequently in the IS literature. Process research questions have only recently appeared in IS (Newman and Robey 1992, Sabherwal and Robey 1993), yet the establishment of empirical connections between sequences of events and successful outcomes would be worthwhile. We do not establish such connection in this paper, given that our objective is to reconcile the basic strategies of variance and process research.

## Reconciling Variance and Process Strategies

## Feasibility of Reconciliation

Mohr (1982) has argued that variance and process theories should not be combined because their theoretical forms are incompatible. In our examples above, variables (V's) and events (E's) are conceived in different ways, as are the outcomes $o _ { \nu }$ and $O _ { E } .$ Mohr's warning has led some to view the two types of theories as antagonistic and irreconcilable. However, his observation on compatibility does not eliminate potential reconciliation and the prospect of having the two strategies be, as he puts it, “mutually informative." In order to achieve reconciliation, we must understand the different ontological assumptions that each strategy makes. The variance strategy, as we have seen, construes social reality as a system of interrelated variables describing the states of different constructs. This fundamental ontological assumption differs from that made by the process strategy, which construes social reality as a sequence of events. Thus, variance and process strategies view the nature of the same reality in different ways. If they viewed the world in identical ways, it would be unnecessary to reconcile any differences. To use a statistical analogy, the results from the two strategies would be “perfectly correlated," and there would be no need for (or benefit from) reconciling them. It is only because the strategies make different ontological assumptions that we can benefit from reconciliation.

Even though the variance and process strategies view the social world differently, they share the epistemological assumption that the social world is objective and observable. That is, we can obtain valid measures of the social world by either measuring the variables that describe it or by measuring the sequence of social events. The sharing of the same epistemological assumption makes it easier for the results generated by the two strategies to be reconciled because reconciliation does not require the resolution of more fundamental questions about whether social reality is objective or subjective. Thus, to continue the statistical analogy, the variance and process strategies are not “perfectly uncorrelated" if they share the epistemological assumptions of positivism. Because they are neither perfectly correlated nor perfectly uncorrelated, it is feasible to seek reconciliation of the variance and process strategies.

## Methods for Combining Variance and Process Strategies

If reconciliation of the variance and process strategies is feasible, different methods for combining these strategies need to be identified and considered. An examination of the literature reveals several methods which may be used for combining variance and process strategies. One of these methods, the contingency approach, introduces contingency variables that determine the appropriateness of alternative process models (Goodhue et al. 1988, Newman and Sabherwal 1991). For example, Newman and Sabherwal (1991) argued that two variables—relative power of IS and users, and the extent to which users perceive the system as a threat—affected which of four ISD processes would be used. The contingency variables incorporated in this strategy are not attributes of the process itself, but instead represent antecedent conditions. Thus, in the contingency approach, both variance and process strategies are used but they are decoupled from one another. Variables are used to describe the conditions that trigger a particular process, after which the researcher employs a description of the process.

In some studies, the stage of the implementation process is used as the contingency factor, with the research variables being measured in a number of a priori stages (Robey and Farrow 1982, Zaltman et al. 1973). To illustrate, Zaltman et al. (1973) proposed that the relationship between organizational structure and innovation varies depending upon the stage of the implementation process. In the initiation stage of innovation, flexible structures lead to successful innovation while in the later implementation stage, mechanistic structures produced successful innovation. Thus, the stage within the innovation process is treated as a contingency variable that helps to account for the relationship between structure and innovation. However, Damanpour's (1991) meta-analysis of 23 studies on innovation failed to support such a proposition. The stage of innovation did not have the expected moderating effect on the relationship between innovation and any of its five determinants.

Some authors have used the causal ordering of relationships among a number of variables to represent a sequential process. For example, Lucas et al. (1990) conceptualized IS implementation as a two-stage process, beginning with management initiation and acceptance of an IS and concluding with user satisfaction with the implemented IS. They identified a number of factors variables relevant to these two stages, including attributes of the process, such as the level of user involvement. All variables are linked through a structural equations model. Lucas et al. (1990) argued that the "development of this structure also helps capture the dynamics of the process in terms of several key process attributes and relationships" (p. 90). However, their results only provide indirect inferences about processes because processes are operationalized as variables, not as sequences of events.

Finally, some authors have applied the variance and process strategies independently and then combined their results to obtain a more complete interpretation (Eisenhardt and Bourgeois 1988, Garnica 1981). This method may be illustrated using the study of politics of strategic decision making by Eisenhardt and Bourgeois (1988). Following data collection, one of the authors computed measures of variables, such as conflict, representing the decision-making process. The other author independently developed event listings for each of the eight processes studied. The results of the independent application of variance and process strategies were later combined and checked against the original cases. Eisenhardt and Bourgeois generated several propositions linking the variables in their study and used the processual details to explain and illustrate each proposition.

In this paper, we use this last method, joint application, for reconciling the variance and process strategies. This method is perhaps the most powerful way of combining variance and process strategies because it preserves the distinctive strengths of both strategies by using them independently before combining their insights. Reconciliation of the different strategies using this method does not blur the important distinctions between them and offers potential synergy in interpreting results.

## Benefits of Reconciliation Through Joint Application

The main value of reconciliation through joint application is the ability to address different, and more complex, research questions than those posed earlier. For example, the consequences of both the level and timing of participation by particular actors can be assessed by using variance and process strategies together. Successful ISD may, for instance, be hypothesized to depend upon high levels of top management participation, but only if it occurs early in the life of the project. A high level of participation by top managers early in the ISD process, i.e., during project definition and approval, may signal appropriate support to other actors engaged in the process (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991). A high level of participation by top managers later in the ISD process, i.e., during testing or training, may reflect serious difficulties. Likewise, user participation early in the ISD process may facilitate more realistic user expectations which, in turn may lead to greater IS success (Ginzberg 1981a). The level and timing of other participants' actions may also be related to the success or failure of a project. In these examples, the variance measure, level of participation, provides information about the extent of various actors' contribution to the overall ISD process, but it does not capture the order of events occurring over time. By contrast, event sequences capture the dynamics of the ISD process but leave out information about the amount of participation of various actors. Levels of participation and event sequences thus provide different insights into the ISD process.

Several IS researchers have provided indirect support for the expectation that levels of participation and event sequences in ISD may be interrelated. One argument is that actors' contributions should be sequenced to meet the needs of a project. Various actors—top management, users, IS personnel, vendors, and joint groups—possess different sets of knowledge, skills, and authority. The need for these actors to participate at any point in the project depends upon the preceding events. For example, top management may participate to a greater extent if the process is triggered by problems than ifit is not (Jarvenpaa and Ives 1991). Moreover, user participation in later stages of the ISD process is less likely if frequent performance problems have been encountered earlier (Hartwick and Barki 1994). A second argument is that the designation of a project as either user-led or IS-led may condition the ISD process to follow different sequences of events. For example, Newman and Sabherwal (1991) described ISD processes as IS-dominated and user-dominated primarily in terms oflevels of participation of IS personnel and users, respectively. But they found considerably different event sequences as well. In the IS-dominated process, system designers encountered user resistance and project redefinition by IS personnel whereas the user-dominated process involved user acceptance and turnover of dissatisfied IS personnel. IS-dominated ISD processes have often been found to encounter user resistance (Markus 1984, Newman and Noble 1990). User-dominated ISD processes tend to exclude physical design and implementation stages from the system life cycle (Athey and Zmud 1986, Doll and Torkzadeh 1989) and give less importance to documentation and security (Lederer and Mendelow 1990). Level of top-management participation also affects the sequence of events. A high level of top-management participation is believed to produce formal plans and agreements between IS personnel and users before programming begins (Doll 1985). Moreover, if the level of top-management participation is high, performance problems may lead to allocation of additional resources and further system development/modification, whereas if the level of topmanagement participation is low, performance problems may lead to project cancellation (Staw 1976, Keil and Mixon 1994).

Questions such as these are rarely posed in research on ISD, although they would seem to be important ones. Perhaps researchers have not considered these more complex questions as feasible within the confines of either the variance or process strategy. The primary advantage of reconciling these related strategies is to place such interesting questions within reach.

## Method

## Data Collection

Data for this study were drawn from descriptions of ISD projects in 50 organizations. Descriptions were generated by student groups as a written assignment in a senior-level university course in IS. Each student team was asked to interview a variety of individuals in a single organization and write the description of the history of the IS development process, including discussion of the process by which the organization decided to develop the system, the design and development process, the system implementation, and the way in which the system had been used. This data collection strategy employs hierarchically organized teams of multiple investigators (Eisenhardt 1989, Miles 1979, Smith and Robins 1982), which is appropriate for obtaining a sample size large enough to address questions about the participation of different actors in IS development. To compensate for the inexperience of the students as researchers, we used only the portions of the case studies that described events occurring during the ISD project. A total of 77 reports were prepared over two years, and 53 of these were initially considered to be detailed enough for this study. Complete details of the means of data collection are provided elsewhere (Sabherwal and Robey 1993).

Confidence in the student teams' reports was bolstered by our awareness of their relatively high level of business and IS experience. Upon collecting some background information from students enrolled in an undergraduate class similar to the ones in which the case studies were done, we found that 83 percent of the students had had some kind of work experience, and 68 percent had worked fulltime earlier. The average total experience of students who had worked earlier was 51 months, while their average full-time work experience was 39 months. Their most common titles were systems analyst, programmer/analyst, and computer operator. Thus, it is safe to assume that students understood the nature of systems work in actual practice. Confidence in the accuracy of the reports was also increased by the relatively recent implementation of the systems studied. The average time elapsed between the completion of system implementation and the start of the project study by the student team was 13 months. In 96 percent of the cases, system implementation had been completed two years or less before the start of data collection, and in 60 percent of the cases it had been completed less than a year before the start of data collection.

The descriptions of the ISD projects in each of 53 organizations were used as the source of event listings. To obtain an event listing for a project, the authors independently wrote down a list of events for each case, treating each event as a separate social action performed by a specific actor or by more than one actor acting jointly.

We emploved four specific rules for writing the events as (1) active sentences that (2) identified an actor and an action, (3) excluded reference to thoughts, worries or other unobservable behaviors, and (4) did not combine actions, using such phrases as "and," "following which," or "which led to," etc.

Rules 1 and 2 may be illustrated using the statement “Head of Microcomputer Systems assigned two programmers from his staff to work on the project." This statement represents an event because it is an active sentence which clearly identifies the actor (Head of Microcomputers, i.e., an IS executive) and the action (assignment of two programmers to the project). In a few cases, however, the students had written some events using passive voice wherein the actor could not be identified. These sentences were rewritten in active voice with the actor being considered missing. For example, the statement “A committee was formed to coordinate the efforts of the users in the implementation of the system," which does not specify the actor that formed the committee, was rewritten as “Someone formed a committee to coordinate the efforts of the users in the implementation of the system."

Rule 3 may be illustrated using the following two statements “The Executive Vice President grew impatient since the project needed to be completed or they would face losing this service and therefore millions of dollars in accounts. He demanded Senior Vice President of Bank Operations and Senior Vice President of IS to put all personal vendettas aside and do what it takes to get things accomplished."Of these, the first statement refers to the Executive Vice President's feeling of impatience but does not involve any action. It was therefore not considered an event. However, the second statement represents an action by the Executive Vice President, and was included as an event.

Rule 4 may be illustrated using the statement "The IS Manager submitted a formal proposal to the company's President, who approved it within a week."This statement refers to two separate actions, and so it was broken up into two sentences: “The IS Manager submitted a formal proposal to the company's President," and “The company's President approved the formal proposal within a week." These two sentences represent the two different actions (submission of proposal and approval/authorization, respectively) performed by two different actors (IS Manager and President, respectively).

After each author had compiled the list of events for a case, the two authors' lists were compared, and a composite list of events for each case was created. This helped ensure that the rules for identifying events were strictly followed so that no stated events were excluded and no nonevents (thoughts, worries, etc.) were included. One author combined the event lists for even-numbered cases while the other author did so for the odd-numbered cases.

## Data Analysis Strategy

Figure 1 provides an overview of the data analysis strategy, which included four components. First, following the variance strategy, the levels of various actors' participation were used to generate a taxonomy of ISD. Second, following the process strategy, the sequences of actions were used to assess the similarities and dissimilarities among pairs of ISD processes. Third, the reconciliation of variance and process strategies was approached by examining whether ISD projects within the same cluster show greater similarity than projects in different clusters. Finally, the benefits ofjoint application were assessed by examining the ISD processes within each cluster.

The level of an actor's participation in a given ISD process was measured as the proportion of the total actions which were performed by that particular actor. This measure represents the proportion of total events performed by a given actor. Zero indicates that the actor performed none of the events, and one indicates that the actor performed all of the actions. Intermediate values reflect the relative level of participation. Thus the level of participation is measured as a variable ranging from zero to one. Although this measure is obtained from the sequence of events, it is a ratio-scale indicator of the extent to which an actor participates in the process. This is similar to the variance measures traditionally used in variance research, which commonly utilize 5-point or 7-point Likert scales to measure the levels of participation of key actors, such as users and top management. Moreover, this measure does not include any processual information because it does not take into consideration the timing or sequence of the actions which an actor performs.

The levels of participation were used to generate a taxonomy of different ISD types The levels of participation for top management, users, IS personnel, vendors/consultants, and joint actions were included, but to avoid multicollinearity,2 the level of participation for external organizations other than vendors/consultants was excluded. The taxonomy of ISD was generated using Ward's (1963) method of cluster analysis, which is considered a useful technique for empirical classification (Lorr 1983, Ulrich and McKelvey 1990).3 To decide the number of clusters, the fusion coefficients at each agglomerative stage for each of the two linkage methods were examined (Hartigan 1975, Ulrich and McKelvey 1990). To examine the differences among clusters, we examined the means of the levels of participation for each of the five actor types—top management, users, IS personnel, vendors/consultants, and joint actors. We also conducted Duncan's Multiple Range Test (Snedecor and Cochran 1980) to compare these levels of participation across the groups.

Process Strategy. In this study, the process strategy treated each ISD process as a sequence of actions. Each action in the sequence belongs to one of.14 types; as mentioned earlier, the 15th category, “others," was excluded. If each ISD process was represented by a set of variables, as in the variance strategy, then the dissimilarity between two ISD processes could have been computed using traditional distance measures, e.g., Euclidian distance. However, such traditional distance measures cannot be used where ISD processes are represented as action sequences. Therefore, we used an optimal matching technique to measure sequence dissimilarity directly (Abbott 1990, Abbott and Hryçak 1990, Sabherwal and Robey 1993, Sankoff and Kruskal 1983).

Optimal matching is a dynamic programming technique for evaluating the distances between pairs of sequences when each sequence is represented by a string of well-defined elements, drawn from a relatively small total set (Abbott and Hrycak 1990). In this study, each action sequence was represented using a string of numbers indicating the action types. For example, a sequence SEQ1 including submission of proposal (action type 2), approval or authorization (type 3), project definition (type 4), assignment of personnel to the IS project (type 1), physical system construction (type 8), and reassignment of organizational roles (type 14), would be represented by the string 2, 3, 4, 1, 8, 14.

The distance of sequence SEQ1 from another sequence (SEQ2), including action types 2, 3, 5, 1, 8, 10, 12, is computed using the number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions required to transform one sequence into the other. One way to transform SEQ1 into SEQ2 involves four changes: substituting the third action (type 4) with another action (type 5), deleting the last action (type 14), and inserting two actions at the end (types 10, 12). If the “cost" of each insertion, deletion, or substitution, is assumed to equal one, this procedure, including one substitution, one deletion, and two insertions, would incur a total cost of four. An alternative way to transform SEO1 into SEO2 involves only three changes: substituting the third action (type 4) with another action (type 5), substituting the last action (type 14) with another action (type 10), and inserting one action at the end (type 12). This procedure involves two substitutions and one insertion, and costs only three. In order to compute the minimum intersequence distance between SEQ1 and SEQ2, we need to compute the costs of all possible procedures for transforming SEQ1 into SEQ2 and then take the least cost method. However, the number of such alternative procedures increases with the product of the lengths of the two sequences, and it would be extremely time consuming to compute the costs associated with all possible procedures for two long sequences. Optimal matching algorithms help solve this computational problem. Such an algorithm is available in the Beldings Program Series, which we used, and in most commercially available DNA-sequencing programs. We assigned all substitution costs equal to one, and all insertion and deletion costs equal to 0.5. These choices are explained in Sabherwal and Robey (1993).

In applying process strategy to understand the clusters, two sequences of events— a “centroid" sequence and an “ideal" sequence——were used for each cluster. The sequence approximating the centroid of each cluster was first identified. Within each cluster, the mean distance of each sequence from the other sequences in that cluster was computed, and then the sequence with the smallest mean distance from the other members of the cluster was used as an approximation to the cluster centroid. In contrast to these centroid sequences, which were actually encountered in thesample, ideal sequences are hypothetical sequences that best capture the common patterns among all of the event sequences in each cluster. Ideal sequences were identified in the following fashion. We visually inspected the various sequences within each cluster to identify the underlying structure (i.e., patterns that were common across sequences) in the action sequences. In doing so, we found it useful to spread the action sequences within each cluster across the page in such a fashion that the matching parts of various sequences were aligned vertically. Such visual techniques are suitable when each cluster has a small number of cases (Abbott and Hrycak 1990).

Reconciliation of Variance and Process Strategies. Empirical reconciliation of the variance and process strategies was assessed by comparing the proximities among sequences within the same cluster with the proximities across different clusters (see Figure 1). This was done using two tests. In the first test, we performed a t-test comparing the distance of each sequence from the sequence approximating its own group's centroid with its mean distance from the sequences approximating the centroids of the other clusters.4 For the second test, we first used the optimal matching technique to compute their distance from the 50 actual ISD sequences. A t-test was then used to compare the distance of each ISD sequence from the ideal sequence for its cluster with the mean distance from the ideal sequences for the other clusters.

Benefits of Combining Variance and Process Strategies. We assessed the benefits of joint application of variance and process strategies for each cluster. We first examined the insights provided by independent use of variance strategy into the levels of various actors' participation. Next, we examined the insights obtained independently through process strategy by studying the ideal action sequence for each cluster. Finally, we identified some insights which result from the joint application of variance and process strategies, but which could not be obtained through either strategy independently.

## Results

Variance strategy was represented by the levels of participation for the five actors— top management, users, IS personnel, vendors/consultants, and joint groups. Cluster analysis of these levels of participation using Ward's (1963) method found major jumps in fusion coefficients to occur for the five-cluster solution. The five-cluster solution was therefore selected.⁵ Three of the clusters included high levels of participation by joint groups (Cluster 1, labeled as “Joint Development"), users (Cluster 2, "User-led Development"), and IS personnel (Cluster 4, "IS-led Traditional Development"). The other two clusters are characterized by high levels of top-management participation. In one of these clusters (Cluster 3, “Top-down In-house Development"), vendors/consultants participate very little, while in the other (Cluster 5, "Top-down Outsourced Development"), they participate to a very great extent.

Process strategy was implemented using sequences of actions for each ISD project. The action sequences for the ISD projects in each cluster and the ideal sequence for each cluster are given in the Appendix. For each cluster, the intersequence distances produced by optimal matching were used to identify the actual “centroid" sequence which was closest to the other members of the cluster. The centroid sequences were useful in developing the hypothetical-ideal sequence for each cluster.

Comparison of the distance of each sequence from its cluster's centroid sequence with the mean distance from the centroid sequences of the other clusters showed within-cluster distances to be lower than across-cluster distances. A similar result was obtained when the distance of each sequence from the ideal sequence for its own cluster was compared with the mean distance from the ideal sequences of the other clusters. The t-statistics for both these tests (4.13 and 5.69, respectively) were significant (p < 0.0001 level), showing that the variance and process strategies may be combined.

For each of the five clusters, we may identify insights drawn from using the variance strategy (examining the levels of various actors' participation), the process strategy (using its ideal sequence, given in the Appendix), and the joint application of variance and process strategies. For each cluster, these insights are summarized in Table 2, and discussed below.

Cluster 1: Joint Development. Variance strategy indicates that this ISD process is characterized by a high level of participation by joint groups. The level of participation for the other four actors also exceeded 0.10, indicating that this process utilizes inputs from the various stakeholders. Process strategy reveals that in this process, personnel are assigned before any proposal is submitted, the scope of the project is defined early, performance is assessed periodically, especially in the latter stages of the process, and resistance is rarely encountered. Based on the joint application of the two strategies, the high frequency of joint actions may mean that the assignment of personnel early in the project represents the formation of a committee to manage the ISD process. Furthermore, the periodic performance assessment may mean that this committee tracks progress by participating in the evaluation of vendor proposals and later in the evaluation of the developed system. The joint nature of system development also explains why resistance is infrequent.

Cluster 2: User-Led Development. According to variance strategy, a very high level of user participation (more than twice that in every other cluster) is a distinguishing feature of this process. By contrast, IS personnel play a minor role. Process strategy shows that technical knowledge is sought early in this process (this action type does not figure in the ideal sequence for any other process), personnel are rarely assigned, performance is not explicitly evaluated, and the project scope is not defined. Moreover. acceptance is encountered during the middle of the process, but later, performance problems lead to resistance. Joint application suggests that the high level of user participation and the low level of IS participation may explain the need to seek technical knowledge/equipment. Users look for external help instead of seeking assistance from in-house IS personnel. The user-led nature of this process also explains why it does not include project definition, assignment of personnel, or performance evaluation. Users tend to be less concerned with such traditional practices.

Cluster 3: Top-down, In-house Development. Variance strategy shows that this process is characterized by a high level of top-management participation (about three times as much as in any other cluster, except cluster 5), but a low level of participation of vendors/consultants. Based on process strategy, the assignment of personnel is done early in this process. Authorization/approval did not appear on the ideal sequence, and the individual sequences in this cluster indicate that project is rarely approved early. Joint application reveals that the high level of top-management participation may explain why approval/authorization is not sought early in the process. The assignment of personnel early in the process explains the medium level of participation by IS personnel and users. Also, the apparently contradictory findings that a vendor/consultant is selected but the level of participation of vendors/consultants is low suggests that the vendor primarily supplies hardware or packaged software.

Cluster 4: IS-led Traditional Development. Variance strategy reveals that this process encounters a much higher level of IS participation than in other clusters. The level of participation of users is much higher than in cluster 5, but much lower than in cluster 2. Thus, the users actively participate in the process, but do not lead it as they do in cluster 2. Process strategy shows that performance is assessed early in this process, followed by the submission of a proposal. Later in the project, periodic physical system construction activities are interspersed with performance problems and reassignment of roles. Project definition is done after the selection of a specific vendor. Based on joint application, the high level of IS participation implies that the early performance assessment reflects the evaluation, by IS personnel, of the performance of the existing system or the feasibility of alternative new systems. The high level of IS participation also suggests that the periodic physical system construction activities may be interspersed with performance problems and reassignment of roles during later stages of the project due to efforts by the IS personnel to “fine tune" the system to suit organizational needs. Finally, the high level of IS participation may explain why project definition occurs late in this process. Apparently, project definition here is bottom up, consisting mainly of IS personnel specifying the system based on user requirements, in contrast to the early project definition in joint development (cluster 1) where a joint group seeks a more top-down project definition.

Cluster 5: Top-down, Outsourced Development. Variance strategy indicates that this process is characterized by high levels of participation for vendors/consultants (twice the level of participation compared to any other cluster) and top management (much greater participation than in other clusters except cluster 3). Process strategy shows that this process encounters multiple instances of approval/authorization, reassignment of organizational roles being done prior to physical system construction, and infrequent assignment of personnel. Joint application of variance and process strategies suggests that the high levels of participation of top-management and vendors/consultants may explain the multiple instances of approval. Apparently, the top management initially approves a project proposal from IS personnel or users, and later approves more specific proposals from vendors/consultants. This is in contrast to cluster 3, where high level of top-management participation makes explicit authorization/approval unnecessary. Moreover, the high level of participation of vendors/ consultants suggests that they participate in hardware/software installation, software development, training, and conversion, in contrast with other clusters where they primarily supply hardware/software. This also explains why in-house personnel are not explicitly assigned to these projects. Finally, the high levels of participation of topmanagement and vendors/consultants, along with the reassignment of organizational roles prior to physical system construction, suggests that the top-management frequently redefines roles to prepare the organization for IS development by external vendors/consultants.

## Discussion

The relative merits of variance and process strategies for studying ISD have been discussed in the IS literature for some time. Overlooked in most of these discussions is the prospect of magnifying our knowledge of ISD through the joint application of variance and process strategies. In this paper we have taken an initial step toward reconciling these two strategies. Despite their distinct theoretical forms, it is possible and beneficial to use variance and process strategies together. Support for this argument has been provided by first demonstrating a significant empirical association between the results obtained from each strategy. The variance strategy aggregated information concerning the levels of participation of five actors for each case and disregarded the nature of actions and the order in which they occurred. By contrast, the process strategy used information on actions and their temporal order, but did not utilize information on the levels of participation of the various actors. We found that cases with similarities based on the levels of participation also displayed similarities based on sequences of events. We conclude that the variance and process strategies reveal different but related information about the ISD process. This suggests that a reconciliation and joint application of the two strategies can be beneficial.

We then summarized the potential benefits from joint application of the variance and process strategies. For each of the five empirically derived clusters, we provided an interpretation based first on the information provided by the variance strategy (levels of participation) and second based on the information provided by the process strategy (sequence of events). Each of these interpretations omits the insights provided by the other. To demonstrate the benefits of joint application of both strategies, we also provided interpretations that could only have been drawn from using both together. We have shown that the levels of various actors' participation, obtained through variance strategy, may be used to explain the sequencing of ISD actions, revealed by process strategy. For example, if users participate actively in an ISD project, the ordering of events would be different than if they participated very little. Conversely, the sequences of actions (process strategy) help to explain why various actors participate at different levels (variance strategy). For example, if the scope of the project is defined early in the ISD process, users may not have to participate much in the overall process. By contrast, if the physical design and programming start without adequate project definition, users may have to provide frequent inputs during later stages of the project and their level of participation in the overall process would be consequently higher. Thus, using the variance and process strategies together can be mutually informative.

In contrast to most research on ISD that examines either the level or the timing of actor participation, the type of analysis being suggested here is one in which the proper timing of participation by an actor can compensate for a relatively minor level of participation. In other words, appropriate timing may reduce the level of participation required to obtain desired results. This sort of research question can only be addressed if the variance and process approaches are jointly applied. Insights into such questions cannot be derived from using either the variance or the process strategy alone.

It is relatively easy to illustrate the advantages of jointly applying the variance and process strategies when single cases are used. However, the advantages are not restricted to improved understanding of individual cases and should be extended to enrich the results obtainable from studies employing large samples.6 The variance and process strategies can be jointly applied in studies with large samples without investing excessive resources. For example, where research questionnaires have been designed to obtain measures of variables during face-to-face or telephone interviews, supplemental measures can be obtained by asking informants to provide a chronology of key actions that occurred during the process. Similarly, longitudinal research in which variables are measured at multiple points in time would benefit from studying, retrospectively at each data collection instance, the events that took place since the previous data collection effort. This does represent some additional investment of time by the informants used for data collection, but the benefits may be significant, as we have tried to demonstrate. It is usually more costly, and sometimes impossible, to gather process and variance data at different times, so our recommendation is that researchers evaluate their opportunities for collecting such data concurrently.

The argument advanced in this paper is not a simple plea for combining qualitative and quantitative data to enrich the findings of research on ISD. Both the variance and process strategies lend themselves to either qualitative or quantitative data collection. Our point is that data in the form of variables, whether finely scaled or representing nominal categories, can be used with processual data to address new and important research questions. The advantages we have described are obtainable with other types of measures than the ones we have used. Indeed, we advise researchers not to adopt our measurements without first assessing their limitations, as discussed further below. We advocate both creative and rigorous approaches to measurement of both variables and sequences of events.

There are three limitations of this study. First, the study is based on cases developed by a number of student teams, which is not the best means for collecting these data. We compensated for this limitation by using only the descriptive portions of the students' project reports, closely monitoring these student teams, and excluding 27 of the original 77 cases because they were not sufficiently detailed. Moreover, the results may have been affected by the differences among the students who collected the case data. Future research may use other methods to further investigate whether variance and process strategies to studying ISD can be reconciled.

A second limitation of this study is that variance and process strategies were both implemented using the same basic data, case descriptions of ISD projects. We did implement the two strategies independently, making sure to avoid any overlaps between them. However, the use of the ISD cases in implementing both the strategies may have caused the two sets of results to be more similar than they might otherwise have been. Future research may try to use different methods to collect the data for the variance and process strategies, for example, questionnaire surveys and personal interviews, respectively. Moreover, while we assumed all actions as being equally important in computing the levels of participation, other, weighted schemes may be examined in future research.

Third, this study is limited by our choice to separate actors from actions, which was done to keep the variance and process strategies distinct, and the use of variance strategy (rather than process strategy) to generate clusters. In addition, level of participation was used to represent the variance strategy. Further research is necessary to examine whether similar results are produced if other variance measures, such as involvement or commitment, are used instead of participation.

In sum, we have tried to emphasize that the differences between variance and process strategies are primarily ontological and methodological. Our empirical study has shown that these two strategies are not incompatible, but can be combined within the same study. Moreover, since each strategy has its distinctive strengths, combining them would provide considerable benefits. Therefore, we encourage researchers to use variance and process strategies together, while being mindful of their separate requirements. We hope that researchers will create opportunities for collecting both variance and process data and not view the two strategies as incompatible or irreconcilable. Combined use of the variance and process strategies can improve our understanding of the ISD process and provide a stronger basis for practical recommendations to improve that process.\*
