# Information Acquisition and Mental Models: An Investigation into the Relationship Between Behaviour and Learning

Betty Vandenbosch • Chris Higgins

Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, 10900 Euclid Avenue, Cleveland, Ohio 44106-7235
bmv@po.cwru.edu

Western Business School, University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada N5Y 3H6 chiggins@novell.business.uwo.ca

A cognitive learning perspective is used to develop and test a model of the relationship between information acquisition and learning in the executive support systems (ESS) context. The model proposes two types of learning: mental model maintenance in which new information fits into existing mental models and confirms them; and mental model building in which mental models are changed to accommodate new information. It also proposes that information acquisition objectives determine the type of learning that is possible. When ESS are used to answer specific questions or solve well-defined problems, they help to fine-tune operations and verify assumptions—in other words, they help to maintain current mental models. However, ESS may be able to challenge fundamental assumptions and help to build new mental models if executives scan through them to help formulate problems and foster creativity.

Thirty-six interviews with executive ESS users at seven organizations and a survey of 361 users at 18 additional organizations are used to develop scales to measure the model's constructs and provide support for its relationships. These results support the model's prediction that mental model building is more likely with scanning than with focused search. ESS also appear to contribute to mental model maintenance much more often than they do to mental model building. Without a clear focus on mental model building, it seems that business as usual is the more likely outcome.

(Mental Models; Learning; Focused Search; Scanning; Executive Support Systems)

The difficulty associated with providing executives with information to support their unstructured decision making has been a focus of MIS theorizing since the beginning of the field as a research discipline. Early writers such as Ackoff (1967), Dearden (1972), Gorry and Scott Morton (1971), and Simon (1977), discussed the issues associated with finding the appropriate balance between relevance and scope. Ackoff (1967) and Argyris (1971) outlined reasons for the difficulties developers have building systems to meet decision makers' needs. In the quarter century since then, technology has made great strides. It is now technically possible to provide managers with far more information in a far more flexible form than was possible in the 1960s and 1970s. However, the problems the early writers discussed have not gone away.

The mixed success that executive support systems (ESS) have experienced is a case in point. ESS are designed to "sit on top" of other organizational information systems in order to make the information the lower-level systems contain available to executives in a form that is easy to access, easy to use, and relevant to decision making. At the same time, they are billed as flexible tools that provide broad and deep information support and analytic capability for a wide range of executive decisions (Houdeshel and Watson 1987; Rockart and De Long 1988).

Data that populate executive support systems can come from a variety of sources including transaction processing systems, financial reporting systems, commercial data bases, and manual data collection. Information contained in these systems varies by organization and runs the gamut from finance through marketing and sales, human resources, manufacturing or operations, to external competitive benchmarks of various types. The reasons why and the ways that executive support systems are used are as varied as the executives who use them. Depending on their functionality, they can be used for performance monitoring, "what-if" analysis, problem identification and resolution, and information scanning. Some executives use their systems every day, or even several times a day, to stay abreast of current developments, typically viewing a consistent set of predefined reports. Some executives define new reports as the issues they face change and develop. Still others use executive support systems sporadically when they require a specific piece of information (Vandenbosch and Higgins 1995).

The business press contains many anecdotes about ESS successes (e.g., Appleby 1992, Hartlen 1991, Main 1989, Prendergast 1993). According to a 1993 Computerworld / Andersen Consulting survey (Maglitta 1993), executive use of desktop computers in the United States has nearly doubled in the four years between 1989 and 1993, increasing from 42 to 81 percent of executives. The market for ESS shell software grew much more rapidly: almost 400 percent between 1987 and 1991 (Gillan and McPherson 1992), and was considered to be the fastest growing business application on the market in 1990 (Economist Intelligence Unit 1991). Currently, ESS shell software is expected to grow 200 percent from \$100 million to \$329 million by 1998 (Kindel 1993). Despite this growth, more than half of the executives surveyed did not believe that their organizations were getting the most from their information technology investments. In addition, Glover et al. (1992) have identified 20 ways that ESS can fail. Clearly, the benefits to be derived from ESS development are far from certain.

We contend that one of the reasons why executives may find their ESS unsatisfactory is that they do not learn from them. Lovell (1980) argues that "all learning . . . implies the reception of new information, its retention over a period of time and its subsequent recall" (p. 22).

Huber (1991) believes that “an entity learns if, through its processing of information, the range of its potential behaviors is changed” (p. 89). Given that ESS are meant to provide executives with information, an appropriate measure of their success may be whether or not they help executives to learn.

The research described here was designed to examine learning in the ESS context. It investigated whether ESS enable executive learning, the character of the learning that is possible, and its antecedents. The remainder of the paper describes the research and its results. It is divided into three sections. In the first, the research model is derived. In the second, the empirical methods and results are described. Outcomes, limitations and contributions of the research are discussed in the final section of the paper.

## Research Model

A fundamental issue facing researchers of learning within organizations is the selection of the level of analysis appropriate to the investigation. One can investigate the phenomenon at either the individual or the organizational level. Proponents of the organization as the appropriate level of analysis believe that organizational learning is not merely aggregated individual learning (Argyris and Schön 1978). While organizations learn through the experiences of individuals (Senge 1990, Simon 1991), they have “memories” that endure beyond the tenure of those individuals (Walsh and Ungson 1991). However, the fact remains that organizations can only learn through the individuals within them.

It is particularly worthwhile to focus on senior executives' learning because they have the greatest capacity to affect their organizations' behaviours and thus, performance. Upper-level managers interpret information for their entire organization (Daft and Weick 1984). They influence their organizations by setting agendas for subordinates' activities consistent with their own personal problem formulations (March and Simon 1958). In addition, executives codify/reify those agendas and problem formulations by establishing and initiating structural and system changes to support them. Hence, this research focused on individual learning at the executive level.

Cognitive theorists believe that understanding the processing of the mind is fundamental to understanding human learning. They believe that human minds are capable of cognitive processing that mediates between environmental stimuli and overt responses. Behaviour is not a property of the stimulus, but rather, is something that emerges from the interaction of the stimulus and the active mind and memory of the recipient. For many cognitive theorists, a change in the potential for behaviour, rather than behaviour itself, constitutes learning (Lovell 1980). Others, however, believe that learning requires more than potential. For example, Norman (1982) defines learning as “purposeful remembering and skillful performance” (p. 3).

The separation between learning and performance often widens with the complexity of the material to be learned. In organizations, and particularly in the case of executives, opportunities to display learning through a change in behaviour can be far removed from the acquisition of information that prompted the change. Many variables may have to be manipulated before a change can occur and many factors moderate the ability to behave differently. As such, following Huber (1991) and Lovell (1980), learning is defined in this research as the potential for change in performance, rather than the behavioral change itself. The cognitive school of learning provides the theoretical base from which executive learning was investigated. A more detailed description of the theory and its concepts is discussed next. In particular, mental models (Craik 1943) will be explained in relation to individual learning.

## Mental Models

There are a number of overlapping constructs and terms to disentangle in relationship to mental models and cognitive theories of learning. Several early researchers laid the groundwork for the cognitive orientation (Leahey and Harris 1989). Tolman (1948, 1959) believed that organisms do more than react to their environments, they learn about them. For Tolman, learning consisted of building representations of the environment that are consulted prior to behaviour. He called these representations cognitive maps.

Bartlett (1932) proposed that memory is guided by a mental structure he called a schema, defined as “an active organization of past reactions, or of past experiences, which must always be supposed to be operating in any well-adapted organic response” (p. 201). The active nature of schemata, as he defined them, implies that they are emergent in nature, and constantly changing and developing in response to experiences (Boland and Greenberg 1992).

Craik (1943) described the workings of the mind in terms of mental models. He argued that reasoning consists of creating models in the mind that have similar relationships and structures to those of the external processes they imitate and then manipulating those models to arrive at conclusions about the external processes themselves. Einstein's thought-experiments provide a compelling example of Craik's concept (Leahey and Harris 1989).

The terms cognitive map, schema, and mental model are widely used in psychology to explain the workings of human memory. We will follow Brewer (1987) who sorts out the various concepts and distinctions among them, concluding that the term 'mental model' can be used to refer to "all forms of mental representation, general or specific, from any domain, causal, intentional or spatial" (p. 193).

A core premise of the cognitive school is that humans come to any task with mental models created out of their prior experience and understanding. These mental models determine how environmental stimuli will be interpreted and incorporated or synthesized, and even whether or not cues will be noticed and used. Mental models also make knowledge and information processing more efficient by making it unnecessary to construct understanding from scratch each time similar stimuli are encountered. They facilitate learning by allowing humans to fill gaps in both information and memory, and to construct updated models of reality.

Mental models are considered indispensable to information processing because they organize knowledge in simple, robust and parsimonious ways, in a world awash with information of staggering complexity (Walsh 1988, Stubbart 1989). However, there is a tendency for people to selectively perceive environmental cues that confirm their existing mental models (Festinger 1957). Consequently, misconceptions can persist and accumulate (Thomas and McDaniel 1990) until reality jolts perceivers out of their beliefs.

Mental models direct the gathering and processing of stimuli, and stimuli, in turn, help to enforce or change mental models. However, radical changes in mental models are probably rare. They are typically augmented rather than replaced outright (Collins and Gentner 1987). Next, we turn to how mental models are related to learning.

## Mental Models and Learning

A cognitive perspective implies a dynamic approach to learning. Learning emerges from the interaction of a stimulus and the mind of the learner, and results in a change to the learner's mental model. It is different from the developmentalist perspective which claims that the mind develops independently of external stimuli, and the behaviourist perspective which focuses predominantly, if not exclusively, on the role of the environment (Leahey and Harris 1989). Cognitive theorists are fairly consistent in their descriptions of the learning process. What follows is a brief description and synthesis of the theories of Maier (1945), Piaget (1954), and Norman (1982), some of the chief proponents of cognitive learning.

Maier (1945) describes two strategies for problem solving: reproductive and productive thought. Reproductive thought occurs when people encounter problems that are similar to ones that have been solved in the past. Typically, they apply previously successful strategies to solve recurring problems. Productive thought, and in Maier's opinion, learning, occurs when problems, or most of their elements, are new. In this case, people restructure and reshape previous experiences in order to solve the problems they face. When people use existing rules to solve a problem, they engage in reproductive thought, and when they tackle a problem that does not correspond to the rules in their repertoires, they engage in productive thought, and develop new rules to solve it. The new rules that result from this problem solving process are then stored for use in future reproductive thought (Lovell 1980).

Jean Piaget (1954) describes individual learning as a process of adaptation. Adaptation takes place when an organism is changed as a result of interacting with its environment so that further interactions benefit the organism. Adaptation consists of two interrelated components: assimilation and accommodation. Assimilation is the process by which the elements of the environment are changed in accord with, and are incorporated into the organism. In accommodation, the organism must adjust itself to environmental elements. Piaget claims both processes interact with each other and uses the analogy of food consumption to describe how they do so. The mouth changes the shape of food by chewing it in order to assimilate it into the body, but at the same time, the mouth itself changes shape to accommodate the food. In the case of information processing, assimilation incorporates information into existing cognitive structures, while accommodation rearranges, redefines or develops mental models to interpret and incorporate new or contradictory information (Flavell 1963).

Norman (1982) identifies three modes of learning: accretion, structuring, and tuning. He describes them as follows:

Accretion: Accretion is the addition of new knowledge to existing schemata. The framework exists, but new data are entered. Accretion is the most common mode of learning.

Structuring: Structuring is the formation of new schemata. The existing models will no longer suffice; new models must be formed. The structuring mode occurs infrequently and usually entails great effort and struggle. But structuring is probably the most important of the modes.

Tuning: Tuning is the fine adjustment of knowledge to a task. The proper schemata exist and appropriate knowledge is within them. But they are inefficient for the purpose, either because they are too general or because they are mismatched to the particular use that is required of them, so the knowledge must be tuned, continually adjusted to the task. Practice is one way of accomplishing tuning. It may take thousands of hours of practice to reach the stage of tuning that characterizes an expert. Tuning is perhaps the slowest of the modes of learning, but it is what changes mere knowledge of a topic into expert performance (81–82).

Table 1 compares the terminology used by these researchers (with the exception of Norman's tuning which is not likely to be facilitated by ESS). Maier's reproductive thought, Piaget's assimilation, and Norman's accretion share a great deal conceptually. Existing mental models are appropriate, or believed to be appropriate, to a given situation. New information fits into them, and confirms what was already held to be true. Consistent with the mental model terminology adopted here, the term 'mental model maintenance' will be used to describe this form of learning. Productive thought, accommodation, and structuring are also similar. They refer to the process of changing mental models to fit with new environments, or to handle disconfirming information. 'Mental model building' will be used to describe this form of learning.

Theories about learning in organizations state that, while both maintenance and building are important, maintenance is the more likely behaviour (Quinn 1980, Kiesler and Sproull 1982). Model maintenance implies that basic routines remain appropriate. In comparison, model building is much riskier and its benefits more remote. New ideas, markets, and products have much longer time horizons, more uncertain outcomes, and garner more indirect results than the exploitation of existing ideas, markets, and products (March 1991). Moreover, frameworks and criteria for their evaluation are lacking. Yet, without model building, entropy commences and the organization ultimately fails (Hall 1984). Indeed, competitive sustainability requires that organizations both explore the unknown and exploit the known (March 1991).

ESS should contribute significantly to managers' abilities to read their environments and manage their organizations' competitive performance. They should help to confirm managers' existing mental models, refute them, and encourage and facilitate the building of new ones. Since model maintenance most likely happens as a matter of course, organizations need to ensure that the conditions for mental model building are present (Miller 1993). ESS should be designed such that mental model building is encouraged, while making best use of executives' current mental models when they are appropriate. Hence, insight into factors that encourage these mental processes is fundamental to furthering our understanding of how ESS can contribute to improvements in organizational performance.

The processes of maintaining and building mental models are stimulated by the information that is encountered and perceived. Hence, the nature and quantity of that information would seem to have a significant impact on the potential for maintenance and/or building. In addition, it would seem that receptivity to new and unusual information could be gauged, in part, by the objectives driving its acquisition.

## Information Acquisition

Information can be acquired in two fundamental ways. Scanning (Aguilar 1967) is the behaviour people exhibit when they browse through information without a particular problem to solve or question to answer, while focused search (Huber 1991) occurs when people are looking for specific information. Both modes can be used for acquiring various types of information, such as facts about the external environment or internal operations.

Scanning is particularly important for senior management (El Sawy 1985). Daft et al. (1988) found that chief executives in high performing companies scanned more frequently and more broadly in response to strategic uncertainty than those in low-performing companies. They hypothesized that scanning and performance may be self-reinforcing, with broad scanning providing opportunities for improved performance, and improved performance providing slack resources for broad scanning. Thomas and McDaniel (1990) found that top management teams with a well-developed ability to access and analyze information have their attention caught by a wider range of variables when making decisions and taking action, and consequently, perform better. Eisenhardt (1989) found that people who are exposed to more information have more developed intuitions, and consequently, respond more quickly and effectively to the information they encounter. Thomas et al. (1993) found that executives who use relatively more information tend to interpret issues as positive for the organization and feel they have more control over them. They also found a strong positive link between a broad range of information sources and profitability. Miller (1993) argued that, in organizations in decline, executives tend to rely on highly formal, focused information systems and conduct relatively less broadly targeted, informal scanning. While most executives focus their scanning on external sources of information (El Sawy 1985), scanning can and should also involve searching within the organization to identify activities and situations that might influence future performance (Thomas et al. 1993).

Typical information dissemination systems are designed with focused search in mind. In many organizations, information systems are designed to make management and decision-making more rational by defining and controlling human performance within acceptable limits (Argyris and Schön 1978). Often, systems reduce discretion and limit intuitive behaviour. They promote rigidity and simplicity rather than flexibility and complexity because they define a view of the world that stipulates assumptions, environmental characteristics, relevance, lists of decision-makers, objectives, units of analysis, and so forth (Hedberg and Jonsson 1978, Miller 1993). It is the nature of most systems to reduce ambiguity and remove redundancy. They filter information and make it easier to absorb because the filtered information fits more easily within preconceived conceptualizations. An obvious trade-off, however, is that information redundancy may be key to mental model building, in that repeated perceptions lead to beliefs. Indeed, without repetition and redundancy, executives may not be able to distinguish between enduring and transitory environmental changes.

Depending on the nature of the implementation, ESS may be able to overcome the narrowness of traditional information dissemination systems. Many ESS go beyond status access to include querying and analytic capabilities (Silver 1991, p. 225) and thereby facilitate both focused search and scanning behaviours. Applegate (1989) describes ESS as having three major levels of functionality: the automated briefing book, exception reporting, and active exploration. The automated briefing book provides automated access to sequential displays of information organized by categories. These categories vary by organization, but typically include function, business unit, geography, time, and project. Given their routine nature, it is unlikely that these sorts of reports would elicit scanning behaviour, since executives are likely to develop set patterns in their ways of reviewing them. In exception-reporting mode, ESS allow users to “drill down” into areas where performance does not match expectations. If used to understand the specific source of an exception, this sort of behaviour would be classified as monitoring behaviour, but if the “drill down” is used more broadly (i.e., not in response to a particular exception flag), it can be considered scanning behaviour. Functionality to support active exploration (ad hoc queries and DSS-type analyses) can elicit scanning behaviour, but only if the ad hoc queries do not become routinized over time.

It would seem that when ESS are used to answer specific questions or solve well-defined problems that executives face in their internal and external environments, they are used primarily to control operations and verify assumptions—in other words, to maintain mental models. When executives are engaged in focused search, in most instances, they will see what they expect to see. Unless the information is wildly different from their expectations, mental model maintenance is most probable, and building is highly unlikely. However, ESS may be able to challenge fundamental managerial assumptions and preconceptions if executives browse through them without having specific questions in mind. In this mode, ESS may help formulate problems, foster creativity, and build new mental models, rather than to answer clear questions that have been previously formulated. El Sawy and Pauchant (1988) observed that there is close interaction between scanning and mental frames of reference shifts. Hence, it would seem that the potential for mental model building is greater when executives are employing scanning behaviour, since scanning implies that executives do not have clear preconceived notions of what they will look at or what they will find. However, if an executive is not receptive to new and unusual information, or if new information simply does not arise during the scanning process, mental model maintenance is the likely outcome.

Figure 1 illustrates the research model. It shows the impact of ESS on two forms of learning. Scanning behaviour will rarely be exhibited in the absence of focused search, but focused search may occur without scanning. When ESS are used for information scanning, both mental model maintenance and building are possible. When they are used for focused search, model maintenance is the more likely result.

## Methods and Results

The empirical research had two objectives: (1) to develop scales to measure the information acquisition and mental model constructs, and (2) to determine how these constructs were related to each other. These objectives were accomplished through 36 interviews with ESS users at seven organizations, and a survey of 361 users at 18 other organizations.

## Interviews

Interviews were conducted to ensure that the acquisition approaches and cognitive processes described in the model, do in fact, take place in organizational settings, and that they are measurable. Data collection and findings will be discussed in turn.

Data Collection. Potential sites were identified by asking ESS vendors and consultants to provide names of organizations with successful systems. Seven ESS were studied in detail. Two of the seven were among the top ten financial institutions in Canada. Four others were among the top three companies in their respective markets. The remaining company was a large government service. One system had been in place for a year, four for approximately two years, one for five years and one for more than eight years. Two of the systems used Pilot's Command Centre, three used Comshare's Commander, one system used Powerplay from Cognos, and one system had been built in-house.

Semistructured interviews were used to collect information from five or six executive users in each company. Each interview lasted from 45–90 minutes and was taped for subsequent analysis. Twenty-nine of the

36 interviews were conducted in person. In seven instances, because of the geographical and time constraints of the interviewees, interviews were conducted by telephone. The mean age of the respondents was 47, and the mean number of layers to the president was 0.96. The most senior executives of four of the seven organizations participated in the research. The mean tenure in the company was 15.8 years and the mean tenure in the job was 4.2 years. The respondents had a wide range of system usage varying from no use at all to 3.5 hours per day.

Focused search and scanning were investigated through questions about how the ESS was used, and during all the in-person interviews, through demonstrations of the ESS. Given their abstract nature, it was not possible to ask executives explicitly about the effect that the ESS had on their mental models. Consequently, a number of questions were developed to help understand the impact of ESS on them. Questions used to determine if mental model building had taken place attempted to determine if the ESS was helping executives create, innovate, and construct new concepts and ways of seeing their businesses and environments. They asked if the ESS contained surprises, ambiguities, and anomalies. If the ESS resulted in business as usual, and had no impact on ideas or behaviour, it was considered to be, at best, maintaining mental models. Appendix 1 contains the questions that were asked to start discussion in the interviews.

Data Analysis. Data were analyzed following the Reich and Benbasat (1990) methodology. First, the interview data were transcribed onto worksheets. Each comment made by an executive user was analyzed to determine if it related to his or her general approach to information, reason for use, type of use, model maintenance, or model building. The transcripts were coded by the principal researcher according to whether or not the themes under investigation were present, rather than how often they were raised (Berelson 1954). Then the summaries were interpreted to determine the absence or presence of the constructs.

Interview Findings. In total 30 of 36 interviewees (83%) exhibited focused search behaviour, 9 (25%) exhibited scanning behaviour, 29 (80.5%) maintained their mental models using the ESS, and 2 (6%) demonstrated mental model building. For the most part, the relationships among the constructs that were put forward in the research model were supported. Scanning was only observed in conjunction with focused search. Not a single individual exhibited scanning behaviour exclusively. The relationship between focused search and mental model maintenance had almost a one-to-one correspondence. If the system was used, it was used for focused search; and if it was used for focused search, the executive's existing mental model was maintained. The limited number of instances of mental model building, while not surprising theoretically (March 1991, Collins and Gentner 1987), precluded the development of strong support for the relationships involving this construct; however, no disconfirming evidence was found. In support of the research model, the two mental model builders both exhibited ESS scanning behaviour.

## Survey

After the interviews were completed, a survey was designed and administered to validate the constructs and test the research model. Survey methodology was chosen for this phase of the research for three reasons: 1) it would permit triangulation by providing quantitative data to augment the qualitative results of the interviews; 2) it would provide statistical evidence about construct reliability and validity; and 3) given the interview results, the theory seemed strong enough to ensure that the results would be meaningful.

Scale Development. Developing reliable and valid scales for the model's constructs was the major challenge of the survey phase. Consequently, the first seven steps of Churchill's (1979) paradigm for developing measures were followed. First, an initial list of items for each construct, based on the literature used to develop the model and the interview transcripts, was developed. A sorting exercise was then undertaken to determine if the items had face and discriminant validity. The items were put onto cards, one item per card, and randomly shuffled. Five academic colleagues independently sorted them into groups that "made sense." Any items that were incomprehensible or did not fit were to be kept separate. Five more colleagues were each provided with the same stack of items as well as the names of the constructs they were intended to represent. These sorters were asked to group the items into the constructs as they felt appropriate, and once again, to hold aside incomprehensible items and misfits. There were no major differences between the groupings created by the sorters who had construct names and those who did not.

The results of the sorting exercise were used to select scale items as follows: any item that was not included in the correct construct at least 70 percent of the time was discarded. In addition, if an item was included in the wrong construct more than once, it was also discarded. Finally, any item that did not end up where the researcher had predicted it would was discarded.

A pilot survey consisting of the items that survived was then developed. Questions were asked using 7-point Likert scales. Anchors for the information acquisition scales were “never” and “regularly.” Anchors for the mental model scales were “to no extent” and “to a great extent.”

Pilot data collection was conducted by asking the interview participants to complete the preliminary survey instrument. The rationale for asking them was that they were committed to the project and would be willing to test an instrument that was not yet stable. Each of the 36 users who had been interviewed in the first phase was sent a letter outlining the purpose of the pilot and a copy of the pilot questionnaire. Thirty surveys were returned for a response rate of 83 percent.

Measure purification involved computing Cronbach's alphas for all of the constructs, and inspecting the correlation matrices of the mental model maintenance and mental model building constructs. The specific questions for items that performed poorly were then inspected and discussed with colleagues. Appropriate changes were made, and the revised constructs were used in the main survey instrument. (Appendix 2 contains the final survey items.)

Data Collection. As with the interviews, contacts were made with ESS software vendors and consultants to obtain the names of companies with ESS. In addition, the researcher obtained a mailing list from an ESS conference organizer. In contrast to the interviews, no attempt was made to find successful ESS. Fifty-two organizations were contacted about participation in the survey. Of these, six did not have ESS, ten had systems that were still in development, six had dismantled their ESS, nine could not commit to sufficient participation, and three indicated that timing was poor. Eighteen organizations agreed to participate. They came from six industry groups: two from natural resources, four from transportation and communications, six from financial services, two from the food industry, two from manufacturing, and two from the public sector. Three organizations were based in the United States, eight in Ontario, four in Eastern Canada, and three in Western Canada. The mean number of employees was 11,000, and only two organizations had less than 1,500 employees.

The ESS ranged in age from six months to four years, with a mean age of 2.3 years. They had a range of 9 to 200 users with a mean of 71. Four systems were implemented using Comshare's Commander, two using Pilot's Command Center, two using Pilot's Lightship, and the remainder using other software products or in-house development. Impetus for development came from three sources: senior line management (3), the IS shop (8), and Finance and Accounting (7).

Six hundred twelve surveys were sent out, and 415 people replied for a response rate of 67.8 percent. Of the 415, 36 provided reasons for why they were not appropriate respondents, 6 indicated they were too busy, and 12 were not interested. Thirty-four of the completed questionnaires had a large number of missing values, leaving 327 usable responses. $^{1}$

The mean age of the respondents was 43, and the mean number of layers to the president was 1.92. The mean tenure in the company was 14.3 years, and the mean tenure in the job was 3.9 years. The respondents had a wide range of system use varying from no use to six hours per day. The mean daily use was 0.62 hours with a standard deviation of 0.71.

Survey Findings. The theories used to support the model and the findings from the case studies indicate that focused search is more usual than scanning, and that mental model maintenance is more usual than mental model building. Paired T-tests were performed to determine if these findings held for the survey respondents as well. Means of the scale items $^{2}$ were calculated for focused search, scanning, mental model maintenance, and mental model building. The parallel construct means were then compared to each other: focused search to scanning, and mental model maintenance to mental model building. In both cases, the expected relationship between the constructs was observed. Focused search had a construct mean of 4.23 while scanning had a mean of 3.83 (significant at $p \leq 0.001$ ). Mental model maintenance had a construct mean of 4.01 and mental model building a mean of 3.57 (significant at $p \leq 0.001$ ).

Partial least squares analysis (PLS) (Lohmoller 1981, Wold 1985) was used to test the model. PLS is a theory-based approach to conceptualization that has been designed to integrate theory and data, and hence, provides a better platform than traditional multivariate techniques from which to construct and verify theory (Fornell 1982, 1984). In the absence of causal modelling, all that can be inferred from survey data are descriptions and correlations. With causal modelling, there is the potential to infer causation with a limited amount of confidence. Another major strength of PLS arises from the fact that theory and data take meaning from each other. Hence, the reliability and validity of the constructs can be verified in light of the theory's nomological network and assessed simultaneously with the estimation of the relationships among the constructs.

Although the measurement and structural parameters are estimated together, a PLS model is analyzed and interpreted in two stages: (1) the assessment of the reliability and validity of the measurement model, and (2) the assessment of the structural model. This sequence ensures that the constructs' measures are valid and reliable before attempting to draw conclusions regarding the relationships among the constructs (Barclay et al. 1995). Hence, the results of the research will be discussed in two parts: first, the quality of the construct measurement, and then, the strength of the relationships in the model.

## Measurement Model

Item Trimming. As a first step, individual reliabilities for the constructs in the model were assessed. A common rule of thumb for the minimum criteria for acceptance of an item is that it has a loading of greater than 0.7 on its respective construct, implying that more than half of the observed variance of the variable is explained by the construct it measures (Carmines and Zeller 1979). The initial factor structure was checked to see if all the items had loadings of greater than 0.7 on their respective constructs, and if they loaded more on the constructs they represented than on the other constructs in the model (Table 2).

For the most part, the initial factor structure indicated that the items met the criteria. However, a few problem items were discarded as discussed below.

Focused Search and Scanning. Focused search had one item that loaded poorly: I5-take the same path through the system. Scanning also had one item that loaded poorly: I10-look at information as the mood strikes me. It seems that I5 and I10, which in many respects are the antithesis of each other, tap behaviours that are not relevant to either scanning or focused search.

Mental Model Maintenance and Mental Model Building. While these two constructs had high loadings, they also had high cross-loadings. Items were removed from each construct that, after reexamination, appeared to overlap with each other. In the mental model maintenance construct the potential overlaps were: M1- confirm your understanding of the business, M7-stay close to the business, and M8-increase your focus. In the mental model building construct they were: M12-improve your insight into the business, M13-broaden your outlook, and M17-test your assumptions. In four of the six cases (M7, M8, M13, M17), the items were not linked closely enough to the concept of mental models. For example, one could conceivably stay close to the business, and still build a new mental model; or one could broaden one's outlook, but maintain existing mental models. It also seemed that M1 and M12 could be causing some of the problems with discriminant validity because of the popularity and consequent lack of meaning associated with the word "insight." While "insight" means "the capacity of understanding hidden truths etc., especially of character or situations" (Concise Oxford Dictionary, 612), and clearly relates to the concept of model building, the common usage of the word may be sufficiently similar to "understanding" thereby making the two scale items indistinguishable from each other.

The PLS model was rerun with the changes indicated above. Table 3 shows the revised measurement model's factor structure.

## Discussion

Both the interviews and the survey provided strong support for the research model and help us to understand how ESS foster learning and the nature of the learning that is possible. In the interviews, focused search was clearly linked to mental model maintenance. The relationship between scanning and mental model building could not be confirmed, but had anecdotal support. The survey provided substantive, statistically significant evidence of the relationships in the model. The data showed strong relationships between focused search and mental model maintenance. Also, as predicted by the model, the relationship between scanning and mental model building was substantially stronger than the relationship between scanning and mental model maintenance. In addition, focused search had a stronger relationship to mental model maintenance than did scanning. Finally, the relationship between focused search and scanning was also as expected in both the interviews and the survey. It would appear that executives are more inclined to use their ESS for scanning if they also use them for focused search. The interviews were with senior executives, while the survey included both senior executives and some middle level managers. While the relationships were directionally the same regardless of position in the organization, they appear stronger for middle managers. It may be that senior managers, either because of their positions or their personalities, are more likely to engage in mental model building in general, while lower level managers receive more stimulation from information scanning.

The results regarding the relationships between scanning and focused search and mental model maintenance and building respectively in combination with the relationships among the model's constructs provide an explanation of how information supports the learning process, and provides additional support for the proposition that higher order learning is difficult to attain. It also provides a reason that might partially explain why. Information scanning is more likely to lead to mental model building than to mental model maintenance, but it is not as frequent as focused search.

The obvious question, of course, is what determines whether focused search, scanning, or both are employed. Looking at the means for scanning and focused search by company provides some insight. The three ESS that had the highest mean scores for focused search all provided daily information on key operating metrics. However, they did not permit the integration of information from different sources. Two of the three ESS that had the highest scores for scanning were extremely flexible, allowing managers to select information from various places to come up with new reports and new metrics. The third high-scanning ESS provided daily updates to key information as well as over 800 different reports. The three ESS that performed poorly did so on both scanning and focused search. One was slow and awkward to use, one provided only budget information and the third provided only financial and project status information.

On a case by case basis, it is easy to see why some ESS elicit particular information acquisition behaviours while others do not. However, a formula for the appropriate combination of factors is not evident from these results and a meaningful evaluation of the interactions among various ESS characteristics would require a sample that is far larger than the 18 ESS that were investigated in the survey phase of the research. Nevertheless, it would seem that companies hoping to increase scanning behaviour through the use of their ESS need to provide their managers with flexible capabilities and a wide variety of information, not just standard financial reports.

A major implication of the research is that ESS contribute to mental model maintenance much more often than they do to mental model building. While some individuals that were studied engaged in mental model building, they were less common than mental model maintainers. A much more frequent outcome was that ESS increased information velocity and improved the timeliness of decision making. Hence, organizations should think carefully about their expectations vis-a-vis their ESS, and whether or not they can be met. Without a clear focus on learning, it seems that business as usual is the more likely outcome.

Finally, it is important to remember that while scanning and focused search and mental model maintenance and mental model building have been conceptualized as bipolar constructs, they are probably continuums. For example, issue tracking is a mode of information acquisition that probably fits somewhere in the middle.

## Strengths and Limitations

Two strengths of the work merit discussion: 1) the interplay between qualitative and quantitative methodologies and 2) the high quality of the respondents.

This research provides both qualitative and quantitative evidence of the relationships under investigation. The interplay between the two methods strengthens the conclusions of both by providing the context within which to understand the quantitative results and quantitative results to support the qualitative findings.

A summary of the organizations and individuals who participated in this research points to a particular strength of the work: the high quality of the informants. In total, 25 ESS in 25 different organizations were investigated. More significant however, are the user respondents. Three hundred and sixty-three users participated in the research. On average, they were about two levels away from the president of their respective organizations, and only a handful were more than three levels away. No other studies have been found in which such a large group of executives has participated in MIS research that relates so directly to their particular information needs.

The research is not without limitations, however. The first relates to its organization bias, and therefore, to its external validity. While attempts were made in the survey phase of the research to include organizations with ESS of varying quality, it seems likely that organizations that are unhappy with their systems would be less prone to participating in research of this nature. Hence, this sample of ESS probably contains a larger proportion of “good” systems than is the case in the population of all ESS. As such, there are probably more instances of the model’s constructs than in the population of ESS in general. It would seem that both scanning and mental model building are difficult to encourage.

A second limitation relates to the notion of causality. Both phases of this research were cross-sectional in nature. Hence, while evidence of causality was provided, causality itself was not proven. Since learning implies a notion of time, this may be one of the reasons why so few mental model builders were found in the interviews. Executives may remember sudden flashes of insight, but not a slower evolution of their mental models.

A third limitation relates to common method variance and same source bias. While different methods were used in the research, each relied on user perceptions and on one principal method to elicit information. Unfortunately, the information acquisition and mental model constructs would have been difficult to obtain for large numbers of respondents in any other way.

A final limitation is related to construct development. A single data collection was used to purify the measures and test the structural model in the survey data analysis. A second data collection would have been more convincing. However, a sample of senior managers in large organizations is difficult and expensive to collect. It was felt that changes to the measurement model were not sufficiently substantive to warrant the use of the resources required for a second data collection.

Limitations notwithstanding, the research provides a strong confirmation of the research model. The strength of the results and their consistency between the two phases encourage its adoption as an appropriate depiction of the relationships among information acquisition approaches and learning.

## Avenues for Future Research

The strong relationship that this research found between scanning and mental model building should encourage the development of a deeper understanding of what it takes to develop systems that stimulate scanning behaviour and consequently, mental model building. As with most technology, it is likely that individual differences and organizational contextual factors as well as system characteristics play a role in determining whether or not scanning will take place. For example, those more innovative and more tolerant of ambiguity may be more likely to scan generally than those who are more adaptive by nature. Systems that are more flexible and provide an array of information from unusual sources and that frequently change the variables that are monitored may be more likely to elicit scanning than those that limit themselves to providing standard reports more quickly. Organizations with a culture including exploration may be more likely to encourage scanning than organizations that have a focus on efficiency. Organizations operating in uncertain environments might also result in more scanning behaviour on the part of their executives than organizations that are in more stable environments. Clearly, which factors are important and how they interact needs to be explored. In particular, it is important to understand whether or not it is possible to encourage scanning and mental model building in executives who do not have predispositions toward these behaviours and cognitive processes. Theory development and empirical research in this area is necessary to understand the factors that may unlock them.

While the focus of this research was on ESS, understanding the relationships among information acquisition approaches and executive learning may be transferable to other technology contexts and other information sources. Certainly, the results are consistent with those found by El Sawy and Pauchant (1988) in a nontechnology context. In their study of the interplay between scanning and mental model building (frame of reference shifts in their terminology), they found evidence that environmental scanning can cause mental models to change. Further research into the interaction between the nature of information that is encountered and the qualities of mental models themselves could help us to understand the sources of new ways of thinking that often differentiate the successful from the unsuccessful.

This leads to another avenue for future research: how mental model maintenance and mental model building affect individual and organizational performance. A great deal of work in the strategy literature has investigated the relationships between cognition and organizational performance (e.g., Huff 1990). However, more work is required to understand the relationship between model maintenance, model building, and individual and organizational performance. Since superior organizational performance is likely dependent upon management's ability to learn and make sense of uncertain and ambiguous competitive environments (McCaskey 1982, Thomas 1988), mental model building should be expected to support improvements in performance. Nevertheless, there is also evidence that a tension exists between efficiency and innovation. March (1991) typifies learning as the exploitation of old certainties and the exploration of new possibilities. He believes that "maintaining an appropriate balance between exploration and exploitation is a primary factor in . . . survival and prosperity" (p. 71). Balancing mental model maintenance and mental model building may be key to managing that tension (Simons 1994).

The results of this research into ESS and the influence they have and can have on learning should help executives and organizations understand and evaluate the potential these systems have for improving organizational performance. These results can also help them develop systems that are better able to support executive learning if they heed the requirement for information scanning. Finally, these results may encourage the education of executives in how to use ESS—not just how to access them, but also how to retrieve, think about, and use the information they contain. $^{3}$
