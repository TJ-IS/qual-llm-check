# Process Structuring in Electronic Brainstorming

Alan R. Dennis • Joseph S. Valacich • Terry Connolly • Bayard E. Wynne
Department of Management, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602
adennis@uga.cc.uga.edu

Decision and Information Systems Department, School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405 valacich@ucs.indiana.edu

Department of Management and Policy, College of Business, University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721
connolly@u.arizona.edu

Decision and Information Systems Department, School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405

One aspect of brainstorming that has received little research attention is how the brainstorming problem should be presented to the group, whether as one all-encompassing question or as a series of separate questions each focusing on one aspect of the problem. This paper reports the results of two experiments in which subjects (MBAs in the first, senior executives in the second) electronically brainstormed on intact problems (where all parts of the problem were presented simultaneously) or on decomposed problems (where three subcategories of the problem were sequentially posed to the groups). In both experiments, groups using the decomposed process generated 60% more ideas. We attribute these differences to the ability of time constraints to increase the rate of idea generation, and the ability of problem decomposition to refocus members' attention more evenly across the entire problem.

(Group Support Systems (GSS); Electronic Brainstorming; Decomposition; Structure)

## 1. Introduction

As a technique for stimulating idea-generation, group brainstorming has had a rather checkered history. The original proposal by Osborn (1953) was that groups would produce more and better ideas than individuals working alone. This claim fared poorly in empirical tests (see Mullen, Johnson and Salas 1991, for a meta-analysis). But this picture has changed dramatically with the development of group support systems that use electronic brainstorming (EBS) as an early phase of problem solving (see Jessup and Valacich 1993, for a survey). These EBS systems allow group members generating ideas to interact anonymously and draw stimulation from one another's ideas without incurring "production blocking" penalties (the enforced silence of all but one group member at any given moment in verbal or oral brainstorming).

The superiority of electronic brainstorming over non-electronic brainstorming has been clearly demonstrated. Recent studies have found electronic brainstorming groups to generate more ideas than verbally interacting groups and more than nominal groups for larger group sizes (Dennis and Valacich 1993; Gallupe et al. 1991; Gallupe et al. 1992; Valacich et al. 1994). The principal reason for this superiority is the reduction of production blocking in EBS groups (Gallupe et al. 1994; Valacich et al. 1994).

At this point, research that focuses on ways to improve electronic brainstorming is needed. One aspect of brainstorming—both electronic and nonelectronic—that has received little research attention is how the problem is posed to the group. Rosch (1978) theorizes that most problems have a hierarchical, or tree-like, structure. The highest element in the structure, or its root, is called the “superordinate” level. This superordinate level can be decomposed into a set of more specific categories called the “basic” level, which in turn can be decomposed into even more specific categories called the “subordinate” level. Of course, these levels are relative, as one level in an organization may view the superordinate level as the highest level, while another, higher, organizational level may see it as simply a subordinate level in a more complex problem.

The question here is which leads to more ideas, posing a problem to a brainstorming group at the highest level as one all-encompassing question, or at the basic level as a series of distinct questions each addressing one subcategory of the overall problem? We report two experiments testing whether electronic brainstorming produces more ideas when the problem is presented as one all-encompassing question or as a series of questions addressed in turn.

## 2. Theory and Research

Group brainstorming is influenced by both cognitive phenomena within individual group members and social (or group) phenomena (Nagasundaram and Dennis 1993). While social phenomena have tended to dominate previous research, we believe that cognitive factors are also important. We first consider cognitive phenomena and then social factors that arise from group interaction. We also consider how time may play a role in influencing the generation of ideas.

## 2.1. Cognitive Factors

Theory suggests that when presented with an all-encompassing problem, individuals will produce a set of related ideas focusing on only a small set of problem subcategories. Anderson (1983, 1987) argues that cognitive behavior is controlled by production rules that specify the steps of cognition and produce ideas when activated. Rules are activated automatically by input stimuli, without conscious control (Anderson 1992). For any given stimulus, there are several rules that could be activated. Each rule has a certain strength or likelihood of being activated.

The ideas generated by an individual member of the group depend upon the stimuli, and the relative strengths of the individual's production rules. As activation spreads through memory, rules that are more closely related to the stimuli and to each other have the greatest strength and are most likely to be activated. These closely related rules will likely lead to the production of closely related ideas. Thus an uninterrupted stream of ideas will tend follow a consistent train of thought in a set of semantically similar subcategories, because related stimuli activate related production rules. This will result in a focus on a narrow subset of the problem space, with many promising other subcategories overlooked.

There is no direct empirical evidence to support Anderson's (1983) theory for brainstorming, but there is abundant evidence from individual problem solving to support it. Individuals presented with an all-encompassing problem tend to focus only on small fraction of the potential solution space, yet believe themselves to have to produce a very complete set of solutions; (e.g., Connolly et al. 1993; Gettys et al. 1987; Gettys and Fischer 1979; Pitz et al. 1980). They explore a few related subcategories in greater depth rather than considering a broader range of subcategories in the overall problem. The result is that individual problem solving often misses key solution opportunities from this narrow focus.

One solution is to adopt a process structuring technique that attempts to change an individual's focus on the problem, either by decomposing it into subcategories or expanding it into a higher level problem (Volkema 1983). Volkema (1983) provides some evidence that problem expansion can improve the number of solutions identified. There is also evidence that problem decomposition—breaking the problem into a set of subcategories which are considered separately—improves performance by encouraging individuals to devote attention more evenly to the entire set of categories (e.g., Armstrong et al. 1975; Pitz et al. 1980). Samson (1988, p. 57) argues that even some modest amount of decomposition—perhaps three or four categories—should lead to better performance. For example, if the objective is to solve a campus parking problem, one might ask pose the problem as one question: "How can we solve the parking problem?" Conversely, one might break the problem into several subquestions and present each in turn: "How could we solve the parking problem by making more parking areas available? How could we solve the parking problem by using existing space more efficiently? How could we solve the parking problem by reducing demand for parking?"

## 2.2. Social Factors

What decomposition does for individual problem solving may be partially achieved by simply by forming a group. Part of the reason for forming a group is to provide a diversity of approaches to the problem to offset the potential narrowness of any one individual's perception. The diversity of ideas proposed by group members may overcome the cognitive limitations faced by individuals.

Nonetheless, a similar phenomenon to that in individuals has been observed in groups. Group brainstorming can suffer from cognitive inertia, the tendency of group discussion to focus on a few line of thoughts in one subcategory. As group members interact, they may consciously or unconsciously adopt behavior norms. These norms or structures can constrain behavior (Giddens 1984). One of the structures typically found in group interaction is one of “not changing the subject” (Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973, p. 382). Repeatedly changing the subject to focus on new ideas becomes socially undesirable. While members may think of unrelated ideas, social structures may inhibit the contribution of these ideas to the discussion. Members are motivated to focus on one train of thought in a narrow set of related subcategories (Maier 1970). Thus groups also constrain their attention to a narrow set of subcategories when faced with one all-encompassing problem statement. Such behavior has also been observed in EBS, and has been associated with the generation of fewer ideas (Dennis and Valacich 1994).

## 2.3. Effects of Time

Decomposing the overall problem into a set of subcategories that are addressed sequentially also divides the total amount of time available into several smaller time intervals, each devoted to one problem subcategory. There is evidence that the pace of group work is affected by externally imposed time constraints (Gersick 1988). McGrath et al. (1984), for example, found that when groups had short time limits imposed, they solved problems faster than when they had more time. In other words, work expands to fill the time available for it (Parkinson 1957). Therefore, breaking the problem into several categories each addressed under tighter time constraints may encourage the group to work faster, resulting in more ideas than presenting the group with the problem framed as one question with more time available.

## 2.4. Hypotheses

Three theoretical arguments suggest that presenting a group with one all-encompassing question should produce fewer ideas than presenting the group with a series of questions, each focusing on one subcategory of the problem. First, cognitive factors encourage individuals to focus on only a few subcategories of the problem, thus overlooking other areas. Second, social factors encourage group members to work on the same area of the problem rather than exploring many different parts simultaneously. Finally, individuals may perceive shorter time constraints and thus work faster when presented with a series of shorter time intervals than one larger interval. Previous research has found the number of ideas to be a reliable predictor of overall quality (see Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Valacich et al. 1994), so we expect that the multiple question formulation should also lead to higher quality ideas. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H1. The multiple question problem formulation will stimulate groups to produce more ideas of higher quality than the single question formulation.

## 3. Experiment 1

## 3.1. Subjects

Subjects were MBA students in a Management Information Systems course at a large midwestern university. One hundred fifty-three students participated as members of 18 groups (nine 8-member and nine 9-member) which were randomly assigned to the treatments.

## 3.2. Tasks

The experimental task came from the content of the subjects' course. Subjects had a major group assignment to prepare a system design for a video store information system. The experimental task was one of the initial analysis steps in preparing the system design; it required them to identify the data elements required in the construction of the system. The one all-encompassing question asked: "What do you feel are the important outputs from, inputs to, and data elements in the proposed computer system for Ace Video Rental?" The multiple question version asked separately for each of the three subcategories. For example: "What do you feel are the important outputs from the proposed computer system for Ace Video Rental?"

In the multiple question condition, the order of questions was held constant: outputs, inputs, and data elements. This was done for two reasons. First, the ordering acted as an experimental control over the brainstorming stimuli. Second, the ordering was consistent with how students were instructed to think about the information systems building process (e.g., first identify outputs, then inputs, and finally all internal working variables). Additionally, both the encompassing and separately styled questions were consistent in terms of style and scope of prior EBS use in both experimental and organizational groups; two experienced EBS facilitators who together have facilitated more than 500 EBS sessions agreed that both the multiple and single question formulations (in both Experiments 1 and 2) were consistent with their use of EBS in both the public and private sector.

Question formulation was also consistent with the advice of Delbecq et al. (1975, pp. 114–116). First, questions were developed directly from the objectives of the group project deliverables. Second, the wording of the questions were pilot tested and refined with a small, representative sample of the subjects. Third, groups were not given criteria to filter the quality or the appropriateness of their ideas. Finally, Delbecq and colleagues (1975) suggest that both encompassing and narrow questions may be appropriate depending upon the group's objectives and other contingencies. Given all of these factors, we are confident that groups in both the single and multiple questions conditions understood the objectives of their sessions, and more importantly, the objectives of the brainstorming stimuli.

## 3.3. Treatments

Groups working in the single question condition were given the task as stated above, and spent 45 minutes generating solutions using the electronic brainstorming (EBS) software described below. Groups working in the multiple question condition spent 15 minutes on each of the three subcategories with order of questions held constant.

## 3.4. Software

The EBS software used in this study presented the user with a screen split horizontally, with the brainstorming question displayed across the top. The user typed in an idea (up to five lines of text) in the lower half of the screen, and "sent" the idea with a function key. The top half of the screen then displayed a (one-item) list from another group member, to which that user appended an idea. When this two-item list was "sent," it was replaced by a third, randomly-drawn list, with two comments. As this continued, the chains of ideas displayed became longer, and users could freely scroll up and down through prior ideas.

## 3.5. Measures

Idea generation performance was first measured by counting the number of unique ideas. Output from each group was printed and coded by a rater blind to both experimental conditions and hypotheses. The coder was instructed to identify all unique (i.e., nonredundant) ideas proposed in each transcript, broken down by the three categories: inputs, outputs, and storage items. A second rater coded a random subset of 6 transcripts, and showed an agreement of 0.92 (calculated as 1—number of disagreements/number of ideas). The coding process was adequately reliable so the first rater's coding was used.

Our second major dependent variable was quality. The quality of each idea was independently assessed by the two faculty members teaching the course from which the task and subjects were drawn using a 1 = Very Poor Quality, 5 = Very Good Quality scale. Inter-judge reliability was adequate (alpha = 0.83). These quality assessments can be combined to produce several possible measures of quality. Diehl and Stroebe (1987) discuss various measures.

We used three measures of quality. The first was total quality, which has proven to be the most consistently reliable measure across most studies. Total quality was calculated by summing the quality scores for each unique idea generated by each group. This measure rewards groups for all ideas they produce, even very poor ideas with low quality scores. For example, a group generating five very poor ideas (each rated a 1 by the judges) would have the same quality score as a group generating one very good idea (rated a 5).

The second measure was mean quality, which has proven to be unreliable across studies. Mean quality, as the name suggests, was the average quality of ideas generated by each group (i.e., total quality divided by the number of ideas). This measure rewards groups that generate high quality ideas and penalizes those that produce less than perfect ideas. For example, a group generating one very good idea (rated a 5) would have a higher mean quality than a group generating three very good ideas (rated 5) and one neutral quality idea (rated a 3).

The third measure was the number of good ideas, which is the measure that Diehl and Stroebe (1987) argue is the most appropriate. The number of good ideas was calculated by counting only those ideas that judges rated to be of quality 3 or higher. This measure attempts to strike a balance between total quality and mean quality so that groups are rewarded for all ideas they produce, except those that are poor (i.e., rated less than a 3 by the judges).

A post-session questionnaire assessed subjects' perceptions of effectiveness (4 items, alpha = 0.83) and satisfaction (3 items, alpha = 0.82). These measures were adapted from previous research (Gallupe et al. 1992) and are presented in the Appendix.

## 3.6. Results

The results are shown in Table 1. Groups in the multiple question treatment generated more unique ideas (F(1, 16) = 36.54, p = 0.001). The multiple question groups also produced ideas of higher total quality (F(1, 16) = 23.79, p = 0.001) and more good ideas (F(1, 16) = 32.65, p = 0.001). There were no differences in mean quality (F(1, 16) = 3.33, p = ns), suggesting that the multiple question groups did not resort to “poor” ideas to any greater extent than single question groups. Thus, the data are consistent with our hypothesis that the multiple question process would lead to more ideas and ideas of higher quality than single question process.

This superiority also appeared in all three subcategories: inputs $(F(1,16)=30.06,p=0.001)$ , outputs $(F(1,16)=12.52,p=0.003)$ , and storage $(F(1,16)=36.22,p=0.001)$ . A check of idea content suggests that the decomposition manipulation had its intended effect. Subjects in the multiple question condition were able to confine their attention to the imposed subcategories. Overall, 79% of the ideas generated by the multiple question participants fell in the categories assigned: 99% for inputs, 49% for outputs, and 81% for storage. Overall, then, participants presented with the elements of the problem one at a time focused on the subcategories, and generated more ideas. No differences between the two groups were seen in the two questionnaire measures.

## 4. Experiment 2

The results of the first experiment were clear. Decomposition led to more ideas and more good ideas. One of our concerns was the generalizability of these findings. We studied students working in a laboratory setting for whom the task was externally imposed. Therefore, we decided to conduct a second experiment. This second experiment could have been undertaken as a straightforward replication of Experiment 1, but if we had done so and found the same results, the additional study would have added little additional insight. Therefore, we undertook a theoretical replication, rather than a methodological replication. We tested the same theoretical question using a different operationalization of the key concepts (e.g., different task, setting, subjects, and procedures). In this way, we could be more certain that the findings from the first study were not artifacts of some quirk in its design. In this case, we conducted a field experiment.

## 4.1. Subjects

Subjects were recruited from the membership of the Chamber of Commerce in a large southwestern city. The

Chamber was offered the use of a GSS facility at the university to address any problem of central interest to the city's senior business executives. The Chamber recruited participants from the top ranks of the city's business community. Participants included Chief Executive Officers, Chief Operating Officers, and board members from organizations including developers, hospitals, charities, and the University Board of Regents, as well as a retired general, and a retired senator. Seventy-two subjects participated as members of 9 eight-member groups.

## 4.2. Tasks

The experimental task was proposed by the President and Group Vice-President of the Chamber of Commerce as an important concern to their members. Problems created by the city's rapid growth over the last decade, especially what they saw as a lack of leadership in the infrastructure planning and implementation process, had reached crisis proportions. They therefore decided that the experimental sessions would be devoted to generating ways to "encourage a higher level of leadership in the community." We probed for possible subcategories and they provided three: actions by elected officials, actions by business leaders, and actions by the general public. The experimental task became: "What can elected officials, business leaders, and the general public do to encourage a higher level of leadership in the community?"

## 4.3. Treatments

Groups working in the single question condition were given the task as stated above, and spent 30 minutes generating ideas. Groups in the multiple question condition spent ten minutes on each subcategory ("What can elected officials do . . .", "What can business leaders do . . .", and "What can the general public do . . ."), with order of questions reversed for half the groups.

## 4.4. Measures

We used the same measures as Experiment 1. Interrater agreement in counting the number of unique ideas was adequate (0.89). Two Chamber of Commerce Vice-Presidents independently rated all unique ideas using a 1 = Very Poor Quality, 5 = Very Good Quality scale. Their ratings were not homogeneous (alpha = 0.31). An examination of the ratings suggests that while one judge generally rated ideas lower than the other, there were true differences of opinion on 12% of the ideas (i.e., one judge rated the idea less than 3 and the other rated it more than 3). These included ideas such as encouraging the press to publish commentary from business leaders, sponsoring public forums, encouraging the involvement of the “silent majority,” working with PACs, encouraging voter turnout, and encouraging businesses to participate in referendums. We analyzed the data for the two judges separately and as a combined measure. All analyses had the same statistical conclusions, so in the interest of space, we report the combined measure.

## 4.5. Results

Results are shown in Table 2. Once again, groups in the multiple question treatment generated more unique ideas (F(1, 7) = 5.86, p = 0.046). The multiple question groups also generated ideas of higher total quality (F(1, 7) = 10.51, p = 0.014). There were no differences in mean quality (F(1, 7) = 0.78, p = ns). Groups in the multiple question treatment generated more good ideas (F(1, 7) = 18.24, p = 0.004). Once again, the data are consistent with our hypothesis.

There were also differences in the number of ideas generated in two of the three subcategories. Multiple question groups developed a greater number of ideas pertaining to business leaders (F(1, 7) = 12.08, p = 0.010) and the general public (F(1, 7) = 14.83, p = 0.006). There were no differences for elected officials (F(1, 7) = 0.02, p = ns).

## 5. Discussion

Results from these two experiments are clear and consistent: decomposition led to more ideas. For two different tasks, subject populations, and settings, decomposing an idea-generating problem into subcategories and directing subjects to work sequentially on each generated some 60% more ideas than did presenting them with the same problem as one question. Decomposition had no effect on subjects' satisfaction or perceived effectiveness. These findings have obvious implications for practice: organizational groups generating ideas should decompose the problem into several subcategories and address each in turn.

They also have important implications for researchers. One long-running research stream has found that group organization (nominal groups versus verbally interacting groups) has considerable effect on performance. Meta-analyses of this research stream suggest effect sizes (expressed as the proportion of variance explained, where zero is no effect, and one is 100%) of 0.54 (Bond and Van Leeuwen 1991) or 0.79 (Mullen et al. 1991). The effect size here (eta squared) was 0.75 in Experiment 1 and 0.46 in Experiment 2. If studying group organization is important, more research is also clearly needed on process decomposition, given its significant effects on performance.

Several studies (Gettys et al. 1987, Pitz et al. 1980) suggest that subjects tend to generate only one or two subcategories within the entire solution space, exploring a few subcategories in detail rather than a broader range of subcategories because cognitive activity tends to favor the processing of related rather than unrelated concepts (see Anderson 1982). On this account, the effectiveness of decomposition flows from its forcing subjects to stop embellishing and refining ideas on subcategories they have developed already, and to start focusing on new and other potentially profitable subcategories.

Experiment 2 provides some support for this interpretation. Subjects in the single question condition focused primarily on one subcategory of the problem (66% of ideas discussed elected officials) to the detriment of the others (18% discussed business leaders, 16% general public). In contrast, subjects in the multiple question condition identified as many ideas about elected officials but produced 2–3 times as many ideas in other two areas. Their focus was more even across the subcategories: 41% elected officials, 33% business leaders, 26% general public. We conclude that decomposition improved performance in Experiment 2 by refocusing attention more evenly across the three subcategories.

But this interpretation does not fit the data from Experiment 1. Subjects in the single question condition generated ideas about equally across all three subcategories; there is no evidence that they missed important subcategories of the problem. Yet, subjects in the multiple question generated more ideas in all three subcategories. It is plausible placing more restrictive time limits on the multiple question groups altered their perceptions of the total time available for the task, encouraging them to work at a faster pace, resulting in more ideas.

In both experiments, two subcategories of the multiple question condition encouraged subjects to generate ideas not related to the category under discussion. Some topics may therefore produce less cognitive inertia than others (suggesting, perhaps, that cognitive reasons alone do not fully account for cognitive inertia). In Experiment 1, consideration of outputs sparked the generation of 22% inputs and 29% storage elements. Information system development methodologies often advocate the identification of outputs before inputs and storage elements because the identification of outputs often helps designers identify the other two (Fitzgerald and Fitzgerald 1987). Thus the higher rate of “off-target” items for outputs may be due to the subjects following previously learned methodologies or due to the inherent tendency of outputs to trigger thoughts of inputs and storage elements.

In Experiment 2, consideration of the general public resulted in the generation of 8% actions for business leaders and 42% for elected officials. Unlike Experiment 1, there is no underlying theory we can draw upon to explain this phenomenon. However, many of the elected officials ideas generated in the general public subcategory pertained to officials' responses to the public's actions (e.g., listen to public input, understand minority viewpoints) or actions officials could take to encourage public participation (e.g., encourage participation in hearings, educate public on issues). So while a large portion of the ideas generated were not actions for the general public, there were not truly unrelated to the general public either.

One question that remains is how far decomposition can usefully go. Would the decomposition in Experiment 2 yield further gains if “elected officials” were further subdivided into “local elected officials,” “state elected officials,” and “federal elected officials”? Still further decompositions are obviously possible: “older, female, incumbent, local elected officials,” and so on indefinitely. Presumably there is some optimal level of decomposition, a point beyond which performance falls off with further splitting. Our evidence here is that this optimal point appears to be at a finer level of division than the original, superordinate form of the problem initially proposed by the group leaders. Exactly how fine a division can be usefully made clearly depends upon the group and the problem, but the key point is that some moderate level of decomposition produced better results than no decomposition.

However, when the solution space for a task is small or when the task is complex, decomposition may not be an appropriate strategy. For small tasks, a group may be easily able to exhaust the solution space without decomposition. For complex tasks, it may be impossible to create feasible solutions without considering all subcategories of the problem simultaneously. For example, ideas developed by focusing on one subcategory, may violate a constraint imposed by some other aspect of the problem and thus be infeasible (e.g., see Campbell 1988).

There are many other questions for future research. One of the limitations of this study is that we used a strong form of decomposition (subjects spent specified times on specified subcategories of the problem). Would we have found similar results if we had not specified the amount time to be spent on each subcategory, but had let the group as a whole (or each member individually) choose how long to spend on each subcategory? Or if each group (or each member individually) had identified their own decomposition (i.e., set of subcategories) before beginning to work on the problem? Even just requiring the group to start over again at preselected time intervals might increase the number of ideas compared to providing no such prompting.

Another limitation is that our intact question groups were given a set of potential subcategories. Had we not provided this information to the single question groups and then found differences, we would have been unable to ascribe them to the work processes or to whether participants were prompted or not prompted for specific types of ideas. It is possible, however, that participants found these three subcategories constraining, and might have produced more ideas without them, although we found no evidence of this. Another limitation is that we studied only EBS groups. Decomposition may also help groups brainstorming verbally, although our data speak only to electronic groups.

Our focus was on improving idea generation, but it is important to note that the decision making process usually does not end with idea generation. While the generation of more ideas should lead to better decisions, there is some evidence that groups do not process all the ideas and information they produce (Dennis 1993). This too is an area for future research.

The experiments reported here raise as many questions as they answer at a theoretical level. We presented three theoretical mechanisms by which EBS could reduce cognitive inertia: cognitive, social, and temporal. This study, being an initial study, made no attempt to assess the relative strengths of these three mechanisms. This, too, is left to future research.

Nonetheless, our results have a clear message for managers and practitioners performing electronic brainstorming. If the problem offers an opportunity for modest decomposition, it should be taken, and time and effort deliberately allocated across the various subcategories. Though concerns clearly remain as to the generalizability of the findings here, we are reassured by the consistency of the results across different tasks, different subject populations, and different settings. The result appears robust, and of substantial size—on the order of 60% more ideas. An effect of this size can be usefully embodied in practical application while theoretical research probes more deeply into the mechanisms by which it operates and the limits to its effectiveness. $^{1}$
