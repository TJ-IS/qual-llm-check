# The Contribution of Information Technology to Consumer Welfare

Erik Brynjolfsson

Sloan School of Management, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139

Over the past two decades, American businesses have invested heavily in information technology (IT) hardware. Managers often buy IT to enhance customer value in ways that are poorly measured by conventional output statistics. Furthermore, because of competition, firms may be unable to capture the full benefits of the value they create. This undermines researchers' attempts to determine IT value by estimating its contribution to industry productivity or to company profits and revenues. An alternative approach estimates the consumers' surplus from IT investments by integrating the area under the demand curve for IT. This methodology does not directly address the question of whether managers and consumers are purchasing the optimal quantity of IT, but rather assumes their revealed willingness-to-pay for IT accurately reflects their valuations. Using data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, we estimate four measures of consumers' surplus, including Marshallian surplus, Exact surplus based on compensated (Hicksian) demand curves, a "nonparametric" estimate, and a value based on the theory of index numbers. Interestingly, all four estimates indicate that in our base year of 1987, IT spending generated approximately \$50 billion to \$70 billion in net value in the United States and increased economic growth by about 0.3% per year. According to our estimates, which are likely to be conservative, IT investments generate approximately three times their cost in value for consumers.

(Consumers' Surplus; Economic Contributions; Productivity; Performance; Econometrics; Computers; Growth)

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. The Question of IT Value

In 1990, American businesses spent approximately \$43 billion on “office, computing, and accounting machinery” (OCAM). Nominal spending has increased substantially over the past several decades, and in real terms, the increase has been even more dramatic because of annual declines exceeding 20% in the real price of computing power (Figure 1).

The economic significance of this spending is apparent in the ubiquity of computers in the work place and the concomitant changes in goods and services. Manufacturing firms now use Computer-Aided Design and Manufacturing (CAD/CAM), Flexible Manufacturing Systems (FMS), Electronic Data Interchange (EDI), and other computer-based systems to increase their quality, flexibility, and responsiveness. A visit to Wall Street reveals products, services, and transaction processing capabilities that would have been simply impossible without enormous computer power. For instance, deposit accounts now give consumers access to mutual funds, automatic deposits and withdrawals, and of course, an army of robot tellers—ATMs. Reflecting these technology-driven advances, the New York bank deposit turnover ratio (annual value of transactions divided by deposits on hand) has increased to 3804 in 1990 from just 156 in 1960. The pattern is similar in other industries: medical technology, manufacturing systems, retailing, and distribution each use IT to deliver far more value per dollar spent than they did just a decade or two ago.

Surprisingly, the benefits to consumers in the broader economy have been difficult to quantify. For instance, Baily and Gordon (1988) ask “Where is the black hole into which all those computers are disappearing?” and government statistics suggest that productivity growth in the service sector, which is the largest consumer of computers, has been insignificant for over a decade.

A number of explanations for this “paradox” have been put forth, but among the most compelling is an explanation which uncovers another paradox: the incongruity of applying traditional productivity metrics to an emergent technology (Brynjolfsson 1993). Managers report that they invest in IT not only to reduce costs but also to improve quality, increase product variety, speed up responsiveness, and enhance customer service (Brynjolfsson 1994). However, these intangible benefits are largely ignored in conventional output and productivity statistics because they are difficult to measure, aggregate, and value. In practice, productivity measurement focuses on increases in the physical quantity of production and reductions in costs, especially head count. The measurement edifice designed for the industrial era does not accurately reflect the sources of value in the emerging information age. Griliches has suggested that the relative stagnancy of measured productivity in the service sector may be due mainly to shortcomings in calculating output for this sector (Griliches 1994).

Furthermore, competition forces firms to provide these intangible benefits as the “cost of staying in the game.” However, the ultimate beneficiaries of lower prices, increased quality, and better customer service are consumers, so company profits and revenues fail to capture their value. Thus, researchers encounter a gap between the sources of value created by computers and the metrics used to evaluate them.

## 1.2. Applying "OCAM's Razor"

Estimates of IT value typically require data on potential benefits such as increased revenues, stock market valuation, user satisfaction, productivity, etc., as well as data on costs and factors other than IT which might be responsible for these benefits, such as other inputs, strategic choices, industry conditions, etc. Even when all these data are collected, the nagging suspicion persists that the person making the IT investment decision considered some benefits or costs which the researcher did not. In this paper, we depart from the approach of estimating such models. Instead, we consider the revealed preference of IT consumers who “put their money where their mouths are” every time they make a purchase. While one can dispute the rationality of such decisions, it seems to us that the simplest explanation for why someone would spend \$100,000 on a new computer system is that it is worth (at least) \$100,000 to them. The purchaser is assumed to make his or her best estimates of all the benefits and costs resulting from the investment, including benefits and costs for which statistical data are not compiled. This simplifying assumption enables us to infer the value of office, computing and accounting machinery (OCAM) from data on OCAM investment alone, and does not require other types of output and input data or the assumptions associated with estimating production, productivity, or profit equations.

Specifically, we apply the economic theory of consumers' surplus. Because available data enable fairly accurate estimates of the demand for IT, this approach provides a promising alternative to productivity, profit, or output-based estimates of the value of IT. Furthermore, since this approach requires different assumptions than those required for productivity calculations, it may enable us to better "triangulate" on the true value of computers and IT. This paper presents several techniques for calculating consumers' surplus, not only to help assess the robustness of the results, but also to introduce these techniques to IS researchers who may wish to estimate consumers' surplus for goods such as software, personal computers, microprocessors or other types of IT.

Our estimates indicate that in the 1980s, IT generated on the order of \$400 billion dollars in net value in the United States after subtracting the costs of expenditures over that period. In our base year of 1987, we estimate that computer equipment created about \$90 billion in value, compared to costs of about \$40 billion. Furthermore, at current rates of price declines and spending growth, consumers' surplus from computers is projected at over \$200 billion annually by 1997.

An important implication of this analysis is that IT's long-term contribution to the U.S. economy can be found in the consumer windfall created by rapid price declines. With 20% price declines annually, uses of IT which are marginally unproductive this year may well become very cost-effective a few years later. In the meantime consumers have more money to spend: the money they would have spent on IT even if the price were higher. If one is interested in determining IT's impact on living standards, the marginal product of computers, which has received most of the attention, is likely to be less important than the simple algebra of lower prices and higher quantities purchased, from which changes in consumers' surplus can be calculated.

The next section discusses the various approaches to estimating IT value with particular emphasis on the theory of consumers' surplus. Section 3 describes the features and limitations of data and the econometric methods used. The results are presented in §4, including some sensitivity estimates. In §5, we conclude with a summary and discussion of future directions for the work.

## 2. The Theory Underlying Three Approaches to IT Value Estimation

Economic theory suggests at least three general approaches for econometrically estimating the value of an input such as IT: 1) output and productivity estimation, 2) correlations with performance metrics such as profits, revenues, or stock values, and 3) consumer surplus from derived demand.

## 2.1. Output and Productivity

The first approach is based on the idea that inputs can be related to outputs by a production function.

$$
\text { Output } = F (\text { computers }, \text { labor }, \text { capital }, \text { etc. }).\tag{1}
$$

Thus, the only way for a firm to increase output is to increase at least one of the inputs, or to change its technology or management so that its production function becomes more efficient. This is the approach taken by Loveman (1994), Barua et al. (1991), Morrison and Berndt (1990), and Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1995, 1996), among others. $^{1}$ Typically a functional form for a production function is assumed, and the parameters are econometrically estimated under the assumption that firms are minimizing costs or maximizing profits. For instance, in estimating a Cobb-Douglas production function such as (2), the parameter $\beta_{1}$ can be interpreted directly as the output elasticity of computers: the amount by which output will increase for a given increase in computer input.

$$
\text { Output } = e ^ {\beta_ {0}} \text { Computers } ^ {\beta_ {1}} \text { Labor } ^ {\beta_ {2}} \text { Capital } ^ {\beta_ {3}}.\tag{2}
$$

A key assumption of this approach is that output is reliably measured.

## 2.2. Business Performance Metrics

The second common approach is to measure the correlation between computer spending and some performance metric, as suggested by Equation (3).

Performance metric

$$
= F (\text { Computers }, \text { Environment }, \text { Strategy }, \text { etc. }).\tag{3}
$$

Typical performance metrics used include (growth in) business profits, (growth in) sales, (growth in) market share, stock price appreciation, Tobin's Q, and various industry-specific measures. While there are numerous ways to operationalize such a relationship, they all seek to isolate the contribution of computers while controlling for other factors. (See for instance, Cron and Sobol 1983, Dos Santos et al. 1993, Harris and Katz 1989, Weill 1992). If managers are rational, economic theory predicts that in equilibrium, high computer investors would not, on average, perform any better than low computer investors by these metrics. If significant correlations are found, they should be interpreted as indicating an unexpectedly high or low contribution of information technology, as compared to the performance that was anticipated when the investments were made.

In addition, a key assumption of this approach is that businesses retain the value created by their investments in IT, or at least capture a significant portion of it.

## 2.3. Consumers' Surplus from Derived Demand

A third approach is based on the theory of consumers' surplus, which has been little applied to the question of IT value. Consumers' surplus uses the theory of demand to compute the total consumer benefit based on revealed spending patterns.

The demand curve for any good plots the price that buyers would be willing to pay for each incremental quantity of that good. Because all the infra-marginal consumers actually pay less than what they would be willing to pay, they get some surplus from the transaction. By adding up all these individual surpluses, or equivalently, by integrating the area under the demand curve between the old price $(p_{0})$ and the new price $(p_{1})$ , one can deduce the total value of consumers' surplus from a price change.

$$
\text { Surplus } = \int_ {P _ {0}} ^ {P _ {1}} \text { Demand(Price, Income) } d (\text { Price }).\tag{4}
$$

Historically, most computers have been purchased by businesses as an input to production, rather than by final consumers. However, Schmalensee (1976) and Bresnahan (1986) have shown that, in a competitive market, producers who purchase an intermediate good act as proxies for the ultimate consumers: they will purchase exactly the quantity that maximizes consumer welfare at any given price. Therefore, the area under the derived demand curve for the intermediate good furnishes a correct estimate of consumers' surplus from the intermediate good. Furthermore, when competition is imperfect, producers will also get benefits in the form of profits from selling a good, so the area under the demand curve will generally underestimate total surplus created by a price change.

Because computer capital is long-lived, stocks of computers continue to create benefits (and possibly costs) after they are purchased. However, purchasers are presumed to pay precisely as much for a capital good as the anticipated net present value of all future rents (benefits and costs) arising from that good (Christensen and Jorgenson 1969). $^{2}$

A key assumption of this approach is that managers are, on average, choosing the right amounts of IT, given its price.

## 2.4. Comparing the Three Approaches

Equations (1), (3), and (4) above can each be used to assess the value created by spending on a given input to production. One or the other approach will be more appropriate depending on the precise question being asked and whether the required assumptions are realistic in the particular case being considered.

As discussed above, there is reason to believe that final output statistics fail to identify the dimensions of output most affected by increased investments in IT. As a result, they may be underestimated or even ignored in productivity calculations, especially at the aggregate level, thereby limiting the applicability of approaches relying on the production functions such as Equation (1). Furthermore, in fully competitive markets, all of the benefits of a price decline for an intermediate good may be passed on to consumers, because of “strategic necessity” (Clemons 1991). This makes the approach embodied in business performance metrics such as Equation (3) problematic. $^{3}$ Finally, with a relatively new and uncertain technology such as computers, managers may have difficulty making correct investment decisions. This will tend to undermine the accuracy of estimates based on consumers’ surplus as in Equation (4).

There are a number of reasons why it is worthwhile to apply consumers' surplus to derive estimates of IT value. First, this approach can tell us how much value the purchasers of IT think they are getting and allows us to identify which sectors and to what extent this values differs from productivity or performance metrics. With this information, we should be better able to find the source of the discrepancy. For instance, by putting upper and lower bounds on the size of the “mistakes” we think are plausible, we can put upper and lower bounds on the total surplus created; and by comparing services (where output measurement is often poor) to manufacturing (where measurement is typically better), we can calibrate the size of potential measurement shortfalls.

Second, the errors in estimating IT value via productivity and business performance metrics are likely to lead to systematic underestimates of IT value, because of underestimates of output and the shift of benefits to consumers, respectively. In contrast, consumers' surplus estimates are based on IT purchasers' willingness-to-pay. While managers will certainly make mistakes in the amount of IT they purchase, there is less reason to believe that these mistakes will systematically lead to over- or underinvestment. Because the consumers' surplus estimates will be approximately accurate if managers are as likely to make either mistake, there should be less bias in these estimates.

Third, each of the three approaches is subject to different types of weaknesses. This makes it valuable to use a variety of methods to triangulate on the true value of IT. In the past, efforts have focused on the productivity or the performance metric approach, to the neglect of consumers' surplus.

Finally, judging the contribution of a technology by the amount that its consumers are willing to pay accords well with the long-standing economic tradition of taking the consumers' preferences as sovereign. Because many of the benefits of IT may be difficult for the statistician or accountant to measure, it seems especially appropriate to heed the actions of the parties who spend the most time evaluating the costs and benefits of each decision.

2.5. Approaches to Measuring Consumers' Surplus Although the basic concept of consumers' surplus is simple, there are a number of ways of operationalizing it. Below, four basic approaches and the relevant formulae are described, including (1) Marshallian surplus,

![](/api/attachments/4T3SDJUF/fulltext/images/0341569f9fff800b72662f3484c3953b9eee00e690164099fc7da5e2e03f8f93.jpg)  
(2) exact surplus based on compensated (Hicksian) demand curves, (3) a “nonparametric” estimate, and (4) a value based on the theory of index numbers. Under the assumptions of perfect competition, each of these approaches is appropriate for use with either demand curves for final goods, or derived demand for intermediate goods.

2.5.1. Marshallian Consumers' Surplus. The most common conception of consumers' surplus is based on Marshallian demand. A Marshallian demand curve plots the relationship between price and quantity demand of a good holding consumer income constant. As shown in Figure 2, a price decline from $P_0$ to $P_1$ will be accompanied by an increase in quantity purchased from $Q_0$ to $Q_1$ . The consumers' surplus consists of two parts, the direct reduction in price on units that would have been purchased anyway (denoted by A) and the increase in welfare from additional units whose cost is now less than the consumers' willingness to pay (denoted by B).

Given a specification for the demand curve, one can directly calculate Marshallian consumers' surplus by integrating it between any two prices. In this paper, we use the log-linear specification given by Equation (5). $^{4}$

This is one of the most widely used functional forms for demand models (Oum 1989). Its principal weakness is that it restricts the elasticities to be invariant for different quantities. $^{5}$

$$
q = e ^ {\gamma} p ^ {\alpha} y ^ {\delta},\tag{5}
$$

where $q =$ quantity, $p =$ price, $y =$ income, and $\gamma, \alpha,$ and $\delta$ are parameters.

Integrating the demand curve from $p_{0}$ to $p_{1}$ yields:

Consumer Surplus (Marshallian)

$$
= e ^ {\gamma} y ^ {\delta} (p _ {1} ^ {1 + \alpha} - p _ {0} ^ {1 + \alpha}) / (1 + \alpha).\tag{6}
$$

Equation (6) can then be used to calculate surplus given prices and the parameters $\alpha$ , $\gamma$ , and $\delta$ .

2.5.2. Exact Consumers' Surplus. As pointed out by Hicks (1956), Marshallian consumers' surplus is not an exact welfare measure. This is because a price decline in a good will increase the effective income available to the consumer (he or she could now buy slightly more of every good) and therefore shift the consumer to a higher utility curve. If one is interested in the effect of relative price changes, holding constant the utility of consumers, then Marshallian consumers' surplus will be misleading. The appropriate demand curve to use for exact consumers' surplus is the Hicksian (compensated) demand curve, which is the amount that the consumer would demand if income were adjusted sufficiently to maintain the same utility level. $^{6}$ As shown in Figure 3, a compensated demand curve will be steeper than the

uncompensated demand curve, but the calculation of surplus is the same conceptually, the sum of areas A and B, now defined with reference to the compensated demand curve.

As shown by Hausman (1981), the value of the exact consumers' surplus can be derived directly from any well-specified demand curve. In the log-linear case given by Equation (5), the appropriate formula is given by (7):

Consumer Surplus (Hicksian)

$$
= \left\{\left[ e ^ {\gamma} (p _ {1} ^ {1 + \alpha} - p _ {0} ^ {1 + \alpha}) / (1 + \alpha) \right] (1 - \delta) + y ^ {(1 - \delta)} \right\} ^ {1 / 1 - \delta} - y.\tag{7}
$$

The additional terms, as compared with (6) above, compensate for the implicit change in real income due to the price change by taking into account the income elasticity of demand. For most goods, including IT, these terms will be of only second-order significance, so the Marshallian estimates are usually not a bad approximation of the exact consumers' surplus (Willig 1976). This is because IT represents a relatively small portion of consumer expenditure, so income effects are not large.

2.5.3. A "Nonparametric" Derivation Consumers' Surplus. The formulas above for Marshallian and Exact Consumers' Surplus assume that the parameters of a functional form of the demand curve can be estimated. While this is generally possible, some error may be introduced in the estimation procedure, especially if the functional form chosen does not fit the actual demand curve well. An alternative approach is to explicitly add up each of the additional increments to consumers' surplus from each price decline. For example, if the price decline from $P_{0}$ to $P_{1}$ can be decomposed into a number of smaller declines to intermediate prices labeled $P_{t}$ , then the sum of price difference times the quantity increase for each intermediate step will be another measure of consumers' surplus. For instance in Figure 4, as in Figure 1, area A denotes the addition to consumer welfare from a decline in price from $P_{0}$ to $P_{1}$ on infra-marginal units. Area E denotes the increase in welfare from the decline in price on the units between $Q_{0}$ to $Q_{t}$ , labeled $\Delta Q^{7}$ .

If the steps $\Delta Q$ are made sufficiently small, this approach will furnish an arbitrarily good approximation of any monotonically decreasing demand curve, regardless of its exact shape. In the limit, of course, it is equivalent to integrating the area between $P_{0}$ to $P_{1}$ . While this approach is more tedious than directly integrating the whole curve, it makes use of data on intermediate points which may not lie exactly on the equation for the estimated demand curve. To account for the growth in the economy over time, each portion of the contribution is multiplied by the ratio of then-current income to income in the period 1. This implicitly assumes that income elasticity is equal to one. The formula used for this approach is given by Equation (8).

Consumer Surplus (Cumulative Method)

$$
= \Sigma (p _ {t} - p _ {1}) \Delta q _ {t} (y _ {1} / y _ {t}), \quad \text { for   } t = 0, \dots , 1,\tag{8}
$$

where $p_{t}$ is the actual price in period t, $\Delta q_{t}$ is the actual change in quantity from period t - 1 to period t, and $y_{t}$ = income in period t.

2.5.4. Applying the Theory of Index Numbers. Consumer welfare is properly a function of the change in utility due to changes in prices and quantities. One can directly deduce the increase in welfare from assumptions about the form of the utility function, without making any direct assumptions about the form of

![](/api/attachments/4T3SDJUF/fulltext/images/3b41f406ce503a35765bc96c728a723281bdbb916466c89adadf0dbf13c04655.jpg)  
the demand curve. Following Bresnahan (1986), we consider a translog utility function, which is one of the least restrictive available. Applying the theory of index numbers (Caves et al. 1982) one can derive the increase in consumer welfare as given by Equation (9).

Consumer Surplus (Index Method)

$$
= \frac {1}{2} (s _ {i t} ^ {1} + s _ {i t} ^ {0}) \log (p _ {0} / p _ {1}) y _ {1},\tag{9}
$$

where $s_{it}^{1}$ and $s_{it}^{0}$ are the factor shares of IT in periods 1 and 0, respectively.

The intuition behind this formula is that it is, like the others, the area under the demand curve, with sides equal to the change in prices and the factor shares of IT. The main difference is that only the endpoints need to be known, since all of the other points are then fixed, given the assumption of translog utility.

While each of these methods is slightly different, in principle, all four methods should yield similar estimates if the assumptions about the choice of functional form are correct and income effects are not large.

## 3. Data and Methods

## 3.1. Data

Two primary sources of data were used for this study: publicly available data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) on "office, computing and accounting machinery" for "OCAM" and for "OCAM

PRICE" and a data set on computer expenditures and prices which David Cartwright, a researcher at the BEA, compiled for "COMPUTERS" and "COMPUTER PRICE." In addition, we used government data on real gross domestic product for "GDP" and the GDP deflator for "INFLATION."

The OCAM data set consists primarily of computers, but also includes peripherals, electronic calculators, and many other office machines. It does not include software, photocopiers, communications equipment, robots, or scientific instruments. The share of computers, while always a majority of the category, exceeded 90% by the end of the sample period. The data for OCAM were available for 1970 to 1990 and are based on U.S. National Income and Product Accounts annual investment expenditures and the BEA's capital flow tables. We grouped the data on two-digit SIC industries into the following eight sectors: agriculture; mining; durable goods manufacturing; nondurables manufacturing; transportation and utilities; trade; finance, insurance and real estate; and other services. In addition, an aggregate was constructed for the economy as a whole.

A price index for OCAM provided by the BEA was used to convert the current dollar flows to constant dollar flows. Thus the total number of units of OCAM purchased each year were made comparable to what they would have cost in a base year based on the mix of goods included and features such as processor speed, memory, storage capacity, and display, weighted by the relative proportions of the various types of equipment which comprise OCAM. The price index for OCAM, divided by the real GDP deflator, was used for the variable OCAM PRICE.

Figure 5 Price and Quantity for COMPUTERS

The separate data series on COMPUTERS provided by Cartwright did not include the other types of office equipment that were included in OCAM. It also had its own hedonic price index (which changed much more rapidly than the one for OCAM, which is used for COMPUTER PRICE). However, unlike OCAM and OCAM PRICE, these data were not available by industry, but only for the economy as a whole.

For gross income for the economy as a whole, we used Real GDP (in 1982 dollars). Real GDP was also compiled by the BEA for each of the eight sectors for each year, providing eight distinct income series. $^{8}$

The data are subject to a number of limitations. First, OCAM, COMPUTERS, and GDP, and their associated price indexes, while based on the most authoritative sources available, are each subject to measurement errors. Second, because the price index used for COMPUTERS was more recent, and significantly different from, the index used for OCAM, they are not directly comparable. Third, all the data are at a fairly aggregate level, which tends to obscure smaller trends in particular companies or types of computers. Fourth, the data do not reflect all types of information processing expenditures and should be interpreted accordingly. In particular, no comparable data were available for software and personnel, which are essential to realizing the value from computer hardware. $^{9}$

However, these data should be useful for estimating the overall contribution of OCAM and computers to the economy as a whole and in the eight sectors. Furthermore, the basic trends which are customarily associated with computers—rapid price declines, significant growth in expenditures, and explosive growth in overall computer power delivered—are all quite evident in these data as well. Intriguingly, when price and quantity are plotted on the same graph, the curve looks suspiciously like a demand curve, even before any estimation, corrections for income effects, etc. (see Figure 5).

## 3.2. Methods

3.2.1. Basic Estimating Equation. The key to our approach is obtaining sensible estimates of the demand for IT. The value for the price elasticity of demand is particularly important for the Marshallian and Exact consumers' surplus calculations. The other two approaches are less demanding of the data. In fact, the index method requires only that starting and ending prices and quantities are known.

As described in §2, the first two approaches assume that the relationship between prices and quantities can be described by Equation (5). In order to estimate this equation, we take the log of both sides and include an error term, yielding:

$$
\log q _ {i t} = \gamma + \alpha \log p _ {i t} + \delta \log y _ {i t} + e _ {i t},\tag{10}
$$

where q = quantity in sector i and year t, p = price in sector i and year t, y = income in sector i and year t, $\gamma$ , $\alpha$ , and $\delta$ are parameters to be estimated, and $e_{it}$ is an error term assumed to be i.i.d.

A nice feature of this specification is that the coefficients, $\alpha$ and $\delta$ , can be directly interpreted as the price and income elasticity of demand, respectively. For a single series on price an quantity, Equation (10) can be estimated by Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression after a correction of serial correlation, which is the technique we used to estimate the demand function for COMPUTERS.

In order to capitalize on the distinct data for each of the eight sectors in OCAM, OCAM price, and GDP, we can estimate these eight equations simultaneously using the technique of Iterated Seemingly Unrelated Regressions (ISUR). If the error terms of each of the equations were uncorrelated with each other, then efficient estimates could be obtained by estimating them separately with OLS. However, if there is some correlation among cross section units, then greater efficiency can be obtained by estimating the equations as a system. In the first stage, each equation is estimated using OLS. The covariances of the estimated residuals then provide consistent estimates of the error covariances, enabling a more efficient estimate of the parameters in a second stage using a generalized least squares approach. The procedure can be iterated and it has been shown to be (asymptotically) efficient by Zellner (1962). $^{10}$ The essence of the benefit is that the “random” errors associated with estimating computer demand in, say, durable goods manufacturing, may be correlated with the error in the equation for, say, nondurable goods manufacturing, and the ISUR method takes advantage of this additional information to increase efficiency.

Because of the potential for serial correlation in time series regressions, generalized differencing was done for all equations by including a first-order autoregressive term in each regression. This resulted in a reduction in the number of observations from 20 to 19 for each equation.

## 3.2.2. Simultaneity.

(1) Technological advance suggests shifts in the supply curve. Our data document the relationship among the three variables $(p, q, y)$ for each year and sector. The relationship is governed by the interplay of supply and demand, giving rise to a problem of simultaneity which can make it difficult to identify which curve is being fitted.

In the case of OCAM and COMPUTERS, this problem is greatly alleviated by the historical record: due to extraordinary technological progress, the cost of supplying a unit of computer power has declined by over 100-fold in the last 20 years (Figure 6). These changes in price reflect scientific, engineering and manufacturing advances in a number of areas, which created a remarkable shift in the supply curve for computer equipment. For instance, the average feature size (e.g., of a transistor) has declined at an exponential rate of 5.5% per year, while die area of memory chips (DRAM) and microprocessors (CISC CPUs) has increased by 7.53% annually, and the number of masking levels has grown by 8% annually (Touma 1993). These improvements have a multiplicative effect on one another, as suggested by Figure 6. A single transistor cost on the order of \$5 in

1960, but a full microprocessor in 1995, like the Intel Pentium Pro, has 5.5 million transistors using a 0.35 micron process, $^{11}$ is far faster and more reliable, and costs only a few hundred dollars. To a rough approximation, the number of transistors produced and consumed has doubled every year. It would have been inconceivable to provide for the current consumption of computer power simply by adding workers and machines to the old production technology. The average factory worker might have produced a dozen transistors in a day in 1960, but in 1990 a worker in the Tateyama plant of NMB Semiconductor produces literally billions in the same amount of time (Gilder 1989). Meanwhile, computer equipment manufacturers have also been investing in research and development to make better use of the advances in the underlying chip technology and to develop complementary innovations. Between 1975 and 1989 R&D spending in the OCAM sector rose from \$3.5 billion to \$9.8 billion in constant (1987) dollars.

This significant shift in the supply curve each year effectively “maps out” the underlying demand curve, since demand has presumably shifted much more slowly (see Figure 7). For most products, economists can only speculate as to how much of the good would have been demanded if the price were 2, 10, or 100 times the current price; and one cannot observe how high prices must be for even the most desperate consumer to forego purchasing. In contrast, in the case of IT, we have historical evidence of prices at which demand was nearly zero, as well as intermediate prices with varying levels of positive demand.

Presumably demand, too, has shifted over time, but it is likely that outward (or inward) shifts in the demand curve are orders of magnitude smaller than movements along the demand curve. For instance, Gurbaxani and Mendelson (1990) considered the alternative hypotheses that the increase in computer demand was either due to diffusion or declines in price. They concluded that since 1970, price declines account for essentially all of the increase.

Likewise, Gurbaxani (1992) estimated a model which included a shift parameter to account for the diffusion of technology over time, but found that it added essentially nothing to the regression that included price and income effects. Accordingly, we follow the tradition pioneered by Chow (1967) of focusing on price and income effects to explain computer demand. However, any unmodeled shifts in demand are likely to mean that the estimates of consumers' surplus we report are underestimates of its true magnitude, as discussed in §4.3.

(2) Three stage least squares estimation for OCAM by sector. While the overall trend in prices for IT has been largely driven by technological advances and shifts in the supply curve, some of the year to year price changes may still reflect changes in industry demand. Therefore, we may have difficulty completely identifying the demand curve with ISUR (or OLS) regression. The standard approach to this type of problem is to use instrumental variables, but appropriate instruments are typically difficulty to find. To further address this concern, we exploit the panel structure of the data for OCAM using the techniques of Hausman and Taylor (1981) and Hausman (1994). Specifically, suppose that the price of IT in industry i in period t can be modeled as:

$$

The first term indicates that costs vary over time $(t)$ , but this change affects all industries. For instance, the costs of standard microprocessors and DRAM do not vary by industry. The second term is a random error term which varies by time and industry and is assumed to have mean zero and be independent across industries. $^{12}$ Under these assumptions, prices in one industry provide instrumental variables which are correlated with prices in other industries (via $c_{t}$ ), but are uncorrelated with the random errors in the demand equation (the $e_{it}$ in Equation (10)). To take advantage of these instruments, we used the technique of Three Stage Least Squares (3SLS), which relates to ISUR estimation much as Two Stage Least Squares relates to OLS. This technique is designed to filter out endogenous variation in the independent variables caused by the possibility that changes in computer prices reflect movements along the supply curve and not only the demand curve. In addition, it addresses the problem of measurement error in the independent variables.

Although the use of instrumental variables may help somewhat with identification of the demand curve, it should be stressed that the key to identifying the demand curve econometrically is the large change in the cost of computer power over the past two decades which will overwhelm more transient or smaller phenomena. In particular, to the extent that the error terms in Equation (11) are correlated across industries, the prices will not be good instruments.

Obviously, for the nonparametric method and the index approach, econometric estimation of the demand curve was not necessary.

## 4.2. Consumers' Surplus Calculations

As a representative year for our welfare calculations, we chose 1987. GDP (income) by sector and spending on OCAM is given in Table 3. $^{18}$

To calculate consumers' surplus, it is generally necessary to extrapolate from observed prices and quantities, using some functional form, to estimated the "virtual price" at which demand would be zero (Hausman 1994). The total computer surplus generated is equal to the area under the demand curve between this "virtual price" and the actual price in 1987. Given the log-linear specification we used, the "virtual price" approaches infinity. However, actual demand for computers becomes very small at prices less than infinity. For instance, in 1970, when computers were about 15 times as expensive as in 1987, demand for computers was only 1/200th as large. Therefore, a reasonable approximation (and slight underestimate) of total computer surplus in 1987 can be obtained by estimating the increase in surplus associated with the price decline between 1970 and 1987. $^{19}$

Applying the methods described in §2.5 and the regression results of §3.1, we calculated such an estimate of surplus for COMPUTERS and OCAM for 1987. (The total gross value generated is equal to consumers' surplus plus expenditures.) Each year, the size of the annual consumers' surplus from computers grows by an amount exceeding 20% of that year's spending on computers, due to continuing price declines and increased purchases.

The sector with the largest surplus by all methods was Finance, Insurance and Real Estate, which ironically is not only a sector with minimal measured productivity gains according to official statistics (and average increase in official measures of output per hour of just 0.1% per year in the 1980s), but also one with among the worst measures of output (Griliches 1994). The claims by some researchers that this sector is the locus of a “productivity paradox” $^{21}$ are all the more striking in light of the fact that our estimates indicate that it is realizing the largest benefits from computerization. When the surplus from each of the eight sectors is summed, the total is about \$50 billion, not far from the estimates of the economy as a whole using the cumulative and index methods, but significantly less than the estimates based on the regressions for economy-wide OCAM demand (i.e., the Marshallian and Exact surplus).

When the OCAM and COMPUTERS estimates are compared, it is surprising to note that more surplus is generated by COMPUTERS than by their superset OCAM, which is supposed to include all computers as well as certain other goods. Many office workers might be tempted to conclude that the additional goods in OCAM, such as hand calculators and typewriters, actually subtract from welfare. However, a simpler explanation exists. As mentioned in §3, the price index used for COMPUTER was more recent and showed substantially greater improvements in price/performance than the one used for computers in OCAM. According to Allan Young, the director of the BEA, the government eventually intends to adopt the COMPUTER price index, created by Cartwright (1986), for OCAM as well. When this is done, we would expect the estimates for consumers' surplus from OCAM to increase commensurately.

The differences in surplus calculations give some indication of how sensitive welfare calculations are to accurate estimates of the true costs of computer power. The problem is even more severe when one considers that even the Cartwright price index may substantially underestimate changes in prices and welfare to the extent that the introduction of new types of IT is ignored (Hausman 1994, Trajtenberg 1989). $^{22}$

## 4.3. Sensitivity Analysis

An interesting type of sensitivity analysis considers an outward shift in the entire demand curve. In particular, our estimates do not include any parameter to account for diffusion of the technology: we attributed the increase in quantities purchased over time entirely to lower prices and greater income. It is interesting to consider how much our results would change if we attribute some of the growth in IT to diffusion. To examine how this could affect our results, we assumed that COMPUTER and OCAM would have grown by 2% per year even if the price had been constant. The net effect of this assumption is to shift the demand curve outward from what we observed. The top, which is based on data from 20 years ago, is shifted out far more (i.e., by about 50%) than the bottom, which being based on more recent purchase decisions presumably already reflects past diffusion. This implies that the true price elasticity of demand is lower (in absolute value) than what we estimated. Under this assumption, the consumers' surplus from computers is found to increase by about 20%.

A related question is whether improved software products (and other complementary goods) are partially responsible for the increased demand for hardware and vice-versa. While software is often thought of as experiencing relatively slow progress compared to hardware, this caricature is misleading. $^{23}$ Furthermore, some software products, such as VisiCalc and Lotus 1-2-3, have been credited with substantially fueling the demand for early microcomputers, suggesting a strong complementarity. $^{24}$ As with diffusion, if the demand curve for computers has shifted outward over time because of the availability of better software, then we have underestimated the consumers' surplus from the declining price of computers, perhaps substantially.

More generally, the formulae we used to calculate consumers' surplus all presume that all other prices in the economy are unaffected by the lower prices of computers. Clearly, this assumption is false for software. Because of high fixed costs and low marginal costs, the unit costs of software decline as demand increases—Microsoft can spread the development costs of an improved version of Excel over millions of users today when PCs are cheap and powerful, but only a few hundred thousand in the mid-1980s. Therefore, the lower quality-adjusted prices of packaged software, $^{25}$ and the resulting increase in consumer surplus, should properly also be attributed to improvements in computer hardware. Given the relative expenditures on hardware and software in the U.S. economy, this effect may be on the same order of magnitude as the direct effect. $^{26}$

A third type of sensitivity test is to relax the fundamental assumption of consumers' surplus estimation that the marginal value to the purchasers of a good is equal to the price the purchasers were willing to pay for the last unit. If for some reason (the modeling of which is beyond the scope of this paper) managers systematically spend too much on IT, $^{27}$ then by definition they would not be getting their money's worth for the last units they buy. However, the infra-marginal units could still be adding to welfare. To be specific, suppose that OCAM and COMPUTERS only generated 80 cents of value for each dollar spent on the margin. This is consistent with an estimate by Morrison and Berndt (1990) regarding the marginal productivity of "high tech" capital in manufacturing industries. This would shift the demand curve downward by 20% and thereby reduce the gross value (consumers' surplus plus expenditures) created by OCAM and by COMPUTERS to about \$80 billion. Under this scenario, we find that OCAM and COMPUTERS would still be adding \$40 billion to \$50 billion more to welfare than they cost, for a net gain. Interestingly, since computer prices decline by over 20% per year, the same applications of computers that generated only 80 cents on the dollar in one year would be productive investments in the following year, assuming that the same benefits were still achievable. One year's dumb idea—putting a computer on every secretary's desk, or assembling a central database of customer orders from thousands of separate stores—could be very cost-effective a few years later with a new generation of cheaper computers.

In fact, if computers and OCAM delivered on average only half of the benefits that managers expected when they made their investments, there would still be a gain from computerization of between \$10 billion and \$20 billion in 1987. Our consumers' surplus estimates indicate that computers and OCAM would have resulted in a net positive contribution to the U.S. economy unless managers have consistently been getting an average of less than 25 cents for each marginal dollar invested in COMPUTERS and 40 cents for each marginal dollar invested in OCAM.

Of course, it is also possible that managers have been doing better than breaking even on their marginal investments in IT. According to Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1996), there is evidence of excess returns to computers in the 1987–1991 time period for their sample of 380 large firms. Assuming that the price of computer capital—including depreciation, taxes, and other costs amount to 40% per year $^{28}$ —their estimates suggest that computers would still create more than 1.20 of additional net output for each dollar invested on the margin. This suggests that actual consumers' surplus from OCAM and COMPUTERS could have exceeded 100 billion annually.

The consumers' surplus approach assumes that all of the benefits of computerization accrue to the purchasers. Recent theoretical work by Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (1995) suggests that there may be positive externalities to investments in high-tech equipment like computers because they are "general purpose technologies" that enable other types of innovations such as new technologies and techniques for manufacturing, banking, or managing. To the extent that this is the case, the consumers' surplus approach will underestimate the true social contribution of IT (as would production function and business value metric approaches). Furthermore, if there are positive network externalities from greater IT use in the economy (e.g., my email, web browser, EDI system, and even spreadsheet become more valuable as other people adopt compatible systems), then the consumers' surplus will also underestimate the total contributions of the technology. It is difficult to estimate the magnitude of network externalities for IT in general, but work by Brynjolfsson and Kemerer (1996) suggests that network externalities accounted for over one third of the value of a spreadsheet like Lotus 1-2-3. There may also be negative externalities associated with IT proliferation (a more impersonal, technocentric world?), but on balance it seems likely that the consumers' surplus approach underestimated the total social benefits of IT.

Finally, we also examined several base years other than 1987. In each case, the value of consumers' surplus was about three times expenditures for COMPUTERS and about equal to expenditures for OCAM. This consistency can be attributed to the fact that when income effects are ignored, consumer surplus from a log-linear demand function will always be equal to a constant $(1/(1+\alpha))$ times the expenditure on the good.

One implication of this result is that from the 1960s through the mid-1980s, computer spending and computer surplus were relatively small compared to total GDP, and therefore were not likely to have had measurable effects, one way or the other, on the “productivity slowdown” at the level of the whole economy. Thus, expectations of a technological “silver bullet” for the productivity slowdown were a bit premature. This is consistent with the modest contemporaneous claims of James Finke, who was president of a leading computer firm (Commodore International, Ltd.) in 1982: “The personal computer market is about the same size as the total potato chip market. Next year it will be about half the size of the pet food market and is fast approaching the total worldwide sales of pantyhose.” $^{29}$

Computers continue to account for a relatively small share of total capital stock—far less than, say, railroads in 1890. However, rapid technological progress gives them a disproportionate impact on welfare. In the past few years, computer spending has grown to be the largest single category of business equipment investment, suggesting that its impacts even on output and productivity statistics for the U.S. economy as a whole should be increasingly visible. Specifically, our calculations indicate that computerization added about 0.2% to 0.3% to GDP growth each year in the 1980s, when computer spending was equal to about 1% of GDP. This contribution is a substantial share of the overall increase in GDP of 2.4% per year (Mohr 1992). The increased output from computers can also be compared with officially reported increases in output per hour which averaged 1% in services and 2.3% in commodities production in the 1980s (Griliches 1992).

In future years, assuming continued progress at historical rates, this analysis indicates that improvements in computers will increase output annually by an amount equal to 20–30% of each year's computer spending. The growth in computer spending and surplus has been essentially exponential in the past; if this trend continues, we can expect a continuing increase in the absolute value of consumers' surplus in the future. For example, if one extrapolates the trends in computer spending, and assumes GDP growth of 2% per year, one can estimate that by 1997, total consumers' surplus from COMPUTERS will be about \$250 billion per year. Of course, all exponential trends eventually tail off and typically assume more of an S-curve shape.

## 5. Conclusion

## 5.1. Summary

This paper presents some estimates of the contribution of IT to consumers' surplus. We applied four different methodologies to data from two different sources and conducted a number of sensitivity analyses. Our estimates indicate that in 1987, OCAM and COMPUTERS added between \$50 billion and \$70 billion to consumer welfare, after expenditures were subtracted, and that the contribution is growing steadily over time. Our estimates using the different methods were fairly consistent with one another and reasonably robust to changes in the underlying assumptions or data.

This approach provides a new perspective on the IT value debate. Improvements in IT lead to large annual declines in its effective price, and as a result, new uses become productive, and old uses become more productive. Our calculations indicate that these improvements in IT ultimately added about 0.2% to 0.3% U.S. GDP and productivity growth in 1987, a substantial, though not dominant, share of overall productivity growth. In recent years, as IT has become a larger share of GDP, this same approach suggests that IT improvements account for as much as 0.5 percentage point of real productivity growth. To put this in perspective, between 1990 and 1994, total output per hour in the U.S. Economy grew by about 1% per year (Council of Economic Advisors 1995). However, because the benefits of IT are often in areas like improved quality, speed, variety, customization and coordination, and because 80% of IT is used in the service sector (especially finance, insurance, and real estate) where output is poorly measured, it is likely that a substantial share of the productivity benefits are not reflected in official productivity statistics.

It should be emphasized, however, that this methodology does not directly address the question of whether managers and consumers are purchasing the right quantity of IT, but rather takes their preferences as given. For instance, the discrepancy between the large consumers' surplus implied by the methodology used in this study and the minimal productivity impact found in some other studies may be due not only to mismeasurement of output, but also to systematic overconsumption of IT by managers.

## 5.2. Extensions

The estimates derived should be considered only a first step toward determining the consumers' surplus from IT. There are several extensions that can be pursued in future papers.

First, computers are a complementary input for software, telecommunications, the information in databases, and even business process redesign. Since each of these variables has grown rapidly over time, theory suggests that part of their growth may be fairly attributed to price declines in computers. In principle, the welfare effects of this growth, and the share that is related to computer price declines, can be calculated. The key will be estimating equations such as 10'a and 10'b:

$$
\log q _ {i t} = \gamma_ {t} + \alpha_ {t} \log p _ {i t} + \beta_ {t} \log p _ {j t} + \delta_ {t} \log y _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t},\tag{10'a}
$$

$$
\log q _ {j t} = \gamma_ {j} + \alpha_ {j} \log p _ {i t} + \beta_ {j} \log p _ {j t} + \delta_ {j} \log y _ {j t} + \epsilon_ {j t},\tag{10'b}
$$

where $p_{jt}$ is the price of the other good, j, such as software. If $\beta_{t}$ is negative and large, then the other good is a strong complement for hardware. If the price of good j has fallen substantially over time, then $\alpha$ , the own-price elasticity of computers is likely to have been overestimated and the magnitude of consumers' surplus growth is likely to have been underestimated. More generally, a system of equations could be set up which simultaneously estimates the demand for both hardware and software (and perhaps other related goods), enabling more accurate estimates of consumers' surplus in this area of the economy. Regrettably, lack of available data poses a major constraint for such an exercise. Interested researchers will likely have to develop new, private sources for the necessary data. $^{30}$

Second, surplus from derived demand is a good estimate of final consumers' surplus if markets are competitive and there are no externalities. However, the extent of monopoly power and technological spillovers can be explicitly estimated with other data and used to improve the welfare estimates.

Third, it would be interesting to compare further the contribution from IT implied by the consumers' surplus estimates with the contribution implied by other techniques, such as production functions, for specific industries. Any differences found may shed light on the nature of measurement error, or other problems with one or the other method.

Finally, the techniques used in this paper could be readily applied to many other IT innovations, enabling researchers to get a sense of which innovations have contributed the most to economic welfare. Software and telecommunications have also experienced significant price declines. Numerous entirely new technologies have been introduced. Where these new technologies are embedded in other products, but not sold separately, their implicit prices can be determined using hedonic techniques enabling the same sort of welfare analysis applied for goods with directly measured prices and quantities. $^{31}$
