# Effects of Communication Mode and Predisclusion Information Distribution Characteristics on Information Exchange in Groups

Ross Hightower • Lutfus Sayeed

Department of Management, Kansas State University, College of Business, Manhattan, Kansas 66506 rth@business.cba.ksu.edu

Department of Business Analysis and Computing Systems, College of Business, San Francisco State University,
San Francisco, California 94132
lsayced@sfsu.edu

One advantage of groups is that they have access to a larger pool of expertise and knowledge than individual group members. However, groups are sometimes ineffective at exchanging information. This tendency has been called biased discussion. The present study examines the effects of communication mode (face-to-face vs. computer mediated), and Prediscussion information distribution characteristics on biased discussion. Biased discussion was found to occur to a greater degree when communication mode was computer-mediated, and the group members were not in conflict prior to the discussion.

(Computer Mediated Communication Systems (CMCS); Group Support Systems (GSS); Information Sharing)

## Introduction

This paper examines how effectively groups exchange information when they use a computer mediated communication system (CMCS). Although much research has been conducted on computer-mediated communications among group members, very little of this research has explicitly addressed information exchange among members of CMCS groups (cf. McGrath and Hollingshead 1994). The few studies that have addressed information exchange have suggested that CMCS adversely affects information exchange (Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Hollingshead 1993). This has important implications because exchanging information is a fundamental group process that affects how well the group functions.

One benefit of CMCS is that group members can communicate even when they cannot be at the same location at the same time. However, CMCS imposes limitations on communication. People rely on multiple communication modalities in face-to-face conversation such as paraverbal (tone of voice, inflection) and nonverbal (body language) cues. CMCS precludes such communication modalities to a varying extent depending on the type of CMCS. For example, electronic mail prevents both paraverbal and nonverbal cues.

The effects of the reduced communication modalities on CMCS group members and the circumstances in which these effects occur has been the focus of much of the CMCS research (cf. McGrath and Hollingshead 1994). Although not definitive in terms of specific effects, the research in this area suggests that CMCS groups communicate differently than face-to-face groups (Hightower and Hagmann 1995, Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Hiltz et al. 1986, Kiesler and Sproull 1992,

McGrath and Hollingshead 1994, Siegal et al. 1986, Wiseband 1992, Wiseband et al. 1995). Based on the previous literature, the present study examines how conflicting prediscussion preferences affects information exchange in CMCS versus face-to-face groups.

The present paper is organized as follows. First, information exchange in face-to-face groups is discussed followed by a discussion on information exchange in CMCS groups. The hypotheses of the study are postulated based on these discussions. The experiment is then described, followed by a presentation of the findings and discussion of the results.

## Information Exchange in Face-to-Face Groups

One motivation for forming groups to solve problems is that they often have access to a larger pool of information than individuals. Not only can group members help one another recall information that an individual member may have overlooked, they may also bring unique information previously unknown to others in the group. Thus, groups are potentially able to make more informed decisions than individuals. However, two factors influence whether a group can realize the potential advantage of making a more informed decision.

The first factor is how much unique information group members bring to the group; an information item is unique when it is known to only one member. The group gains little or no information advantage if all the available information is common (information known to all group members). The more unique information there is, the more a group can realize, through information exchange, the potential advantage of making a more informed decision than the individual group members.

The second factor is how effectively the group exchanges information. Group members must recall common information and bring unique information to light to make the most of their information advantage. However, studies of information exchange in face-to-face groups suggest that these groups failed to take advantage of their information pool (Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Stasser et al. 1989, Stasser and Titus 1985 and 1987).

Stasser and his colleagues have developed a useful method for studying information exchange in face-to-face groups (Stasser et al. 1985, 1987, 1989). In these studies, group members were provided with several information items on a number of alternatives from which they must choose the "best" alternative. The provided information items are manipulated so that some are common and some are unique. The groups then discuss the alternatives and attempt to come to a consensus as a group on which is the best alternative. Some of the factors that affect information exchange that have been studied by Stasser and his colleagues are group size, information load, proportion of unique information, structured vs. unstructured discussion, and initial information bias of the information provided to the groups.

These studies have shown that face-to-face groups rarely exchange information effectively. Unique information is often not brought to light and groups base their evaluations of the alternatives on only a portion of the evidence available to them. This may lead the group to make suboptimal decisions if important information is not considered. Stasser et al. (1985, 1987, 1989) have explained group discussion in terms of an information sampling model. According to this model, in deciding what information to discuss, group members sample from the information available to them. Two factors affect whether the group will discuss specific information items: how many people know the information, and the likelihood that anyone will mention it.

The more people who know an information item, the more likely one of them will recall it and mention it. Thus, the likelihood that a specific information item will be mentioned depends on the number of people in the group who know it. Common information, since it is known by all group members, is more likely to be mentioned than unique information. Content analysis of group discussion has shown that common information is indeed more likely to be mentioned than unique information (Gigone and Hastie 1993). As group size increases, the likelihood of common information being mentioned increases relative to that for unique information. Gigone and Hastie's study (1993) found that common information had a greater influence than unique information on the group's decision.

The second factor that affects whether an information item will be mentioned is whether anyone will mention it. For a person to mention an information item the person must recall it, have the opportunity to mention it, and decide to mention it.

One factor that affects information recall is how often a person is exposed to an information item (Stasser et al. 1989). The more a person is exposed to the information item the more easily (s)he will recall it. Stasser et al. found that if a group member mentioned a common information item during discussion, the group would be likely to repeat the item later during the discussion. In contrast, even when mentioned, unique information was rarely repeated. Stasser et al. speculated that when a group member mentions common information, (s)he increases the information item's salience to other group members since they were exposed to it previously. In contrast, when a group member mentions a unique information item, it is the first time that most of the group members have been exposed to it. Therefore, even after being mentioned, unique information is less salient than common information and less likely to be recalled later in the discussion or after discussion.

Other factors that affect information recall are individual preference, amount of information, and familiarity with the information. Preference consistent information is more easily recalled (Stasser et al. 1987). Similarly, the more familiar a person is with an information item the more likely s(he) will recall it. Also, a large amount of information reduces the likelihood of any one item being recalled (Stasser et al. 1989).

The opportunity to mention information is affected by a number of factors. Time constraints or a high information load may reduce the opportunities to mention an information item. Group discussions tend to follow themes, rehashing the same information until a new theme arises (Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973). The time available for information exchange may not be used efficiently. Also, the fact that only one person can talk at a time limits the amount of information exchanged (Diehl and Stroebe 1987). When time is limited and/or information load is high, some of the information may never be discussed.

Social influences can also limit a group member's ability to mention information during discussion. Lower status group members have fewer opportunities to mention information items (Weisband et al. 1995).

Having recalled an information item and given the opportunity, a person must still decide to mention it. An individual may decide not to mention an information item for a variety of reasons. People are often reluctant to mention information that counters the prevailing sentiment in the group (Hartwick et al. 1982, Myers and Lamm 1976). They may bias their comments to match what they perceive as the preferences of their audience. As mentioned above, the first person who speaks often has an inordinate influence on the group's discussion and ultimate decision. Also, people tend to take an advocacy role with respect to their own preferences and are less likely to mention information that undermines those preferences (Gigone and Hastie 1993, Stasser and Titus 1985).

The importance of prediscussion preferences was illustrated by Gigone and Hastie (1993). In their study, specific information items had little influence on the groups' final decisions when the individual group members' prediscussion preferences were held constant. The authors suggest that groups regard group discussion as a means to negotiate consensus rather than to share information.

The net result of all these influences is that common information tends to dominate group discussion. Common information is more likely to be mentioned because it is known to more people. This information becomes more salient to others in the group because it confirms something they already know. The chance of unique information being discussed decreases as the amount of common information increases. Group members will mention information that supports their own predispositions initially. Information exchange is likely to be less efficient when the group members' prediscussion preferences are similar (Stasser and Stewart 1992). As a group consensus emerges, group members bias their comments to support the emerging consensus, and the information supporting this consensus becomes relatively more salient than nonsupporting information. Stasser and Titus (1985) called this phenomenon "biased discussion."

The distribution of the information available to the group is important because it determines the relative amounts of common information and also because it affects the predispositions of the group members. Information items available to a group can be distributed in three ways: common, partially common, or unique. Common information is known to all members of the group; partially common information is known to only a subset of group members; while unique information is known by only one member. The present study is concerned with situations in which each information item is either common (known by all) or unique (known by one).

Even with this simplification a variety of possible information distributions may occur. Table 1a shows a situation in which a group faces three alternatives: A, B, and C. Alternative A is still the optimal choice when all the information is considered. However, the information available to each group member is biased toward alternative B. In this situation the potential value of information exchange is high since the group will not be able to choose the optimal alternative (i.e., A) unless they pool the unique information and discover the hidden profile. Previous research suggests that groups rarely decipher the hidden profile because their discussion fails to correct the group members' biased views of the information pool and the group's final choice is based on only a portion of the available information (Stasser and Titus 1987).

## CMCS and Information Exchange

Three characteristics of CMCS affect information exchange in groups: reduced communication modalities, more uninhibited communication, and more equal participation (McGrath and Hollingshead 1994).

## Reduced Communication Modalities

Many of the cues present in face-to-face discussion that help regulate the flow of conversation, provide feedback, and convey subtle meanings are not present in CMCS groups. The absence of such cues reduces the amount and richness of the information discussed (Hightower and Hagmann 1995, Hightower and Sayeed 1995, McGrath and Hollingshead 1994).

A number of studies have shown that the total amount of information discussed in CMCS groups is less than that in face-to-face groups (Hightower and Hagmann 1995, Hiltz et al. 1986, Siegal et al. 1986). Without paraverbal and nonverbal cues group members are not able to duplicate the normal give-and-take of face-to-face discussion. Comments appear sometimes to be out of context or the conversation may appear to lack focus because multiple group members are “talking” at once. This is exacerbated by the fact that people type and read at different rates. Group members who type slowly or edit more thoroughly may find their comments are no longer relevant when they are ready to transmit them. Moreover, because every group member can transmit their comments simultaneously, group members may be required to process a large number of comments in a short period of time.

Another factor that reduces the amount of information that a CMCS group can discuss is simply that users must type their comments. Siegal et al. (1986) attributed part of the inefficiency they observed to the fact that using a keyboard to enter comments takes longer and requires more effort than speaking.

The lack of paraverbal and nonverbal cues also reduces the richness of the information transmitted by CMCS group members Daft and Lengel (1986) define media richness as “the ability of information to change understanding within a time interval.” The richness of a medium is determined primarily by the communication modalities it allows. Rich media allow multiple information cues (the words spoken, tone of voice, body language, etc.) and feedback. It takes more effort by group members to achieve the same level of mutual understanding in a lean medium, such as CMCS, than in a rich one, such as face-to-face communication.

The effect of reduced amount of information exchange and lower media richness is that CMCS groups may find it more difficult to communicate than face-to-face groups. CMCS groups usually take longer to complete tasks and are less likely than face-to-face groups to reach a consensus (McGrath and Hollingshead 1994). CMCS groups have also been found to spend more time coordinating their activities relative to face-to-face groups (Hightower and Sayeed 1995). These factors may make it difficult for CMCS groups to exchange information effectively.

## Increased Uninhibited Communication

Research has shown that group members are more likely to express their opinions and engage in extreme behavior toward others in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups (Kiesler and Sproull 1992). Siegal et al. (1986) suggest that “anonymity and lowered salience of social controls . . . leads to feelings of loss of identity and uninhibited behavior” (p. 183). Group members are more likely to respond to others emotionally in CMCS groups because they concentrate more on the content of messages than on the message senders.

One result of uninhibited communication is that conflicts are typically more pronounced in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups (Kiesler and Sproull 1992). In face-to-face groups when the first member speaks, succeeding speakers tend to agree with the first person. This is because group members are often reluctant to contradict the prevailing sentiment in the group (Hartwick et al. 1982). However, this is not the case in CMCS groups. CMCS group members contradict one another more readily because they feel freer to express opinions (Weisband 1992) A willingness to contradict one another and respond emotionally often leads to deeper conflicts in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups.

The effect of uninhibited communication on information exchange is probably positive within certain bounds. A lively debate would presumably increase information exchange but name calling, or “flaming,” may reduce task-oriented information exchange.

## Increased Participation by Group Members

A third CMCS characteristic that may promote information exchange is that CMCS have been found to promote more equal participation by group members (Kiesler and Sproull 1992). In face-to-face conversation when one person speaks, others must listen. When one person, or a few people, dominate the discussion others don't get the opportunity to speak. People who are more garrulous or who have higher social status tend to dominate group discussion (Weisband et al. 1995). With CMCS, group members can "talk" at the same time, so every group member has equal opportunity. CMCS may also reduce the effect of differing social status because they mask physical and social cues by which people judge others (Clapper et al. 1991, George et al. 1990; see Weisband et al. 1995 for contradictory evidence).

The extent to which these three CMCS characteristics affect information exchange probably depends on the task faced by the group. The communication difficulty that CMCS groups experience because of reduced communication modalities can have a detrimental effect on information exchange. Siegal et al. (1986) suggest that CMCS group members may sometimes get frustrated trying to support their viewpoint and will simply state their preference with minimal supporting information. This type of behavior would tend to reduce the efficiency of information exchange. Previous studies suggest that information exchange is less efficient in groups using a CMCS (Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Hollingshead 1993). Thus, our first hypothesis is:

HYPOTHESIS 1. CMCS groups will exchange information less efficiently than face-to-face groups.

As found in previous experiments, a higher proportion of common information will lead to less efficient information exchange (Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Stasser et al. 1985, 1987, 1989, 1992). Thus, Hypothesis 2 is:

HYPOTHESIS 2. More common information will lead to less efficient information exchange in all groups.

The presence of conflicting prediscussion preferences will increase the efficiency of information exchange. This is true for CMCS as well as face-to-face groups. Thus, Hypothesis 3 is:

HYPOTHESIS 3. The existence of opposing prediscussion preferences will lead to more efficient information exchange in all groups.

However, with conflicting prediscussion preferences, the effect on information exchange would be more pronounced in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups, since CMCS group members would feel freer to contradict one another and participation is more equal in CMCS groups. Therefore, our fourth hypothesis is:

HYPOTHESIS 4. The existence of opposing prediscussion preferences will lead to a greater improvement in information exchange in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups.

The effect of conflicting prediscussion preferences will also be affected by the proportion of common information. When most of the information is common, the differences among the alternatives is relatively minor. Thus, group members will feel less certain of their prediscussion choices when most of the information is common. Because it appears that the information they have is the same as others, group members will be more easily swayed by the arguments of others in the group. However, with a higher proportion of unique information the group members may feel more strongly about their prediscussion choice. With less common information available, unique information will play a larger role in the discussion and will be remembered, since group members will emphasize it in trying to make their cases stronger. Thus our final hypothesis is:

HYPOTHESIS 5. The existence of opposing prediscussion preferences will lead to a greater improvement in information exchange in groups with less common information.

## The Study

## The Task

We adapted a case from one described by Pfeiffer and Jones (1977). The case involves choosing the most likely suspect in a murder mystery. This case was chosen rather than a business oriented case because it does not require any functional knowledge such as accounting, finance, marketing, etc. The case can be solved using simple logic. The subjects were supplied the case description and descriptions of three suspects. The subjects were told the descriptions were the result of an initial investigation.

The case description was a half page in length and mentioned eight attributes considered important for identifying the murderer. The suspect descriptions listed attributes about the suspects that were consistent with the suspect having committed the crime (an incriminating attribute) and attributes that were not consistent with the suspect having committed the crime (a vindicating attribute). Table 2 shows examples of incriminating and vindicating attributes that coincide with two characteristics of the case description. Suspect C had five incriminating attributes and three vindicating attributes. Both suspects A and B had four incriminating attributes and four vindicating attributes.

The subjects provided two types of information before and after the group discussion. First, they indicated who they thought was the most likely suspect and rated the certainty of this preference on a seven-point Likert scale before the discussion (Very Uncertain–Very Certain). Second, they recalled as many incriminating and vindicating attributes of each candidate as they could. The subjects recalled an average of 90% of the items available to them on the pretest. The same information was collected after the discussion.

## Subjects

The subjects, who were upper division undergraduates at a large state university, completed the experiment as part of a course requirement. The grade they received was based, in part, on their participation in the experiment. The subjects were randomly assigned to three-person groups. Three of the groups had members who failed to appear at their assigned time and make up times could not be scheduled. This created an unbalanced design with a sample size of 87. There were 55 males and 32 females (15 face-to-face groups and 14 CMCS groups).

## The CMCS

The software used was the Electronic Discussion System (EDS) developed at the University of Arizona (Pendergast 1989). EDS is a synchronous computer conferencing system (SCCS). SCCS permits group members to communicate by typing their comments into a computer and then broadcasting the comments to other group members. The system is interactive in the sense that group members "meet" at the same time although they are separated physically. EDS allows synchronous conferencing and voting. The voting feature was not used in this study. The interface consists of two windows. In one window, comments made by the group members appear and scroll up the screen. In the other, the group members enter and edit their comments before broadcasting them.

## Procedure

After we assigned subjects to groups, we gave them a brief explanation of their task and then gave them the case description and the reports on each suspect. The subjects were not told the identities of other members in their group. They kept the case materials for one night so that they could study it. The instructions we gave to the groups pointed out that each group member might have different information about the suspects. We asked that the subjects not discuss the experiment with anyone.

When the subjects arrived the following day, they returned the case material and were given the pretest. After each group member had completed the pretest, they were told their task was to discuss the three suspects and to choose the most likely suspect. No time limit was mentioned. The most likely suspect was defined as the one whose attributes most closely matched the characteristics of the case. The CMCS groups were given a brief introduction to the software at this point. Care was taken so that the CMCS group members would not know the identities of other members in their group. When the groups had reached a decision they were given the post-test.

## The Dependent Variables

Two dependent variables were used. The first is a measure of the extent to which groups tended to concentrate on a single alternative and is called discussion diversity. The second is the amount of unique information group members learned during discussion and is called unique information gained.

Discussion Diversity. The first dependent variable was adapted from Stasser and Titus (1985 and 1987). Their dependent variable was based on the following argument. Stasser's model predicts that the group will focus on information items that support their preferred alternative including incriminating information about their preferred alternative (i.e., the murder suspect) and vindicating information about the other alternatives. The incriminating information about the group member's preferred alternative and vindicating information about the other alternatives become more salient relative to non-supporting information. Thus, we measured four categories of information items gained through discussion, by identifying those attributes appearing on the post-test that did not appear on the pretest:

The first dependent variable used in the analysis was computed by summing items two and three and subtracting the sum of items one and four. The discussion diversity variable is a measurement of one of the effects of biased discussion predicted by Stasser and his colleagues. As such it is an indirect measure of the efficiency of information exchange.

Unique Information Gained. The second dependent variable is the proportion of unique information items group members reported after discussion that they could not have known prior to the discussion. It is calculated by first counting the number of unique items on the subjects' post test that they could not have known without information exchange. This number is then divided by the number of items that were unique to them. In the 33%-common distribution this number is either 10 or 11, and for the 66%-common subjects it was either 4 or 6 (see Table 4). This is a more direct measure of how effectively the group members exchanged information than the discussion diversity variable. The only way they could have known these items was through information exchange during group discussion. As with the discussion diversity variable, the more biased discussion, a smaller proportion of unique information would be gained. We called this variable the proportion of unique information gained during discussion.

## Discussion

At least for the type of task used in this study, CMCS groups exchange information less efficiently than face-to-face groups. This effect was unequivocal and was not affected by the presence of conflicting prediscussion preferences or variations in the proportion of unique information. The information exchange task used in this and other experiments is simplified and may represent a best-case scenario for information exchange in CMCS groups compared to face-to-face groups. All of the experiments conducted thus far have used an information pool that was simply a list of information items (Gigone and Hastie 1993, Hightower and Sayeed 1995, Stasser and Stewart 1992, Stasser et al. 1989, Stasser and Titus 1985 and 1987). In a more realistic task the information distribution would contain ambiguous and irrelevant information, or some effort would be required on the part of the group members to extract the information. This would complicate the information exchange process since it may involve explaining the meaning and significance of information to the other group members rather than simply conveying facts. The relative leanness of the medium would tend to make explaining complex or ambiguous information a more difficult task for CMCS groups than for face-to-face groups.

The presence of conflicting prediscussion preferences improved information exchange in all groups. This result is not surprising since it confirms that conflict, when it is not personal, improves team performance (Brightman 1988). Techniques such as nominal groups and devil's advocacy are designed to foster conflict to force the group's members to be more thorough in their analysis of a problem. This finding also suggests that groups with members who have different viewpoints may be more efficient at exchanging information than those whose members have similar views.

However, conflicting prediscussion preferences did not improve information exchange more in CMCS groups than in face-to-face groups as we expected. Examination of the cell means shows that conflicting pre-discussion preferences improved information exchange dramatically in face-to-face groups, and very little in CMCS groups. This suggests that increased uninhibited communication and more equal participation had little effect on information exchange in CMCS groups. It may be that even though group members are more likely to contradict one another in CMCS groups, they are unable to resolve conflicts sufficiently due to the difficulty they have communicating. Groups may resort to means other than information exchange to settle conflicts. CMCS group members have been found to rely more on emotional and less on informational means to influence one another than face-to-face group members (Hightower and Hagmann 1995, Hollingshead 1993).

However, we must be careful in concluding too much from the results of this experiment. Using a CMCS was a unique experience for most of these students. It is likely that people would become more effective using a CMCS with practice (Hollingshead et al. 1993). Frequent users of discussion forums on the Internet and on-line services develop ways to convey more meaning in their messages as a means of replacing paraverbal and nonverbal cues. The use of symbols called “emoticons” is on example (McGrath and Hollingshead 1994). One needs only to spend some time reading the messages posted on some of the more controversial on-line forums to determine that quite detailed and emotional discussions are possible. Asynchronous media, such as e-mail or electronic bulletin boards, are more conducive to carefully constructed arguments than a synchronous conferencing system as was used in this experiment.

Information exchange was not less efficient for groups having a higher proportion of common information. Neither dependent variable showed a significant effect. The effect of common information on the discussion diversity variable was affected by the presence of conflicting prediscussion preferences. As expected, conflict groups with less common information had more diverse discussions.

Finally, the results of the present study must be evaluated taking into consideration the limitations of the experiment. First, the members of the CMCS groups were not aware of who was in their group. The effects of anonymity may confound the effects of the CMCS medium and it would be impossible to determine from this experiment the relative effects of these two factors. The second limitation is that these were student groups who met for the first time for this experiment and who had little if any experience using a CMCS. These limitations are obvious and efforts should be made to extend the research by using ongoing groups and working managers with more CMCS experience.

## Future Research

Future research can extend the present findings in several ways. Improving information exchange in groups means increasing the likelihood that a group member will mention an information item. The effects of group interventions such as nominal group technique and Delphi techniques are reflected in the value of this likelihood. Systems with similar capabilities have been called level 2 GDSS (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987). Something as simple as a “blackboard” where information that is mentioned could be posted might be beneficial (Gallupe and DeSanctis 1988, Gallupe and McKeen 1990). This would ensure that when unshared information is mentioned it will be remembered (Stasser et al. 1990).

Another avenue is to investigate the effects of other characteristics of the available information pool on information exchange. For example Stasser et al. (1990) suggest that in a real-world setting group members may be aware that their membership is based on the information they bring to the group. For example, an expert is aware that s(he) is part of the group to share his or her expertise and is more likely to share what s(he) knows. Also, group members may know what information is unique and can be more efficient in exchanging it. On the other hand, an expert may choose not to share certain information for a variety of reasons. Future research may investigate whether factors such as communication mode, conflict and distribution affect an expert's information sharing.

Finally, studies should be conducted that include a temporal element. Studies should examine how information exchange changes as the group's dynamics change over time. Also, the effect of familiarity with the communication medium should be studied.

## Conclusion

This study provided powerful evidence that information exchange is more difficult for groups using a CMCS than for face-to-face groups. This difference was not mitigated by the presence of conflict. Our experience is that group members are unaware that they did not exchange information efficiently. This raises some concerns about relying heavily on CMCS when efficient information exchange is desirable. Yet, end users report that information exchange is one of the primary uses of CMCS (Kim 1995). Also, with the increasing popularity of the Internet and the accompanying awareness of computer-mediated forms of communication, CMCS will likely become more pervasive than they are now. IS researchers and practitioners need to explore two issues in this regard. The first is to match different types of tasks with appropriate CMCS. The second issue is to explore how to improve CMCS design to facilitate information exchange in these tasks.
