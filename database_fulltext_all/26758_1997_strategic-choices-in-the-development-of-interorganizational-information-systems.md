# Strategic Choices in the Development of Interorganizational Information Systems

Vivek Choudhury

226 Mervis Hall, Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260
vivekc@vms.cis.pitt.edu

This paper discusses two critical strategic choices that a firm must make in developing inter-organizational information systems (IOISs)—what kind(s) of IOIS(s) to develop, and how to develop the IOIS(s). With respect to the kinds of IOISs, it proposes a typology of three different types of IOISs: electronic monopolies, electronic dyads, and multilateral IOISs. With respect to development approach, a firm can develop an IOIS competitively or cooperatively. Based on a case study of the development of IOISs in the aircraft parts industry, two sets of transaction characteristics—demand uncertainty and market variability—were found to influence the choice of IOISs. Two factors—strategic significance of the IOIS, and size and bargaining power of the firm—were found to influence the choice of development approach. The observations from the case study are used to develop a set of propositions about when a firm will choose a particular IOIS and whether a cooperative or a competitive approach will be used.

(Interorganizational Information Systems; Aircraft Parts Industry; Transaction Characteristics; Cooperation and Competition)

## 1. Introduction

Interorganizational information systems (IOISs) are automated information systems shared by two or more companies (Cash and Konsynski 1985). Well cited examples of IOISs include American Hospital Supply Corporation's ASAP (Petre 1985, Pillsbury 1982, Short and Venkatraman 1992), Foremost-McKesson's ECONOMOST (Clemons and Row 1988), and American Airline's reservation system SABRE (Copeland and McKenney 1988). Electronic data interchange (EDI), or the electronic transfer of business documents in standard format, which is one kind of IOIS, is becoming an increasingly common way of doing business.

There is, however, considerable variation among IOISs, as illustrated by even the small sample cited above. ASAP and ECONOMOST are both on-line order entry systems designed to link customers with their sponsors—in fact, users of ECONOMOST typically are linked only to McKesson. SABRE, on the other hand, makes it easier for users to compare the offerings of multiple airlines. It is reasonable to expect these different IOISs to have very different impacts on participating firms and industries. How does a firm decide which IOISs are appropriate for its particular situation? This question has been largely unaddressed in the literature. Similarly, in the development of EDI systems, some firms, for instance, in the grocery (Little 1980) and trucking industries (Kavan and Van Over 1990), voluntarily initiated cooperative efforts to agree on industry-wide standards for message formats, while others, for instance, some firms in the retail (HBS 1991) and insurance (HBS 1987) industries, adopted proprietary formats. What motivated these different choices?

The above discussion suggests that there are at least two critical choices a firm must make in developing an IOIS strategy: (i) what kind(s) of IOIS(s) to develop, and (ii) the development approach, or how to develop the IOIS(s)? Our objective in this paper is to draw on a case study of the development of IOISs in the aircraft parts industry to develop specific, empirically-derived propositions about the two choices.

We begin, in §2, with a simple typology of IOISs that identifies the options available to a firm in answering the first question. The typology includes three IOISs: electronic monopolies, electronic dyads, and multilateral IOISs. Section 3 discusses the distinctive benefits of each IOIS, specifically, the tradeoff between electronic integration and electronic brokerage. Section 4 presents the competitive and cooperative options available to a firm. These include independent development, entering into a strategic alliance, and developing a public good. Section 5 presents a study of IOISs in the aircraft parts industry. In §§6 and 7, we draw on the observations in the industry to develop propositions on the choice of IOISs and the development approach. The choice of IOISs was based on two transaction characteristics—nature of demand uncertainty and market variability. Two factors—strategic significance of the IOIS, and size and bargaining power of the firm—were responsible for the choice of development approach. We conclude in §8 with some suggestions for further research.

## 2. A Typology of IOISs

Although a number of authors have developed frameworks for classifying IOISs (Table 1), there does not exist a standard, widely accepted typology of IOISs in the literature. The typology we propose in this paper is for IOISs that support an exchange relationship. $^{1}$ Thus, in selecting an IOIS, a firm actually selects the kind of relationship it wishes to implement with its suppliers/buyers. A useful typology will, therefore, have a direct correspondence between the proposed IOIS choices and the nature of the underlying relationship supported by the IOIS. This has not always been the case with previous frameworks.

For instance, Barrett and Konsynski (1985) and Cash and Konsynski (1985) both define the possible levels of technical involvement but do not outline different IOIS configurations or exchange relationships within the context of which these may be implemented. Malone et al. (1987), Benjamin et al. (1990), and Bakos (1987) do propose distinct IOIS types that consider, directly or indirectly, the nature of the underlying relationship but even their typologies are different. Malone et al. (1987), perhaps because of their focus on transaction cost theory and governance structures (Williamson 1975, 1985), include just two choices—electronic markets and hierarchies. Bakos (1987) proposes additional configurations but from the viewpoint of the entire value system (Porter 1985, Porter and Millar 1985).

In this paper, we present a simple typology that borrows from, and builds on, the work by Bakos (1987) and Malone et al. (1987). We propose that a firm can choose among three IOISs:

(i) Electronic Dyads: These are bilateral IOISs, where a buyer (seller) establishes individual logical links with each of a selected number of sellers (buyers) for a product (Figure 1). EDI links are common examples.

Figure 2a Electronic Shopping/Broadcast Sales Systems  
![](/api/attachments/W7ZQKEF7/fulltext/images/b8d571139420818c7c4f7eb8a80f246fa46d1bf8fc1b264d04d9bde2e95eedc5.jpg)

(ii) Multilateral IOISs: A multilateral IOIS allows a firm to communicate with a large, potentially unlimited, number of trading partners over a single logical inter-organizational link. Thus, a multilateral IOIS effectively serves as an intermediary between a firm and its trading partners.

The only example of a multilateral IOIS that has been discussed in the literature to date is an electronic market (Figure 2b) that brings together multiple buyers and sellers over a single system (Malone et al. 1987). However, it is also possible for an individual firm, or an alliance of firms, to develop a multilateral IOIS. Thus, a buyer can potentially develop a multilateral IOIS, which we will call an electronic shopping system, to facilitate comparison of multiple sellers' product offerings (Figure 2a). Or a seller may develop a multilateral IOIS to enhance its market access by advertising its product offerings to, and potentially even comparing offers from, a large number of buyers—we term this a broadcast sales system (Figure 2a).

(iii) Electronic Monopolies: As the name suggests, an electronic monopoly is an IOIS that supports a sole source relationship for a product (or set of products). Thus, it is a special case of an electronic dyad in that it is also a bilateral IOIS but with the additional constraint that it is the only electronic link established by the buyer for the product(s). An electronic monopoly may be initiated by a buyer seeking efficiencies or by a seller trying to gain market share. $^{2}$

$^{2}$ Theoretically it is also possible to conceive of an electronic monopsony as an IOIS that is the only electronic link established by a seller.

It is important to emphasize that an electronic monopoly is not the same as a market monopoly. In a market monopoly, a buyer does not have a choice of sellers. While an IOIS that supports a market monopoly will, by default, be an electronic monopoly, the electronic monopolies we are largely concerned with in this paper are where the buyer has a choice of sellers—that is, there is no market monopoly—but chooses to implement a sole source contract. An example is Saturn Motors' decision to purchase all its metal cutting tools from a single supplier, Kennametal (Feder 1992). The decision to implement a sole source contract may be motivated by factors external to the benefits from the IOIS, for instance, because it increases the supplier's incentives to invest in quality (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1993). The more interesting case, from the viewpoint of this paper, is when the benefits from the IOIS are a salient factor in persuading the buyer to establish an exclusive relationship.

The proposed typology adds to previous research in significant ways. First, it fulfills our stated criterion that the IOISs should bear a direct correspondence to the underlying exchange relationships. From a buyer's point of view, the appropriate level at which to consider these relationships (and, hence the choice of IOISs) is the individual product. Our premise is that a firm can follow one of three strategies (relationships) in acquiring a product from an external source: (i) routinely make all its purchases from a single supplier (sole sourcing, supported by an electronic monopoly), (ii) make each purchase from one of a small set of preferred suppliers (relational market exchanges, supported by electronic dyads), or (iii) shop the entire market for each purchase, potentially buying from a different seller each time (spot market exchanges, supported by a multilateral IOIS).

For a seller, these are the different kinds of relationships (and, hence, IOISs) it can aspire to implement with its buyers. Thus, a seller can: (i) become an exclusive supplier to a buyer (with an electronic monopoly), (ii) establish ongoing relationships with buyers for whom it is one of a select set of preferred suppliers (with electronic dyads), or (iii) engage in spot market exchanges (with a multilateral IOIS) where each sale is potentially made to a different buyer.

The relationship perspective helps us to extend previous research in the area. Thus, it expands the range of choices presented by Malone et al. (1987). It also clarifies that the set of configurations proposed by Bakos (1987) where a single customer/seller links up with many sellers/customers may be either electronic dyads or a multilateral IOIS—the distinction is important because electronic dyads and multilateral IOISs support different exchange relationships. Hence, we would expect the two to be used for transactions with different characteristics.

Our first objective in this study, therefore, is to examine the development of IOISs in the aircraft parts industry to identify the specific transaction characteristics that explain the choices made by firms with respect to the kinds of IOISs implemented.

Further, consistently considering the viewpoint of a single firm making decisions with respect to IOISs highlights the important distinction between the two dimensions—what and how—that we present in this paper. Consider, for instance, the relationship between an electronic market as defined in the literature to date and a multilateral IOIS as we have defined it. From the point of view of an individual buyer (seller), the transaction cost efficiencies, and the nature of the supported relationship, from an electronic shopping system (broadcast sales system) developed alone are no different from those offered by an electronic market that also includes other buyers (sellers). Why, then, should a firm develop an electronic market (the cooperative approach) and not an electronic shopping system/broadcast sales system (the competitive approach)? We expect this decision to be based on a different set of considerations than the transaction characteristics that influence the choice of IOISs.

Our second objective, therefore, is to draw on the observations in the aircraft parts industry to identify the factors that influence the choice of development approach. That is, when do firms choose to cooperate or compete in the development of IOISs?

## 3. Distinctive Benefits of Each IOIS

Our essential premise in the paper is that a firm's choice of IOISs depends on the fit between the distinctive nature of the transaction cost benefits offered by a particular IOIS and the benefits most valuable to the firm given the characteristics of the transaction to be supported by the IOIS.

Malone et al. (1987) suggest that IOISs can offer three kinds of transaction cost efficiencies: electronic brokerage, electronic communication, and electronic integration. Electronic communication refers to faster and more efficient transmission of information between the linked firms. Electronic brokerage reduces a firm's search costs and therefore increases a firm's chances of finding the optimal source or outlet for a product it is trying to buy or sell.

Electronic integration, on the other hand, increases the efficiency of a dyadic relationship by creating electronic linkages that go beyond just the exchange of routine order/invoice information between the firms. It is useful to think not just in terms of whether or not electronic integration exists between two firms but the degree of such integration. Thus, a simple EDI link that automates merely the transmission of orders from the buyer to the seller does not create any electronic integration at all. A basic level of electronic integration may be when the linked firms develop product code translation tables so that employees at the firms can place/receive orders using their internal product codes. A higher level of electronic integration may be when the buyer's computer determines a need for a product (based on preset reorder levels) and automatically transmits an order to the supplier's order entry system with no human intervention. Finally, at the highest levels of electronic integration, the firms can create close electronic coupling among the processes that create/use the information being exchanged (Malone et al. 1987), for example, between the customer's sales process and the seller's production planning process, thereby achieving "vertical quasi-integration" (Zaheer and Venkatraman 1994).

While all the IOISs in our typology provide electronic communication benefits, they differ in the extent to which they offer benefits of electronic integration and electronic brokerage. We discuss below the distinctive benefits of each IOIS.

## 3.1. Multilateral IOIS

A multilateral IOIS is designed to maximize electronic brokerage benefits and reduce search costs to a buyer (seller) of finding the optimal source (outlet) for a product it is trying to buy (sell). The benefits of electronic brokerage, and, therefore, a multilateral IOIS, are greatest for a spot market exchange process—when there is no ongoing relationship between buyer and seller and each transaction pairing is individually determined. As a result, a multilateral IOIS offers little opportunity for electronic integration between transacting firms. Thus, a firm will choose a multilateral IOIS only if it has more to gain from an extended search for each transaction than from optimizing selected dyadic relationships.

## 3.2. Electronic Monopoly

The distinctive advantage of an electronic monopoly is that it permits the linked firms jointly to optimize the dyadic relationship and increase the efficiency of the total value system by creating a level of electronic integration not possible with any other IOIS. Consider again the relationship between Kennametal and Saturn. Every time Saturn uses a metal cutting tool, it scans the information and conveys it electronically to Kennametal which then ships replenishments as and when needed.

Kennametal, in turn, can use the consumption information from Saturn to plan its manufacturing more accurately, thereby reducing inventory in the entire system while still providing just-in-time delivery. Without a sole source agreement, however, that is, if Saturn explicitly chose among several suppliers for each purchase of metal cutting tools, Kennametal would not be able to use the consumption information as effectively because it could never be sure how much of Saturn's usage would translate into demand for Kennametal. Nor could Kennametal and Saturn implement the automatic replenishment arrangement they currently have in place. $^{3}$

An additional motivation for a buyer to implement a sole source contract may be if the seller uses the electronic monopoly as a platform to provide services that allow the buyer to manage aspects of its operations that do not directly involve the exchange of information with the seller. For instance, Saturn is relieved of the need to manage inventory for metal cutting tools because Kennametal provides this service. Similarly, Foremost-McKesson provides pharmacies that use ECONOMOST with a “management purchase report” that shows the items ordered by the pharmacy, the prices at which the items were sold, and the profit margins (Clemons and Row 1988).

However, since an electronic monopoly, by definition, requires a buyer to limit purchases to one seller, it has an opportunity cost. The buyer must give up any potential benefits from being able to search the market for every transaction. Thus, a firm will choose an electronic monopoly only when the opportunity costs of foregoing market search are lower than the benefits of optimizing the efficiency of its relationship with a single supplier. From a seller's point of view, whenever feasible, an electronic monopoly is a clearly desirable choice since it is then assured of all the buyer's business.

## 3.3. Electronic Dyads

Electronic dyads represent the middle ground between a multilateral IOIS and an electronic monopoly. A firm will choose this option when it does not wish to commit itself to a sole source contract but, at the same time, does not wish to search the entire market for every transaction. Thus, electronic dyads support a relational, but not an exclusive, exchange mode. Typically, the buyer chooses among a short list of preferred sellers for each purchase of a product. Under these circumstances, establishing electronic dyads, and being on the buyer's short list of preferred sellers, also represents a seller's best chance of earning the buyer's business.

Electronic dyads allow for greater electronic integration than a multilateral IOIS but less than an electronic monopoly. For instance, a buyer (seller) may be able to develop product code translation tables for a few selected sellers (buyers) but cannot implement the kind of automatic replenishment arrangement that Saturn has with Kennametal.

## 4. Cooperative and Competitive IOISs

In addition to deciding what IOIS to develop, a firm must also decide whether to develop the IOIS competitively or cooperatively, $^{4}$ that is, with the involvement of other firms at the same level in the value system. $^{5}$ If a firm does adopt a cooperative approach, it must further choose between a strategic alliance and a public good.

(i) Public Goods: The first kind of cooperative effort is directed at the creation of a public good. A public good is one such that “if any person in a group consumes it, it cannot be withheld from others in that group” (Olson 1965). An example is a product standard—even firms that do not contribute to its original development can use it. In the context of an IOIS, a public good approach amounts to developing an IOIS (or standards for IOISs) where participation is open to all firms. Public goods are often most valuable when there exist network externalities, that is, when the benefits of the cooperative effort increase with the number of participants (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer, 1996), for instance, in the case of standards, or the Internet.

(ii) Strategic Alliances: A strategic alliance is a cooperative venture among a few selected firms. Unlike a public good, the benefits of the collective action are restricted to members of the alliance. Joint ventures, mergers, and cartels are all examples of strategic alliances. In the context of an IOIS, this means that a set of buyers (sellers) enter into an alliance to implement an IOIS and participation in the IOIS is restricted to those select buyers (sellers) only.

$^{5}$ It is important to reiterate that we refer here to cooperation between two or more firms at the same vertical level in the value system who may potentially even be in different industries. Strategic alliances are clearly also possible between a buyer and a seller—in fact, an electronic monopoly is an excellent example of this in the IOIS arena. However, that is not our focus in this section.

## 4.1. Multilateral IOIS

(i) Competitive: A competitive multilateral IOIS may be an electronic shopping system developed by an individual buyer to compare product offerings from a large number of sellers, or a broadcast sales system developed by a seller to advertise to, and potentially compare offers from, a large number of buyers.

An example of a competitive broadcast sales system is in the diverter segment of the grocery industry (Smith 1995). Diverters largely take advantage of special deals offered by manufacturers to selected market segments. Thus, a cereal manufacturer, trying to gain market share in California, may offer a 10% discount to supermarkets in California. A supermarket in California may take advantage of this by buying 10 more cases of the cereal than it expects to sell at the retail level. It can then sell these additional 10 cases to a diverter who can, in turn, sell them to a supermarket in some market where the cereal manufacturer has not offered the discount. Many large diverters have set up their own broadcast sales systems that any prospective buyer (typically grocery stores) can call into.

(ii) Strategic Alliance: A set of firms may also enter into a strategic alliance to develop an electronic shopping/broadcast sales system. An example is EUROSELECT in the European grocery industry (Klein and Kronen 1993). As this industry became increasingly dominated by large, international wholesalers, smaller, national wholesalers, such as Van Eerd Wholesalers in the Netherlands, found that unlike their larger competitors, they were unable to take advantage of the frequently significant price differences among the EC countries. Van Eerd, therefore, formed an alliance with similar sized wholesalers in other European countries to develop an electronic shopping system called EUROSELECT. EUROSELECT has a national partner in each of a number of different European countries, whose responsibility it is to provide product and price information to the central EUROSELECT database. By gaining the same access to price information that larger wholesalers enjoyed all along, these smaller wholesalers improved their competitive positions. However, “EUROSELECT is essentially a closed user group in which wholesalers participate to strengthen their competitive positions” (Klein and Kronen 1993). That is, participation in EUROSELECT is restricted to a small strategic

(iii) Public Good (Electronic Market): A public good multilateral IOIS is an electronic market through which large, unrestricted numbers of buyers and sellers do business. There are many examples of such electronic markets, such as the airline reservation systems SABRE and APOLLO; American Gem Market System (HBS 1988), a system designed to bring together dealers and buyers in the precious stones industry; TELCOT, matching buyers and sellers in the cotton industry (Lindsey et al. 1990); and Inventory Locator Service (ILS) in the aircraft parts industry (HBS 1990).

## 4.2. Electronic Dyads

Cooperation in the development of electronic dyads is largely for the establishment of common message standards for electronic data interchange (EDI). Standards allow a firm to communicate electronically with multiple trading partners using just a single interface program.

(i) Competitive: Competitive electronic dyads are based on firm-specific message formats. For instance, firms such as Wal-Mart and K-Mart, and some firms in the property and casualty insurance industry, initially established EDI links based on proprietary formats.

(ii) Strategic Alliance: Theoretically, it would be possible for a set of firms to enter into an alliance to develop EDI standards to be used by these firms only. However, since standards are subject to network externalities, we expect this will be relatively rare—in fact, we know of no such example. $^{6}$

(iii) Public Good: Most EDI standards, just like product standards, are public goods, in that they are available to all buyers and sellers that wish to use them, regardless of whether they were involved in the initial effort to develop them. Many industry associations, such as the Transportation Data Coordinating Committee have developed such standards for their industries. In addition, cross-industry standards, such as X.12 and EDIFACT, have also been developed by standards making bodies.

## 4.3. Electronic Monopoly

(i) Competitive: The competitive approach is when a single seller, on its own, establishes an electronic monopoly with a buyer(s). This is the case, for instance, with the examples of Kennametal and Foremost-McKesson cited earlier.

(ii) Strategic Alliance: A set of firms may enter into a strategic alliance such that buyers implement sole source contracts with the alliance rather than with an individual firm. An example is the Integrated Suppliers Alliance (ISA) formed by Warner Industrial Supply, Adams Nut & Bolt, and six other distributors in the maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) business (Johnson 1995). Their objective is to obtain exclusive contracts to provide MRO supplies to large corporations.

(iii) Public Good: Since electronic monopolies are, by definition, designed to restrict a buyer's business to a single seller (or a strategic alliance of sellers), the notion of a public good electronic monopoly is not meaningful.

## 5. The Aircraft Parts Industry $^{7}$

## 5.1. Data Collection

The data in this section is based on interviews with: the materials manager at a large airline; the division manager of an FBO (Fixed Base Operator—a hangar/maintenance facility); the materials manager at a package delivery company; SPEC 2000 representatives from two large OEMs; owner of a repair shop/broker; a broker/distributor; and the program director for SPEC 2000 at Air Transport Association (ATA), a trade association of major airlines around the world. A total of 13 interviews were conducted between the summer of 1993 and January, 1994. Each interview lasted between 1 and 4 hours for a total of approximately 30 hours of interviews. Nine of the interviews were taped and transcribed—the rest were designed largely for clarification and follow up of points made in earlier interviews and were not transcribed.

## 5.2. Background

The aircraft parts industry supplies replacement parts and repair services for the maintenance of aircrafts. Annual sales exceed \$5 billion. Customers in the industry range from large commercial airlines to individual plane owners. The industry classifies parts into three major categories—rotables, repairables, and expendables. Rotables are high cost units that, in theory, can be refurbished to a fully serviceable level as often as needed over the full life of the aircrafts on which they are installed, for example, landing gear. A repairable part can also be refurbished to a fully serviceable level but only a limited number of times, that is, it's expected life is less than the full expected life of the aircraft. Finally, expendables are parts that cannot be repaired and must be replaced during normal maintenance.

## 5.3. Nature of Purchases

There are hundreds of thousands of parts on each aircraft, most of which need to be replaced infrequently. In addition, there is a high degree of uncertainty in trying to predict when a particular part will need to be repaired or replaced—the exception being some parts that must be replaced or repaired after a fixed number of flying hours or cycles (takeoffs/landings).

Purchases in the industry can be divided into two categories:

(i) Purchases for Inventory: This refers to the regular purchase of parts to keep in inventory in anticipation of demand. When a customer first buys a particular type of aircraft, it receives information from the OEM on suggested inventory levels. With experience, the owner develops its own estimates of expected consumption. By aggregating the expected consumption forecasts over the size of its fleet, the owner can decide which parts, and in what quantities, to stock.

(ii) Aircraft on Ground (AOG): These are emergency purchases in response to an aircraft that is grounded and needs a part. Because of the high cost of a grounded aircraft, the primary imperative in an AOG situation is to find the needed part as soon as possible.

## 5.4. Market Sectors

The industry can be divided into two major market sectors. (a) The OEM sector, which includes OEMs and authorized distributors, is the largest, in terms of sales volume. (b) The Surplus Sector can be further divided into two segments: (i) The direct sale of parts by one airline to another: Sometimes an airline makes available for sale parts in its inventory it no longer needs, either because it has retired or reduced a particular model of aircraft, or simply because it overestimated demand. This segment, however, is a very small proportion of sales in the industry. (ii) The Brokered Market: This segment includes brokers and repair shops that buy and sell parts, both used and new.

The primary advantage of the OEM sector is assured quality. The quality of parts sold directly by airlines is also usually reliable. The quality in the brokered surplus market, on the other hand, can be quite variable. In spite of FAA requirements that all parts be appropriately tagged, $^{8}$ policing is difficult and it is possible to get parts that are not usable as certified. The OEM sector typically also has the highest prices for routine purchases. However, for AOG purchases, the OEM sector may actually be cheaper in some cases. In the surplus market, prices are determined strictly by negotiation—when word gets out that an airline is shopping for an AOG, the airline may end up paying a hefty premium.

Finally, for routine purchases, the OEM sector is usually a reliable source of parts. The primary advantage of the surplus sector, with respect to availability, is in AOG situations when immediacy is of prime importance and the OEM/distributors do not have the part in stock. The surplus sector is also useful for owners of out of production aircraft models because the OEM often does not carry parts for these models in stock and charges a premium to manufacture them to order in small lots.

## 5.5. IOISs in the Aircraft Parts Industry

There are three major IOISs in the industry which correspond very closely to the market sectors discussed above.

## 5.6. SPEC 2000

SPEC 2000 originally started as SPEC 200 in 1958 under the auspices of the ATA. SPEC 200 established data format standards for punch cards exchanged between the airlines and their parts suppliers. In the 1980s, ATA began the process of moving to a standard EDI mode. SPEC 2000 was released in 1987 while the database (see below) was established in 1989.

SPEC 2000 is organized into a set of chapters (Table 2). Chapters 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 14, and 15 set standards for the exchange of transaction information between buyers and sellers. Chapter 2 is a central procurement database. Suppliers list their part numbers, prices (optionally), and lead times. At the time of the study, there were 62 users, mostly airlines, accessing the data base, 123 supplier codes, and about 1.7 Million parts on the data base. Buyers can access the database in one of two ways.

(i) They can receive a monthly, customized tape including listings for those suppliers and/or aircraft models they specify in advance. 95% of the usage of Chapter 2 is in this fashion. $^{9}$

(ii) They can query the database on-line. The on-line query can also be restricted at the discretion of the buyer or the seller. For instance, an airline may restrict the search to only those suppliers who have been approved by its Quality Assurance department. Suppliers can prevent other suppliers from viewing their data, or even customers with whom they may not want to do business, perhaps because of an unfavorable dealing in the past.

Chapter 8 is much like Chapter 2, except that instead of suppliers listing parts catalogs, repair agencies list their capabilities. In our typology, therefore, SPEC 2000 combines aspects of an electronic market (on-line usage of Chapter 2 and Chapter 8) with standards for EDI.

## 5.7. Airline Inventory Redistribution System (AIRS)

In 1959, a group of eight airlines pooled their list of excess Pratt and Whitney parts and circulated it to other airlines and parts brokers. The idea was soon expanded upon and, in 1961, responsibility for administering the program was assumed by the ATA. In 1966, the list was computerized and came to be known as the Airline Inventory Redistribution System (AIRS). AIRS is an electronic market with participation open to all firms who choose. While its initial purpose was to allow airlines to exchange information on excess inventory, since 1980, the system has expanded in scope and now includes listings of parts from non-airline suppliers as well. In fact, of the 205 participants on AIRS in 1994, only 59 were airlines. ATA recently decided to make AIRS a part (Chapter 12) of SPEC 2000.

## 5.8. Inventory Locator Service (ILS)

ILS was started in 1979 by two brokers/dealers in the parts business (HBS 1990). ILS is a central database where sellers list their parts inventory and repair shops list their capabilities. Although the system provides a field for price, it is almost never completed. A buyer looking for a part or trying to find a repair shop with a specific capability can enter a query and get a list of all sellers listing that part or all repair shops certified by the FAA to perform the repair. The actual transaction is not conducted over ILS but negotiated directly between buyer and seller. There are over 10 million records in the ILS database and 1500 clients. The system receives over 10,000 inquiries a day for almost 50,000 parts. ILS is an electronic market—participation is open to any firm willing to pay the access charges.

## 5.9. A Large, Commercial Airline

The airline maintains a fleet of 443 aircrafts with an average age of 10.4 years. Because of the strong safety concerns, the highest priority for the airline is quality. As a result, the airline makes 90% of its purchases from the OEM sector. Every month, the airline receives a tape from SPEC 2000 that contains the prices and lead times of selected suppliers who deal in parts for its aircraft fleet and who are approved by its Quality Assurance department. $^{10}$ The airline also receives tapes of the current listings on AIRS and ILS. These tapes are then loaded into the airline's internal inventory control system. The purchasing process of the airline can be divided into three categories (Table 3):

(i) For about 15% of the items it purchases—typically the high volume hardware items—the airline has implemented sole source contracts. For these items, when the stock falls below a set level, the airline's inventory control system generates a purchase order for the chosen supplier. If the purchase order exceeds a prespecified amount, it must be approved by a planner/buyer, otherwise it is automatically, and electronically, transmitted to the supplier. The airline plans to continue implementing sole source contracts for some of its other high volume items as well.

(ii) For each part not under exclusive contract, the airline has a small list of preferred suppliers, typically including the OEM and 3–4 distributors. When the inventory for one of these parts falls below preset levels, the airline's inventory system generates a stock action report (SAR). The SAR also contains a list of suppliers along with prices (where available) and availability. In an overwhelming majority of the cases, a buyer then places an order with either the OEM or one of the preferred distributors. If none of the preferred suppliers is able to provide the part within the specified time, which happens less than 5% of the time, the buyer will then access other sources on the SAR (including those from ILS and AIRS).

(iii) A small portion of the airline's purchases (approximately 1% for rotables, 3% for repairables, and 6% for expendables) are in response to an AOG. The procedure for an AOG purchase is virtually the same except that the SAR is manually initiated by a planner. Even here, however, the airline first tries to find the part in stock with either the OEM (for instance, Boeing has a 24-hour AOG desk) or one of its preferred distributors. However, because of the time pressure in the case of an AOG, the airline must turn to alternate sources much more frequently. Thus, the airline uses ILS and AIRS on about 30% of its AOG purchases. $^{11}$

## 5.10. Other Firms' Use of IOISs

We also spoke with a few other buyers, all of whom used just ILS. The FBO is responsible for maintaining approximately 60 aircrafts. As with the airline, the FBO usually buys all the parts that it routinely stocks from the OEM or an authorized distributor. In contrast with the airline, however, the FBO has a higher percent of AOG purchases (approximately 10%) partly because it has fewer aircrafts of each model so that it is difficult to keep many parts in inventory and also because it maintains planes for other owners over whose maintenance schedules and usage it has much less control. For AOG purchases, its purchasing criterion depends on what the owner of the plane wants. For customers who prefer newer parts even at a premium, the FBO will initially try the OEM sector and if the OEM does not have the part in stock, the FBO will try to find it through ILS. For customers who want low price, the FBO will go directly to ILS. Overall, the FBO uses ILS on about 10% of its purchases.

The package delivery company has a fleet of 35 DC-8s with an average age of over 25 years. For parts that are still being manufactured, the company makes its routine purchases from the OEM sector. However, because DC-8s are a very old model of aircraft, many of the parts it needs are no longer being routinely manufactured by the OEM so that the company must turn to the surplus sector, and used parts, for a portion of its routine purchases, particularly for rotables and repairables. The company routinely uses ILS for all its purchases, both routine and AOG, from the surplus sector, even if it is only to gather market intelligence so it can negotiate better prices with one of its preferred distributors that specialize in used parts for DC-8s. Its procedure for AOG purchases (about 10–15% of its total purchases) is the same.

The repair shop/broker uses ILS for a variety of reasons—to gather market intelligence before buying a part to refurbish and resell, to locate a part for one of its customers (who does not have direct access to ILS), or just to search for potentially good deals on parts it can refurbish and sell. It also uses ILS to list parts and repair capabilities. It buys 40% of its parts and sells 60% of its parts and 50% of its repair jobs through ILS.

Finally, we spoke to two large OEMs. They both list their catalogs on Chapter 2 of SPEC 2000 and engage in

EDI through SPEC 2000. Neither of them uses distributors. The OEMs also use ILS, although very infrequently. If one of their customers (without direct access to ILS) has an AOG and the OEMs do not have the part in stock, they will help the customer locate the part. Occasionally, if they have surplus items in stock that are not selling, they list them on ILS.

In the next two sections, we examine the factors that account for the choices made by firms in this industry with respect to (i) the kinds of IOISs and (ii) the development approach.

## 6. Choice of IOISs

## 6.1. Transaction Characteristics

Very little research to date has focused on the factors that influence a firm's choice of IOISs. One exception is the work by Malone et al. (1987) who proposed that transactions for products with high asset-specificity and/or complexity of description will be supported by electronic hierarchies while transactions for other products will be supported by electronic markets. Our observations in the aircraft parts industry are not consistent with this prediction. Parts in this industry are low in both asset specificity and complexity of description. Yet, we observe examples of all three IOISs in our typology. In fact, a relatively small proportion of the transactions are conducted over electronic markets.

This suggests that the proposition by Malone et al. may only be partly valid. Since highly specific products, by definition, have very few buyers and also typically very few sellers, an electronic market designed to facilitate comparison shopping is likely to be of low value. Similarly, by making it difficult for firms to describe products accurately over an electronic forum, high complexity of product description may make it difficult to implement an electronic market. However, as the aircraft parts industry shows, low asset specificity and complexity of description do not preclude the development of either electronic dyads or electronic monopolies. This is also reflected in the examples of the two electronic monopolies cited earlier—ECONOMOST and the relationship between Kennametal and Saturn—where the products are neither asset-specific nor complex to describe. Similarly, EDI links (electronic dyads)

have definitely been implemented for products that are low on both dimensions. $^{12}$

Rather, as we will discuss shortly in greater detail in the context of each IOIS, in the aircraft parts industry, the choice of IOISs was based largely on two factors: (i) whether it is a routine purchase for inventory or an AOG purchase, and (ii) the market sector. Generalizing from these two factors, we propose the following two transaction characteristics as determinants of the choice of IOISs.

(i) Demand Uncertainty: This variable captures the difference between purchases for inventory and AOG purchases. Purchases for inventory refer to parts that buyers purchase on a regular basis—the primary uncertainty is with regard to the volume of demand. On the other hand, for AOG purchases, each part is purchased extremely infrequently and in very low volumes—the uncertainty is with respect to the specific part that will constitute a buyer's next AOG purchase. Thus, we can conceptualize two forms of demand uncertainty: $^{13}$

(a) Technological Uncertainty of Demand: A product is subject to high technological uncertainty if its demand is highly infrequent, unpredictable, and low volume. Thus, a firm faces high technological uncertainty if it purchases a large number of products with high technological uncertainty of demand. Such a firm faces significant uncertainty about which product will constitute its next purchase. A firm with low technological uncertainty purchases a stable set of items on a frequent, ongoing basis.

A hypothetical example may help to clarify the concept. An automobile manufacturer, producing a specific make and model of car, faces low technological uncertainty because it needs a standard set of parts on a regular, ongoing basis. On the other hand, an auto repair shop that handles a variety of different makes and models cannot anticipate what part it will need next since that depends on the particular cars that customers bring in for repair. The repair shop, therefore, faces high technological uncertainty. Over time, it will buy a wider range of parts than the manufacturer but it will buy each part very infrequently and in small quantities.

In the aircraft parts industry, AOG purchases are high in technological uncertainty since (i) they occur because the owner was unable to anticipate a need for a particular part, and (ii) there are thousands of parts on each aircraft that can cause an AOG $^{14}$ but a specific part is likely to cause an AOG very infrequently. The overall level of technological uncertainty faced by a firm, therefore, depends on the number of its AOG purchases which, in turn, depends on the size and mix of its fleet.

(b) Volume Uncertainty of Demand: The demand for products that a firm purchases on a regular basis, that is, products with low technological uncertainty, can further be characterized in terms of the level of volume uncertainty, defined by Walker and Weber (1984) as “assessment of fluctuations in the demand for a component and the confidence placed in estimates of the demand.” However, the aircraft parts industry suggests that the impact of this uncertainty on inventory and transaction costs, and hence on the benefits from an IOIS that improves coordination between buyer and seller, is greater for products with higher absolute volume levels. Thus, we define volume uncertainty as a combination of (i) the unpredictability of demand volume across time periods and (ii) the absolute volume.

In the aircraft parts industry, the volume uncertainty of demand of regularly purchased parts (for inventory) varies with the nature of the part. The highest volume uncertainty is for parts that are not specific to a particular aircraft model, and which are often used in significant quantities on a plane, typically hardware-oriented items such as screws, nuts and bolts, rivets, and O-rings. Absolute demand volumes for these parts can be very high. In addition, the quantity used during any given period is based on very unpredictable repair needs and is, therefore, highly variable. Aircraft model-specific parts, which constitute a majority of the parts purchased by plane owners, typically have lower absolute demand levels and, therefore, lower effective volume uncertainty. Within this category, parts that need to be replaced after a fixed number of flying hours or cycles have lower unpredictability in volume levels as well, so these have even lower volume uncertainty than those parts whose demand is based strictly on unpredictable repair needs.

Finally, it is useful to note that a firm can exhibit high technological uncertainty (because it buys a large number of items very infrequently) and also have a set of items that it purchases on a regular basis. In the aircraft parts industry, for instance, this is true for any airline that has both a significant number of AOG purchases as well as a large number of items purchased regularly for inventory.

(ii) Market Variability: This dimension reflects the differences among the three market sectors in the aircraft parts industry. These three sectors vary significantly on the following two attributes, which we will together define as market variability: (a) Market Fragmentation: Fragmented industries (Porter 1980) with a large number of atomistic firms have greater variability in products and prices than concentrated industries with a few large players. $^{15}$ (b) Market Volatility: This is the rate at which prices and players in the industry change over time.

In the aircraft parts industry, the OEM sector has very low market variability. First, the level of fragmentation is low. For a large proportion of parts, the OEM is the only authorized manufacturer. $^{16}$ Thus, a buyer looking for a part has a small and, equally importantly, stable supplier set—the OEM and a small number of distributors (typically no more than a dozen, although the two large OEMs that we spoke to do not use distributors at all). Market volatility is also low. Most OEMs change prices only once a year. With distributors, a little more negotiation is possible with respect to the discount rates but even here, the variance is usually no more than 5% and prices are only renegotiated once a year.

The variability of the segment with the direct sale of parts between airlines is higher than that of the OEM sector. Since airlines are selling excess inventory and are not in the business of selling parts, a buyer looking for a part cannot be sure which airline, if any, will have the part available. Thus, the potential supplier set for any part is effectively equal to the number of airlines in the world operating that model of aircraft. Further, prices in this segment are determined strictly by negotiation and are, therefore, more variable and volatile.

Finally, market variability is greatest in the brokered segment of the surplus sector. Market fragmentation is high. There are more than 15,000 distributors, brokers, and repair shops whose business it is to buy and sell parts, largely used but also some new parts. Further, this segment is largely opportunistic. For instance, a broker may buy a retired plane, disassemble it, and offer the parts for sale. Or, repair shops may buy unserviceable parts, and refurbish them for sale. Hence, the inventory carried by a firm can vary significantly over time. Thus, the potential supplier set for any purchase is extremely large. Further, in this segment too, all prices are determined by negotiation and are highly variable and volatile.

## 6.2. Electronic Monopoly

The electronic monopolies in the aircraft parts industry are the IOISs supporting the exclusive contracts implemented by the airline for approximately 15% of the products it purchases, largely for the items with the highest volume uncertainty, such as hardware.

A significant factor motivating these electronic monopolies is that they have allowed the airline to implement a degree of electronic integration with suppliers—automated reordering without human intervention—that would not have been possible otherwise. As the manager of the materials management process noted, he himself generates over 8,000 orders every month. Anything that reduces the transaction costs, and the workload on his buyers/planners, is welcome. The high volume uncertainty items are the natural first target for such exclusive contracts. First, they generate the maximum number of orders so that the transaction cost savings from automated ordering are greatest for these items. Second, the high volume means that these items have the potential for creating the maximum amount of slack (inventory) in the system, thereby increasing the importance of efficient coordination between buyer and seller.

The airline tries to reduce inventory in the system by providing the suppliers for these exclusive arrangements usage information for the previous two years so that the suppliers can better gauge expected demand and improve their own production/procurement planning. Note, however, that this is done manually, not through an electronic link. Even so, the ability of a supplier to use this information is enhanced by the fact that it is an exclusive supplier. Finally, the airline purchases these products from the OEM sector, which has low variability. The airline, therefore, feels that the potential benefits from comparison shopping for each transaction are very low.

Thus, the benefits of electronic integration facilitated by an electronic monopoly are greatest for regularly purchased products with high volume uncertainty of demand. At the same time, the opportunity costs to a buyer of tying itself to a single supplier and foregoing comparison shopping are low when the market variability is low.

PROPOSITION 1. Firms will implement electronic monopolies for products with low technological uncertainty and high volume uncertainty of demand, and low market variability.

## 6.3. Multilateral IOIS

There are three multilateral IOISs in this industry, all electronic markets—ILS, AIRS, and Chapter 2 of SPEC 2000. Both AIRS and SPEC 2000 are currently used only by the airline (among buyers) but ILS is used by all the firms we spoke to. Thus, we will concentrate our discussion on ILS.

Both the airline and the FBO use ILS primarily for AOG purchases from the highly variable surplus sector. The high technological uncertainty of AOG purchases means that the firms are never sure which product they will need to purchase next to resolve an AOG situation. The high level of market fragmentation in the surplus sector means that the range of parts that may cause AOGs is not available from one or two large suppliers. Further, because of the opportunistic nature of the business in this sector, a buyer cannot be sure which supplier will have the part it is looking for, even if it has bought the part from a seller before. Finally, since there is potentially high variation in the prices of different suppliers in this segment, it pays to locate as many suppliers as possible with the part in stock and compare prices. All these factors together mean that buyers typically engage in a spot market exchange process, potentially selecting a different seller for each transaction, when making AOG purchases from the surplus sector. This need to search the market for each transaction makes the electronic brokerage benefits of an electronic market like ILS very valuable. $^{17}$

Note that Proposition 2a, unlike Proposition 1 for electronic monopolies, is stated at the firm level and not at the individual product level. This is because, unlike products that are purchased regularly (that is, products with low technological uncertainty), a product with high technological uncertainty, by definition, is purchased too infrequently to justify a dedicated IOIS. Hence, we have to consider the level of technological uncertainty faced by the firm as a whole. When this is high, that is, when a firm purchases/sells a significant number of products with high technological uncertainty of demand, it can justify the cost of a multilateral IOIS that allows it to shop for/sell these items.

In the aircraft parts industry, the airline, the FBO, and the package delivery company all have sufficiently large fleets, and therefore, sufficient number of AOG purchases, to justify the cost of direct access to ILS. This is not true for every buyer. In fact, both the repair shop owner/broker and the broker/distributor claim an increase in their brokerage business since ILS. Very small planeowners, without the necessary volume of AOG purchases to justify the cost of direct access to ILS, have increased their use of brokers because, with ILS, brokers can now provide better service to these customers. Thus, in a firm where the overall level of technological uncertainty is low, the few products with high technological uncertainty of demand and high market variability that the firm purchases may not be directly supported by any IOIS.

Proposition 2a is further reinforced by the use of ILS by sellers. The repair shop/broker, which operates almost totally in a world with high technological uncertainty (since it is never sure which part it is going to buy, sell, or repair next) and high market variability (exclusively in the surplus sector) makes very heavy use of ILS. On the other hand, the two OEMs, who operate largely in an environment of low technological uncertainty and low market variability, make very limited use of ILS.

The package delivery company also uses ILS for AOG purchases from the surplus sector. But, as noted above, because of the age of its fleet, it also has to make a share of its regular purchases for inventory (with low technological uncertainty) from the surplus sector, for which it also uses ILS. These routine purchases have low volume uncertainty because the purchases from the surplus sector are typically model-specific parts, particularly repairables and rotables, that it does not buy in large quantities. As we argued above, the value of electronic integration is highest for parts with the greatest volume uncertainty. Thus, for parts with low volume uncertainty, the opportunity costs of foregoing electronic integration—the price of implementing a multilateral IOIS—should be lower than the benefits of comparison shopping for each transaction in a highly variable market—the distinctive advantage of a multilateral IOIS.

## 6.4. Electronic Dyads

The only buyer in the industry that has implemented electronic dyads is the airline. As noted above, for about 15% of its parts, the ones with the highest volume uncertainty, the airline has implemented electronic monopolies and it continues to implement additional sole source contracts for its other high volume items. For the regular purchase of other parts, however, that is, parts with lower volume uncertainty of demand (and low technological uncertainty since these are regular purchases), from the OEM sector, with low market variability, the airline buys from a small set of preferred suppliers with whom it has implemented electronic dyads through SPEC 2000.

To the airline, for parts with low volume uncertainty, the value of automated ordering (electronic integration) is too limited to justify sole source contracts. At the same time, the low market variability of the OEM sector, from which the airline makes its regular purchases, makes a multilateral IOIS less desirable, so it doesn't use the electronic market feature in Chapter 2 of SPEC 2000 (which is directed at the OEM sector).

By implementing electronic dyads with a small set of preferred suppliers, the airline is able to use the price exchange feature in SPEC 2000 to establish a very basic level of electronic integration, allowing its buyers to place orders without having to contact the suppliers for prices before each order. At the same time, it maintains some flexibility in choosing among suppliers for each purchase.

PROPOSITION 3a. A firm will implement electronic dyads for products with low technological uncertainty and low volume uncertainty of demand and low market variability.

The airline also uses electronic dyads, and a small set of preferred suppliers, for its AOG purchases (with high technological uncertainty of demand) from the OEM sector. Although the technological uncertainty is high, because market fragmentation in the OEM sector is low, the airline can obtain the wide range of products from a small and stable set of suppliers, for instance, the airplane and component manufacturers. Further, the low variation in prices means that, particularly for AOG purchases, which are very low volume (typically a single part) and where the main criterion is speed, the benefits of shopping around, once the part is located in stock, are minimal. Thus, even for AOG purchases from the OEM sector, the airline does not use the electronic market feature in Chapter 2 of SPEC 2000, preferring electronic dyads instead.

PROPOSITION 3b. A firm will implement electronic dyads for transactions characterized by high technological uncertainty of demand and low market variability.

## 7. Development Approach: Cooperation and Competition in Developing IOISs

The aircraft parts industry offers examples of both co-operative and competitive IOISs. The electronic dyads in the industry are all based on industry-wide standard formats. All the multilateral IOISs in the industry are electronic markets. On the other hand, the electronic monopolies established by the airline are with single sellers although even these are based on standard formats.

As we shall discuss shortly in the context of each IOIS, the choices in the industry with respect to development approach were driven largely by two considerations. The first is that, for the most part, the firms initiating the IOISs were not motivated by expectations of competitive advantage. The second important consideration was whether the firms had the necessary size and market power to justify and/or successfully implement the IOIS(s) independently. We now discuss these two determinants of the choice of development approach in greater detail.

(i) Strategic Significance of the IOIS: This is the extent to which the firm initiating the IOIS believes the IOIS can enhance its competitive position (Clemons 1986, Johnston and Vitale 1988). $^{18}$ This may be because a seller feels the IOIS will help it capture additional market share, and/or create switching costs for the buyer, before competitors develop similar systems. ASAP and ECONOMOST are well cited examples of IOISs that have been sources of significant advantage for their sponsors. Or, a buyer may be able to implement electronic links with its suppliers that reduce its purchase and logistics costs, or enhance its ability to provide customer service, so that the buyer can favorably alter its competitive position with respect to its own customers. This may be true in the case of Wal-Mart, for instance, whose distribution capabilities, supported by EDI, are considered one of its major strengths.

Frequently, the greatest advantage accrues to the first movers. A cooperative approach may compromise some of these competitive or first mover benefits. With a strategic alliance, the firms in the alliance can collectively still gain advantage over firms not part of the alliance. With a public good, on the other hand, the competitive benefits are typically eliminated. Thus, the strategic significance of an IOIS influences the motivation a firm has to develop a system competitively.

(ii) Size and Bargaining Power: These attributes determine whether a firm has the ability to execute a competitive strategy. If an individual firm does not have the volume of transactions necessary to justify the fixed costs of a competitive IOIS, a cooperative approach that distributes these costs over a larger base of users may be warranted. For instance, standards reduce the number of interface programs required to establish electronic linkages with multiple firms. In other cases, cooperation may be necessary because an individual firm does not have the necessary scope of operations to implement the IOIS of choice. Thus, in the EUROSELECT example cited above, the firms entered into an alliance because no individual firm had the necessary presence in multiple countries to implement the system independently. In addition, sometimes a competitive approach imposes an additional cost on the trading partners of the firm, for instance, by forcing them to develop dedicated interface programs for EDI based on idiosyncratic formats. Firms with greater bargaining power (Porter 1980) are more likely to be able to “persuade” their trading partners to incur these costs.

We now examine the cooperative and competitive choices in the aircraft parts industry to develop propositions on the choice of development approach for each IOIS.

## 7.1. Electronic Dyads

Cooperation for the development of electronic dyads in the aircraft parts industry began in 1958 as SPEC 200 (under the aegis of the ATA) which established standards for the electronic exchange of order information between airlines and their suppliers. SPEC 200 evolved into SPEC 2000—today, almost all EDI in this industry is based on the SPEC 2000 standards.

In light of its very large transaction volume for the purchase of parts, a large airline has both the size to justify implementing EDI independently as well the bargaining power to persuade its suppliers to implement EDI based on proprietary formats. Thus, any airline could have adopted this route if it had wished to do so. However, at the time SPEC 200 was initiated and developed, airlines operated in a regulated environment with limited competition. They found it efficient to share inventory and help even competing airlines when the latter's plane was grounded and needed a part. Thus, at the time, there was little expectation or motivation on the part of the airlines to use EDI links with parts suppliers for competitive advantage. That is, the strategic significance to the airlines of the EDI links was low.

This suggests, therefore, that when the strategic significance (motivation) is low, even if its ability (size and bargaining power) to implement competitive electronic dyads is high, a firm will adopt a cooperative approach to take advantage of the economies of standards.

PROPOSITION 4. A firm will develop cooperative electronic dyads when the strategic significance of the electronic dyads is low, even if it has the size and bargaining power to implement competitive electronic dyads.

As discussed earlier, in the development of electronic dyads, we expect this cooperative approach to be in the form of public goods since EDI standards are subject to network externalities. This is, in fact, the case in the aircraft parts industry—the SPEC 2000 standards are available to all who wish to use them.

## 7.2. Multilateral IOISs

The first multilateral IOIS in the aircraft parts industry—AIRS—was developed in 1959 for the express purpose of allowing airlines to exchange surplus inventory information with each other. Thus, the possibility of one airline developing such a system independently did not exist.

ILS was established in 1979 by a couple of brokers/ dealers. ILS is an electronic market with participation open to any firm willing to pay the access charges. ILS is targeted largely at the brokered surplus sector of the industry where each firm is typically both a buyer and a seller (in different transactions, of course). Thus, there would have been little value to the dealers (or to any other firm in this sector) from just an electronic shopping system or a broadcast sales system. The dealers needed a system that facilitated both buying and selling, that is, an electronic market. Thus, the strategic significance to the dealers of a competitive multilateral IOIS was low.

In addition, the dealers were relatively small businessmen with limited product scope, and little bargaining power over other firms in the surplus sector or over buyers, such as airlines. Thus, if they had tried to develop a competitive multilateral IOIS, say a broadcast sales system targeted at airlines, their limited product scope would make the system relatively unattractive, while their low bargaining power would make it difficult for them to persuade airlines to use the system. Thus, the dealers chose a public goods approach, effectively assuming the role of electronic market makers, and earning revenues from the market making activity itself. $^{19}$

There is one case not shown in this table—when the firm has the size and bargaining power to implement a competitive multilateral IOIS, but the strategic significance of the system is low. We, therefore, extrapolate from the empirically derived hypothesis for electronic dyads under the same conditions and propose that, under this scenario, firms will choose a public goods approach and develop an electronic market. This leads us to the following set of propositions (Table 8).

PROPOSITION 5a. A firm will implement a competitive multilateral IOIS, that is, an electronic shopping system or a broadcast sales system, if the strategic significance of the IOIS and the firm's size and bargaining power are both high.

PROPOSITION 5b. A firm will enter into a strategic alliance to develop an electronic shopping system or a broadcast sales system if the strategic significance of the system is high but the firm's size and bargaining power are low.

PROPOSITION 5c. A firm will implement a public good multilateral IOIS, that is, an electronic market, when the strategic significance of the IOIS is low.

## 7.3. Electronic Monopolies

The electronic monopolies in the aircraft parts industry are between the airline and the suppliers that have been awarded the exclusive contracts for 15% of its parts. In each case, the sole source contract is with a single supplier which, in our definition, is a competitive electronic monopoly. However, three factors are of interest.

First, the electronic monopolies were initiated largely by the airline and not by the sellers. From the perspective of the airline, these monopolies, while a significant efficiency measure, do not have high competitive significance. For a seller, an exclusive contract with a large airline probably does have high strategic significance. This emphasizes that the strategic significance of an IOIS (the motivation) should be considered from the viewpoint of the firm initiating the IOIS.

On the other hand, in the context of electronic monopolies, the size and bargaining power (that is, the ability) need to be considered always from the perspective of the seller, even when the systems are initiated by buyers. A buyer does not need a minimum size to implement a sole source contract. Further, most sellers are presumably happy to implement a sole source contract and do not need to be “persuaded” by a buyer with significant bargaining power. However, a seller can implement an electronic monopoly independently only if it has the necessary scale and scope to meet all of a buyer’s needs.

Third, the electronic monopolies in the aircraft parts industry also used the SPEC 2000 industry-wide standards. This suggests that, for electronic monopolies, we should consider cooperation and competition at two levels. At one level, as discussed earlier, an electronic monopoly may be established by a single seller acting independently or by an alliance of sellers. This choice is based on the size of the seller. Thus, in the aircraft parts industry, the electronic monopolies were implemented by individual sellers because they had the necessary size and scope to meet all of the airline's needs for the products concerned.

On the other hand, in the example of the Integrated Suppliers Alliance cited earlier (Johnson 1995), the firms joined forces because none of them individually had the necessary scope of operations to provide all of a firm's MRO needs. Interestingly, they felt the need to join forces to be able to compete for exclusive contracts because a large competitor, who did have the size and scope to do so independently, had recently been able to obtain an exclusive contract. As Jim Zachmann, President of one member of the alliance, Warner Industrial Supply, put it, "Originally we did this as a defensive measure. A competitor pursued one of our customers who we never thought would go single source but they did. Grainger was making inroads here as well . . ." (Johnson 1995, p. 28).

In the aircraft parts industry, the strategic significance of the electronic monopoly to the airline (the initiating firm) was low and the system is based on industry-wide standards. PROPOSITION 6d. When the strategic significance of an electronic monopoly (to the initiating firm) is low, and the sellers are small, the electronic monopoly will be implemented by a strategic alliance of sellers, and will be based on standard message formats.

Before concluding this section, it is useful to note that the decision to cooperate or compete in the development of IOISs is a dynamic one. For instance, it may be that in the very early stages of EDI development in an industry, even a medium sized firm is able to use proprietary formats. However, once standards are developed in the industry, perhaps at the initiative of other firms, it is likely to be much more difficult for the firm to persuade its trading partners to adopt proprietary formats. Only a much larger and more powerful firm may be able to execute a competitive strategy at the later stage of development. Thus, the appropriate development approach even with respect to the same IOIS may change over time, because strategic significance as well as size and bargaining power are dynamic constructs that need to be evaluated in light of the overall state of IOIS development in the industry.

## 8. Conclusion

In this paper, we examined the development and use of IOISs in the aircraft parts industry to develop propositions about two strategic choices that a firm must make in developing IOISs—what kinds of IOISs to develop; and the development approach, or how to develop the IOISs. We developed a typology with three IOISs—electronic monopolies, electronic dyads, and multilateral IOISs. Drawing on the data in the aircraft parts industry, we concluded that two transaction characteristics—demand uncertainty and market variability—influence a firm's choice of IOISs. In addition, a firm must also decide whether to develop the IOIS competitively or cooperatively. This decision depends, in turn, on the strategic significance of the IOIS and the size and bargaining power of the firm initiating the IOIS.

The ideas presented in the paper should offer useful guidance to practitioners making decisions about implementing IOISs. First, the proposed typology highlights the fact that in implementing an IOIS, a firm effectively decides the kind of relationship it wishes to implement with its trading partners. In some cases, the nature of the existing relationship will dictate the appropriate IOIS. In other cases, a firm may be able to use an IOIS to alter fundamentally the nature of an existing relationship, for instance, by implementing an electronic monopoly to take advantage of the benefits of electronic integration. We also described the distinctive benefits of each IOIS and proposed specific transaction characteristics under which each is likely to be optimal.

The paper also highlighted that the two choices of what kind(s) of IOIS(s) to develop and how to develop the IOIS(s) are, in fact, based on a very different set of considerations. Much of the previous literature on IOISs has focused on a seller trying to capture additional business by creating switching costs for the buyer. In this paper, we argued that it may be possible, and, in fact, more appropriate in some cases, for a firm to implement a broadcast sales system or an electronic shopping system for competitive advantage. Not all spot market exchange relationships have to be supported by electronic markets. Conversely, a firm can adopt industry-wide standards and still implement an electronic monopoly with a customer.

Researchers should be able to obtain some interesting ideas for further research from the paper. For instance, one limitation of the paper is that it is based largely on a study of a single industry. The propositions need to be tested in a variety of settings for validity and generalizability. Further, we proposed in a footnote in the paper that the choice of development approach will depend partly on the choice of IOIS. But our observations in the aircraft parts industry did not allow us to examine the interaction between these two choices. A future study should be able to shed some light on this issue.

Another possible area of research may be the impact of IOISs on the role of intermediaries. This paper implies that a fruitful way to proceed may be to compare the value added by an intermediary under different transaction characteristics, with the IOIS appropriate for those transaction characteristics, and ask whether the role of the intermediary is likely to be enhanced or diminished, and what the intermediary can do to influence the outcome. For instance, for transactions characterized by high technological uncertainty and high market variability, an electronic market may be able to replace the search function performed by intermediaries. On the other hand, if an individual intermediary is able to preempt an electronic market by developing an electronic shopping system to enhance the service it offers buyers, it may be able to improve its competitive position. Or, for a product with high volume uncertainty of demand and high price variability among manufacturers, a firm may hesitate to establish an electronic monopoly directly with a manufacturer. An intermediary, on the other hand, may be able to perform the price comparison among the manufacturers for the buyer, thereby persuading the buyer to implement an electronic monopoly with the intermediary. The buyer can then combine the flexibility of comparative shopping with the electronic integration benefits of an electronic monopoly. The intermediary, in turn, can gain additional business both at the expense of other intermediaries and direct sales by manufacturers to customers.

Another topic that has received much attention recently is the emergence of the networked organization. Miles and Snow (1986) propose a distinction between dynamic and stable networks. A multilateral IOIS would typically support a dynamic network whereas an electronic monopoly or electronic dyads would support a stable network. Thus, examining the transaction characteristics that support these IOISs may suggest conditions under which dynamic or stable vertical networks are more appropriate.

Finally, much has been written about the impact of IOISs on the number of suppliers a firm uses. The analysis in this paper suggests that this impact should vary with the characteristics of the transaction. For instance, one possible hypothesis may be that transactions that are appropriate for a multilateral IOIS will be conducted with an increased number of sellers whereas transactions that lend themselves to electronic monopolies will be conducted with fewer suppliers. $^{20}$
