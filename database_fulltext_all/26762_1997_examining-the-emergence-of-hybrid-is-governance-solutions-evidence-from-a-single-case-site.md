# Examining the Emergence of Hybrid IS Governance Solutions: Evidence from a Single Case Site

Carol V. Brown

Decision & Information Systems, Graduate School of Business, Indiana University, 801 W. Michigan Street, Indianapolis, Indiana 46202-5151
cbrown@indyvax.iupui.edu

The prior IS literature points to the importance of organizational context for predicting a firm's IS governance solution. However, for the most part this literature assumes that firms adopt a uniform IS governance solution for all business units and that this solution can be predicted by context variables at the overall organization level. The purpose of this study is to increase our knowledge about why firms implement a hybrid IS governance solution in which a subset of IS functions that includes systems development is decentralized to some business units, but not to other business units, in the same enterprise. A theoretical framework of context variables at the business unit level is first developed. An embedded, single case study provides an initial test of eight propositions derived from the framework, as well as an opportunity for theory building. Data are collected utilizing both deductive and inductive methods from IS and non-IS executives of a divisionalized Fortune 500 firm in which a uniform decentralized solution for systems development in place for almost a decade has recently been replaced by a hybrid solution. The case study findings suggest that a configuration of four variables characterizes a business unit context conducive to decentralized systems development governance (organic decision-making, high business unit autonomy, a differentiation competitive strategy, and an unstable industry environment). As predicted, however, the influence of these variables is likely to be overridden and a “deviant” solution adopted when deficiencies in IT capabilities are perceived and there is a culture that supports structural change at the business unit level. Additional interview and survey data collected from the key stakeholders are then analyzed in order to develop a richer understanding of the dimensions of the IT capabilities construct at the business unit level. The notion of absorptive capacity provides a theoretical argument for the emergent findings. Implications for researching today's increasingly complex IS governance forms are then drawn.

(Organization Design; Structure of the IS Function; IS Centralization/Decentralization; IS Alignment)

## 1. Introduction

As investments in information technology (IT) have become one-half of all capital expenditures (Keen 1991) and the number of CEO's subscribing to the belief that IT can play a strategic role in their firm has steadily risen (Hildebrand and Booken 1993), strategies and organization designs that better align the Information Systems (IS) organization with the enterprise have become critical IT management issues (Watson and Brancheau 1991, Henderson and Venkatraman 1992). One of the key organization design decisions is to what degree IS responsibilities should be decentralized to business units vs. performed by a central (usually corporate) IS organization.

The IS literature of the past decade seems to be in agreement about the different IS objectives achievable under a centralized vs. decentralized IS governance solution: operational efficiencies and synergies due to integrated IS solutions under centralized designs, division control and the flexibility to respond quickly to customer needs under decentralized designs. This IS literature also documents a trend in large, divisionalized firms toward the adoption of an IS governance form in which responsibilities for the management of technology (including computer operations) are centralized, but the responsibilities for the management of the use of technology (including systems development) are decentralized to business units (Zmud et al. 1986, Dixon and John 1989, Earl 1989). Termed a “federal” form, this new IS governance design makes it possible for an enterprise to simultaneously work toward IS objectives achievable under centralized and decentralized forms—“the best of both worlds” (LaBelle and Nyce 1987, Von Simson 1990). Less well documented has been a second trend: the adoption of a hybrid IS governance solution in which the management of the use of technology functions are decentralized to some business units, but not to other business units, within the same enterprise (Allen and Boynton 1991, Brown and Magill 1994).

The IS research of the past decade also shares the conclusion that there is no universal “best” IS governance structure; rather, the best IS governance solution for a given firm is contingent on its organizational context (e.g., Rockart et al. 1982, King 1983, Zmud 1984). However, for the most part the empirical research has assumed that firms adopt a single, uniform IS governance solution for all business units, and that this solution can be predicted by context variables at the overall organization level. Yet the abovementioned trend of different governance solutions for different business units within the same firm suggests a growing management interest in customized solutions in order to exploit the unique capabilities of a given strategic business unit (Bartlett and Ghoshal 1989). By inference, then, context variables at the business unit level need to be addressed to understand why business units within the same firm exhibit the same or different IS governance arrangements.

The overall purpose of this study is to increase our knowledge about the relationships between context variables and hybrid IS governance solutions in which centralized and decentralized designs co-exist. The primary research question is as follows:

Why do firms adopt a hybrid IS governance form in which centralized and decentralized governance solutions for the management of systems development co-exist?

In the next section we synthesize the findings from prior IS research and contingency studies from the organization sciences in order to develop an a priori framework of context variables at the business unit level.

## 2. Towards a Theoretical Framework

The IS literature over the past decade documents the emergence of new IS governance forms to meet changing IS role demands. The separation of decision-making authority for the management of systems operations from decision-making authority for the management of systems development, as forecasted by King (1983), begins to be documented in the literature by the mid-1980s. Zmud et al. (1986) coin the term “federal government role” for a governance arrangement in which a central unit has primary responsibility for the IT infrastructure, but the business units plan, build, and run their own application systems. Dixon and John (1989) describe internal and external pressures moving large firms toward a similar IS governance paradigm in which line management is responsible for the management of the use of technology, while the central (corporate) IS department retains responsibility for the computer and communications “utility” functions as well as responsibility for coordinating IS planning and operational activities across decentralized IS units. Finally, Allen and Boynton (1991) forecast the adoption of more complex IS governance arrangements in order to better align the IS functions with the strategy and structural characteristics of firms of the 1990s—including the presence of both centralized and decentralized systems development units within the same firm.

The first four empirical studies in Table 1 examine bivariate relationships between the governance choice for a single IS function (e.g., systems development) and single organizational context variables from “classical” contingency theory (e.g., Lawrence and Lorsch 1967, Hickson et al. 1969, Blau 1974). Significant relationships are reported for three variables: organization size (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982), competitive strategy (Tavakolian

1989), and the locus of decision-making authority/control (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, Ahituv et al. 1989). Centralized IS governance solutions are associated with smaller firms, defender (conservative) strategies, centralized control, and mechanistic decision-making. Decentralized IS governance solutions are associated with large firms, prospector (aggressive) strategies, decentralized control, and organic decision-making.

The next two empirical studies in Table 1 adopt an exploratory interview design in an attempt to identify relationships between multiple context variables and different IS governance forms (including the federal governance form). Earl finds the host organization's overall structure and control systems to be good predictors: functional structures and centralized control are associated with centralized IS governance; holding company or decentralized line-of-business structures and decentralized control are associated with decentralized IS governance; and hybrid (including matrix) structures within multidivisional firms are associated with federal governance. Earl also suggests that an IT heritage variable influences IS governance decisions—i.e., “tomorrow’s arrangements are much influenced by today’s and yesterday’s experiences” under the existing governance structure.

The frequently cited conceptual study by King (1983) explicitly emphasizes behavioral (political) issues—vs. economic (rational) issues—as being critical for understanding IS governance decisions, and provides conceptual support for a direct association between IS governance and a locus of decision-making variable. King also proposes that performance problems under present IS governance arrangements can “stimulate demands for major change”; this proposition is similar to the IT heritage variable later reported by Earl (1989).

In sum, the prior IS literature provides evidence for context variables at the overall organization level being good predictors of uniform centralized, decentralized, or federal IS governance designs. However, no prior study focuses on the identification of context characteristics to explain why in large, multidivisional firms the management of the use of technology may be decentralized to some business units, but not to others. This lack of uniformity suggests that managers are devising customized IS governance solutions to meet specific business unit needs. By inference, then, context variables at the business unit level also need to be investigated.

Indeed, the cumulative findings from the prior bivariate studies suggest that one strategy and two structural variables may be good predictors of decentralized vs. centralized solutions for the management of use of technology functions for a specific business unit: the competitive strategy of the business unit, the degree of mechanistic (vs. organic) decision-making within a business unit, and the degree of decision-making autonomy from the parent. In addition, there is conceptual support for IS-related variables (IT heritage) being important predictors for IS governance change.

Although none of the studies in Table 1 addresses potential contingency characteristics at the business unit level, recent management researchers have pointed to a parallel phenomenon in organizational alignment studies: the neglect of influential context factors at the business unit level (e.g., Govindarajan 1986, Powell 1992). Unfortunately, no organization science research on governance issues for a staff function such as IS is known to exist. The approach taken here, then, is to develop a theoretical framework of potentially influential context variables at the business unit level based on a synthesis of IS governance research and other related contingency studies by IS and management researchers.

This conceptual task resulted in the identification of eight context variables at the business unit level that may be good predictors. Six of these variables are predicted to distinguish business units with decentralized vs. centralized systems development responsibilities. The remaining two variables are expected to identify business units that adopt an IS governance solution that in some way conflicts with other context variables—i.e., predictors of some sort of “deviant” IS governance solution.

For ease of readability, several terminology conventions are adopted for the remainder of this paper. The first refers to the use of the centralized vs. decentralized terminology. As defined in the introductory section, this terminology is commonly used to describe uniform, enterprise-wide governance solutions for the IS function: all IS responsibilities are either decentralized to business units, or performed by a central (usually corporate) IS organization. Here, the centralized vs. decentralized terminology will refer to corporate vs. business unit governance for a specific business unit. $^{2}$ Second, this study focuses on centralized vs. decentralized solutions for a subset of IS functions introduced earlier as the management of the use of technology. As defined in Brown and Magill (1994), these functions include systems development and maintenance, end-user computing support, and application planning. For ease of communication, the term systems development refers to this set of functions.

Further, this inquiry focuses only on the centralized versus decentralized systems development solutions for business units within large, multi-divisional firms in which the management of technology functions are centralized. As defined in Brown and Magill (1994), the management of technology functions include computer operations, communications/network operations, emerging technologies, and technology planning. This distinction is important for two reasons. First, decentralized governance for the management of technology functions implies decentralized governance for systems development (King 1983, Brown and Magill 1994). Second, corporate IS units with enterprise-wide management of technology responsibilities also typically have systems development units that not only support corporate departments, but also develop and maintain applications common to two or more business units—even if systems development responsibilities have been decentralized to those business units (Earl 1989). Without corporate IS expertise in systems development, centralized systems development would be an unlikely option.

For the remainder of this paper, the following terminology shortcut is therefore adopted: the term systems development governance henceforth refers to the centralized vs. decentralized choice for the management of the use of technology functions for a specific business unit, within firms where the management of technology functions are centralized.

The rationales for the eight context variables at the business unit level are presented below; the resulting theoretical framework is provided in Table 2. The operationalizations used to test the propositions derived from the framework are provided in the Appendix.

2.1. Predictors of Systems Development Governance Based on a synthesis of prior IS and management research, six context variables at the business unit level are expected to predict decentralized vs. centralized systems development governance in large, multidivisional firms. Each is described separately below.

Mechanistic vs. Organic Structuring. Contingency theorists view this locus of decision-making variable as a structuring variable (Dalton et al. 1980) with mechanistic and organic forms as endpoints on a continuum (Burns and Stalker 1961). Daft's (1992) definitions of these endpoints include centralization of control, the extent of rules, and task specialization. The prior IS governance literature reports a relationship between the degree of centralization of decision-making at the overall organization level and the degree of centralization for IS governance (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, King 1983, Ahituv et al. 1989, Earl 1989). Under the assumption that this relationship holds true for context variables at the business unit level, business units with organic decision-making would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance; business units with mechanistic decision-making would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance. For example, high levels of business management involvement in IS application planning, systems development (or acquisition), and implementation are characteristics of organic decision-making. Decentralized systems development governance would ensure the high levels of business management participation and interaction with IS personnel and local project control associated with the organic form.

Business Unit Autonomy. Given that firms with a federal IS governance form typically have multidivisional (SBU) structures (Earl 1989), a second locus of decision-making variable that may be an important predictor is the degree of business unit—vs. corporate—autonomy in overall decision-making. A “corporate style” variable has been proposed as an organizational context variable that distinguishes firms with pressures toward “increasing the hub” vs. “increasing distribution” (Cash et al. 1988). However, not all SBUs may be given the same level of autonomy (Bartlett and Ghoshal 1989). Based on prior IS literature (LaBelle and Nyce 1987, Sewell 1987), business units with high autonomy in decision-making would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance; business units with little or no autonomy in decision-making would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance.

Competitive Strategy. The competitive strategy variable at the business unit level captures how each business unit competes in its industry. Recent strategy research has pointed to the importance of a business-level strategy variable as a “source of contingency” for organizational designs (Govindarajan 1986). Tavakolian (1989) found an association between the two extreme business strategies (defender vs. prospector) of Miles and Snow (1978) and centralized vs. decentralized IS governance solutions respectively. A more widely used classification scheme of two primary business strategy types has been proposed by Porter (1980): low cost vs. product/service differentiation. According to Miller (1986), the prospector strategy of Miles and Snow is a type of differentiation strategy.

By inference, then, business units with a differentiation strategy would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance. The flexibility to respond quickly to customer needs, as required by a differentiation strategy, can be better achieved when a business unit manages its own application portfolio and has direct control of its software development resources. In contrast, business units with a low cost strategy would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance. That is, operational efficiencies for IS resources can be better achieved under centralized systems development governance due to economies of scale and other integrated IS solutions (Allen and Boynton 1991).

Industry Stability. Environmental instability is associated with greater levels of uncertainty (Leblebici and Salancik 1981), and higher uncertainty in decision-making is associated with increased needs for information processing (Galbraith 1973). Business units competing in industries with high levels of environmental instability—such as volatility of net sales or profits (Bourgeois 1985)—would therefore be expected to have higher internal information processing needs. This suggests that business units competing in highly unstable industry environments would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance; business units competing in highly stable industries would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance. The responsiveness capability associated with decentralized governance would be a major benefit for business units with high environmental instability, but not business units with stable industry environments (Allen and Boynton 1991).

Workgroup Interdependence. Thompson (1967) proposes that all of an organization's actions are based on its workflow needs as captured by three types: long-linked or serial interdependence (such as an assembly line), mediating interdependence (such as a bank), and intensive workflow (such as a hospital). Greater information processing equivocality is associated with the "nonroutine" technology of the intensive type; lower equivocality is associated with the more routine or “engineering” technology of the long-linked type (Daft and Macintosh 1981). By inference, business units with an intensive type of workflow would value the local control and responsiveness associated with a decentralized IS governance form as a major benefit, while business units with a long-linked type of workflow would not. Thus, business units with an intensive workflow would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance; business units with a long-linked workflow would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance.

Information Intensity of Products/Services. Based on Cash and McLeod (1985), information intensity is defined as the degree to which a unit is strategically dependent on IT for four dimensions: a) to introduce new products/services, b) to create product/service differentiation, c) to improve market access, and d) to establish competitive advantage. This suggests that business units with high information intensity would be more likely to have decentralized systems development governance; business units with low information intensity would be more likely to have centralized systems development governance. That is, IT is a strategic resource in high information intensive business units. The direct control over systems development resources associated with decentralized governance would therefore be much more highly valued in business units with high levels of information intensity.

This leads us to the following propositions:

Within large, multidivisional firms that have centralized governance for the management of technology functions,

P1. Business units with organic (mechanistic) decision-making are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

P2. Business units with high (low) business unit autonomy are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

P3. Business units with a differentiation (low cost) competitive strategy are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

P4. Business units with an unstable (stable) industry environment are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

P5. Business units with an intensive (long-linked) type of workflow are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

P6. Business units with high (low) information intensity are more likely to have decentralized (centralized) systems development governance.

## 2.2. Prerequisites for Systems Development Governance Change

Based on a synthesis of prior IS and management research, two context variables at the business unit level are expected to characterize business units that adopt a new IS governance solution that in some way conflicts with the six predictor variables proposed above. In other words, the following two variables are predicted to be prerequisites for some sort of “deviant” systems development governance solution.

IT Capabilities. The IS governance literature suggests that an IT heritage variable is associated with IS governance changes (King 1983, Earl 1989). Cash et al. (1988) suggest that an IT capabilities variable that takes into account not only today's and yesterday's IT experiences—i.e., inherited IT capabilities—but also future anticipated IT needs, is important for predicting IS governance changes. That is, anticipated "shortfalls in IT performance and capabilities" are associated with major changes in IT leadership and structure. In particular, when a business unit's future systems need to be high in strategic impact, but its existing information systems are low in strategic impact, a shortfall in IT capabilities is perceived and a change in governance is likely (Cash et al. 1988).

A theoretical rationale for an IT capabilities predictor variable that takes into account anticipated IT needs in relation to existing (inherited) IT capabilities is provided by absorptive capacity theory. Absorptive capacity is a function of prior related knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Thus, insufficient prior investment in an area of expertise can foreclose the possibility of developing a capability requisite for the near future in that area. By inference, then, when business management anticipates needing significantly greater IT capabilities in the near future, a change in systems development governance that in some way "deviates" from the solution predicted in Propositions 1–6 above is more likely. In other words, perceived deficiencies in IT capabilities relative to future needs is a prerequisite for a “deviant” governance solution. When business management anticipates little need for a change in IT capabilities (i.e., no major deficiencies in IT capabilities), a change to a “deviant” governance solution is not likely.

Culture—Attitudes Toward Change. The capacity of an organization to change has been associated with organizational structure change vs. inertia (Hannan and Freeman 1984). Kaufman (1971) suggests that organizations can develop “mental blinders” which preclude the ability for an organization to change. Meyer (1982) views this capability as a cultural variable that can be measured as an attitude toward change.

A culture that supports innovative structural change would appear to be an additional prerequisite for a business unit to adopt an IS governance change not predicted by the six context characteristics above (P1–P6). For example, a business unit with high business unit autonomy (P2) and an organic decision-making structure (P1) that chooses to adopt a deviant centralized solution due to perceived deficiencies in IT capabilities, would also be expected to have a culture that strongly encourages structural innovations and emphasizes the benefits from change in general. In the absence of such a culture, a “deviant” governance solution would likely be perceived as a context-design misfit that could result in lower performance (Gresov 1989). By inference, then, a culture that places a high emphasis on change is an additional prerequisite—i.e., is a necessary, but not sufficient, predictor variable—for a “deviant” systems development governance change.

This leads us to two additional propositions:

Within large, multidivisional firms that have centralized governance for the management of technology functions,

P7. Business units with systems development governance designs that deviate (do not deviate) from the predictions in P1–P6 are more likely to perceive deficiencies (no deficiencies) in IT capabilities at the time of these governance decisions.

P8. Business units with systems development governance designs that deviate from the predictions in P1–P6 are likely to place a high emphasis on change at the time of these governance decisions.

## 3. Methodology

Given the objective of addressing “why” questions about a contemporary phenomenon within a complex natural setting, a case research strategy was selected for this study (Benbasat et al. 1987). The specific research design is an embedded, single case study: data are collected at the corporate level as well as from four subunits within one case firm (Yin 1984). The case site—referred to here as Firm M—became accessible to the author as part of a comparative case study that focused on organization-level antecedents for recent IS governance changes within seven firms (see Brown and Magill 1994). Firm M was selected for the larger comparative study because its sector (for-profit), size (Fortune 500), and overall structure (divisionalized) matched the population of firms studied by earlier IS governance researchers, and its lack of financial problems suggested that a major structural change would not be due to a turnaround situation.

However, the initial exploratory interview with Firm M's CIO uncovered the fact that its recent IS governance change involved abandoning a uniform federal solution for a hybrid design in which systems development responsibilities remained decentralized for only two of its four largest business units. More specifically, two large divisions (referred to here as SBUs A and B) had been in place since the early 1980s when systems development responsibilities were first decentralized from a central IS group to these business units; both of these SBUs still retained systems development responsibilities. However, two other large divisions (SBUs X and Y) were "new" (newly independent) due to an internal restructuring, and the presidents of these SBUs had recently requested a recentralization of systems development responsibilities for their business units.

Firm M thus offered a unique opportunity for both a “critical” and a “revelatory” case study (Yin 1984) for investigating why centralized and decentralized systems development governance designs co-exist within the same enterprise. It provides a “critical test” of the theoretical framework for two reasons: i) the business unit presidents initiated the change, suggesting that differences at the business unit level existed between "old" SBUs A and B and the "new" SBUs X and Y, and ii) although SBUs X and Y were "new," they each had in-place an application portfolio with which to measure the IT capabilities variable—making a test of Proposition 7 feasible. Because the key executives involved in the decisions to relinquish systems development governance to the central IS group were in the same roles at the time of our data collection, Firm M also provided the opportunity for a "revelatory case": data from a pool of observers could be collected on-site for theory generation should the a priori framework prove to be insufficient to explain Firm M's evolution from a uniform to a hybrid IS governance form (Yin 1984, Benbasat et al. 1987, March et al. 1991). Finally, since the CIO had an eight-year tenure with Firm M, a firsthand account of relevant historical events would be available.

The protocol for the larger comparative study was therefore expanded to take advantage of this opportunity for an embedded, single case study. The data collection methods are described in detail below; the data sources are summarized in Table 3.

## 3.1. Data Collection by Interview

Data were collected via on-site interviews from the CIO, the Director of Systems in the central IS group (whose responsibilities included the newly re-centralized systems development functions), a top non-IS executive in each of the four SBUs, and the IS executives in the two SBUs (A and B) that still had their own systems development units. The first site visit included an unstructured interview with the CIO in order to explain the research inquiry, obtain buy-in to the study, capture relevant background data on the firm including its IS governance history, and to target the other participants. The CIO obtained buy-in from the general managers and relevant IS executives associated with each of the four SBUs. On the subsequent site visit, a focused interview (Yin 1984) was held with the CIO, followed by focused interviews at three additional site locations with the other participating executives.

The focused interview protocols supported two data collection objectives: i) to confirm (or disconfirm) the explanatory capability of the theoretical framework (Table 2) by collecting data on-site from the four non-IS executives, and ii) to capture explanatory variables from the IS and non-IS participants in order to generate rival explanations should the a priori framework not explain well the change to a hybrid IS governance solution. Two different interview guides were used: one for the IS executives, and one for non-IS executives. All focused interviews began with a reaffirmation of the study's purpose and the measurement (or confirmation) of the relevant IS governance solution. Next, an open-ended question was asked to elicit potential "drivers or enablers" for the relevant IS governance solution. $^{3}$ The interviews with non-IS executives ended with the measurement of the eight context variables in the theoretical framework utilizing a prepared survey instrument (see Appendix). This sequencing ensured that the prestructured instrumentation would not bias the executives' responses to the open-ended question on drivers. The remainder of the IS executive interviews was less structured; the primary objective here was to gain a better understanding of the IS context—both organizational and technological. Archival documents were also provided to the researcher as the participants considered them relevant.

Following the completion of all on-site interviews, the responses to the open-ended question about drivers for the IS governance solutions were transcribed and analyzed utilizing a “pattern-matching” approach (Yin 1984). The data on drivers and the context variable measures were then utilized to customize a follow-up survey instrument, as described below.

## 3.2. Data Collection by Mailed Survey

The customization of a survey instrument to capture ratings of importance (7-point Likert-type scales) for potential drivers for a firm's recent IS governance change was part of the protocol of the aforementioned multiple case study conducted by the author. As described in Brown and Magill (1994), these procedures included the incorporation of emergent variables captured during interviews at this site and prior sites, along with variables identified from literature searches. The protocol was modified for Firm M in that not only was a survey instrument administered to SBUs X and Y where the systems development functions had recently been re-centralized, but also to SBUs A and B where systems development units remained decentralized. The survey for SBUs X and Y asked for ratings of importance for each item for the re-centralization of systems development for these two SBUs; the survey for SBUs A and B asked for ratings of importance for the unchanged allocation of IS functions for these two SBUs. The objective, then, was to provide not only triangulation of the interview responses for SBUs X and Y, but also comparative data to help explain why systems development units were retained by SBUs A and B. The preliminary versions of the survey forms were reviewed by the CIO for accuracy and answerability before administration. Both survey forms were distributed by mail with a cover letter directly to the participating executives within three months of their on-site interview with the author.

## 3.3. Confirmation of Findings

A comprehensive report with narrative descriptions of the IS governance history at Firm M, the drivers elicited from the interviews, narrative and graphical presentations of the average importance ratings captured by the follow-up survey, along with interpretive comments, was first prepared for validation by the primary contact, and then distributed to each participating executive. $^{4}$ Feedback was received from the CIO on this report and an earlier draft of this article.

## 3.4. Summary

The above research methods were designed to satisfy the logical tests of construct validity, internal validity, and reliability (Yin 1984, March et al. 1991). Construct validity was achieved by data collected from multiple informants, IS and non-IS; the utilization of open-ended questions as well as structured survey instruments; and the participant review of case study reports. Internal validity was ensured by pooling over observers and analyses that included pattern-matching and explanation-building approaches. Reliability was achieved by the use of case study protocols. Stated differently, the research design has many elements of a “qualitative approach” that combines qualitative and quantitative methods in order to introduce “both testability and context into the research” (Kaplan and Duchon 1988).

## 4. The Emergence of Hybrid IS Governance at Firm M: Case Study Findings

Firm M is a process manufacturing and distribution firm that has been in existence for more than a half-century. A member of the Fortune 500, it had more than 30K employees and total sales exceeding \$10B at the time of data collection. Prior to the 1980s, Firm M pursued a strategy of vertical integration and had evolved into a highly centralized organization. Under a new CEO in the early 1980s, it adopted a related diversification strategy at the corporate level, established an SBU structure, and began to place a strong emphasis on SBU autonomy.

The four largest divisions at Firm M, representing more than 80% of annual sales, participated in the study. SBUs A and B are in related process manufacturing businesses. Some of A's products are inputs to B's manufacturing process. SBUs X and Y were divisions of SBU A until 1988–89 when, under a second major restructuring initiative, they became independent subsidiaries. SBU X is in a manufacturing business (related to SBU A) in which product distribution had become increasingly important for establishing a competitive advantage at the time of the restructuring initiative. SBU Y is primarily in a distribution business (with products related to A and X), which as early as 1983 was designated a growth segment for the firm.

## 4.1. IS Governance History and Current Form

Prior to the 1980s, a corporate IS department had authority and responsibility for the full range of IS functions. The change from centralized to federal IS governance in the early 1980s was viewed as a top management initiative to align the IS functions with a new corporate context. That is, systems development responsibilities along with other corporate functions (including Human Resources) were decentralized to the newly autonomous SBUs in the early 1980s as part of a restructuring initiative under the new CEO. Also during this time period, corporate IS and several other service units (including those with accounting and risk management responsibilities) were combined into a separate business unit. $^{5}$ A corporate policy statement that delineates central IS and SBU responsibilities under a federal IS governance form was issued by the end of 1985.

A corporate chief information officer (CIO) role was established by early 1986. The central V.P. of IS reported being asked to “wear two hats”: one as the newly created CIO, and the other as the head of a central IS organization with operational responsibilities. The CIO role responsibilities include providing strategic IS direction and coordination at the corporate level; for example, the CIO critiques each SBU’s five-year IS plan for the CEO and his direct reports. The new CIO role also provides a structure for identifying opportunities for synergies from the sharing of applications, data, and IS expertise across SBUs. The CIO also understood his responsibilities to include the “rebuilding of relationships” with SBU management. The operational “hat” includes shared (central) services for all SBUs (computer operations, communications/network operations, technology planning), as well as systems development and support for corporate applications and applications common to more than one SBU.

Prior to 1989, when SBUs X and Y were divisions of A, the management of use of technology functions (a.k.a. systems development) were performed for SBUs X and Y by SBU A. As part of the 1988–89 restructuring, IS personnel—along with Human Resources personnel—were formally re-assigned from SBU A to the newly autonomous SBUs X and Y. Because the 1985 federal governance policy statement was still in effect at this time, SBUs X and Y were expected to absorb the IS functions performed by SBU A—i.e., to continue to rely on the central IS unit only for the management of technology. However, by mid-1991 Firm M was implementing a hybrid IS governance solution: SBUs A and B continued to have decentralized systems development governance, but centralized systems development governance had recently been adopted for the newly independent SBUs X and Y.

The events that led to this change from a uniform federal design to a hybrid IS governance solution actually took place over several years. First, the independent IS group at SBU A, which had previously performed systems development functions for SBUs X and Y, continued to do so for an interim period. By 1989, SBU Y had taken over the systems development responsibilities and had physically transferred IS personnel from SBU A, but SBU X had arranged for a re-centralization of its systems development personnel to the central IS group. Then, by mid-1991, a re-centralization of SBU Y's systems development personnel (to the central IS group) had also been initiated.

Figure 1 summarizes the corporate context and IS governance history at Firm M on a timeline extending back more than a decade prior to the data collection. As predicted by prior research, the decentralization of systems development governance (creating a federal design) in the early 1980s was a top-down initiative to align the IS function with the new SBU structure. The implementation of a hybrid governance design, beginning in 1989, also occurred after a corporate-level restructuring: the creation of SBUs X and Y as independent subsidiaries. However, the hybrid IS governance solution appears to be in opposition to the restructuring initiative. In fact, the prior research would predict uniform decentralized systems development governance under a federal design based on the firm's overall organization characteristics and the increased business unit autonomy for SBUs X and Y.

In the next two sections the key drivers and events related to the independent governance decisions at SBUs X and Y are reported based on the on-site interview data collected from four Firm M executives: the CIO, the presidents of SBUs X and Y who initiated the re-centralization decisions, and the systems director in the central IS unit to whom IS directors with application planning and systems development responsibilities for these SBUs now report. All four of these informants were in their current positions and played a role in the events that led to the emergence of a hybrid IS governance solution.

SBU X. Following the creation of SBU X as an independent subsidiary, the president reported being unsure that his management team had the skills and experience to manage the application planning and systems development functions previously performed by SBU A. He also expressed the concern that his SBU would not be able to “provide challenge and career opportunities for IS folks” as needed under Option #1. On the other hand, he also did not consider Option #2a to be viable, due to what he viewed as inadequate performance of these IS functions under SBU A’s management prior to 1989.

More specifically, under SBU A's management the small IS group he was to "inherit" (about ten personnel) had been occupied primarily with systems maintenance tasks and was "getting nowhere with new programs and development." The demand for new systems development projects was also growing; for example, an enhanced point-of-sale system was needed for a recently acquired retail division, and X's largest customers were beginning to demand of their suppliers an interorganizational order entry system (utilizing EDI). The president also perceived a significant increase in the strategic impact of the SBU's future vs. current information systems (as seen in IT capabilities measure in Table 4-B), and he considered the inherited IS group to be too small in size to provide the "critical mass" of technical skills anticipated for some of these new systems initiatives. Options #2b and #3 therefore appeared to be the only viable alternatives: purchasing services from an outside vendor, or asking the central IS group to take on these IS management responsibilities—as an exception to the uniform federal governance form currently implemented at Firm M.

The president of SBU X chose solution #3: to relinquish the new responsibilities to central IS. He chose option #3 over contractual arrangements with an outside vendor because he trusted the CIO and "felt comfortable" with central IS management. The main argument for requesting this exception to the corporate policy statement issued in 1985 was that SBU X was "trailing the industry" in new systems development and implementation. IS issues had become "essential to their survival." Successful strategic development projects were required "just to keep competitive, without even establishing competitive advantage"—and this organizational arrangement appeared to be the best solution for achieving that success. The central IS group also offered a large "pool" of IS personnel with a wide range of technical expertise.

In early 1989, shortly after the recentralization decision had been discussed with the CIO, SBU X contracted with an outside consulting firm for what became a nine-month strategic systems planning effort, with the central IS director for systems development playing a key role. Through this planning process the central IS unit achieved a better understanding of X's strategic issues and future IS needs, as well as SBU management “buy-in” to a strategic IS plan. At the time of our data collection, SBU X had two years of experience under this arrangement, and was reportedly making good progress toward implementing the strategic IS projects identified in the 1989 study. Within central IS, a new liaison position (IS project manager) had been created. This IS manager had a solid-line report to the director of systems development within central IS, but was physically located alongside X’s management and was perceived by the president to be “part of the management team,” involved in day-to-day actions. The president reported having had “good times and bad times—like a marriage” with the central IS group over the past two years, but overall satisfaction with the re-centralization was quite high. His managers had gained “IS leadership expertise” and X’s users in general were more involved in systems projects and had an increased “understanding of the important potential” of strategic systems: “people are now coming forth with their own new ideas” for IS applications. The president reported that his industry had become increasingly dependent on IT and that SBU X was “not ahead, but had made gains” under this arrangement.

Finally, both the CIO and X's president independently confided that this centralized governance solution was also viewed as an interim one by both executives: the possibility existed for SBU X to take over the management of the re-centralized IS functions sometime in the future. In other words, SBU X might later choose decentralized governance—such as currently in place for SBUs A and B—should the current concerns about IS-related capabilities become invalid. On the other hand, the CIO and the central systems director also shared the president's concerns about small IS unit size: the larger central IS unit not only was able to leverage “economies of scale” but also could accommodate the slack needed for learning curves associated with new technologies.

SBU Y. Following the creation of SBU Y as an independent subsidiary, its new president felt hampered by inadequate operational data from systems developed under SBU A; faster and better performance of the systems development unit was needed. Of the three IS governance options above, Option #2a—continuing to purchase services from SBU A—thus did not appear to be the best solution. In 1990, when the subsidiary moved to a new location, the president chose Option #1: incorporating the re-assigned IS personnel and “driving their own IS.” A V.P. of Administration at Y was assigned the new IS responsibilities. The choice of a decentralized solution was perceived not only as in alignment with the culture at Firm M in general, but also, according to the president, reinforced the new culture at SBU Y in which “skunkworks” and “the freedom to fail” were stressed.

In mid-1990, the president of SBU Y commissioned a study by a consulting firm with objectives that included IS strategic planning and IS personnel recommendations. The steering committee for the project included the CIO and was completed by early 1991. At that time, little progress had been made on new systems projects under SBU Y's management. The need for a user friendly point-of-sale system for a sales workforce characterized by "marginal" general literacy (i.e., in the 3R's) and "high turnover" was also envisioned. Similar to SBU X, the president of SBU Y perceived a wide gap between current and future system needs. Based on the commissioned study, the president concluded that IS was essential to their survival and competitive advantage, but that Y's inherited IS group lacked the requisite technical expertise. SBU Y's traditionally low IS budget and continued emphasis on maintenance (vs. new systems development) were viewed as variables that contributed to this dilemma.

The resignation of the V.P. of Administration in April 1991 triggered the president's reassessment of the viability of Option #3, chosen by SBU X two years earlier: relinquishing the decentralized IS functions to the central IS organization. In mid-1991, the president of SBU Y requested that the central IS group—now its “next-door neighbor”—take over all of Y's IS functions, absorbing the decentralized IS personnel as an intact group. Option #3 was chosen over infusing expertise into the current IS group by external hires (under Option #1) as well as over purchasing services from an outside vendor (Option #2b). The primary reason given by the president was that it was expected to “shorten the learning curve”: central IS was already familiar with the systems needs of Y and had the necessary “breadth” of technical and business skills to quickly bring SBU Y closer to its desired state. In this case, SBU X also provided a successful precedent. Similar to the president of SBU X, the president of Y also confided that he may ultimately re-claim the relinquished IS responsibilities at some date in the future; the CIO independently corroborated this expectation.

Summary. In order to develop a richer understanding of why decentralized systems development governance was abandoned by SBUs X and Y, we analyzed the qualitative responses to open-ended questions by the executives involved in the recent re-centralization decisions. A fuller explanation of the IT capability shortcomings at the business unit level was identified: a lack of IT management skills and experience on the part of SBU management and an inadequate pool of technical expertise (small “inherited” IS groups), in combination with internal and external (industry) pressures for quick development of new types of information systems (including POS and EDI applications). The re-centralization solution was viewed as the best solution, but not an ideal one; in fact, the choice to abandon decentralized governance by SBUs X and Y was characterized by one participant as the “lesser of evils.”

## 4.4. Mailed Survey Findings: Triangulation and Extension of Interview Findings

The objective of this section is to compare the mean ratings of importance between the two SBUs with centralized systems development and the two SBUs with decentralized systems development, for a larger set of non-IS context variables and IS-related capability variables. As alluded to in §3.2, the surveys included context variables identified as being influential for IS governance choices i) in prior IS or management literature, ii) in the on-site interviews with Firm M participants, as well as iii) in other case studies conducted concurrently by the author. The importance ratings were measured on a scale from 1 (not at all important) to 7 (extremely important).

Looking first at the results for SBUs X and Y (Part A of Table 5), the top seven drivers are all IS-related capability variables. Four of the variables capture deficiencies in the delivery of systems planning and development at the SBU level: a lack of IT management expertise within SBU management (6.5), a lack of alignment between IS and SBU strategic plans (6.5), a level of investment in new systems development projects lower than desirable (6.5), and the small size of the IS groups inherited from SBU A at the time of the restructuring (6.1). Two other IS-related variables capture what the presidents viewed as sources of competitive threats for their SBUs, given these IS-related deficiencies: an emerging belief in the strategic role of IT for their SBU (6.4) and a high rate of change in the computer & communications (C&C) industries (5.9). The last top-rated variable was characterized by the SBU presidents as an enabler of their re-centralization decisions: the partnership relationship between SBU management and the CIO (6.0). Although not among the top seven drivers, the two prerequisite variables in the a priori framework (Propositions 7–8) were among the next highest rated variables: strategic impact grid (5.5) and attitudes toward change (5.4). In contrast, only one of the six variables in the a priori framework predicted to distinguish centralized from decentralized governance (Propositions 1–6) had a mean rating greater than 5.1: competitive strategy (5.4). Note in particular that three structural characteristics predicted by prior research to be a misfit with a centralized governance solution are indeed rated as the non-IS context drivers of lowest importance: Firm M's overall SBU structure (4.6), high business unit autonomy (4.6), and organic decision-making at the business unit level (4.1). The survey results in Part A therefore provide strong support for the interview findings reported above: IS-related capability variables at the business unit level, rather than non-IS context variables, were the key drivers for the abandonment of decentralized systems development governance.

Looking next at the survey results for SBUs A and B (Part B of Table 5), the three top drivers are again IS-related capability variables. Here, however, the results provide a rationale for retaining decentralized systems development governance. The high level of business knowledge of the current systems development personnel was the leading driver for retaining decentralized governance (6.8), followed by two variables capturing satisfaction with the application planning, approval, and prioritization tasks being conducted at the SBU level (6.0 ratings). Similar to the survey results for SBUs X and Y, the context variable referencing the strategic role of IT at SBUs A and B was also highly rated (5.8). Two non-IS characteristics predicted by prior research to fit a decentralized governance solution were also highly rated: high business unit autonomy (5.8) and Firm M's overall SBU structure (5.6).

The survey data in Table 5 also provide evidence for the importance of an additional context variable not pointed to in prior IS research, not in the a priori framework, and not emphasized as a driver in the interviews with the Firm M executives: geographic proximity of the SBU management to the central IS group. $^{7}$ The same relatively high rating on both surveys (5.6) suggests that geographic proximity to the central IS group can be an influential context factor at the business unit level. SBUs A and B were on separate campuses far removed from the location of the central service unit, whereas SBUs X and Y were in close proximity to the physical facility that housed the central IS personnel. Face-to-face contact between SBU management and the CIO, central Systems director, and IS project team members, was facilitated by (and less costly) due to close physical proximity. Indeed, one could speculate that the ability of the president of SBU X to work on the same “campus” as the CIO for an extended period of time enabled him to make an early re-centralization decision, in contrast to the president of SBU Y who was new to the job, and therefore had neither a prior relationship nor close geographical proximity to the CIO until the year preceding his own re-centralization decision.

In summary, the survey data provide strong support for the influence of IT capability variables on systems development governance decisions at the business unit level. The level of business knowledge of IS personnel is a highly influential variable for the retention of decentralized governance. Deficiencies in IT management expertise and other business unit capabilities to plan and deliver new systems in response to an emerging strategic IT role—as reported in the interview findings—are key drivers for the abandonment of decentralized governance at Firm M.

## 5. Discussion

This research study was motivated by the desire to improve our understanding of why large, multidivisional firms implement hybrid IS governance solutions in which systems development is decentralized to some business units, but not to others. Under the assumption that firms adopt a uniform solution for all business units, most prior IS governance research has investigated predictor variables at the overall organization level. But this assumption no longer holds true in practice. The initial contribution of this study, then, is the development of a theoretical framework of predictor variables at the business unit level, based on prior IS and management literature. Six variables that predict decentralized vs. centralized systems development governance, and two prerequisite variables for a “deviant” governance solution, are proposed. Data collected from a single case site in which a uniform federal design had recently been abandoned for a hybrid IS governance solution provided a “critical test” of eight propositions derived from the framework.

The case study findings suggest that a configuration of four variables characterizes a business unit context conducive to decentralized systems development governance: organic decision-making, high business unit autonomy, a differentiation competitive strategy, and an unstable industry environment (Propositions 1–4). In other words, highly autonomous business units with organic decision-making, that are competing in an unstable industry environment with a differentiation competitive strategy, are likely to control their own systems development resources. The case findings also suggest, as predicted, that the influence of these four variables will be overridden and a “deviant” governance solution adopted in the presence of perceived deficiencies in IT capabilities (Proposition 7) by business units that have a culture that places a high emphasis on change (Proposition 8).

Two contingency variables suggested by prior research were not found to be good predictors for systems development governance: no support was found for the workgroup interdependence variable as a predictor (Proposition 5), and mixed results were found for the information intensity variable (Proposition 6). In fact, the case results in Table 4 suggest that the operationalization used here for current information intensity (based on Cash and McLeod 1985) captures a construct similar to that measured by the vertical axis of the strategic impact grid of McFarlan et al. (1983). (That is, the scaled ratings of the information intensity variable reported for each SBU in Part A are similar to the low-high "existing system" plots on the strategic impact grids reported for each SBU in Part B.)

In order to develop a richer understanding of the dimensions of the IT capabilities construct we then analyzed additional qualitative and quantitative data collected from the key stakeholders at Firm M. Both the interview and survey data point to the importance of IT management capabilities at the business level. More specifically, a lack of IT management expertise among the SBU's top managers, inadequate progress on new systems development (vs. maintenance) projects, poor business/IS plan alignment, and an inadequate pool of technical expertise (IS size) were important shortcomings at SBUs X and Y—given the increased strategic role information systems were expected to play in the near future for these two SBUs. IT capabilities at the business unit level were also the top-rated drivers for the retention of decentralized systems development governance by SBUs A and B; here, however, the IT capabilities were not considered deficient, and the key dimensions were business knowledge of the SBU IS personnel, business/IS plan alignment, and SBU control of systems project approvals and prioritization. Taken together, these case study findings suggest support for the conclusion by Boynton, Zmud and Jacobs (1994) that it is the “synergistic combining” of IT-related knowledge of business managers and business-related knowledge of IS personnel that is the dominant factor explaining differences in IT application portfolios (IT use).

Absorptive capacity theory appears to also explain well this richer view of the IT capabilities that are associated with a “deviant” IS governance solution—here, the abandonment of decentralized governance by SBUs X and Y. As introduced earlier, absorptive capacity is a function of prior related knowledge, and a lack of early investment in an area of expertise may foreclose the possibility of future development of a technological capability (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Recall that SBUs A and B acquired the systems development responsibilities in the early 1980s, and began to develop their own IT management expertise. By the late 1980s, the IT personnel at SBUs A and B had acquired high levels of business knowledge, and the business managers at these SBUs participated in planning and developing IT applications that kept pace with their strategic IT needs. In contrast, SBUs X and Y remained dependent on SBU A for all their systems development needs for most of the decade. By the late 1980s, SBUs X and Y not only faced a significant gap between the strategic impact of their existing systems and their anticipated system needs, but also perceived deficiencies in their abilities to manage the systems development function. Accepting the theory's basic premise that an organization needs prior related knowledge to assimilate and use new knowledge, then, the management at both SBU X and Y recognized that they lacked the requisite IT management skills and experience to effect the quantum changes required to deliver the new systems projects they needed. As a result, these SBU managers chose to relinquish the systems development responsibilities to the central IS unit, even though other context characteristics of SBUs X and Y were more conducive to a decentralized design—and indeed identical to SBUs A and B.

Further, the notion of absorptive capacity provides a theoretical rationale for why the president of SBU Y expected a turnaround to be quicker under a re-centralization solution, than under an outsourcing solution (see reference to a shorter learning curve for the central IS personnel in §4.3 above). Recall that the central IS organization had a systems development unit that developed and supported applications common to more than one SBU. Further, the CIO had recently participated in an IS strategic planning study by an outside consulting firm for SBU Y. This suggests that considerable relevant business knowledge was already resident in central IS, and that it was reasonable to expect these insiders would need a much shorter time to acquire SBU-specific business knowledge than outsiders. $^{8}$

There are also several limitations to take into account when assessing the overall contribution of this study. First and foremost, it should be reaffirmed that the single case research strategy employed here only allows generalizability to a research model, which in turn needs to be tested under a multiple case study design or by other field methods. In addition, this embedded case study did not allow for a complete testing of the framework; that is, business units with context characteristics matching those predicted to be conducive to centralized systems development governance did not exist at Firm M. Several other aspects of this case study also need to be explicitly recognized. For example, at Firm M the hybrid IS governance solution emerged as a result of governance decisions that were initiated by business unit management, not corporate management. Further, the corporate IS unit was organized as part of a central service division rather than organizationally a part of corporate headquarters. That is, the possibility exists that an SBU president is more likely to relinquish IS functions to a central unit than a corporate unit.

## 6. Implications for Future Research

Although the above limitations need to be recognized, the cumulative findings of this study along with the prior literature suggest that four categories of variables are associated with IS governance solutions. First, non-IS context factors at the overall organization level are good predictors of uniform centralized and decentralized governance solutions, as well as the feasibility of a federal governance solution for one or more business units (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982, King 1983, Ahituv et al. 1989, Earl 1989, Tavakolian 1989, Brown and Magill 1994). Second, a configuration of four non-IS context factors at the business unit level appear to be good predictors of decentralized systems development governance. Third, for those business units with a context conducive to decentralized systems development governance, IT capabilities at the business unit level—including IT management expertise of business managers, business knowledge of IS personnel, other “IT heritage” variables (King 1983, Earl 1989), and a size dimension (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982)—appear to be good predictors of whether or not a business unit adopts, retains, or abandons a decentralized solution. Fourth, other variables may be additional prerequisites at the business unit level for the adoption of a governance solution that “deviates” from that predicted by non-IS context factors—including attitudes toward structural change and characteristics of the relationship with the central IS unit (CIO partnership, geographic proximity).

Two additional implications for future research designs of IS governance studies are also suggested. First, this case study documents that IS governance changes can be both revolutionary—i.e., the “pendulum swings” referred to in the literature—and evolutionary. At Firm M, a major pendulum swing occurred in the early 1980s: the decentralization of systems development and other corporate functions, resulting in a uniform federal design. However, the hybrid IS governance form found after mid-1991 was more of an evolutionary design change—as first one SBU and then a second SBU abandoned the uniform federal design. Events over four years (1988–1991) proved important for understanding why four large business units, with similar non-IS context characteristics, had different IS governance solutions by 1991. Second, the fact that the re-centralization decisions at Firm M were driven by business management and not IS management suggests that IS governance alignment studies based on data from IS executives only may be missing a key stakeholder’s perspective.

Finally, it is recognized that this IS study predates the potential effects of several structural trends that are converging by the mid-1990s—including the movement toward flatter and more flexible overall organization forms, frequent acquisitions and spinoffs, and “virtual” linkages across organizational boundaries. It is hoped that the findings here on predicted and emergent variables at the business unit level will stimulate future researchers to investigate the IS governance impacts of such changes from an expanded set of influential context variables. In particular, future researchers are encouraged to examine non-IS context variables at the business unit level, as well as an IT capabilities construct that captures both business-related knowledge of IT managers and IT-related knowledge of business managers. $^{9}$

## Appendix

The theoretical framework for this study consists of eight context variables at the business unit level (see Table 2). In keeping with the prior

IS governance literature, the operationalizations for these variables are based on prior contingency studies from the organization theory and strategic management literatures. Out of respect for the time constraints of the senior executives being interviewed, there was also an attempt to utilize familiar terminology and parsimonious measures. All but the workgroup interdependence variable were measured at the end of the interview with each non-IS executive; a copy of the measures was shared with the participant in order to ensure communication of the item wording and scales.

For the presentation of results (§4.2 and Table 4), the scores for the variables measured on 7-point scales with high/low expected values are labeled as follows: 7 = very high, 5 or 6 = high, 4 = midpoint, 2 or 3 = low, 1 = very low.

Mechanistic vs. Organic Structuring. Based on Daft (1992), the mechanistic and organic structural forms were operationalized as endpoints on a 7-point continuum, with a score of 1 as Mechanistic, a score of 7 as Organic. The following definitions were provided for the endpoints:

Organic Form: Knowledge and control of tasks located anywhere in business unit; Communication is predominantly horizontal; Weak hierarchy of authority and control—few rules; Employees contribute to the common task of their department; Tasks are adjusted and redefined through employee interaction.

Mechanistic Form: Knowledge and control of tasks centralized at top of business unit; Communication is predominantly vertical; Strong hierarchy of authority and control—many rules; Tasks broken down into specialized, separate tasks.

The executive was asked to provide a rating on a 7-point scale with labeled endpoints.

Business Unit Autonomy. The executive was asked to indicate how strongly Firm M emphasizes or encourages “local autonomy” for decision-making for their business unit on a 7-point scale, with a score of 1 as never emphasized/encouraged, a score of 7 as highly emphasized/encouraged.

Competitive Strategy. Due to its widespread dissemination, Porter's (1980) classification scheme was utilized, with the following definitions:

Low Cost: Striving to be the low-cost producer in the industry

Differentiation: Seeking to differentiate the product/service offering from rivals' products/services.

In addition, whether or not a business unit used a Focus (or niche) strategy was measured, with the following definition:

Focus: Focusing on a narrow portion of the market

The executive was first asked to indicate which of the two business strategies best described his business unit. Then he was asked whether his organization strived “to serve the entire market for its product” or whether it utilized a focus strategy.

Industry Stability. The instability of the industry of the business unit was defined as the volatility of net sales (or profits) over the past five years (Bourgeois 1985) on a scale from 1 (highly stable) to 7 (highly unstable). The executive was asked to rate the volatility of the core/ dominant industry for the past five years on the 7-point scale.

Workgroup Interdependence. Three categorical types of workflow were utilized, based on Thompson (1967):

Long-linked: Workflow involves serial interdependence in the sense that act Z can be performed only after successful completion of act Y, which in turn rests on act X, etc. (example: assembly line).

Mediating: Workflow links customers and producers who are or wish to be interdependent (example: banks).

Intensive: Work selection, combination, and ordering are determined by feedback from the object itself (example: hospitals).

The business unit's type of workflow was categorized based on the core industry of the SBU.

Information Intensity of Products/Services. Information intensity was operationalized as the degree to which the business unit was strategically dependent on IT for the following four dimensions (Cash and McLeod 1985):

a) introduce new products/services

b) create product/service differentiation

c) improve market access

d) establish competitive advantage

A 7-point scale was utilized for each dimension, from 1 (no dependence) to 7 (highly dependent). The executive was asked to indicate how dependent on IT the business unit was by providing a rating on the 7-point scale for each of the four dimensions.

IT Capabilities. The IT capabilities of the business unit were operationalized as the difference in strategic impact between future anticipated applications and existing systems, based on Cash et al. (1988). A large difference ("gap") in strategic impact is a measure of perceived deficiencies in IT capabilities. A reproduction of the $2 \times 2$ strategic impact grid of McFarlan et al. (1983) was provided to each participant. The horizontal axis was labeled "strategic impact of future applications (development portfolio)"; the vertical axis was labeled "strategic impact of existing systems." The executive was asked to "plot" his business unit within the grid after a brief verbal introduction to the two axes. Although not on the grid furnished to the informants, in Part B of Table 4 a diagonal has been added to facilitate the reporting of the results. A plot below the diagonal indicates a "gap" in strategic impact between future and current applications; a large distance below the diagonal is reported as a "large gap." A plot very close or on the diagonal indicates little difference in strategic impact—and no major perceived deficiencies.

Attitudes Toward Change. For this study, attitudes toward change (based on Meyer 1982) at the business unit level were collected on a 7-point scale, from 1 (never emphasized/encouraged) to 7 (highly emphasized/encouraged), for two dimensions of change: a) organizational/structural innovations and change, and b) the benefits that can be expected from change. The executive was asked to indicate how strongly the culture within the business unit emphasizes or encourages each dimension of change.
