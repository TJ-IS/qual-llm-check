# Business Strategic Orientation, Information Systems Strategic Orientation, and Strategic Alignment

Yolande E. Chan • Sid L. Huff • Donald W. Barclay • Duncan G. Copeland
School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Ontario, Canada K7L 3N6

chany@qsilver.queensu.ca

Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada N6A 3K7
shuff@ivey.uwo.ca
dbarclay@ivey.uwo.ca
dcopelan@ivey.uwo.ca

Information systems strategic alignment—the fit between business strategic orientation and information systems (IS) strategic orientation—is an important concept. This study measured business strategic orientation, IS strategic orientation, and IS strategic alignment, and investigated their implications for perceived IS effectiveness and business performance. Analyses of data gathered in a mail survey of North American financial services and manufacturing firms indicated that 1) business strategic orientation, IS strategic orientation, and IS strategic alignment are modeled best by utilizing holistic, 'systems' approaches instead of dimension-specific, 'bivariate' approaches, 2) three generic IS strategic orientations can be detected, 3) user information satisfaction does not capture important strategic aspects of IS effectiveness, 4) IS strategic alignment is a better predictor of IS effectiveness than is strategic orientation, and 5) business strategic orientation, IS strategic alignment, and IS effectiveness have positive impacts on business performance.

(Information Systems Strategy; Strategic Orientation; Strategic Alignment; Fit; Systems/Technology Effectiveness; Business Performance)

## Introduction

Information systems and technologies are used to leverage unique business competences, merge companies, restructure industries, and facilitate global competition. The relation of information systems (IS) to company strategy and the attainment of competitive advantage has been the focus of much discussion during the past decade. Despite the fact that there have been numerous calls for research in this area (e.g., King 1987, Wiseman 1988b), few empirical studies have been carried out to determine the extent to, and manner in, which information systems complement company strategy. If such research findings were available, practitioners would be better equipped to prioritize relative IS investments. IS planners would be able to adjust portfolios of systems so that they provide relatively more business strategy support.

A central purpose of this study was to develop a set of tools that could be used to provide this kind of information—recognizing that no one toolkit would provide all the answers that companies look for when they review past IS investments and plan future investments. This study describes a conceptual model that illustrates proposed links between realized (as contrasted with planned) business and IS strategy, alignment, and performance. It presents survey instruments developed to operationalize these constructs, and describes the results of empirical tests of the measurement and structural models.

The article is organized as follows. First, the motivation for the research is discussed further. Next, the conceptual model is introduced. The development of research instruments and data gathering activities are then outlined. This is followed by a discussion of 'systems' and 'bivariate' research models and data analyses. Key findings are then presented, and management implications are highlighted. After this, research limitations and suggestions for future research are described. Finally, a summary of the key contributions made by the research is provided.

## 1. Motivation for the Research

IS strategic alignment is said to be one of the top ten challenges faced by CIOs (e.g., Bartholomew and Caldwell 1995, Niederman et al. 1991). However, alignment remains a nebulous concept that is difficult to understand and measure. This study attempts to improve our understanding of the concept. It presents different ways in which the term IS strategic alignment can be interpreted and describes alternative procedures for operationalizing this alignment.

This study addresses both researcher and practitioner needs. It develops and evaluates instruments to measure IS strategy and IS strategic alignment. These instruments have since been used within organizations to examine existing portfolios of company information systems in terms of the support they provide for business strategy, and to provide a measure of the alignment of IS strategy with business strategy. They have been used to highlight IS alignment 'gaps' and identify the kinds of systems that could usefully be added to a firm's IS portfolio.

## 2. Conceptual Model

Venkatraman (1985, 1989a) defined the 'strategic orientation of business enterprises' (STROBE) as:

... the general pattern of various means employed to achieve the business goals, with a particular emphasis on the business-unit level of the organizational hierarchy. (Venkatraman 1985, p. 25)

His conceptualization of strategic orientation represented a broad, holistic perspective of strategy built on research by Miles and Snow (1978) and examined “a broader [than product-market] package of managerial choices made to achieve the desired business objectives” (Venkatraman 1985, 24). Venkatraman viewed organizations as having characteristic orientations evident with respect to their marketplace behavior. These orientations were stable, i.e., more enduring than individual marketplace initiatives, and were not industry-specific, thus permitting cross-industry comparisons. Venkatraman proposed, and found empirical support for, the hypothesis that business strategic orientation, or realized business strategy, directly influences business performance (Venkatraman 1989a).

The conceptual model underlying the current research is depicted in Figure 1. It extends Venkatraman's work into the IS area, examining the relationships between strategy and performance at the IS level as well as at the business unit level. In the conceptual model, realized IS strategies are hypothesized to affect IS effectiveness, i.e., the perceived success and business value of organizational information systems. The term realized IS strategy refers to strategy evident in IS investment decisions and IS deployments, as contrasted with vocalized or documented IS strategy. Intended strategies may not be realized (Mintzberg 1978). The conceptual model also depicts the proposition that IS strategic alignment, the fit between business strategic orientation and IS strategic orientation, is directly related to IS effectiveness. The model is comprehensive. Although no one theoretical framework or argument can be cited to support all of its hypotheses, separate studies have examined the links between business strategy and performance (e.g., Beard and Dess 1981, Rumelt 1974, Snow and Hrebiniak 1980, White 1986, Venkatraman 1989a), business strategy and IS strategy (e.g., Bakos and Treacy 1986, Das et al. 1991, Henderson and Venkatraman 1992, King 1978, McFarlan 1984, Pankratz 1991, Parsons 1983, Rockart and Scott Morton 1984), IS strategy and IS effectiveness (e.g., Earl 1987, Henderson and Sifonis 1988, Lederer and Mendelow 1986), IS effectiveness and business performance (e.g., Clemons 1986, Copeland and McKenney 1988, Ives and Learmonth 1984, Johnston and Vitale 1988, Sethi 1988, Wiseman and MacMillan 1984), IS strategy and business performance (e.g., Earl 1989, Weill 1990), and the importance of strategic alignment (e.g., Drazin and Van de Ven 1985, Venkatraman and Camillus 1984, White 1986).

The relationships between constructs, largely discussed and tested in pairs in previous studies, are examined in this research in a more complete context (i.e., with several constructs being considered simultaneously). Paths could have been hypothesized between each possible pair of constructs in the model (e.g., between IS strategic orientation and business performance, and between business strategic orientation and IS effectiveness). However, our aim was to develop parsimonious yet powerful models. For example, no path was proposed between business strategic orientation and IS effectiveness, since business performance, not IS effectiveness, is the primary goal. Still other paths could have introduced unnecessary multicollinearity, and were therefore omitted from the model.

As will be discussed in detail later, the model was conceptualized at two levels. Figure 1 captures a 'systems' approach to the problem whereas an alternate 'bivariate' model would examine relationships at a lower-level of abstraction, i.e., between dimensions of the higher-order constructs in Figure 1.

It was recognized from the outset that it would be difficult to find empirical support for the model. The constructs being studied were high-level and 'messy' in nature. Also, because many other constructs not in the model (e.g., the structure of the business and the structure of the IS organization) would influence performance at the IS level and at the business unit level, it would be difficult to explain much of the variation in the dependent constructs.

To illustrate the dimensionality of STROBE, consider two business units. Business Unit A might score very highly on aggressiveness, external defensiveness, proactiveness and innovativeness, while scoring moderately on analysis, internal defensiveness, futurity and risk aversion. This could be a small relatively new operation trying to rapidly increase market share through marketplace alliances and several new product market offerings, while deemphasizing detailed risk analyses, efficiency controls, and long-term planning. Business Unit B might have a very different profile. For example, it might be part of a large organization, required to produce a great deal of documentation for its head offices. This kind of business unit might generate high scores for analysis, futurity, and risk aversion but lower scores for proactiveness and innovativeness. Depending on business priorities and goals, its scores for aggressiveness and internal and external defensiveness might be high or low.

The business strategic orientation data gathered by the modified STROBE instrument were subjective. The respondent, generally the CEO, was asked to describe the nature of the business unit's orientation or 'personality.' There may have been a tendency for CEOs to overstate the strength of their orientations—for example, to think of their companies as being innovative when in fact they were not. However, we were encouraged to proceed with the use of a refined version of STROBE because of Venkatraman's reports of its validity (Venkatraman 1985, 1989a). Also, inflated assertions would only reduce, not increase, the variation in the data, and consequently research findings would be conservative.

## 3.2. Business Performance

The second instrument assessed business performance, a complex, multi-faceted concept. Different stakeholders, such as employees, customers, or shareholders, employ different measures of performance. In the business strategy literature, it has often been suggested that multiple measures be used when trying to assess business performance (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1986). Venkatraman (1985, 1989a) related each of the STROBE dimensions to perceptions of two dimensions of business unit performance: market growth and profitability. As a result of our pilot testing, these two dimensions were split into four distinct dimensions: market growth, profitability, product-service innovation, and company reputation. These additional dimensions were supported by our review of the business strategy and performance literature (e.g., see Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1986). No new questionnaire items were added to the instrument, although a few questions were reworded. This modified version of Venkatraman's business performance instrument was then utilized in the research, as presented in Table 1, §2 and in Table 2, §2.

Performance measures were based on respondents' perceptions and statements. It was possible to verify the accuracy of respondent data in the pilot test activities. Questions which yielded unreliable data were not included in the final version of the business performance questionnaire. However, due to the large numbers of companies assessed in the mail survey, independent data, i.e., data not provided by respondents, were not gathered in the survey phase of the research. Independent documentation such as the annual reports of corporations would have been of limited value. Our focus was on business units. Our experiences with pilot test companies indicated that their profit and loss statements and balance sheet information frequently were not clearly specified in the corporate annual reports. We opted to use subjective, readily comparable data, while acknowledging that, although subjective data were frequently used in this kind of research and considered acceptable (Venkatraman and Ramanujam 1987, Dess and Robinson 1984), the subjective nature of the data gathered was a limitation of the research.

## 3.3. Information Systems Effectiveness

Delone and McLean identified six different aspects of 'IS success' that have been used in past IS research (Delone and McLean 1992). For our study, it was not feasible to develop an IS effectiveness instrument that included measures of all six categories. Our IS effectiveness instrument was but one of four instruments that had to be completed by different respondents in each cooperating organization. We did not wish to make this one instrument so large that it in turn required completion by multiple respondents. We chose to employ an IS effectiveness instrument composed primarily of measures from two key categories—user information satisfaction, and organizational impact—and to accept the limited coverage of the concept.

To measure user satisfaction, the study utilized a short form of the user information satisfaction (UIS) instrument which had repeatedly demonstrated high construct validity (Pearson 1977, Bailey and Pearson 1983, Ives et al. 1983, Baroudi and Orlikowski 1988, Galletta and Lederer 1989). The instrument was modified so that respondents were asked to comment on their satisfaction with company systems, instead of personal systems, and to make its format consistent with the other instruments in the study.

To determine how to measure IS strategic impact, an important component of IS organizational impact, a review of the literature was conducted. The strategic impact measures uncovered focused on business process, marketplace, and economic effects of information systems (e.g., Downs 1988, Ives and Learmonth 1984, Johnston and Vitale 1988, McFarlan 1984, Wiseman 1988a, 1984). Items were pilot tested and refined before being used. Table 1, §3 presents the seven dimensions of IS effectiveness measured. Three of these dimensions assess user information satisfaction; the remaining four assess IS strategic contributions. Table 2, §§3-A and 3-B provide sample IS effectiveness items.

## 3.4. Realized Information Systems Strategy

The fourth research instrument was created by the researchers to assess the 'strategic orientation of the existing portfolio of information systems applications' (STROEPIS). This instrument was to determine the nature of the information systems that had been used by business unit personnel for a year or more to allow for a performance time lag (Weill 1988). As with business strategy, the emphasis was on actual, or realized, IS strategy. End users and IS managers were not asked to describe documented strategies or plans. The systems used by company personnel, and other pertinent information technology deployments, such as those used by customers and suppliers, were examined.

The STROEPIS instrument was designed explicitly to parallel STROBE. For each individual STROBE questionnaire item, a parallel STROEPIS item was created to determine whether company personnel had access to information systems that supported that particular aspect of business strategic orientation. Consider, for example, a small business unit with traditional accounting, budgeting, payroll, and human resource software packages. It might provide low to moderate IS support only for analysis, risk aversion, and internal defensiveness. That is, the average scores for these STROEPIS dimensions might be low to moderate, between 1 and 3 out of 5. Contrast this profile with that of a larger business unit with a suite of packaged and custom-built information systems which could include market intelligence systems (which could provide varying degrees of support for proactiveness, external defensiveness, and aggressiveness), executive information systems (which could support proactiveness and analysis), brainstorming and decision support systems (which could support innovativeness and proactiveness), expert systems (which could support analysis, risk aversion and internal defensiveness), and sophisticated forecasting systems (which could support futurity and analysis). This unit would also be likely to have a more solid IS infrastructure, e.g., networks in place could provide additional support for analysis, proactiveness, innovativeness, and internal defensiveness. The average scores for the STROEPIS dimensions would probably be quite high, ranging between 4 and 5 out of 5. Clearly, different companies have different application portfolios and infrastructures. A particular deployment of information systems may lend itself more or less well to providing support for routine business activities and/or specific initiatives.

STROEPIS captures one aspect of IS strategy—actual or realized deployments of hardware, software, and networks. It does not capture factors such as end user education and implementation approaches (King 1978, Mensching and Adams 1991). Taking such factors into account would probably strengthen the links between IS strategy and IS effectiveness. Realistically, however, there was only so much data that could be gathered from respondents without lowering response rates to unacceptable levels.

It must be emphasized that STROEPIS focused on IS activity, not on IS plans or IS intentions. In a similar way, STROBE focused on typical company activity and emphasized realized business strategy as opposed to business plans or intentions. Many companies do not make verbal or written IS plans. Despite this, most do have information technology-based information systems in place. These systems tell us something about their realized IS strategies, regardless of (the lack of) formalization of their IS planning processes.

## 3.5. IS Strategic Alignment

IS strategic alignment was viewed as the alignment between business unit strategic orientation and IS strategic orientation. A priori, it was expected that most companies would employ the kinds of systems that supported their strategic orientations, i.e., companies would tend to have good IS strategic alignment. However, it was recognized that for various reasons, such as resource constraints or internal company turbulence, some companies would be more successful at developing appropriate systems than others—that is, some companies would have better IS strategic alignment.

A calculation was performed upon the data received from the STROBE and STROEPIS questionnaires to determine each organization's IS strategic alignment. As is discussed below, the research models were cast at two levels. The 'systems' model (Figure 2) involved the computation of a single overall IS strategic alignment profile or score. The 'bivariate' model (Figure 3) rested on the premise that overall strategic alignment could be meaningfully disaggregated into components examining particular STROBE and STROEPIS dimensions (Venkatraman 1989b). A third, natural selection model described by Drazin and Van de Ven (1985), is appropriate only when there are no dependent variables of interest, and did not apply in this research.

From a measurement approach perspective, 'matching' or difference scores were calculated as were 'moderation' or product scores (Venkatraman 1989b). Matching approaches utilized difference scores in the calculation of alignment; moderation approaches utilized interaction terms or weighted scores. Venkatraman (1989b) described these and four other alignment measurement approaches—mediation, covariation, profile deviation, and gestalts.

## 4. Instrument Refinement

The four research instruments were pilot tested and refined. It was not assumed that because instruments had been shown to be reliable and valid in other research settings, they would be reliable and valid in our own research context. The instruments were reviewed initially by a panel of fifteen academics. An instrument item sorting exercise was carried out by another eleven colleagues in order to further improve the face validity and discriminant validity of each of the constructs being measured (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Items that were not grouped as expected were reworded or removed.

Feedback on the appropriateness of the questionnaire instruments was then obtained from several senior personnel at Andersen Consulting. Minor changes in instrument layout and the wording of general instructions were made. The research instruments were also pilot tested in twelve North American manufacturing, banking and insurance organizations. In each organization, four separate meetings were held with senior executives who completed the instruments, commented on their user friendliness and comprehensiveness, and suggested appropriate key informants for the survey which followed.

## 5. Survey Design and Execution

To gather the data required to empirically test the measurement and structural models, a mail survey was administered in accordance with the procedures advocated by Dillman (1978). The mailing was to North American firms operating in the financial services and manufacturing industries. These industries were considered to be information-intensive. In general, firms in these industries process high volumes of data and rely heavily on computer-related technologies for information handling. It was thought that the more important information technology was to company operations, the more readily discernible would be the links among IS strategic orientation, IS strategic alignment, and business performance.

Dun and Bradstreet directories were used to compile a list of 1200 North American companies that had more than 100 employees and were classified under one of the following Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes:

Manufacturing

## 7. Data Analyses

Data gathered from 164 of the 170 companies providing complete sets of questionnaires were used. In five instances, respondent background information supplied indicated that a single executive might have completed more than one of the four company questionnaires. In another instance, the data were received after the start of the analyses. Thus six sets were excluded.

## 7.1. Assessing the Dimensionality of the Higher-Order Constructs

Principal components analyses were carried out in two ways in order to determine whether the expected factor structures would emerge: 1) using all the questionnaire items in all four instruments in one factor analysis, and 2) carrying out four separate factor analyses—one each for the items in each of the instruments. The findings from the first set of analyses were as expected—all items loaded correctly on the instrument for which they were intended (Chan 1992) and are not presented here. The findings from the second set of analyses were not entirely as expected, and are discussed further in Appendix 2 and summarized below.

The factor analyses of the STROBE and business performance instruments resulted in emergent factor structures that largely resembled the hypothesized structures. The factor analysis of STROEPIS indicated that, although distinct, the hypothesized STROEPIS factors were interdependent. The analysis suggested three emergent factors (IS Support for Analysis, IS Support for Action, and IS Support for Anticipation) as a simpler, alternative, orthogonal dimensionalization of IS strategic orientation.

The factor analysis of the IS effectiveness instrument revealed a clear separation between the factors measuring User Information Satisfaction (UIS) and the factors measuring the strategic contributions of IS. This provided support for the view that UIS measures, despite modifications to focus assessments on the business units' information systems rather than on personal systems, provide limited assessments of organization-level IS impacts (Raghunathan and King 1988; see also the discussion of inter-rater reliability in Appendix 2).

Overall, the hypothesized factor structures of STROBE, STROEPIS, business performance and IS effectiveness received substantial empirical support. STROEPIS, however, could have been modeled more simply.

## 7.2. Selecting the Most Appropriate Model for IS Strategic Alignment

An important research objective was to determine how best to assess IS strategic alignment. This required an examination of alternative bivariate and systems, and matching and moderation, models of alignment to identify the model(s) providing the greatest explanation and prediction of the data gathered. The systems moderation, i.e., a weighted overall, conceptualization of alignment was judged empirically to be the most useful model (Chan and Huff 1993b). It was employed in the analyses described in Appendix 2.

## 8. Discussion of Findings and Research Implications

## 8.1. Research Instruments

The instruments to assess business strategic orientation, IS strategic orientation, IS effectiveness, and business performance were generally shown to be valid and reliable and are recommended for use by other researchers. Some valuable lessons were learned about the IS strategic orientation and IS effectiveness instruments that might be useful in future studies.

The IS strategic orientation construct potentially has a simpler structure than that originally hypothesized in this article. Three core dimensions—IS Support for Anticipation, IS Support for Analysis, and IS Support for Action—appear to be relatively independent dimensions of IS strategic orientation. More fine-grained dimensions, although feasible, are clearly interrelated. $^{4}$ In effect, this study's empirical findings suggest a taxonomy of three generic realized IS strategies. Information technology deployments in organizations can be viewed in terms of combinations of, and relative emphases on, these three orientations.

The seven dimensions of IS effectiveness formed two separate clusters—UIS and strategic impact—suggesting that IS satisfaction measures were not just another way of assessing IS strategic importance; these factors were very different. This in turn implies that studies investigating organizational IS effectiveness may be well-advised not to rely solely on UIS-based measures.

The IS effectiveness instrument also demonstrated a significant degree of user specificity. The finding that different end users had different perceptions of IS effectiveness is not surprising. It points to the dilemma many firms face today when they attempt to evaluate their IS functions. Many regard IS as a 'black box,' and are hard pressed to identify, and agree on, specific contributions that information systems make. In future studies and in this study, obtaining multiple perspectives on IS performance could permit the aggregation of data to produce less user-specific measures. In this study, this would have required that additional questionnaires be sent to each company, and might have further reduced the overall survey response rate.

## 8.2. Appropriate Models of IS Strategic Alignment (Matching vs. Moderation Approaches)

Both matching and moderation models of alignment suggest that high IS support for unimportant aspects of a business unit's strategy (i.e., LOWSTROBE, HIGH-STROEPIS) represents poor alignment. High IS support for more important aspects of business strategy represents far greater alignment. While both models are useful, in this research, the moderation model was selected as the preferred model for IS strategic alignment. $^{5}$

Our findings suggested that a HIGHSTROBE\* HIGHSTROEPIS combination was associated with peak perceived performance. Such a STROBE\*STROEPIS combination appeared to impact performance very differently from a LOWSTROBE\*LOWSTROEPIS combination. This perspective differs from the matching, or difference score, approach which predicts that a LOW-LOW combination would be equally effective as a HIGH-HIGH combination because there would still be a good match between STROBE and STROEPIS (Schoonhoven 1981; see also Appendix 1). For the firms studied, a matching perspective was not well supported (Chan and Huff 1993b). The data analyses suggested that future studies go beyond a simple equivalence conceptualization of IS strategic alignment and consider adopting a more complex but realistic contingency perspective. This issue is illustrated more fully in Appendix 1.

In our study, the moderation model represented an importance-weighted sum-of-IS-support scores. $^{6}$ STROBE determined the relative importance or weights of the alignment dimensions. STROEPIS indicated the degree of IS support for each alignment dimension. Thus IS strategic alignment—modeled using a moderation approach—was a useful weighted measure of the degree of support provided by the existing portfolio of IS applications for the business unit's realized strategy.

From a descriptive perspective, Table 4 shows the distribution of IS strategic alignment scores from the moderation model. IS strategic alignment was generally high, as the mean scores illustrate, suggesting that companies were utilizing systems that supported their strategic orientations. However, there was still a good deal of alignment variation, as seen by the minimum and maximum alignment scores, and room for improvement.

## 8.3. Appropriate Models of Strategy, Alignment and Performance (Bivariate vs. Systems Perspectives)

The findings suggest that systems models of alignment provide more useful information than do bivariate models. The bivariate research model (see Figure 3) involved the examination of numerous 'fine-grained' rerelationships (over 100 bivariate relationships were tested). Many of these relationships, although statistically significantly, were weak and nonsubstantive. Because the unidimensional constructs were not independent, the bivariate findings proved to be somewhat unstable. However, reliable findings were produced when the systems model was examined—that is, when the emphasis was placed on testing the five key relationships among higher-order constructs (see Figures 2 and 4).

## 8.5. Strategy-Related IS Theory Development

The concept of IS strategic orientation is somewhat novel and is an area ripe for future research. Viewing systems in terms of the support they provide for various company orientations appears to yield valuable information that could be used to determine IS strategic alignment, which in turn yields valuable predictive information regarding perceived IS and business performance. Perhaps, just as individuals find that certain tools (e.g., a calculator or blackboard) better enable them to be effective, the same is true for the use of information systems by companies.

## 9.1. The Importance of Realized IS Strategy

This study highlights the importance of realized, as opposed to planned, IS strategy. It challenges managers to think not only in terms of their planned IS portfolio and infrastructure investments, but to explicitly assess and reckon with emergent IS strategy. The systems that do get installed, and successfully used, may be only a fraction of the ones that received formal planning approval and/or may have been created without the assistance of IS personnel; realized and intended IS strategies frequently diverge.

Our research is also a reminder that it is not enough just to plan. It is not the IS plan or vision that forms the basis of our assessments of IS alignment. Rather, we have monitored the systems that are in place. The study suggests that whereas it may be useful to deflect current dissatisfaction with systems by referring to systems which are planned, there is a limit to the usefulness of such an approach. IS managers must come to terms with the strengths and weaknesses of the support they currently are, or are not, providing.

## 9.2. The Importance of IS Strategic Alignment

The findings suggest that companies with high IS strategic alignment are better performing companies. Although the findings implied that there were 'several ways to win' (i.e., no one strategic orientation was clearly superior in all situations), alignment between business and IS strategic orientations was linked to IS effectiveness and business performance. The findings were convincing to the point that we have developed, and are now using, practitioner-oriented versions of the instruments to enable managers to assess and improve their business and IS strategic orientations and strategic alignment.

We also conclude that companies should think in terms of enhancing IS strategic alignment when allocating scarce IS resources. Organizations should provide

IS to support their particular business strategic orientations which are producing desired marketplace results, allocating more resources to IS projects which provide this support. Organizations must keep in mind the saying: 'To thine own self, be true.'

The findings also suggest that decisions regarding the direction, staffing, and use of the IS function have potentially important performance consequences. If it can be assumed that intended IS strategy is linked to realized IS strategy which we know is linked to IS strategic alignment, then, in information-intensive industries, the formulation of appropriate IS strategy should have the attention and participation of senior executives. Without this attention, important goal congruence might not be realized.

## 9.3. The Importance of a Comprehensive (as Opposed to Complex) IS Strategy

Another finding was that aspects of IS strategic orientation were interrelated to a greater degree than aspects of the other constructs studied. Three distinct kinds of IS may need to be provided in organizations—IS which support action, IS which support analysis, and IS which support future orientation or anticipation. It would seem that there need not be eight distinct types of information systems visible within an organization in order for there to be support for each of the eight dimensions of business strategic orientation. A small number of system types could provide support for several aspects of realized business strategy.

In addition, not all three kinds of IS seemed to be equally important for IS effectiveness. For instance, for a large group of companies studied, IS support for analysis was linked least to IS performance; IS support for action and IS support for anticipation were associated with more valuable IS contributions to organizational effectiveness (Chan and Huff 1993a). This has implications for the evaluation and prioritization of existing and proposed company IS. Systems' actual or expected support for company action, analysis, and anticipation could be identified, and these forms of support ranked in terms of company importance.

## 9.4. Potentially Important Relationships Among Strategy, Alignment and Performance

Several complex relationships among business and IS strategy, alignment and performance were detected.

This provides practitioners with empirical evidence that IS strategy and alignment are linked to perceived IS and business unit performance. It provides support for current management judgement and intuition indicating that information systems do make contributions to business performance. This is heartening after much recent discussion of the poor payoff of IS and the 'productivity paradox' (e.g., Roach 1991). This study supports the importance of continued senior management attention to, and investment in, information technology. At the same time, it underscores difficulties involved in detecting narrow bivariate performance gains. IS benefits often will need to be examined more holistically.

## 10. Research Limitations and

## Suggestions for Future Research

A number of research limitations and suggestions have already been discussed. Here we simply summarize and add to this discussion. First, it must be recognized that this study is based on recent theory in the business strategy reference discipline and is somewhat exploratory. The research models presented represent only a subset of the possible models that could have been formulated. Should we have included a path between realized IS strategy and IS effectiveness, for example? Or, could we not have argued a priori that the influence of realized IS strategy on IS effectiveness is experienced only through IS strategic alignment? We feel it appropriate to leave such questions for future studies.

This study focused on the content and nature of realized IS strategy, strategic alignment, and effectiveness. The processes involved in formulating strategy and achieving alignment (e.g., Reich and Benbasat 1992) were not the subject of this study. Other researchers may wish to focus on related process issues.

Despite the fact that sufficient data were gathered to carry out the necessary model tests, the survey response rate may appear somewhat low—just under 20%. A number of researchers have argued that for studies of this kind which involve senior company executives, address sensitive strategic issues, and require multiple informants, this response rate is actually quite good (e.g., Venkatraman 1989a, Raghunathan and King 1988). However, we were constrained in terms of our ability to split the dataset into subgroups of interest (e.g., high performance companies versus low performance companies, or U.S. versus Canadian companies). Studies that examine IS alignment in narrow domains of interest (e.g., a survey of top performing companies) could add much to the IS literature.

Also, as mentioned earlier, subjective data (e.g., on company reputation) were employed in the study. Company annual reports and other publicly available corporate data could not be employed meaningfully because the unit of analysis was the business unit, which was usually not the parent corporation. Also, because many of the organizations had different accounting practices, the public data that could be gathered were not readily comparable.

The findings with respect to IS effectiveness, in particular, must be interpreted cautiously. Responses regarding IS effectiveness were somewhat user specific, as opposed to company specific, despite the use of key informants. Ideally, we should have obtained multiple assessments of IS effectiveness which could then have been aggregated. Ideally, we should have been able to identify which respondents should provide the IS effectiveness data in each organization, and not have left that decision to the CEO who, as a reviewer pointed out, may have delegated the decision to the CIO who in turn may have selected a friendly business partner instead of a VP who used information systems that were strategically important to the business unit. However, given the large numbers of firms invited to participate in the study, and the great variety of these firms, it was not feasible to identify specific respondents within each firm. Researchers are encouraged to continue their search for meaningful, objective measures, and robust, subjective measures, of IS performance.

Finally, caution is required in generalizing the study's findings beyond small to medium-sized 'for profit' manufacturing and financial services organizations in North America. Additional research is required to explore the applicability of IS strategic orientation and our conceptualization of IS alignment in large firms, other industries, and other countries.

## 11. Summary of Research Contributions

In summary, this study contributed to our understanding of IS and business strategy in the following ways:

First, this study validated, integrated, and expanded on, earlier research in the area (e.g., Venkatraman 1989a, Bailey and Pearson 1983, Downs 1988). Modified instruments were developed. An entirely new instrument, STROEPIS, who also introduced. The usefulness of the IS strategic orientation concept for IS researchers and practitioners was demonstrated. In addition, different procedures for evaluating IS strategic alignment were described, and a moderation approach to assessing alignment was recommended for future use. In this way, the study provided tested approaches for the quantification and assessment of realized IS strategy and IS strategic alignment—constructs described only qualitatively in much of the current IS literature.

Second, the study presented and examined systems and bivariate models of business and IS strategy, alignment and performance. A systems perspective was advocated.

Third, relationships among realized business strategy, realized IS strategy, alignment and performance were examined. Empirical links between IS strategic alignment and IS effectiveness, and IS strategic alignment and business performance, were demonstrated. $^{7}$

## Appendix 1. A Comparison of Matching and Moderation Models of Alignment

## A Basic 'Matching' Model of IS Strategic Alignment

Assume that for a given company the score received for the STROBE analysis 'we rely on formal planning techniques' indicator was 3, and that the score received for the parallel STROEPIS 'our information systems provide planning tools' indicator was also 3. A 'misalignment' score of 0, or an alignment score of 4, would be assigned. (The individual STROBE and STROEPIS scores ranged from 1 to 5. In the case of perfect alignment, they would be coincident. In the case of poorest alignment, the misalignment score would be 5 minus 1, i.e., 4, and the alignment score would be 0. The bigger the misalignment score, the smaller the alignment score, and vice versa.) If, on the other hand, the score received for the STROEPIS 'our information systems provide planning tools' indicator was 5, and the STROBE 'planning techniques' score remained equal to 3, then a misalignment score of 2 (the difference between 3 and 5) would be assigned, and the alignment score (computed by deducting the absolute value of the misalignment score from 4) would be 4 minus 2, i.e., 2. Alignment scores could be computed for all the indicators for a particular dimension, and averaged to produce one overall IS alignment score for that dimension of strategy.

A bivariate, matching model would involve examining the relationship between an individual STROBE-STROEPIS dimension and performance. A systems, matching approach, on the other hand, would involve considering all the STROBE-STROEPIS dimensions simultaneously. In our research, we weighted these dimensions equally.

## A Basic 'Moderation' Model of IS Strategic Alignment

The 'matching' difference score approach to measuring alignment has limitations which a 'moderation' or interaction approach avoids (Edwards forthcoming). The theoretical interpretation of alignment modeled as interaction is quite different from that of alignment modeled by difference scores. Instead of the match, or degree of parallelism, between STROBE and STROEPIS influencing both business performance and IS effectiveness, the proposition was instead that STROEPIS moderated the relationship between STROBE and business performance. In a similar fashion, STROBE moderated the relationship between STROEPIS and IS effectiveness. It was the combination of, or synergy between, STROBE and STROEPIS rather than the difference between the two, that was most important. STROBE\*STROEPIS product terms were computed instead of STROBE-STROEPIS differences. In other respects, the alignment computations remained unchanged.

Both matching and moderation models of IS strategic alignment were tested in this research. Support for the moderation model implied that the greater the value of STROBE, the greater the impact of STROEPIS on business performance; the greater the value of STROEPIS, the greater the impact of STROBE on IS effectiveness (Schoonhoven 1981). The effect of STROBE was increased by higher values of STROEPIS and vice versa. To achieve high alignment, STROEPIS did not need to parallel STROBE. Instead, STROEPIS could act as a catalyst or multiplier.

The moderation approach has been utilized frequently within business strategy contingency studies (Venkatraman 1989b). Within the context of this IS study, moderation implied that the form and/or strength of the effect that company IS strategy had on IS effectiveness was contingent on business strategy; similarly, the form and/or strength of the effect that business strategy had on business performance was contingent on IS strategy (Prescott 1986).

As with the matching model, both bivariate and systems moderation models were employed. The bivariate approach focused on individual STROBE\*STROEPIS pairs and their implications for performance. The systems approach incorporated all these pairs simultaneously, weighting the dimensions equally.

## Appendix 2. Detailed Data Analyses

## Assessing the Dimensionality of the Constructs

STROBE. When the STROBE items were factor analyzed, the factors that emerged were predominantly those that were expected thereby confirming the hypothesized dimensionality. Company Futurity, Proactiveness, Risk Aversion, Aggressiveness, and Analysis all emerged clearly as factors. However, the hypothesized External and Internal Defensiveness dimensions emerged as one Overall Defensiveness factor. This was not entirely unexpected. Originally, Venkatraman hypothesized that Internal and External Defensiveness formed a single dimension. It was his later data analyses that suggested that Defensiveness be split (Venkatraman 1985).

In addition, a First Mover Orientation factor emerged, comprised of items intended to measure several theoretical dimensions—Innovativeness, Proactiveness, Aggressiveness, and Defensiveness. These items appeared to indicate a preference for being on the leading edge with respect to process and product innovativeness.

STROEPIS. When the STROEPIS items were factor analyzed, three independent factors emerged:

\- IS Support for Futurity (or Anticipation) emerged clearly as a factor. The questionnaire items loading highly on this factor emphasized a long-term, forward-looking orientation.

\- IS Support for Risk Aversion and IS Support for Analysis emerged as one overall IS Support for Analysis factor which represented the use of IS to carry out conservative, detailed analyses.

\- IS Support for Action emerged as a factor comprised of items measuring IS Support for Aggressiveness, IS Support for Internal and External Defensiveness, IS Support for Proactiveness, and IS Support for Innovativeness. These kinds of systems supported an 'action' orientation and appeared to be related.

Although distinct (see the discussion on discriminant validity below), the hypothesized STROEPIS factors were interdependent. This led to the three emergent factors as a simpler, alternative, orthogonal classification of IS strategic orientation.

Information Systems Effectiveness. The factor analysis of the IS effectiveness items revealed a clear separation between the factors measuring user information satisfaction (UIS) and the factors measuring the strategic contributions of IS. The three proposed UIS dimensions emerged clearly as factors, although two of the three (Satisfaction with IS Staff and Services, and Satisfaction with End User Knowledge and Involvement) emerged together as one factor.

The hypothesized dimensions of the IS effectiveness construct which measured strategic contributions of IS, also emerged clearly. IS Contributions to the Establishment of Market Linkages emerged as a factor in its own right. However, some of the items originally intended to measure this dimension also grouped together with items intended to measure IS Contributions to Products and Services. The factor combining these two sets of items was labelled IS Contributions to Market Growth. A final IS effectiveness factor emerged measuring IS Contributions to Operational Efficiency and Management Effectiveness.

Business Performance. Substantial support for the proposed dimensions of the business performance instrument was received. Only the Company Reputation factor did not emerge as clearly as it could have. This was not surprising, however, as this dimension, which was introduced as a result of the pilot testing of the business performance instrument, was measured by only two items. See also the discussion of Company Reputation in the Discussion of Findings and Research Implications.

In summary, overall, the hypothesized factor structures were well supported empirically, with the exception of STROEPIS which could have been modeled using fewer dimensions. In the data analyses described below, the hypothesized, as opposed to emergent, factor structures were employed. This was done in order to test the a priori research propositions and to facilitate IS strategic alignment computations by maintaining parallel STROBE and STROEPIS structures. However, analyses also were carried out using the emergent factor structures. The research findings did not change substantially.
