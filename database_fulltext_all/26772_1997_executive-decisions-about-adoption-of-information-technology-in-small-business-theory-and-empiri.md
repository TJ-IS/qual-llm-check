# Executive Decisions About Adoption of Information Technology in Small Business: Theory and Empirical Tests

David A. Harrison • Peter P. Mykytyn, Jr. • Cynthia K. Riemenschneider
Department of Management, University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, Texas 76019
harrison@uta.edu

Department of Information Systems and Management Sciences, University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, Texas 76019 mykytyn@uta.edu

Department of Computer Information Systems and Quantitative Analysis, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, Arkansas 72701

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) was used to explain and predict small business executives' decisions to adopt information technology (IT). These theories were tested in a multiphase field study involving 162 small businesses ( $25 \leq n \leq 200$ employees) from a broad set of industries considering a variety of ITs. Results indicate strong support for a decision process based on attitude (perceived positive and negative consequences for the firm), subjective norm (social expectations), and perceived control (resources to overcome obstacles) regarding IT adoption. Additional variables such as firm and individual executive characteristics had no unique effect on adoption decisions. However, as business size increased, so did the importance of expectations from the (social) environment, while the importance of intra-firm consequences and control over potential adoption barriers declined.

(Information Technology Usage in Small Business; Theory of Planned Behavior; Information Technology Adoption Decisions)

## 1. Introduction

The adoption and use of information technology (IT) to achieve a competitive advantage has received a great deal of attention in recent information systems (IS) literature. In fact, Niederman, Brancheau, and Wetherbe (1991) found the topic to be one of the top ten most important issues facing IS executives. They concluded that it is closely related to and provides a basis for many other important IS challenges. King and Sabherwal (1992) also showed the continuing importance of this issue by empirically examining key characteristics of strategic IS, such as the form of competitive advantage provided, and the relationship of IS with contextual factors such as the type of organization and its environment. Other perspectives on adopting IT for competitive advantage include staying in business and beating the competition (Sprague and McNurlin 1993). To achieve a competitive advantage is one of the many, and perhaps one of the most important, reasons that firms consider investing in a particular IT. That is, this fairly recent work on competitive advantage provides a new perspective in an extensive body of research on how and why firms adopt ITs.

Despite the importance of keeping pace with technological progress, however, the efforts of many organizations to adopt and apply IT lag behind their opportunities to do so (Niederman et al. 1991). Indeed, little research has been conducted to improve our understanding of the relationships between adoption of IT innovations, their management, and their successful use in organizations (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Brancheau and Wetherbe (1990) also report there is little empirical research that addresses how to facilitate the organizational learning required to adopt and use IT. Yet, the lack of a strong, general theoretical or organizing framework to investigate IT adoption decisions is a problem that has caused considerable difficulty, and has resulted in mixed and inconclusive research results in this area (Moore and Benbasat 1991).

## 1.1. Large and Small Firms

Despite mixed results, IT adoption research is still active, especially in large businesses. Leonard-Barton and Sinha (1993) reported on internal technology transfer relating to 34 software projects in four multibillion dollar electronics companies. Leonard-Barton (1992) also examined technology innovation in another study involving Ford Motor Co., Chaparral Steel, Hewlett-Packard, and other firms. Agarwal et al. (1991) studied the introduction of new expert systems technology by the MIS group to users at Carrier, one of the United States' largest manufacturers of air conditioning equipment. Attewell and Rule (1991) recognized this focus on large companies and stated that, in general, research dealing with just the largest thousand or so corporations creates a potential bias in substantive conclusions about IT because firm size can affect several crucial organizational processes. Further, since more than half the working population in the United States is employed by firms of 500 persons or less, such IT research fails to provide an accurate assessment of the effects of IT on persons in many organizations.

At the same time, increasing numbers of small firms have adopted widely available ITs or have continued to upgrade what they have (Cragg and King 1993). Thus, it is reasonable to suggest that smaller businesses might hope to achieve competitive advantages or other benefits by adopting IT. Additionally, the nature of smaller businesses has changed considerably, with terms such as small-business office, mobile office, and satellite office emerging. Seymour (1995) indicates that these alternative work arrangements facilitated by IT can provide improvements or efficiencies over more traditional settings. Furthermore, decreasing costs of hardware and software useful for smaller businesses: LANS, graphically oriented applications, voice, scanning, and fax integration, provide opportunities for these types of organizations that were unavailable or too costly to consider until recently. DeLone (1988) supports this perspective, indicating that many of the IT adoption and implementation issues facing large organizations are also present in their smaller counterparts, although the ways that small versus large firms deal with those issues can differ greatly.

Most IT adoption research dealing with small firms has been exploratory. Such research is informative and may serve to identify important aspects of IT such as computing practices (Palvia and Jackson 1990), information satisfaction (Montazemi 1988), and success factors (DeLone 1988). For the most part, however, it has not been grounded in a broad theory that can lead to general inferences about how small businesses adopt IT. Our paper addresses this lack of general theory, as well as the scarcity of research dealing with IT in small firms—especially the lack of research on the adoption of IT for competitive purposes in small firms. The overriding goal of our research, therefore, is to use broad theory to identify the factors underlying decisions made by small business executives regarding strategic adoption of an IT: that is, the decision to use IT in a way that executives believe would help them compete.

## 1.2. Broad Theoretical Perspectives

In terms of theory development, IS researchers have suggested that intention models or behavioral decision theories from social psychology can provide a foundation for research on IT adoption by firms and IT usage by individuals (Swanson 1982, Christie 1981). Fishbein and Ajzen's (1975) and Ajzen and Fishbein's (1980) Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) and Ajzen's (1991) Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) are well-researched intention models that incorporate grounded concepts and principles. Both have been successful in predicting important behaviors in a wide variety of domains. As both theories describe an active, deliberate decision process within the constraints of social expectations and limited resources, they should be especially useful for studying the strategic adoption of IT in small businesses. In this paper, we empirically examine the capability of the TRA and the TPB to predict and explain how senior executives in small business decide to adopt an IT to improve their competitive position. As the TPB is a later and expanded version of the TRA that also incorporates TRA's constructs, we emphasize the TPB throughout the rest of the paper.

It should be stated that Rogers (1983), among others, conducted research on adoption and especially diffusion of IT innovations. Rogers' work can also serve as a point of comparison for understanding general IT adoption decisions, which are more general than decisions to innovate with IT. We have chosen to base our IT adoption research on the "grounded" TPB, (i.e., for each application of the theory, one must elicit salient cognitions or perceptions about the behavior or decision in question, which are then fitted into a general framework describing how the cognitions relate to one another in determining the eventual decision). We highlight features of the TPB that are similar and distinct from the research of Rogers (1983) and Moore and Benbasat (1991) when discussing specific theoretical components.

## 1.3. Assumptions

Despite some of the advantages of using the TPB in this context, we also need to note important assumptions and preconditions of its applicability. For example, the theory assumes a degree of forethought and attentive information processing. For the TPB to be relevant, IT adoption decisions need to be important enough to be actively deliberated. Executives must connect such decisions with meaningful outcomes for their firms. For example, the purchase of a new mouse would be a relatively trivial decision, and would probably not be taken seriously or given much thought. Therefore, our use of the theory does not include such minor IT adoptions. On the other hand, new or upgraded LANs, imaging technology, or improved access to computer-based information by customers would be more consequential for a firm. Considering such ITs would tend to focus executives' thinking, and would provide more meaningful insights into the adoption decision process. Accordingly, a precondition of our use of the theory was that executives associated the IT adoption with improving their competitiveness as a small business. In this way, the adoption decision was also a strategic one, as defined by King and Teo (1996) as well as Ives and Learmouth (1984).

Another important assumption of the TPB is that it applies to individual decisions. Its extension and fit to group decision-making has not been demonstrated. Therefore, we use and test the theory with regard to decisions made by individual executives, rather than to a top management team. The extent that these individual decision processes accurately reflect firm-level decisions is an important concern that we attempted to address. First, the fact that our investigation deals with small businesses (many with just one senior executive) reduces potential agency conflicts between individual-level and firm-level decisions. Second, our respondents reported job titles or position descriptions which conveyed that they were the sole or primary decision makers regarding major IT adoptions. Finally, respondent judgments about how much the firm's final IT adoption depended on their individual opinion revealed that most of the sampled executives felt they were the sole or primary decision maker.

In the next section of this paper, we discuss relevant research on adoption and diffusion of IT, present a brief review of IT/IS research involving small businesses, lay out the basic components of the TPB, and present our specific hypotheses. Then, we review our research method, including procedures and instrumentation. Following that, we present empirical results. The paper concludes with a discussion of our findings and suggestions for further study.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Small Business Research on IT

There is a diverse, unintegrated body of literature on many aspects of IT adoption and use in small business. Raymond (1988) investigated the impact of computer training on the attitudes and usage behavior of 91 small business managers working at 34 wood and metal products manufacturers. Using structured interviews and questionnaires, Raymond found that managers with computer education and training had stronger feelings of participation and understanding. They used computers more often and in more ways, and they had more computer experience.

Montazemi (1988) surveyed 83 small service and manufacturing firms to examine the relationship between computing issues in small companies and end-user satisfaction. One hundred sixty-four interviews involving end users and data processing personnel revealed that end user literacy, participation, and a formal analysis of information requirements generated higher user satisfaction. In tune with much of the reorganizational activity of the 1990s, end user satisfaction was higher in decentralized organizations.

DeLone (1988) studied 98 small manufacturing firms, defining small as those companies with ≤300 employees. He found effectiveness of computer use to be linked to the involvement of the CEO in computerization efforts, to on-site computers, and to the coordinated implementation of planning and controls. CEO involvement was the strongest factor. He also found that effectiveness was not linked to external programmers, employees' enthusiasm for computer technology, length of time of computer use, formal training, or planning. DeLone concluded that small firms need CEOs who are knowledgeable about computers and who are committed to participating in strategic decisions about IT. This finding is especially relevant to the current research in that our study deals with senior executives' decisions about IT adoption.

According to Treadgold (1990), three main groups govern the speed of adoption of an innovative product such as a new IT: the role external agents play in influencing the adoption, the character of the population of potential adopters, and the nature of the innovation. He found that those small retail businesses with high IT adoption rates had been influenced by four agents of the distribution channel: trade associations, wholesalers, voluntary groups and franchisers that encouraged IT adoption. Treadgold's findings also lend insight to the current study, in that both deal with some of the sources of social expectation regarding adoption of an IT.

Kagan et al. (1990) surveyed 884 randomly selected small business firms in the upper Midwest to examine differences in information uses and needs as a function of firm type (e.g., manufacturing, retailing). Results indicated that use of IT in small business is unique to each small business sector, that the intensity of computing use varies among sectors and firms, and that wholesaling and retailing sectors were the most sophisticated software users. Their research supports some of the methodology in the present study, as their findings show that research on IT adoption in small business should be flexible enough to involve a wide variety of ITs.

Cragg and King's (1993) longitudinal case analysis examined the evolution of small-firm computing, to help understand what motivates or inhibits this evolution. Cragg and King focused on applications growth and used six small manufacturing firms to identify such motivating and inhibiting factors. Motivators were classified into four categories: relative advantage, competitive pressure, consultant support, and managerial enthusiasm. "Relative advantage . . . refers to the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes" (p. 53). It is also identical to one of Rogers' (1983) characteristics describing an innovation, discussed in the next section. Cragg and King found that three firms wanted to save time and effort by improving their outdated accounting systems. Four firms also sought economic benefits by improving planning and control through the effective use of predominantly internal data. In addition to one firm considering it necessary to have high-quality IT to compete effectively, the owner also wanted to use the staff more effectively and saw IT as an enabling technology that could make the firm more flexible and profitable. The results indicate that application growth tended to take place in firms where the owner was enthusiastic toward the technology. This agrees with DeLone's (1988) findings. Inadequate financial resources, inadequate managerial time, and lack of internal expertise discouraged growth. Each of these findings provided clues as to the types of consequences, social dynamics, and obstacles small firms face in the adoption of IT—factors that are part of the general theory proposed below.

In summarizing this section regarding small businesses and IT, only the Cragg and King study was based on an existing model or framework. Also, each of the studies tended to highlight different factors in a small firm's internal or external environment that are related to the use or adoption of IT. That is, the existing research is fragmented in terms of the findings and the conceptual approaches. Moreover, most previous research has focused on particular business sectors. In response, our study includes a heterogeneous sample of many types of small businesses. Furthermore, our study helps to reduce some of the uncertainty concerning the evolution of computing in small firms addressed by Cragg and King (1993) by providing a better understanding of the decision making process involved in adopting an IT. That improved understanding is provided by a general theory of behavioral decision making, the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). Following a discussion that examines IT innovations and presents some parallels to the TPB, we address the basic components of the theory.

## 2.2. IT Adoption and Diffusion of Innovations

Considerable research based on classical diffusion of innovation theory has contributed concepts and a large body of empirical research relevant to the study of technology evaluation, adoption, and implementation (Fichman 1992). One of the most comprehensive treatments of this subject area was conducted by Rogers (1983). His review of over 3,000 studies of adoption and diffusion of innovations revealed five general attributes of innovations that regularly influence adoption. He defined them as:

\- Relative Advantage: how much better an innovation is perceived to be compared to its predecessor,

\- Compatibility: how consistent an innovation is perceived to be with regard to existing values, past experiences, and the needs of the targeted adopters,

\- Complexity: how difficult an innovation is perceived to be to learn and use,

\- Observability: how visible the results of an innovation are to others, and

\- Trialability: how much an innovation may be experimented with prior to adoption.

Tornatzky and Klein (1982) reviewed 105 articles in this area and added five other characteristics to the list: cost, communicability, divisibility, profitability, and social approval.

More recently, Moore and Benbasat (1991) were concerned that instrumentation based on Roger's five characteristics lacked validity and reliability. They also felt that no instrument was available to measure the variety of perceptions of innovations that existed. Consequently, based predominately on the work by Rogers (1983) and Tornatzky and Klein (1982), Moore and Benbasat developed a comprehensive instrument designed to examine the decision to adopt an IT innovation. They also added two other constructs: image, defined as "the degree to which use of an innovation is perceived to enhance one's image or status in one's social system" (Moore and Benbasat 1991, p. 195), and voluntariness of use, "the degree to which use of the innovation is perceived as being voluntary, or of free will" (Moore and Benbasat 1991, p. 195).

The work by Moore and Benbasat (1991) provides researchers interested in studying IT innovations with an overall instrument to measure various perceptions. It is based on sound theory, data, and psychometric principles. Yet, we felt that their instrument was not appropriate for our study for several reasons. First, their research focuses on the diffusion of an innovation throughout an organization and its adoption by single users. Our research concentrates on the initial adoption decision by organizations. Second, all ITs are not necessarily innovative, and to equate adoption of any IT with adoption of an innovative IT would be circumspect. Third, although diffusion of IT innovations and initial IT adoption have some substantive overlap, they are not the same. The characteristics listed by Moore and Benbasat (1991) apply to perceptions of adoptions of particular kinds of IT. Thus, they are special cases of adoption behavior subsumed under the general TPB. Last, the TPB is a general theory that is grounded in subject perceptions. It is very flexible. The unique insight and features of adoption decisions identified in our elicitation study (Phase 1: discussed in §3.2) might have gone unnoticed had we based our research solely on the instrumentation developed by Moore and Benbasat (1991).

## 2.3. Theory of Planned Behavior

2.3.1. Introduction. The TPB is a later and more general form of the TRA that includes all of the former theory's constructs plus those unique to the TPB (see Figure 1). Therefore, for ease of reading and presentation, this paper refers primarily to the TPB. Readers interested in TRA-based research are referred to work by Sheppard et al. (1988), Davis et al. (1989), Yeaman (1988), Trower and Willis (1993), and Taylor and Todd (1995). All elements and relations in the TRA and the TPB are shown in Figure 1.

The TPB has been used in a variety of situations. Schifter (1984) used it to successfully predict weight loss behavior. Ajzen and Madden (1986) used the TPB to predict college students' intentions to attend class and earn a good grade. Mathieson (1991) predicted a person's intention to use a specific information system. Kreuger and Carsrud (1993) provided support for the use of the TPB in studying the emergence of new businesses (entrepreneurship). Taylor and Todd (1995) used the TPB to predict student usage of a computer resource center. The validity of this theory in such a broad set of situations suggests that it might have some validity in the matter of small business executives deciding about adopting an IT (to help a firm compete).

2.3.2. Behavior. Ajzen (1991) developed the TPB to explain how specific groups of cognitions are determinants of volitional behavior or goal attainment in virtually any setting. In most situations, the theory has been used to predict who pursues a particular course of action. "Adopting an IT (to gain a competitive advantage)," the behavior of interest in our research, is one example of a course of action.

2.3.3. Intention. In the TPB, a person's behavior (B), such as an executive adopting an IT to gain a competitive advantage, is a positive function of the intention ( $I_{B}$ ) to perform it—as long as the behavior is under a person's volitional control (actual control is also an input to behavior). Intention is defined as the strength of conscious plans to perform the target behavior. Intention should be the best predictor of behavior if nothing in the environment has occurred to cause a change in plans. It is the channel through which all cognitive and evaluative processes have an impact on behavior. In the present context, it represents the strength or potency of the decision to adopt an IT for one's small business.

Ajzen (1991) views such a decision (intention) as a function of three variables: (1) the attitude a person has toward performing the behavior ( $A_B$ : positive or negative evaluation of $B$ ), (2) a person's subjective norm regarding the behavior ( $SN_B$ : perceived social pressure to perform or not perform $B$ ), and (3) the perception that one has the ability and resources to perform the behavior ( $PBC_B$ : perceived control over $B$ ). Weights are assigned to the $A_B$ , $SN_B$ , and $PBC_B$ determinants, which can vary across individuals and situations. Equation (1)

depicts the relationship among behavior, intention, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control, where $\alpha$ means “is directly proportional to” (intention, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control do not have a fixed origin or fixed unit of measure; therefore, the equation contains proportions rather than equal signs).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Behavior } \propto \text { Intention } _ {B} \propto w _ {1} (\text { Attitude } _ {B}) \\ \quad + w _ {2} (\text { Subjective   Norm } _ {B}) \\ \quad + w _ {3} (\text { Perceived   Behavioral   Control } _ {B}) \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

2.3.4. Attitude. A person's attitude toward a particular behavior, such as "using IT to gain a competitive advantage," is a product of the strength of beliefs $(bb_{i})$ that the behavior will lead to each of $i = 1,\dots,I$ salient consequences, multiplied by corresponding evaluations $(ev_{i})$ of those consequences. A salient consequence of adopting a new technology might be "improved communication." Cragg and King (1993) addressed "relative advantage" over a previous IT, which could be tied to a set of salient consequences, such as "savings in time and effort," "economic benefits" and "decreases in discomfort." Another way to describe these concepts is to think of the $bb_{i}$ as subjective probabilities and the $ev_{i}$ as valences or anticipated utilities of the salient consequences. Equation (2) summarizes this relationship (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975):

$$
\text { attitude } _ {B} \propto \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {I} \text { behavioral   belief } _ {i} * \text { evaluation } _ {i}.\tag{2}
$$

In general, someone who believes that doing a given behavior will, with high probability, lead to mostly positive outcomes, will have a favorable attitude toward it. If negative outcomes are expected from performing the behavior, a person will hold a negative attitude toward it. Fishbein and Ajzen (1975), Ajzen and Madden (1986), and Ajzen (1991) have stated that correlations between this sum of cross-products and attitude are generally in the range of 0.40 to 0.80.

2.3.5. Subjective Norm. The second factor influencing intention is subjective norm $(SN_{B})$ . It is determined by a person's normative beliefs $(nb_{j})$ that j = 1, ..., J salient individuals or groups (referents) think the behavior of interest should or should not be performed, multiplied by a person's motivation to comply $(mc_{j})$ with each of the referents. The cross-products are then summed over all the salient referents. This relationship is shown in Equation (3):

subjective norm\$\_B\$

$$
\propto \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \text { normative   belief } _ {j} * \text { motivation   to   comply } _ {j}.\tag{3}
$$

According to the theory, this sum of $nb_{j}*mc_{j}$ cross-products should correlate strongly with a subjective norm. Ajzen and Madden (1986) and Ajzen (1991) have stated that correlations between this sum of cross products and subjective norm are also generally in the range of 0.40 to 0.80. In the context of small business executives making decisions about adopting a new IT, Cragg and King (1993) identified “managerial enthusiasm,” and DeLone (1988) noted that “executive commitment” are motivators for small-firm computing. Both of these concepts could be represented as strong normative beliefs from a CEO, board of directors, or top management if the individual making the adoption decision was an IS executive. Treadgold (1990) identified the effect of another potentially important social referent: distribution channel agents. Their social influence or that of employees and/or customers might also be represented as normative beliefs.

2.3.6. Perceived Behavioral Control. One of the constructs in the TPB not shared with its predecessor (the TRA) is actual control (AC). Actual control refers to the amount of requisite opportunities and resources (time, money, skills, cooperation of others) someone possesses to be able to carry out the course of action in question. Intention and actual control jointly determine whether or not the behavior is performed. For example, a small business executive may decide that the purchase of notebook computers for field salespeople is an important competitive use of IT. Yet, if there was a serious (unforeseen) budget shortfall, that IT would not actually be adopted—or at least it would be postponed until those resources were available. Unfortunately, actual control is very difficult to operationalize in a standardized way, and it has rarely been examined in tests of the TPB (Ajzen 1991). As our study deals with adoption decisions (intentions), we turn to a discussion of perceived behavioral control.

Perceived behavioral control (PBC), on the other hand, refers to someone's perception of the ease or difficulty of performing the behavior of interest. It is assumed to reflect past experience as well as anticipated impediments and obstacles (Ajzen 1988). PBC is conceptually distinct from actual control. Although actual control should serve as an input to perceptions, perceived control is not necessarily an accurate reflection of it (Ajzen and Madden 1986). This view of PBC is most compatible with Bandura's (1977, 1982, 1991) concept of task-specific self-efficacy which "is concerned with judgments of how well one can execute courses of action required to deal with prospective situations," (Bandura 1982, p. 122.) Research by Bandura and others has indicated that a person's behavior is strongly influenced by this confidence in one's ability to perform it.

Beliefs about resources, opportunities, and potential barriers to action underlie PBC. These “control beliefs” ( $cb_{k}$ ) could be based in part on a person’s previous experience with the particular behavior, but they will often be influenced by second-hand information, by experiences of friends and acquaintances, and by other factors that increase or decrease the perceived difficulty of performing that particular behavior (Ajzen 1988). Each control belief is multiplied by the perceived power ( $pp_{k}$ ) of the particular control factor to facilitate or obstruct the performance of the behavior. This relationship is illustrated in Equation (4):

$$
\text { perceived   behavioral   control }
$$

$$
\propto \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \text { control   belief } _ {k} * \text { perceived   power } _ {k}.\tag{4}
$$

Cragg and King (1993) identified consultant support as one of the motivators for small-firm computing. As a facilitator, consultant support could be integral to perceived behavioral control. They also identified insufficient managerial time, inadequate financial resources, and lack of internal expertise as inhibitors of small-firm computing. These might also be seen by executives as perceived barriers to control over IT adoption.

## 2.4. Summary

We have noted that there is limited research dealing with small business. Most of it is descriptive in nature, and none was found that examines the decision to adopt IT. A large body of research has investigated IT innovations and their diffusion to users throughout the organization; however, that work only indirectly applies to the focus of our research—IT adoption decisions at the organizational level. The TPB is a framework that is potentially quite useful in this area. We describe our application of the theory throughout the rest of the paper, focusing on specific hypotheses in the next section and then on methodology and results.

## 2.5. Hypotheses

2.5.1. Direct Determinants of IT Adoption Decisions (Intentions). The model presented in Figure 1 and in the text of our preceding discussion lays out the components and relations of the TPB. Below, we describe each of the hypotheses in the theory that we tested in our investigation. All the general hypotheses above are stated in terms of the specific context of explaining an executive's strategic decision to adopt an IT (i.e., to help one's business compete). Except for Hypothesis 1e, the formal statements below correspond to the equations given in the sections above.

HYPOTHESES 1a–1c. In small businesses, decisions (intentions) to adopt an IT are a positive linear function of each of the following determinants: (a) attitude toward adopting that IT, (b) subjective norm regarding adoption of that IT, and (c) perceived control over adopting that IT.

HYPOTHESIS 1d. No other variable has a direct impact on the decision (intention) to adopt an IT. Attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control are sufficient for explaining adoption decisions.

The TPB is explicit about the constructs that do or do not have a direct impact on intentions. However, the theory is flexible enough to incorporate other variables that might moderate the proposed relationships in the model. The central set of relationships involve the effect of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control on IT adoption decisions (intentions). As many studies have shown, these effects can be weaker or stronger depending on a variety of situational or individual characteristics (Ajzen 1988).

2.5.2. Moderators of Direct Effects. One moderating characteristic might be firm size. In fact, the notion that strategic decisions about adopting and using an IT are different for large versus small firms is one of the underlying premises and sources of debate in this research domain (e.g., Delone 1988; for an opposing view, see Premkumar and King 1994). A recent meta-analysis shows that larger firms tend to adopt more technological innovations (Damanpour 1992). According to the present theory, however, this would likely not be a direct effect of size, but an interactive one, perhaps through the strengthening of particular inputs to adoption decisions. Indeed, a wide-ranging review of the literature supports this moderating perspective, suggesting that size operates as a contingent factor in many domains of strategic decision making (Bluedorn 1993).

In one relevant domain, research in strategic management suggests that larger firms have greater numbers and varieties of internal and external stakeholders to consider when making strategic decisions (Drucker 1989, Pearce and David 1987). Executives at larger firms also tend to do more “scanning” of their social environment when making strategic decisions or formal plans (Bluedorn 1993, Bluedorn et al. 1994, Diffenbach 1983, Mohan-Neill 1995, Thomas et al. 1993). In addition, Lindsay and Rue (1980) suggest that when under competitive pressures, smaller firms tend to rely more than larger firms on their own judgment than on social information. For strategic adoption of an IT, these findings suggest a stronger impact of subjective norm.

Larger firms tend to have greater financial and intellectual resources at their disposal (Ein-Dor and Segev 1982). Moreover, smaller firms are less likely to be found in munificent or resource-rich environments (Keats and Hitt 1988). It is also feasible to suggest that for smaller firms (with fewer resources) the “threat bias” documented in how executives approach strategic decisions will be stronger; that is, in larger firms (with greater resources) executives will be more likely to view such decisions as “opportunities” (Jackson and Dutton 1988). Each of these conceptions suggests a weakened impact of perceived control on adoption decisions in larger firms.

Finally, size and resources are associated with greater "slack" (Bourgeois 1981). This allows for more risk-taking and experimentation (Rhyne 1985). It is also consistent with findings that larger firms tend to initiate and fully implement larger numbers of innovations (Kimberly and Evanisko 1981). Consequently, consideration of the immediate costs and benefits that are part of attitude might become less important in decision making.

HYPOTHESIS 1e. Firm size will moderate the effects of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control on executive decisions to adopt ITs. Specifically, the effect of subjective norms on such decisions will be stronger in larger firms, and the effect of attitude and perceived control will be weaker.

2.5.3. Components of the Determinants of IT Adoption Decisions. The preceding hypotheses all dealt with direct or moderating determinants of strategic decisions. The following hypotheses break the direct determinants into their constituent elements. Once again, the verbal statements are parallel to the equations given in §2.3 of this paper.

HYPOTHESIS 2. Attitude toward the adoption of a new IT is an additive function of beliefs about the likelihood of anticipated consequences, with each belief multiplied (weighted) by a judgment of the value of that consequence.

HYPOTHESIS 3. Subjective norm regarding the adoption of a new IT is an additive function of beliefs about expectations of salient social referents, with each belief multiplied (weighted) by a judgment about the firm's motivation to comply with that referent.

HYPOTHESIS 4. Perceived control over the adoption of a new IT is an additive function of beliefs about the likelihood of (firm-specific) anticipated obstacles or barriers to adoption, with each belief moderated by a judgment about the adequacy of the firm's resources for overcoming those obstacles.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Identification and Selection of Sample

We obtained access to a commercial database containing over 1,500 firms located in a large (more than 2.5 million people) southwestern metropolitan area. Major industries in the area include finance, aviation, defense, oil and gas, agriculture, heavy and light manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, and nonprofit. The random sample of firms that eventually participated in all phases of the study included firms representing a broad cross-section of manufacturing, service, and non-profit sectors (e.g., accounting, advertising, computer-related, engineering, healthcare). A subset of the firms in the database were considered small businesses, which was our target population. "Small" was based on number of employees, which has been used as a criterion in prior research dealing with small businesses and IT (DeLone 1988, Kagan et al. 1990, Cragg and King 1993). Other categorizations such as those involving revenue, total capital, and so on are often proprietary and rarely obtainable for smaller, privately held companies (Montazemi 1988).

The number of firms in the database was reduced such that only those employing between 25 and 200 persons remained. Based on preliminary discussions with several small businesses, firms with fewer than 25 employees would likely not have developed crystallized strategies regarding IT adoption decisions. The maximum number of employees was restricted to no more than 200, so we could make reasonable comparisons to the few prior studies involving IT and small businesses. In addition, firms that were identified as branch offices were excluded from consideration as decisions about their ITs are often made at home office locations.

## 3.2. Phase I: TPB-Based Elicitation Study

Previous research by Mykytyn and Harrison (1993) outlined steps necessary to develop an appropriate TRA-based instrument for assessing and predicting a specific course of action. Although their research did not deal with the TPB, the same procedures applicable to the TRA pertain to the development of TPB-based instrumentation (Ajzen 1991).

First, the particular population of interest must be identified. From this, a small sample is selected for an elicitation study to identify and determine the specific elements of the questionnaire. In this way, the specific consequences, social referents, and facilitating or barrier factors in the decision about adopting an IT are "grounded" in the population of small business executives. Also, the terms and phrases used by such executives are then used to craft more meaningful survey questions. Several of the requirements for instrument development that Mykytyn and Harrison (1993) noted in their research include: (1) clearly defining the course of action in question, (2) eliciting perceived consequences of it, the social referents associated with it, and the possible obstacles or barriers to it from an appropriate subsample of the population, (3) choosing the most often cited (most salient) consequences, referents, and obstacles from the elicitation, (4) creating measures of behavioral beliefs, evaluations, normative beliefs, motivations to comply, control beliefs, and perceived power based on the most often-cited cognitions, and (5) creating measures of intention, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control, all from the earlier definition of the behavior of interest. A larger sample is then selected to administer the final version of the questionnaire.

In his development of the TPB, Ajzen (1991) indicates that the best way to identify the salient consequences, referents, and resources/obstacles is to conduct elicitation interviews or surveys involving small (20–50 person) samples of respondents who are representative of the population (see also Ajzen and Fishbein 1980). For this study, a random sample of 50 small businesses was selected to lend greater validity and reliability to the responses regarding items (2) through (5) above. These 50 firms were chosen from the same database that was later used to select firms for the administration of the final instrument. One hundred seventy-six firms were contacted by telephone to obtain the 50-firm target. The 50 contacts were senior executives in their firms, and they were either the sole decision maker or an integral part of the decision making process regarding IT adoption.

The questionnaire to be used during the elicitation process was tested with four senior executives of small businesses. This pilot testing led to changes in the wording of some questions. Another pilot test was conducted to review the revised instrument using the same executives as well as two new ones. All agreed that the wording was clear, requiring no other changes.

The most important question asked of these executives during the elicitation survey was "What system or use of computerized information would you like to have to help you compete better?" Recall from §1 (Introduction) that it was important to focus executives' attention on IT adoptions that would be consequential to their firm; thus, the elicitation study and all subsequent phases dealing with the development of TPB-based instrumentation were centered on ITs that executives thought would help their firm compete better. Identification of the specific IT each executive thought of was also central to Ajzen's (1991) conceptualization of a specific behavioral "target" in the TPB. Each executive's response to this question was then used to identify advantages and disadvantages (salient consequences) of adopting that IT, individuals or groups who would approve or disapprove (salient referents) of adopting that IT, and potential obstacles pertaining to (salient resources) adopting that IT. In turn, these responses were used to construct all TPB-based items used in the final questionnaire during Phase III. Additional information and an in-depth discussion concerning these 50 firms, the executives spoken with, firm size, and so on are discussed by Riemenschneider et al. (1995).

## 3.3. Phase II

The second phase of this study consisted of randomly selecting remaining firms from the database cited above, for purposes of contacting a senior executive from each firm by telephone. The database contained firm name, address and phone number, firm size, and the name of at least one senior executive. Criteria for being included in this phase of the study were the same as for the elicitation survey: employee size between 25 and 200, involvement by a senior executive who actually participated in the decision process regarding the potential adoption of IT, and a willingness to speak with a research team member on the telephone. Likewise, firms that were branch offices or firms where it was not possible to reach a senior decision maker were excluded.

The primary purpose of Phase II was to obtain a response to the question: "What computer-based system or use of computerized information would you LIKE to have, to help you compete better (that is, something you don't have already)?" The response obtained from each participant allowed us to customize each questionnaire mailed during Phase III of the study. If executives initially mentioned more than one IT, we asked him or her to specify the one that would best help them compete. If no preference was stated, we used the first one mentioned, which happened in less than $5\%$ of the firms. The specific ITs varied considerably. Some dealt with software relevant only to the respondent's business (e.g., CD-ROM databases of potential customers); others dealt with new types of hardware (e.g., imaging capabilities).

Three hundred nine firms were contacted during a 30-day period. A total of 276 firms of the 309 contacted were eligible to continue with Phase III of the study. Eligibility was based on each firm's size being between 25 and 200 employees, the executive stating that he or she was the major person or an important person in the IT adoption process, and the executive's willingness to complete a questionnaire to be mailed during Phase III.

## 3.4. Phase III

Phase III consisted of mailing the final instrument to the same individuals spoken with during Phase II. Each instrument was personalized according to the specific IT the executives told us (in Phase II) that they thought would help their firm compete. Two hundred seventy-six questionnaires were mailed out, accompanied by a cover letter and a postage-paid return envelope. Participants were told that their responses would remain confidential. They were told to include a business card with the questionnaire when it was mailed back if they wished to receive an executive summary of the study's results. The business card was necessary because questionnaires did not request the respondent to identify himself/herself, or the name and address of the firm. As a result of these efforts, 98 questionnaires were returned over a six week period.

When it became clear that no additional responses would be received, we spent several days telephoning each firm's contact to ascertain whether he or she had received the questionnaire and whether it had been returned. Since responses were anonymous, the only firms not contacted were those who had included a business card with the questionnaire when it was mailed back. An additional 64 responses were received after making the phone calls, producing a final response rate of 59% based on the 276 firms that agreed to participate. Based on the 309 firms that were originally contacted in Phase II, the response rate was also quite good—52.4%.

## 3.5. Instrument for Phase III

The importance of reliable and valid instrumentation in MIS research is critical. Moore and Benbasat (1991) pointed out that inadequate measurement of constructs has plagued IS research on many topics. Fortunately, during the last several years, methodological and psychometric issues have received greater attention, due in large part to research such as that by Bailey and Pearson (1983), Ives et al. (1983), Doll and Torkzadeh (1988, 1991), and Straub (1989).

Based on Ajzen and Fishbein (1980), Fishbein and Ajzen (1975), and Ajzen (1991), the final instrument for our study dealt with the following components: intention, attitude, subjective norm, perceived control, and the beliefs underlying those attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived controls. Each of these components was reviewed earlier. Ajzen and Fishbein (1980) also stress that the various elements of the target course of action must be clearly understood by survey participants: the firm's potential adoption of a particular IT (to help the firm compete better). The questionnaire items are contained in Appendix A.

Reliability estimates (coefficient $\alpha$ ) for measures of intention, attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control were 0.95, 0.91, 0.71, and 0.80, respectively. As intention is future-oriented, the reliability of the intention measure was estimated using data from only those who had not yet adopted their specified IT. According to criteria by Brown (1976), these coefficients possess high reliability for this type of research. Internal consistency estimates for sets of behavioral beliefs, normative beliefs, and control beliefs were not calculated. Fishbein and Ajzen (1975) and Ajzen and Fishbein (1980) strongly rule against calculating such assessments for these items because it is reasonable to assume that people will possess contrasting (positive and negative) beliefs about the particular attributes of any reasonably complex course of action. In a sense, then, internal consistency estimates such as coefficient alpha for $bb_{i}$ , $nb_{j}$ , and $cb_{k}$ would not be meaningful because such estimates assume a homogeneous unidimensional construct.

One important aspect of instrument validity is content validity, the degree to which instrument items represent the domain (universe) of the concept under study (Cronbach 1971). In this regard, those who participated in the first phase of the study (the elicitation survey) were senior executives representing a variety of industries, interested in a variety of ITs. Their responses pertaining to advantages, disadvantages, obstacles, referent groups, and so on were examined carefully and pilot-tested three times. Those responses also led to the development of questionnaire items dealing with underlying beliefs of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control. This process is consistent with the “groundedness” principles in the TPB and also with the suggestions provided by Cronbach (1971) who advocated a review by content experts.

Construct validity is also a prerequisite for instrument validity. According to Cronbach (1971), construct validity concerns whether the measures chosen are true constructs describing the event, or merely artifacts of the methodology itself. One way to assess construct validity is to establish the instrument's factorial validity. Estimating factorial validity is also a way of ensuring that theoretical constructs are distinct, or contain divergent validity with respect to each other. This procedure has often been used in IS research, most notably in the user satisfaction area.

For our research, an iterated principal axes analysis with a promax rotation (Loehlin 1992) was employed to examine the factor structure of underlying behavioral (bb), normative (nb), and control beliefs (cb). An oblique rotation was used because it was likely that the factors would be correlated (Ajzen 1991); estimated correlations among the belief factors ranged from r = 0.03 to 0.37, with a median of r = 0.13. Using the parallel analysis criterion (Longman et al. 1989), four factors emerged. One pertained to the control beliefs only and a second pertained to the normative beliefs only, lending support to the construct validity of this portion of the instrument and to its theoretical underpinnings in the TPB. The third and fourth factors contained items relating only to the behavioral beliefs: one pertained to the positive and the other to the negative consequences of adoption. Table 1 contains the factor loadings for these four factors, in the full sample of executives, and in the sample of firms that had not yet adopted their desired IT by the time the survey was completed (see §4.1, Overview of Analysis). These factor analytic results demonstrate that executives did indeed distinguish behavioral beliefs, normative beliefs, and control beliefs in their own perceptions, supporting their existence as unique constructs.

A corresponding factor analysis was conducted for the measures of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control. Three factors were detected for this part of the analysis, one for each of the proposed constructs. This analysis lends further support to the construct validity of the instrument based on the TPB. Table 2 contains the factor loadings for these items in the original and “non-yet adopted” samples. Correlations among these factors ranged from r = 0.22 to 0.56, with a median of r = 0.36.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Overview of Analyses

Each set of hypotheses in §2.5 was tested using variations of well-known and robust multiple linear regression techniques (Darlington 1994). Although Figure 1 may suggest application of structural equations techniques such as LISREL, lack of multivariate normality, single indicator scales (but not single items) for each construct, and the predicted presence of moderator effects make multiple regression more appropriate. In several cases, the regression analyses involved hierarchical procedures in which hypothesized or potentially important effects were added sequentially to an existing model. In each case, residual diagnostics were checked carefully for assumption violations and lack of fit. Firm size, which was used in tests of Hypotheses 1d and 1e, was strongly skewed to the right (most firms were in the range of 25–100 employees, with a few firms reaching the upper limit on our size criterion). Therefore, it was log-transformed to reduce the inflated leverage of firms at the upper end of our size distribution.

Of the original 162 firms who returned questionnaires, a subset could not be used for testing our hypotheses. On a set of background questions, six respondents indicated they had no input or influence (0%) on their business's strategic decisions about adopting a new IT. They were excluded from subsequent analyses because we felt their answers would be unlikely to give an accurate portrayal of the firm's decision process.

Another 59 small businesses reported that by the time they received a questionnaire (Phase III), they had already adopted the IT that they earlier wished for (in Phase II). Intention is a future-oriented construct. For the firms who already adopted, intention to adopt the desired IT therefore held little meaning. These 59 firms were not included in explicit tests of the hypotheses. The remaining 97 firms (162 at the start, minus 6 not involved in the decision process, minus 59 who had already adopted) were included in each of the hypothesis tests discussed below.

## 4.2. Hypotheses 1a–1e: Determinants of Strategic Intentions to Adopt a New IT

Hypotheses 1a–1c predict that an executive's strategic decision (intention: $INT_{adopt IT}$ ) to adopt an IT to gain a competitive advantage will be a function of attitude ( $A_{adopt IT}$ ), subjective norm ( $SN_{adopt IT}$ ), and perceived control ( $PBC_{adopt IT}$ ) regarding adoption. As Table 5 shows, this part of the theory was strongly supported. Each proposed determinant explained a significant, unique portion of the variance in adoption intentions (all at p < 0.05). The significant weight for perceived control is especially important. It shows that the more general TPB, which is different from the earlier TRA in that it accounts for the effect of perceived obstacles on intentions (PBC), provides a better fit to IT adoption decisions in small businesses.

Hypothesis 1d proposes that attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control are sufficient for explaining strategic intentions to adopt a new IT. That is, according to the theory, the direct effect of any variable on adoption decisions will be mediated by the three hypothesized determinants. To test this mediating effect, other variables were added to the regression equation, individually and in sets, after attitude ( $A_{adopt IT}$ ), subjective norm ( $SN_{adopt IT}$ ), and perceived control ( $PBC_{adopt IT}$ ) were already in the model (James and Brett 1984). In the first mediation test, we entered the theoretical antecedents of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control: $\Sigma bb_{i}*ev_{i}, \Sigma nb_{j}*mc_{j}$ , and $\Sigma cb_{k}*pp_{k}$ , respectively. Regression results in Table 5 show that none of these individual variables explained any unique variance in intentions. As a set, these variables also did not improve the predictiveness of the model ( $\Delta R^{2} = 0.03; F_{3,87} = 1.02, p = 0.39$ ). Next, we entered several variables “outside” the theory that might have had an effect on adoption decisions, or that have been studied in past research (see §2.1 above). These other variables included (the log of) firm size, the firm’s age, the respondent’s tenure with the firm, and the extent to which adoption decisions typically depended on the respondent’s opinion (expressed as a %). None of these characteristics of the firm or the respondent explained unique variance in adoption intentions, either individually (see Table 5) or as a set ( $\Delta R^{2} = 0.02, F_{4,87} = 0.73, p = 0.58$ ). Therefore, Hypothesis 1d was strongly supported. No variable measured in this study helped to explain strategic decisions to adopt an IT beyond the three hypothesized determinants.

The final proposal about what determines IT adoption decisions is Hypothesis 1e. In it, we predicted that firm size would moderate the relationships of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control with intention. Specifically, we proposed that the IT adoption decisions of larger firms would depend more on social expectations, and less on perceived control and attitudinal considerations. To test this hypothesis, we created regression terms that were linear-by-linear interactions of log-size with attitude (log size\* $A_{adopt\ IT}$ ), with subjective norm (log size\* $SN_{adopt\ IT}$ ), and perceived behavioral control (log size\* $PBC_{adopt\ IT}$ ), and added them to a regression model that already contained the simple main effects of those terms (Darlington 1994). The results generally support our prediction, and are shown in the bottom of Table 5. There was a significant “enhancing” or positive effect of size on the Subjective Norm to Intention relation, and a “neutralizing” or negative effect of size on the Attitude to Intention and Perceived Control to Intention relations. Although the coefficient for the latter term was only marginally significant (p = 0.08), we still consider it important. Many authors have documented the low power of detecting true moderating effects in correlational data (McClelland and Judd (1993) note that field studies require as many as 10–20 times more observations than randomized experiments for detecting interactive influences that have the same effect size). The improvement in prediction for these moderating variables as a set was also significant ( $\Delta R^{2} = 0.10$ , $F_{3,89} = 4.46$ , p < 0.01).

## 4.3. Hypotheses 2–4: Determinants of Attitude, Subjective Norm, and Perceived Control

Relations of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control with the summed cross-products of their proposed antecedents were statistically significant and in the proposed direction. The correlation of attitude with $\Sigma bb_{i}*ev_{i}$ was $r_{95}=0.53\ (p<0.01)$ ; the correlation of subjective norm with $\Sigma nb_{j}*mc_{j}$ was $r_{95}=0.73\ (p<0.01)$ ; the correlation of perceived control with $\Sigma cb_{k}*pp_{k}$ was $r_{95}=0.34\ (p<0.01)$ . These correlations are similar in magnitude to previous studies of the TRA and TPB (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980, Ajzen and Madden 1986, Ajzen 1991).

Simple correlations, however, do not provide unequivocal evidence that these constructs arise from multiplicative composites. The relations of multiplicative composites to any variable are dependent on the scale intervals and origins of the terms that make up the composite. Both Evans (1991) and Ajzen (1991) outline procedures for testing the predictive contribution of the sum of multiplicative terms to a dependent variable, unique from their additive contributions. We performed such hierarchical regression analyses and report the results below.

4.3.1. Attitude. The multiplicative term $\Sigma bb_{t}*ev_{t}$ explained unique variance in $A_{adopt IT}$ beyond the additive effects of $bb_{t}$ and $ev_{t}$ terms ( $\Delta R^{2}=0.10, F_{1,93}=6.15; p<0.05$ ). This regression equation produced an “optimally re-scaled” composite (Ajzen 1991, p. 193) that had a multiple correlation of R=0.55 (p<0.01) with attitude toward adopting a new IT. Hypothesis 2 was supported. This notion of optimal empirical rescaling arises from the problem mentioned earlier, namely that multiplicative composites are sensitive to the zero points of the scales they are built from. The regression equation provides an estimate of the correlation one would observe if the zero points were chosen in a way to maximize the correlation of the multiplicative composite with some external criterion (in this case, $A_{adopt IT}$ ).

4.3.2. Subjective Norm. The multiplicative term $\Sigma nb_{j}*mc_{j}$ was similarly added to a regression equation to see if it explained unique variance in $SN_{adopt IT}$ beyond the additive contributions of $nb_{j}$ and $mc_{j}$ terms. In this case, the cross-product term was not significant ( $\Delta R^{2}=0.004$ , $F_{1,93}=0.12$ , p=0.58) and Hypothesis 3 was not supported. Still, the resulting optimally rescaled composite had a strong multiple correlation with subjective norm (R=0.73, p<0.01). One possible methodological reason for the lack of support for Hypothesis 3 is the low reliability of the $SN_{adopt IT}$ measure, which reduces any observed correlation it can have with proposed antecedents. Another reason is the high redundancy of the sum of $nb_{j}$ and $\Sigma nb_{j}*mc_{j}$ ; their correlation was r=0.95. This redundancy, however, may be less of a methodological problem than a substantive one. Apparently, the motivation to comply ( $mc_{j}$ ) terms did little to change or modify the impact of the $nb_{j}$ terms when both were multiplied in the composite. It is possible that only the $nb_{j}$ are necessary in the theory in this context.

4.3.3. Perceived Control. Finally, the multiplicative term $\Sigma cb_{k}*pp_{k}$ was added to a regression equation to see if it explained unique variance in $PBC_{adopt IT}$ beyond the additive contributions of $cb_{k}$ and $pp_{k}$ terms. For perceived control, the cross-product term was significant ( $\Delta R^{2} = 0.07$ , $F_{1,93} = 11.63$ , p < 0.01). Hypothesis 4 was supported. The resulting optimally re-scaled composite had a substantial correlation with perceived control (R = 0.68, p < 0.01).

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Summary

We found strong support for the TPB as a description of the process by which small business executives decide to adopt an IT for competitive purposes. That decision is a function of attitude toward adoption (positive or negative anticipated consequences of adoption), subjective norm about adoption (perceived social expectations regarding adoption), and perceived control over adoption (the perceived obstacles or facilitators to adoption). We also examined the relationship between the predictive contribution of the sum of multiplicative terms as components for attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control, versus the simple additive contributions of those terms. In general, these findings are mathematically consistent with what the TPB predicts. The only variable in which we did not find this type of support was subjective norm. As we discussed, this may be a problem with the reliability of our $SN_{adopt\ IT}$ measure.

Nothing in our study excluded variables outside the TPB from potentially having a direct affect on the decision process used by small business executives to adopt an IT. Indeed, many variables we measured have been studied in previous IS research. These include executive position within the firm (Kirby and Turner 1993) and the firm's size (Palvia and Jackson 1990). Therefore, it is significant to note that our analyses indicated that most of these variables added nothing to our understanding of the decision process beyond the variables intrinsic to the TPB. A firm's size indirectly affected the process, by changing the strengths of theoretical inputs into the decision.

This finding has both theoretical and practical implications. On the one hand, it is appealing to researchers who may try to predict or explain some important IT-related behavior, such as adopting IT for competitive purposes. If an explanation is parsimonious, it is then easier to manage and understand. Likewise, business executives are interested in practical explanations, rather than those which are complex and hard to interpret. Thus, from each perspective, the TPB components of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control provide both completeness and simplicity.

## 5.2. Limitations

As with all empirical research, our study has limitations that place boundaries on its internal and external validity. First, the data were correlational. The patterns of covariation we observed are necessary, but not sufficient, conditions for inferring that attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control all have a causal influence on the strategic decision to adopt a new IT. Fortunately, many lab- and field-based experiments of the TRA and the TPB have been conducted that support the causal impact of these proposed components on decisions (Ajzen 1988). Still, few have been done in the context of adopting a new IT, and none have been done with small business executives as subjects. Field experiments in which these components are manipulated to have different levels across subsets of small businesses are both feasible and necessary for more confident causal statements. Mykytyn and Harrison (1993) recommend ways that $A_{\text{adopt IT}}$ might be manipulated; parallel manipulations could easily be developed for $SN_{\text{adopt IT}}$ and $PBC_{\text{adopt IT}}$ .

A second limitation is the fairly small sample that we were eventually able to use in testing our hypotheses (n = 98). This did limit our power to detect weak effect sizes, but provided reasonable power to detect moderate ones (1 - b = 0.79, Cohen 1978). Furthermore, the predicted effects were strong enough to be reliably observed, as our results show.

A third limitation is the response rate to the survey. We tested for differences between our final sample and the sampling frame on several firm-level characteristics (e.g., size, % of firms in broadly defined industries, age of firm). We found no systematic biases or significant lack of representativeness relative to the large database of small businesses that we started from. The heterogeneity of our final sample in terms of size (within the stated constraints of 25–200), industry, years in operation, and type of person responding, all mitigate this problem. The possibility remains, however, that other, unmeasured factors covaried with whether or not a firm took part in all the phases of our investigation. Replication in different geographic regions, in more rural businesses, or in firms with fewer employees (i.e., less than the 25-person minimum we used) would add greatly to the external validity of our findings.

Another limitation is the fact that all our data were obtained via self-reports from a single executive in each small business. The potential for inflated correlations due to common method variance is high. This has long been a criticism of tests of the TRA and the TPB in other contexts (Ajzen 1991). Misleadingly high correlations would be particularly serious if simple correlations between proposed independent and dependent variables were the only evidence offered in support of the model. In our study, however, we tested for and found unique contributions of individual independent variables to specific dependent variables in the TPB. Common method variance generates more shared variance among independent variables, making it less likely that any one of them would have made a unique contribution to the dependent variable. One example is the multiple regression tests we conducted for determinants of intention ( $INT_{adopt IT}$ ). If measuring intention and its determinants on the same survey had kindled a common method variance problem, it would not only have created greater empirical overlap of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived control with intention, it would also have created greater overlap (multicollinearity) among the three independent variables. This multicollinearity would have reduced the unique contribution of each variable to intention. Yet, we found each proposed antecedent added significant, incremental variance to the explanation of intention in the regression equation.

Collecting later criterion data (did the firm actually adopt within six months of their stated decision?) would also have lessened the common method variance problem. Unfortunately, because we promised firms that their responses to the Phase III survey would be anonymous, we did not add identifying information to allow a clear match of survey responses to later reports of whether or not the firm had adopted the specified IT.

And, only a small set of firms volunteered their identities in Phase III answers: approximately 20 executives enclosed business cards asking for a summary of our findings. Still, evidence for the predictive validity of intentions in a variety of domains is overwhelming (e.g., Shepard et al. 1988), limited only by the legality or morality of the course of action being studied (few people report to researchers that they have strong intentions to perform illegal or immoral acts). As the strategic adoption of a new IT has none of these problems, the intention or decision to adopt is likely to be an excellent predictor of later actual adoption.

## 5.3. Research Directions

In our research, firm size ranged from 25–200. It would be interesting and useful to see if further differences might be noted if we examined firm sizes of 500, 1,000, or the very large firms that have been the focus of most IS research. In part, this would involve extending the TPB to group contexts, and might involve an assessment of consensus among a group of decision makers; or, more interestingly, one might get multiple respondents from each firm and model the extent to which an averaging or differential influence model best predicted actual adoptions. At the same time, it would also be of interest to know whether the same underlying components of the theory, that is, advantages, disadvantages, resources, obstacles, and so on that we identified in the elicitation survey conducted in Phase I, apply to larger firms as well as to other types of IT-related decisions.

If these specific components (e.g., important social referents such as vendors, clients, top management, and so on) were generalizable across contexts, IS researchers would have a useful set of concepts to take to business executives to help them manage the decision making process regarding IT adoption. In essence, it would provide these executives with the variables that most firms consider about adopting a new IT.

Another perspective that should be investigated is the relationship between the decision to adopt IT for competitive advantage and the individual persons who would be its primary users. In essence, the adoption decision would then be examined at a more micro level, one that involves individual users or decision makers—this is a context in which the diffusion of technology is paramount. Therefore, it is a domain in which the TPB and the work of Rogers (1983) and Moore and Benbasat (1991) might profitably overlap. In today's environment, many applications that become strategically important can begin as standalone programs designed and used by end users. Eventually, through initiatives by management or even the users themselves, these applications are identified as being important to the success of the firm, not just to the individual user.

## 5.4. Conclusion

We have successfully applied the TPB to an important decision that executives in small firms make regarding IT, a decision that few researchers have studied. The results are compelling and consistent with the theory. We found that a small business executive's decision to adopt an IT to help his/her firm compete is a function of attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. Attitudes are a function of the positive and negative consequences executives see. Subjective norms flow from stakeholder positions inside and outside the firm regarding the IT adoption decision. Perceived control reflects anticipated resources and barriers to adopting the new IT, as well as executives' beliefs about overcoming them. These are empirically and conceptually distinct factors. They make independent and important contributions to the decision process.

We are confident that these results are applicable across the broad set of industries included in our sample, as opposed to a specific industry such as manufacturing or healthcare. The results also apply to the adoption of a broad set of ITs. Our study did not focus on a single technology, such as a LAN, piece of software application or operating environment; rather, the target IT varied across firms who responded. Therefore, we believe this study makes a strong first step toward providing theory and data describing the process by which small firms adopt ITs. $^{1}$
