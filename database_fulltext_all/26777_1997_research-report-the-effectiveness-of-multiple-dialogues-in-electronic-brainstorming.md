Members of brainstorming groups often pursue the same set of ideas rather than considering a wide and diverse range of ideas, which may reduce the number of ideas they produce. One way to reduce this cognitive inertia may be to encourage groups to engage in several simultaneous discussions or dialogues. This experiment, which studied groups brainstorming electronically, found that groups generated more ideas, more high-quality ideas, and more novel ideas when using multiple dialogues than when using single dialogues.

(Brainstorming; Idea Generation; Group Support Systems; Groupware; Cognitive Inertia)

## The Effectiveness of Multiple Dialogues in Electronic Brainstorming

One popular technique for group idea-generation is brainstorming. The original proposal for brainstorming suggested that groups using a set of brainstorming rules would produce more ideas than individuals working alone (Osborn 1953). An early test of brainstorming found that brainstorming groups generated fewer ideas than individuals working separately (Taylor et al. 1958), a finding that has been repeated in many subsequent studies (Mullen et al. 1991).

There are at least three major interpretations for the low performance of brainstorming groups (Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Taylor et al. 1958). The first two, that members fail to contribute all ideas for fear of criticism and that the need to take turns speaking blocks the contribution of ideas, have been well studied (Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973). Both also have been largely overcome by the use of electronic brainstorming, which enables members to exchange anonymous ideas by typing them into computers (Dennis and Valacich 1994, Gallupe et al. 1991, Gallupe et al. 1994, Valacich et al. 1994).

The third major interpretation, that people working in groups are more likely to pursue a narrow set of ideas at the expense of other ideas, has received little research attention. This phenomenon, termed cognitive inertia by Lamm and Trommsdorff (1973), is the subject of this study.

## Cognitive Inertia in Group Brainstorming

## Sources of Cognitive Inertia

Individuals brainstorming alone tend to approach problems differently and generate a diverse set of ideas. However, as individuals brainstorm in groups, group interaction constrains idea diversity (Taylor et al. 1958) so that “later contributions tend to more closely resemble earlier ones” (Jablin and Seibold 1978, p. 340). Groups tend to “persist in working along the line of the first idea regardless of how fruitless it may be” (Maier 1970, p. 355). This focus on a few narrow topics or themes, called cognitive inertia, inhibits a group’s ability to generate a large number of ideas. Rather than devoting time and energy to many topics, the group pursues a narrow set of topics and misses large portions of the overall problem solution space that contain many useful ideas. Cognitive inertia is influenced by three primary theoretical sources: cognitive, social, and procedural (Camacho and Paulus 1995, Nagasundaram and Dennis 1993).

Cognitive Factors. Cognitive behavior is controlled by production rules—rules specifying the steps of cognition—that, when activated, produce ideas (Anderson 1983, 1987). Rules are activated automatically by input stimuli without conscious control (Anderson 1992). Stimuli activate several different rules, each having a certain strength based on past experiences. Thus, the ideas generated by an individual depend upon the stimuli and the relative strengths of the individual's production rules. Individuals tend to recall items that are linked together (Meyer 1970). As activation spreads through memory, rules related to the stimuli and to each other have the greatest strength and are most likely to be activated. The use of closely related rules leads to the production of closely related ideas.

Social Factors. Social factors are those that arise from the presence of other group members (Camacho and Paulus 1995). Two social factors are potentially important sources of cognitive inertia. First, as group members interact, they adopt behavioral norms or structures that can constrain behavior (Giddens 1984). One structure typically found in group interaction is that of "not changing the subject" (Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973, p. 382). Thus, members are motivated to focus on the same general topics or themes, resulting in little exploration of other topics and themes. Individuals may even intentionally withhold ideas that are not related to the dominant theme (Maier 1970).

A second social factor is social comparison. Members of brainstorming groups tend to compare their performance to others in their group and to match the rate at which they generate ideas to the rate at which they perceive others to generate ideas (Camacho and Paulus 1995, Paulus and Dzindolet 1993). However, members systematically overestimate their contributions and underestimate the contributions of others (Paulus et al. 1993). This results in a downward matching spiral: members underestimate the contributions of others and reduce their rate of contributions, which causes others to perceive a reduction in contributions and to reduce their contributions and so on in an ongoing process (Camacho and Paulus 1995, Paulus and Dzindolet 1993).

Procedural Factors. A final set of factors influencing cognitive inertia is the intentional use of procedures that promote cognitive inertia. For example, groups following the piggybacking rule suggested by Osborn (1953)—building on the ideas of others—are more likely to produce a narrow set of similar ideas at the expense of a more diverse set of ideas.

## Single Versus Multiple Dialogues

The essence of group interaction during brainstorming is the exchange of ideas among group members. In traditional verbal brainstorming, all members hear the same comments and contribute to the same single exchange or "dialogue" (presuming they actually participate). Electronic brainstorming (EBS) has the ability to change this. With EBS, computer-mediated electronic communication replaces verbal communication. Instead of contributing spoken ideas, members type ideas into computer workstations which record and send them to the others' workstations. Members read the ideas and respond by building on them to create new ideas, by ignoring them and generating unrelated ideas, or by doing nothing. Recent studies have found EBS groups to generate more ideas than verbally interacting groups and as many or more ideas than nominal groups (Dennis and Valacich 1993; Gallupe et al. 1991, 1992; Valacich et al. 1994).

EBS systems can be structured to provide a single dialogue similar to verbal brainstorming. In this case, members type ideas into their workstations, which save them in a single list of ideas—a single dialogue. As members type ideas, the ideas are appended to the bottom of the idea list, in the same way spoken ideas are added to the end of a verbal discussion. Two key differences from verbal brainstorming are that members of EBS groups can contribute ideas simultaneously and anonymously (Gallupe et al. 1992). However, in this situation, the EBS system does not specifically address cognitive inertia.

EBS systems also enable the use of multiple dialogues. Rather than placing all ideas into a single dialogue, the list of ideas can be partitioned into several smaller dialogues. Group members can then choose in which dialogues to participate. For example, members could start in one dialogue, read others' ideas, add ideas, then switch to a new dialogue, participate in it, and so on. Most prior studies of EBS have used multiple dialogues rather than single dialogues (e.g., Dennis and Valacich 1993; Gallupe et al. 1991, 1992; Valacich et al. 1994).

However, there is always the possibility that groups will not actively use multiple dialogues. Given a choice about how many dialogues to use, and how many ideas to contribute to one dialogue before moving to a new one, group members might tend to continue to use their “traditional” structures. For example, if several dialogues were available to the groups, members still might choose to focus on only one or two dialogues and ignore the others because this is cognitively simpler, or because it is closer to learned social behaviors. In this case, cognitive inertia would be unchanged.

To counter this, groups could adopt formal rules or heuristics about how many ideas members could contribute to a dialogue before moving to another one and how the next dialogue is selected. Hackman and Kaplan (1974) note that one of the greatest problems with such rules or heuristics is that groups often don't follow them.

Computer systems offer additional opportunities to impose structure on individual and group behavior. Systems can restrict users' actions so that it is difficult to not follow the rules or heuristics embedded in a system (Silver 1990). When used effectively, such restrictiveness promotes more consistent use of effective techniques, and provides a more powerful intervention so that groups are more likely to use the system as intended by its designers (DeSanctis et al. 1989, Poole and DeSanctis 1992).

If an EBS system is sufficiently restrictive to induce group members to use multiple dialogues, we anticipate that it will affect all three sources of cognitive inertia; see Table 1. From the cognitive perspective, partitioning ideas into separate dialogues will encourage the development of different stimuli in each dialogue. Dialogues may focus on different topics or idea categories, either because they start out differently or because they evolve differently. Each dialogue may activate a different set of production rules, resulting in the consideration of many different topics, reducing cognitive inertia.

From the social perspective, multiple dialogues may help break down pre-existing social structures that inhibit the contribution of ideas unrelated to the central topic or theme of the discussion. Each dialogue is more likely to focus on a different topic, making it more difficult for one central topic to emerge. This will encourage members to consider many topics within the solution space, thus reducing cognitive inertia. In addition, techniques can be used that promote more accurate social comparison. For example, the technique used in this study (described in the Method section below) attempts to present subjects with at least one new idea (typically several new ideas) from other group members for each idea they contribute. This should encourage members to perceive the contributions of others more accurately and mitigate the downward spiral in the rate of idea contribution. This too is likely to reduce cognitive inertia.

From the procedural perspective, a multiple dialogue approach may still encourage piggy-backing on the ideas of others, but with many different dialogues, the emerging stream of piggy-backed ideas will still go in many different directions. This also will reduce cognitive inertia.

This reduction in cognitive inertia means that groups using multiple dialogues should explore a larger portion of the overall solution space. There is evidence that exploring more of the solution space results in more ideas (Armstrong et al. 1975, Pitz et al. 1980). In addition, multiple dialogues expose group members to diverse sets of ideas and there is evidence to suggest that exposure to a diverse set of ideas leads to more ideas (Hoffman 1959, Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973, Taylor et al. 1958). Therefore, we hypothesize:

HYPOTHESIS 1. Groups will generate more ideas when using a restrictive multiple dialogue technique than when using a single dialogue technique.

Performance on idea generation tasks has traditionally been assessed by examining the number of ideas produced, but this is not the only assessment possible. In some cases, the goal is to generate many ideas, while in others, it is to find a few high quality or highly novel ideas. In general, idea quality and novelty are influenced by the number of ideas. Groups that generate more ideas will also generate more high-quality and more novel ideas (and more low-quality and less novel ideas) (Osborn 1953, Diehl and Stroebe 1987). Therefore, we hypothesize:

HYPOTHESIS 2. Groups will generate more high quality ideas when using a restrictive multiple dialogue technique than when using a single dialogue technique.

HYPOTHESIS 3. Groups will generate more novel ideas when using a restrictive multiple dialogue technique than when using a single dialogue technique.

## Method

## Subjects

Two hundred junior and senior business students participated as members of 20 ten-member groups. The average age was 21.5 years. Sixty-five percent were male.

## Independent Variable

The experiment used a repeated measures design in which all subjects participated in two treatment conditions, the order of which was randomly determined. The independent variable was the idea generation technique used: multiple or single dialogue. All subjects used both techniques. Subjects were instructed to use computer workstations for all interaction; no verbal communication occurred.

The single dialogue treatment was implemented using the Group Outliner module of the GroupSystems V software package (see Nunamaker et al. 1991, Valacich et al. 1991). This software divided the screen into two windows, a large upper window used to display the entire list of ideas and a smaller lower one used to type new ideas. Subjects could type their ideas and save them into the idea list at any time. When subjects saved their ideas, the ideas were appended to the bottom of the list of ideas and were immediately available to all participants.

Subjects in the multiple dialogue treatment used the Electronic Brainstorming module of GroupSystems V which had a user interface identical to that of the single dialogue treatment. This module was configured to break the lists of group ideas into eleven separate dialogues. Each subject began the experimental session with a different empty dialogue and started by typing an idea into it. This dialogue then was replaced by another dialogue (the most recently updated one), and the process was repeated. This is analogous to being at a party where every time you make a comment to one group, you are immediately whisked to another group to whom you listen, make another comment, and immediately move to another group.

An alternative would have been to permit subjects to choose into which dialogue to contribute ideas and when to move among dialogues. However, lessening restrictiveness to permit any individual choice would have weakened experimental control; as discussed above, individuals might have chosen to contribute most or all of their ideas into one dialogue, thus weakening or eliminating differences between the experimental treatments.

The choice of eleven dialogues was arbitrary; in the absence of strong theory or prior empirical work, it was difficult to make compelling arguments that favored a particular number of dialogues. Eleven is the default value recommended by the software developers for ten member teams (see the GroupSystems manual). One more dialogue than the number of participants allows subjects to have access to another dialogue immediately after finishing one.

## Tasks

All subjects performed two tasks drawn from prior research. The first asked subjects to generate ideas to encourage more tourists to visit the United States (Taylor et al. 1958). The second sought ideas to improve campus security (Dennis and Valacich 1993).

## Procedures

The experimenter described the experimental session and trained the subjects on the software. Subjects were instructed to read the ideas of others and type their ideas. They were asked to keep their ideas short and succinct. Subjects in the multiple dialogue treatment were told that each person had a different file of ideas, much like a different piece of paper. They were told that after they typed their idea on this file (or piece of paper) and pressed the button to share it with the others, they would receive a new file which contained the ideas of others.

Subjects worked on one of the two experimental tasks for 15 minutes using either multiple or single dialogues. Next, subjects worked on the other experimental task for 15 minutes using the other technique (the task and treatment order were randomized). The subjects were then debriefed. Our choice of 15 minutes was arbitrary; 15 minutes has been the most commonly used time period for brainstorming in prior EBS and verbal brainstorming research.

## Dependent Variables

There were three dependent variables: the number of unique ideas, idea quality, and idea novelty. The number of unique ideas was determined by counting nonredundant ideas. An idea was identified as unique when it added a new piece of task-related information. Ideas that only agreed with a previous idea or shared the same meaning were not counted. Statements that did not relate to the task were excluded from the analysis (e.g., "I'm hungry"). The idea lists were first analyzed by one rater, who counted all the unique ideas. Uniqueness was determined on a per group basis, so if the same idea appeared in several different dialogues for the same group, it was counted only once. A random subset of transcripts from five groups was independently analyzed by a second rater. Inter-rater reliability was adequate at 0.94 (measured as 1 – number of disagreements/number of ideas).

Idea quality can be assessed in many ways. See Diehl and Stroebe (1987) for a discussion of different techniques. We used the “number of good ideas” approach recommended by Diehl and Stroebe (1987) as being the most reliable. The first rater evaluated all the unique ideas as either “good” or “not good.” A second rater independently evaluated a randomly selected subset (70%) of the ideas; interrater reliability was adequate at 0.85 (measured as 1 – number of disagreements/number of ideas). Quality was determined by counting only those ideas that were categorized as “good” by the first rater.

Idea novelty can be measured in many different ways. See MacCrimmon and Wagner (1994) for a discussion of different techniques. We used the frequency count approach: the less often an idea was mentioned, the more novel it was. The novelty of each idea was assessed by counting the number of times each idea was mentioned in all twenty groups. The most novel idea was mentioned only once, while the least novel idea was suggested 42 times. There are many ways these novelty scores can be used to calculate the overall novelty of each group's ideas. We used the same approach that we used for determining overall quality: number of novel ideas. An idea was considered novel if it was mentioned less than the mean novelty of the ideas in each task (2.01 for the tourism task and 2.82 for the security task). Thus the novelty score for each group was determined by counting only those ideas that were mentioned once or twice across all groups.

## Results

A repeated measures ANOVA (general linear models with treatment as a repeated factor) with task and order as covariates were used in all analyses. Groups using the multiple dialogue treatment generated more unique ideas $F(1,17)=16.61, p<0.001$ , more high-quality ideas $F(1,17)=49.39, p<0.001$ , and more novel ideas $F(1,17)=9.62, p=0.006$ . See Tables 2 and 3. Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 are supported.

There was also a significant main effect for order and significant interaction effects for treatment $\times$ order and treatment $\times$ task for the number of unique ideas, idea quality, and idea novelty. These unexpected main and interaction effects are discussed below.

## Discussion

This study found that when groups used multiple dialogues they generated more ideas, more high-quality ideas, and more novel ideas than when they used single dialogues. We argued that the use of multiple dialogues would produce more ideas because it would help overcome the cognitive, social, and procedural factors that encourage cognitive inertia. Creating many separate dialogues would encourage different dialogues to evolve into different topic areas and activate different cognitive production rules, resulting in more ideas. The use of multiple dialogues also would reduce two social factors that foster cognitive inertia. First, it would help break down pre-existing social structures that inhibit the contribution of ideas unrelated to the central topic of the discussion. Second, it would promote a more accurate social comparison and break the downward matching spiral that can occur in single dialogues, resulting in more ideas. The use of multiple dialogues also would reduce procedural factors that encourage a narrow range of piggy-backing on a few ideas.

The results generally support our theoretical arguments. One unexpected result was the significant main and interaction effects due to order. In general, groups generated fewer ideas for the second task on which they worked, suggesting that they experienced some fatigue or loss of motivation in the second half of the experimental session. We examined the data more closely to better understand the meaning of the order interaction effects and found an unusual pattern. The results for the tourism task were exactly as hypothesized: when the tourism task was administered first, the means for the single dialogue and multiple dialogues were 75.3 and 83.5, respectively; when the tourism task was second, the means were 70.7 and 74.4, respectively.

The security task was different; it experienced much greater fluctuations in means. When it was administered first, the means for the single and multiple dialogues were as hypothesized: 59.0 and 98.3, respectively. However, when it was second, the pattern was reversed: 71.7 and 63.5; the single dialogue treatment resulted in more ideas, but the differences were not statistically significant ( $t(11) = 1.29$ , p = 0.22). We examined the data and found six anomalies where the number of ideas in the single dialogue treatment was greater than or equal to the number of ideas in the multiple dialogue treatment. We also found two anomalies where multiple dialogues exceeded single dialogues by more than twice the mean difference between the treatments.

Why was performance on the security task so volatile? As the study began, there was a series of three highly publicized rapes on or near campus in a ten-day period (a highly unusual event for this campus). During the course of data collection, three issues of the campus newspaper had major cover stories on improving campus security. A total of seven experimental sessions occurred within three days of these stories being printed, and these seven sessions accounted for six of the eight anomalies. Therefore, we do not believe the fluctuations in means were due to an interaction with treatment order. Instead, we attribute most of the unexpected results to the interaction of these news stories about security with our security task; the random assignment of treatment orders just happened to place more of one treatment order close to the news stories than another.

## Future Research

One question for future research is how many dialogues are most beneficial. In this study, we chose eleven dialogues, one more than the number of participants. Increasing the number of dialogues might prove beneficial by further reducing cognitive inertia, resulting in more ideas. But, introducing a large number of dialogues could fragment the discussion into very small idea lists and brainstorming may require a critical mass of ideas before many ideas are produced (Dennis and Valacich 1993, Valacich 1995). Fragmentation may also cause greater redundancy because group members cannot see all ideas and may unknowingly duplicate an idea already contributed by another member. Duplicate ideas add little value. In this study, groups in the multiple dialogues generated a greater percentage of duplicate ideas (29 percent of all ideas were duplicates versus 23 percent in the single dialogue treatment $F(1,17) = 5.24$ , p = 0.035). Therefore, while increasing the number of dialogues may further reduce cognitive inertia and increase the number of ideas, at some point fragmentation and duplication become a significant problem and reduce the number of unique ideas.

There is at least one other problem with multiple dialogues. Splitting discussion into many dialogues makes it more difficult for a group to converge to a consensus of opinion. Thus, while multiple dialogues may benefit idea generation, it may impair the performance of other tasks such as decision making.

There are many other directions for future research. Much prior electronic brainstorming research has compared EBS systems that provide parallelism, anonymity, and multiple dialogues to verbally interacting groups (e.g., Gallupe et al. 1992) or to nominal groups (e.g., Dennis and Valacich 1993, Valacich et al. 1994). The combination of these three components (parallelism, anonymity, and multiple dialogues) has proved superior to verbal or nominal group brainstorming. One important question is the relative contribution of each of the three; that is, which is most important? Would EBS with just parallelism (without anonymity and multiple dialogues) enable groups to outperform verbal groups or nominal groups, or are anonymity and/or multiple dialogues critical to higher levels of success?

In this study, we used a restrictive form of the multiple dialoguing process. The number of ideas each subject contributed into each dialogue at one time was tightly controlled (only one idea) as were the dialogues into which they entered (the most recently updated dialogue). While this is the most commonly used approach to multiple dialoguing in field studies (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1991), it is certainly not the only approach.

Would we have found similar results had we not specified the maximum number of ideas to be contributed to each dialogue at one time, but let the group as a whole (or even each member individually) choose how many ideas to contribute to one dialogue before moving to on another? Likewise, we do not know whether the results would have been much different if we had permitted each group (or each member individually) to choose which dialogues to enter.

One might speculate that if cognitive reasons are the strongest force promoting cognitive inertia in single dialoguing groups (i.e., it is cognitively simpler to generate related ideas), given the choice of how to allocate their effort, members might be tempted to spend most of their time contributing to only a few dialogues because this would require less effort. However, this would defeat the intent of multiple dialogues and likely result in fewer ideas. Clearly, this is an empirical question. And clearly, too, more research is needed to better understand the benefits and limitations of multiple dialogues. $^{1}$
