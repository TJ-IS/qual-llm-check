# Portfolios of Control Modes and IS Project Management

Laurie J. Kirsch

Katz Graduate School of Business, The University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260
lkirsch@katz.business.pitt.edu

In spite of the attention it has received, there is still much that is not understood about the management of systems development efforts. This research examines one aspect of the management process: the function of control. In this paper, control is viewed broadly, encompassing all attempts to ensure individuals in organizations act in a manner that is consistent with meeting organizational goals and objectives. Control is categorized into formal modes (behavioral, outcome) and informal modes (clan, self). Formal and informal control modes are implemented via a variety of mechanisms, such as linking pay with performance, socialization, and team-building. Relatively little is known about the modes of control used to manage information systems development efforts. The objective of this research is to address this lack of understanding in the literature by exploring how control modes are implemented during systems development projects and by investigating why IS and user stakeholders implement particular combinations of control modes. To meet this objective, a series of four case studies of systems development projects was conducted. The results reveal that users, as well as IS managers, play a critical role in controlling systems development projects. Moreover, the results suggest that all stakeholders implement a portfolio of control modes that typically includes both formal and informal modes. This portfolio contains a mix of overlapping and redundant mechanisms used to exercise these modes of control. The results also suggest that constructing a portfolio of control modes is a process that includes selecting appropriate pre-existing mechanisms of formal control; designing new mechanisms with which to implement formal control, if necessary; and supplementing the mechanisms of formal control with mechanisms of informal control. Throughout this process of construction, the choice of particular control mechanisms depends on task characteristics, role expectations, and project-related knowledge and skills.

(IS Project Management; IS Project Control; Organization of Work)

## Introduction

Information systems development (ISD) is not just a technical process of building an information system, but also a social process involving stakeholders from multiple organizational units. This set of stakeholders possesses critical and complementary skills and knowledge that will be called upon during the course of an ISD project. Successfully building systems, therefore, requires effective management of relationships among these stakeholders to elicit their contributions and cooperation, while, at the same time, maintaining progress toward the project's goals (Beath and Orlikowski 1994, Walz et al. 1993). Exercising control is one powerful approach that managers can use to ensure progress by fusing together the complementary roles and capabilities of ISD project participants, motivating individuals to work in accordance with organizational goals and objectives. Research has shown that control strategies affect the management of ISD projects and the performance of project teams (Henderson and Lee 1992, Kirsch and Cummings 1996). The practitioner literature has also noted the critical role that control plays in effective project management (Project Management Institute (PMI) Standards Committee 1996, Purba et al. 1995).

While there are existing theories and frameworks of control, for several reasons it is not clear how well they explain control in the ISD context. First, many of these theories focus on how a supervisor controls a subordinate. But during ISD efforts, it is not just a particular supervisor-subordinate relationship that is paramount, but any number of controller-controllee pairs. Further, the critical relationships may not be dyadic at all, but instead may take the form of a network of peers whose behaviors might not be amenable to influence through traditional control modes. Second, most of the theories address the issue of control in the context of formalized organizational relationships. Many key relationships in the ISD context, however, are not governed by formal organizational mechanisms. For example, job descriptions and work assignments do not typically articulate how relationships between IS and user personnel should be structured and governed. Third, much existing empirical work ignores the interplay among multiple modes of control and controllers, and instead examines the use of a single mode of control in a particular situation (e.g., Eisenhardt 1985, Snell 1992, Kirsch 1996). However, there is evidence that controllers in ISD settings structure a portfolio of control modes in order to manage the complexities and subtleties of a task that involves people with various knowledge and skills across time (Boland 1979, Orlikowski 1991, Henderson and Lee 1992). While individuals need a certain amount of autonomy to produce parts of the solution, all pieces must eventually be integrated into a deliverable system (Constantine 1993, Walz et al. 1993). Management of ISD projects therefore requires a balance between autonomous self-control and control modes designed for a manager to coordinate project activities (Kirsch 1996). Finally, much of the ISD project management literature focuses on the roles and perspectives of IS managers (see, for example, Henderson and Lee 1992, Nidumolu 1995). While this literature is informative, it does not provide much insight into the role of users in the management and control of ISD efforts.

The reasons articulated above suggest that current conceptualizing on control is inadequate to explain the control of ISD projects, and that current empirical work is not rich enough to shed sufficient insight on a situation of multiple controllers, multiple controllees, and multiple control modes. Thus, the specific research questions addressed in this paper are: (1) how do IS and user stakeholders exercise control to manage ISD projects, and (2) why do they choose to structure portfolios of control modes as they do? To address these questions, four case studies were conducted. In the next section of this paper, background literature is reviewed. The research methodology is then explained. The next section analyzes how and why control was exercised in the manner observed; this is followed by a discussion of the results. The paper ends by noting limitations and drawing conclusions.

## Background Literature

In this paper, control is viewed in a behavioral sense, that is, as attempts to ensure that individuals working on organizational projects act according to an agreed-upon strategy to achieve desired objectives (Jaworski 1988, Merchant 1988). Taking this broad view allows for an examination of multiple approaches to control, and avoids problems associated with more narrow perspectives. For example, the practitioner literature typically takes a cybernetic view of control, in which outputs are compared against standards and corrective actions are taken to address deviations; the PMI Standards Committee (1996) offers the following definition: "the process of comparing actual performance with planned performance, analyzing variances, evaluating possible alternatives, and taking appropriate corrective action as needed" (p. 161, emphasis in original). This cybernetic view assumes that outcomes are known, standards can be set, and corrective action is possible (Markus and Pfeffer 1983, Merchant and Simons 1986, Jaworski 1988). However, desired outcomes, standards, and corrective actions are not always obvious in an environment such as ISD, rendering these assumptions problematic and suggesting the need for a broader interpretation of control.

The definition of control adopted in this paper implies that control is exercised via mechanisms—devices used by controllers to ensure proper controller behavior—which, when successfully implemented, result in the regulation of behavior—that is, the controller adjusts his behavior accordingly. For example, rules and regulations serve as mechanisms of control since they articulate acceptable behavior; control is effected when the controller adjusts his behavior to act in accordance with those rules and regulations. Organizations implement a variety of mechanisms to ensure that employees conduct their work in a way that is consistent with organizational goals. This paper examines mechanisms used to implement four modes of control—behavior, outcome, clan, and self. Each mode can be characterized as formal or informal.

## Formal Modes of Control

Formal control is viewed by organizational and agency theorists as a performance evaluation strategy (Eisenhardt 1985). There are two modes of formal control: behavior and outcome (Ouchi 1979, Eisenhardt 1989a, Klepper 1990). In behavior control, specific rules and procedures are articulated, which, if followed, will lead to desired outcomes. Controllers observe the behaviors of the controllees, who are rewarded based on the degree to which they follow these desired procedures. For example, a detailed systems development methodology may be viewed as a mechanism of behavior control if it articulates the precise steps to follow to successfully develop a system (Alavi 1984, Mahmood 1987, Necco et al. 1987). Control is effected by rewarding individuals according to how faithfully they follow the methodology. The results of empirical studies suggest that behavior control is implemented when appropriate behaviors are known or when controllee behaviors are observable to the controller (Eisenhardt 1985, Snell 1992, Kirsch 1996).

To implement outcome control, desired outcomes or goals are articulated, and controllees are rewarded for meeting those goals. Researchers have viewed the articulation of goals as the setting of performance targets (Snell 1992, Eisenhardt 1985), interim milestones for a particular activity (Henderson and Lee 1992), or specific project goals (Kirsch 1996). In this research, the latter view is adopted. As an example, in the ISD context, outcome control can be exercised by setting a target implementation date for a system under development. Controlees are then rewarded or sanctioned for meeting or missing the target date. Empirical studies have found that outcome control is implemented when outcomes are measurable (Eisenhardt 1985, Snell 1992, Kirsch 1996), that is, when the controller is able to assess whether the target was reached.

## Informal Modes of Control

In contrast to formal controls, informal modes of control are based on social or people strategies (Eisenhardt 1985, Jaworski 1988). Because formal controls tend to present a mechanistic view of the control process, they ignore interpersonal or self-regulating dynamics that also govern behavior (Ashford and Tsui 1991). Jaworski (1988) argues that informal controls are typically undocumented and differ in terms of level of aggregation. This research focuses on two modes that represent different levels: clan (group level) and self (individual level) controls.

Clan control is implemented by promulgating common values, beliefs, and philosophy within a clan, which is defined as a group of individuals who are dependent on one another and who share a set of common goals (Ouchi 1980). By carefully selecting and socializing members, the clan propagates these norms and values, resulting in a group of individuals who share a common ideology, who have internalized a set of values, and who are committed to the clan (Boland 1979, Ouchi 1979, Orlikowski 1991). Rather than requiring employees to follow a written set of procedures (which often do not exist), the socialization process, as well as rituals and ceremonies, serve to identify and reinforce acceptable behaviors. Further, specific goals or objectives are typically unknown at the beginning of an activity, but instead evolve over time (Ouchi 1979). For example, consider the following scenario in which clan control could be exercised. Many IS organizations have undocumented development standards, such as an unwritten policy of using field names as defined in a data dictionary. If, within a project team, members are socialized to believe that successfully developing systems includes using the standard field names in the data dictionary, and team members are informally rewarded for adhering to the standard (e.g., told that “they did a good job”) and informally sanctioned for not (e.g., ostracized by team members), then clan control is operating. Note that this mode of control is independent of others that might be operating such as formal performance evaluation and reward systems. According to Ouchi’s (1979) framework, clan control will be implemented when neither outcomes are measurable nor appropriate behaviors are known.

A second mode of informal control is self-control or self-management, in which an individual sets his own goals for a particular task, and then proceeds to self-monitor, self-reward, and self-sanction (Erez and Kanfer 1983, Manz et al. 1987). Unlike clan control, in which control stems from sharing norms and values with a group, self-control is a function of individual objectives and standards (Jaworksi 1988) and intrinsic motivation (Manz et al. 1987). Self-managed individuals define their own goals and processes for the task. These goals or processes may or may not be formally documented, but what is important is that they emanate from the individual. An ISD project manager, for example, may believe that a quality control (QC) function is critical within an ISD team and so sets a goal of delivering a "quality" system. To meet this goal, she designs a process incorporating quality mechanisms into the development effort; that process includes defining specific QC tasks and monitoring the effectiveness of the QC function. She then intrinsically rewards herself for successfully building quality control into the development process. All of this takes place entirely outside the exercise of formal or clan control. In organizations where self-control is valued and encouraged, rewards are based, in part, on how well individuals self-control (Manz and Sims 1980). The literature suggests that there are both organizational and individual antecedents of self-control, including task complexity, ambiguous individual performance evaluation, lack of rules and procedures for completing a task, desire to exercise self-control, and individual abilities (von Glinow 1983, Greenberger and Strasser 1986, Manz et al. 1987). Several empirical studies support these arguments. For example, the results of Hrebiniak's (1974) study suggest that freedom to determine how to handle a job increases as task predictability decreases. Manz and Angle (1986) found that as rules and procedures increase in number, individuals perceive decreased self-control and discretion in their work. In more recent work, Kirsch and Cummings (1996) found that perceptions of self control are highest for IS professionals with long organizational tenure who refine and extend existing work procedures and methods.

## Control in the IS Context

Using a variety of theoretical frameworks, IS researchers have conceptualized control in numerous ways. This multiplicity of views has led to the articulation of an assortment of mechanisms used to implement different modes of control. For example, Henderson and Lee (1992), drawing on several theoretical perspectives such as organizational control, leadership, and cybernetics, argue that control mechanisms include role clarification, work assignment, and feedback loops. Kling and Iacono (1984) place control in a political framework, viewing it as a means of influence. From this perspective, controllers create support for their own interests and preferences, building coalitions whose members share ideologies and legitimating their own actions by institutionalizing rules and policies. Klein and Kraft (1994) maintain that individuals who are “empowered” or who work in self-managing teams control their own activities; control may also be implemented via traditional hierarchical relationships. They note that specific control mechanisms can include shared ideologies achieved through careful hiring and training practices, and communication networks, which allow management to closely observe individual activities by providing continuous feedback. Orlikowski (1991) conceptualizes control in terms of internal and external forms of control. Internal forms include personal observation of a subordinate by a supervisor, job descriptions, rules and procedures, and technical infrastructure (for example, when developers must conform to the ISD production technology embedded within a CASE tool). External forms of control include professional indoctrination and training.

## Methodology

To design this study, two points about the ISD context were noted. First, projects typically span organizational departments, involving both IS and user groups. Since IS and users are stakeholders in ISD, that is, both have much to lose if the effort fails, this study focused on the ways in which both exercise various modes of control. Moreover, this study sought to understand the role that both user and IS stakeholders play in controlling ISD projects. Thus, the IS systems development manager (the immediate supervisor of a project leader) and the user contact (the key person responsible for providing the user's perspective) were considered the controllers. It was also recognized at the outset that neither of these two individuals could assume the role of a clan, since a clan is a group of individuals. However, it was thought that either of these individuals might be part of a clan exercising control, and that potential clans include project teams, organizational units, and the entire organization. Second, since the IS project leader plays a pivotal role in many ISD projects and is usually the focal point for IS and user management, this study designated the project leader as the controller of interest. As explained in the Data Collection section, the views and perceptions of project leaders, IS managers, and user contacts, as well as other IS and user personnel associated with the projects, were gathered for this study.

## Design of the Research Study

Since this research examined how and why specific control modes are implemented to manage systems development projects, a case study design was appropriate (Yin 1984). The unit of analysis is the project embedded within the organization. To strengthen the generalizability of this study, to produce enough data to suggest modifications to control theories, and to provide “empirical grounding,” four case studies were conducted (Yin 1984, Eisenhardt 1989c).

## Site and Project Descriptions

The selection of the projects and sites was guided by two principles suggested by other researchers (Eisenhardt 1989c, Orlikowski 1993). First, cases were selected that were similar in domains critical to the research. Thus, an attempt was made to identify sufficiently large projects (at least six months in duration), organized with an IS project leader, who reported directly to an IS manager, and who directed the project team and managed the day-to-day effort. Each project must have an in-house client, with a readily identifiable user contact. In addition, the project could employ any one of a number of development approaches that are based on the separation of process and data, breaking the process into specific phases (e.g., systems development life cycle, information engineering). Second, the case selection explicitly sought variation in other organizational characteristics such as current systems development activity, departmental or firm structure, organizational culture, and industry. Because part of the intent of this study is to explain the exercise of different control modes, possibly extending the boundaries of current theoretical thinking, variation in context was desirable.

Balancing these two principles of similarity and variation, four projects in four organizations were identified. All four of the firms are large, with annual revenues (at the time of the study) exceeding two billion dollars. The criteria regarding the similarity and variation principles were met. In particular, the firms differed along several dimensions, including industry, structure, and level of ISD activity. Moreover, while the projects varied in terms of purpose, size, scope (local versus global), and phase of development, they were similar in terms of organization, development approach, and expected development timeframe (greater than six months). The four organizations and projects are briefly described below, and summarized in Table 2. $^{1}$

M&M Bank: Voice Response Unit. A decentralized financial institution, M&M Bank is comprised of a number of autonomous business units. Each unit has its own IS budget, and is not required to contract for its technology-related services with corporate IS, a wholly owned subsidiary of the corporation. Corporate IS consists of a number of divisions, including the Distributed Technology division, which was the site of this particular case study. At the time of the study, this division was involved in fifty active projects, with budgets ranging from \$10,000 to \$20 million. The budget for the voice response unit project was \$470,000. This project was initiated in the late 1980s, when the manager of the customer service group began a departmental project to replace an outdated voice response unit with a more sophisticated, user-friendly system. When corporate IS heard about this project, they recognized an opportunity to introduce and propagate a standard voice response product across all M&M regions. While the customers were skeptical of IS involvement, fearing a longer timeframe and increased costs, IS argued persuasively that the users should spend three months with them exploring available technology to ensure they implemented a system that would meet present and future needs. The logic of this argument, coupled with IS's offer to conduct this initial feasibility study on a non-billable basis, convinced the customers to work with IS. The project started in September 1990, with a target implementation date of June 1991. The system was installed in July 1991, then backed off because of technical problems. It was put back into production in August 1991.

Roseland Corporation: Worldwide Financial Reporting System. Roseland Corporation is an international manufacturing firm that was decentralized until recent economic conditions prompted the decision to centralize operations. This decision affected the unit IS managers, who, instead of reporting to the business unit controllers, now reported directly to the corporate controller. To accommodate the move to a more centralized structure, the corporate controller initiated a project to build a corporate-wide financial accounting system in the early 1980s. Because of lack of progress, the project was reorganized in 1984. At this time, a new strategic plan was created, which broke the project into discrete phases, and laid out high-level requirements and deadlines. The plan covered the period from 1984 to 1991. The first phase was to design and build a consolidated database, which was implemented in 1987. Shortly after, another project reorganization occurred. The Roseland project that was the subject of this case study lasted four years (1988–1991), and covered the development and implementation of four phases. During this time period, this consolidation project was one of the few new development projects ongoing at Roseland; most ISD activity was focused on maintenance.

Megamax Products: Cost Accounting and Budgeting System, Phase 1. A domestic manufacturer, Megamax Products is subject to government regulations that require detailed justification of its cost accounting and budgeting practices. After recently reviewing required documents, a government agency expressed a lack of confidence in Megamax's procedures, which prompted Megamax to reexamine the way its systems are tied to organizational objectives. From this reexamination came the decision to revamp the cost accounting and budgeting system over two phases. The goal of the first phase was to make it easier for the users to access and run the existing mainframe budgeting programs by creating a more user-friendly interface, and allowing online job submission, output retrieval, parameter and table changes. The goal of Phase 2 was to completely rethink budgeting and cost accounting practices within Megamax. Phase 1, the focus of this study, began in March 1991 and was implemented in January 1992.

TransAtlantic Foods: Commercial System. TransAtlantic Foods (TAF) is a decentralized, multinational organization in commodities trading. The U.S. grain processing subsidiary relied on a commercial system (to manage functions such as contracting, shipping, and receiving) that had been developed for an unrelated division within TAF; because this system was not entirely compatible with the grain processing business, the division decided to build a new one. At the same time, the European grain processing subsidiary, which had developed an in-house commercial system, was preparing to rewrite the application. The global president of the division decided that one new system should be designed and built to meet the needs of all grain processing subsidiaries. Consequently, an international project team was assembled, including IS personnel from both the United States and Europe. The systems development manager and user contact were employees of the U.S. grain processing subsidiary; the project leader worked in the European office; and IS developers from both locations were members of the project team. The project started in April 1991, with an estimated completion date of early 1993.

## Data Collection

The data were collected between June 1991 and April 1992 via interviews and a review of project documentation. The documentation consisted of project planning documents with an initial statement of purpose for the project, proposed budgets, and schedules; official project notebooks, containing organization charts of the project, project statements, schedules of work, target implementation dates and budgets, roles and responsibilities of the project team; memos to or from project team members or management; and project status reports.

One interview (of the European project leader) was conducted by phone; the remaining thirty were conducted by the researcher at the site of the case study. The interviews of the project leaders, IS managers, users, and other project personnel generally lasted between one and two hours. The interview questions, primarily open-ended, were informed by the literature on control and designed to elicit data about the theoretical constructs of interest. $^{2}$ Any one question might shed some insight on more than one key concept. For example, the following question, posed to the project leader, might reveal information about both the extent to which the controller observed the controller's behaviors, plus the level of project leader autonomy: "How did you let your manager know what you were doing to meet those [project] goals?"

It was critical, during data collection, to identify and elicit information about particular incidents of control. Consequently, a specific interviewing technique was designed. Recognizing that control is purposive (Green and Welsh 1988), four common goals of ISD projects were identified: to develop systems on-time and within budget, to satisfy end-users' requirements, and to conform to IS development standards (Tom 1987,

Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen 1990, Moynihan 1990, Banker and Kemerer 1992, Kirsch 1996). A series of interview questions focused on how controllers regulated controller behavior in order to meet each of these four goals. For example, the IS managers and user contacts were asked:

Did the project leader incorporate the standards into the system?

Why or why not?

Was there a time when you were concerned about the project

leader adhering to standards? What did you do?

These particular questions were useful for eliciting information about how project leader behavior was regulated to ensure that standards were followed. Another general set of questions sought to uncover additional critical incidents and how they were handled. To uncover such incidents, respondents were asked "to recall events that caused problems for which the organization had no ready solution, or events that challenged existing norms and solutions . . . , or anything interpersonal that was unusual or tension provoking and required some kind of response" (Schein 1987, p. 120). For example, the following questions, based on Schein (1987), encouraged the respondent to recall specific project events and to discuss how particular situations were handled:

Take a minute to think about this project.

Was there a time when things weren't going well?

What happened? Who was involved? How was it resolved?

During data collection, construct validity and reliability issues were addressed. Construct validity, which is concerned with establishing that the correct measures are used, can be increased by using multiple sources of data (Yin 1984). In this research, multiple sources of data were tapped to ensure that responses converged. Inconsistencies were resolved by consulting project documentation or other individuals for clarification. For example, if the participants could not remember (or disagreed on) the exact budget, or the timing of the various development phases, that information could typically be found in the project documentation.

Reliability is concerned with consistency (Yin 1984): if the study were repeated, would the same results follow? One of the primary means of establishing reliability is to build a case study database in order to be as explicit as possible about the way in which observations were made and recorded (Kirk and Miller 1986, Yin 1984). Several techniques were used in this study to improve reliability. First, all but three interviews were tape recorded. $^{3}$ Second, all interviews were transcribed, which yielded approximately four-hundred pages of single-spaced transcripts. All transcriptions and related documentation were filed by case by individual. Third, the sources of all documents and data were recorded. Finally, an individual write-up for each case was prepared where the data were sorted and organized by construct. The purpose of this last step was to reduce the raw data to a more manageable and meaningful structure.

## Analysis

The analysis strategy was designed to answer the two research questions: how were various control modes exercised, and why did the stakeholders build portfolios with specific modes of control. First, to discover how control modes were exercised, a matrix was built for each of the four sites, identifying the mechanisms used to regulate project leader behavior. An excerpt from the Roseland matrix is shown in Table 3; complete matrices can be found in Kirsch (1992).

As previously discussed, interviewees were asked specific questions to elicit incidents of control. Data from the field notes describing these incidents were summarized and entered into the appropriate matrix if (1) the user contact, IS manager, or project leader discussed how the mechanism of control was used to regulate project leader behavior; and (2) the use of the mechanism was confirmed by at least one other person or document.

Each entry identified in the four matrices was classified as a mechanism used to implement behavior, outcome, clan, or self control. The classification process was a crucial step in the analysis and was undertaken in the following manner. Reviewing the characteristics of control modes (Table 1), it is evident that each mode involves (1) the identification or specification of acceptable behaviors to follow or outcomes to produce, and (2) the evaluation of the controller's behaviors or outcomes. While these two characteristics may be fairly straightforward for behavior and outcome controls (Eisenhardt 1985), they may be less obvious for informal modes of control. Nevertheless, the characteristics are present. Clans both identify and reinforce acceptable behaviors. Members of the clan evaluate individual behavior to ensure that it is consistent with the norms of the clan, and encourage individuals to continue engaging in appropriate behaviors (Ouchi 1979). Exercising self-control, individuals articulate goals and procedures and self-monitor; in addition, their managers value and reward self-control skills. To classify the mechanisms observed in this study, it was not enough to recognize that they may be mechanisms of identification or evaluation. It was also necessary to differentiate them as mechanisms of either behavior, outcome, clan, or self-control. This step was aided by noting Jaworski's (1988) argument that mechanisms used to implement formal and informal control modes can be differentiated on the basis of whether the mechanisms are written (i.e., has the organization formally documented the desired behavior or outcome?), and the source of the mechanism (i.e., who has articulated the mechanism—the individual, his manager, a group?). In particular, if a mechanism that specified or identified behaviors for the controllee to engage in was initiated by a manager and formally documented, then it was classified as a mechanism of behavior control. For example, a project plan that articulates the expected approach to a development effort falls into this classification. Another type of mechanism classified as one of behavior control was one that allowed a manager to evaluate and guide project leader behavior, mechanisms such as meetings or progress reports in which specific task behaviors were assessed. Note that it is not the act of monitoring progress itself that makes the mechanism one of behavior control, but it is the controller's use of appropriate information that constitutes an exercise of behavior control. Alternatively, if the mechanism instituted by a manager formally documented a desired outcome, which would be evaluated at a later point in time, then the mechanism was classified as a device of outcome control (e.g., written standards). Further, if the mechanism allowed for evaluation of outcomes (e.g., system testing), then it was also classified as one of outcome control. If the mechanism was not formally documented and was instituted by a group of individuals, then it was classified as a mechanism of clan control. For example, organizational norms or culture might serve to identify acceptable, but unwritten, behaviors for a particular group of individuals. Another instance in which a mechanism would be classified as one of clan control is if the mechanism allowed the clan to assess whether the controllee was acting in a manner consistent with group values and norms. For example, if a cultural norm is to drop functionality when a deadline is in danger of being missed, then a meeting in which behaviors are reviewed and the controllee is praised for acting in accordance with this norm is an instance of clan control. Finally, if the mechanism was the specification of task-related behaviors and outcomes by the individual, or if management articulated that controllee self management skills were valued, then the mechanism was categorized as one of self-control. Further, if the mechanism allowed the controllee to self-monitor, or it allowed the manager to evaluate how well the controllee self-managed, the mechanism was also classified as one of self-control.

The second stage of the analysis explored why controllers chose specific control modes. The goal of this stage was to build a theory about the construction of portfolios of control modes. However, there is relatively little guidance about how to build theories: "Unfortunately, the literature on this topic [theory construction] is sparse and uneven, and tends to focus on outcomes and products rather than process" (Weick 1989, p. 517). One of the reasons for this sparseness is that building theory is a difficult, creative process that resists exact specification:

... [extant] descriptions portray theorizing as mechanistic, with little appreciation of the often intuitive, blind, wasteful, serendipitous, creative quality of the process. . . . Most existing descriptions of the theorizing process assume that validation is the ultimate test of a theory and that theorizing itself is more credible the more closely it simulates external validation at every step (Weick 1989, p. 518–519).

For this theory-building effort, no one methodology was followed, but instead an amalgam of approaches was utilized, built on the work of Yin (1984), Strauss and Corbin (1990), Wolcott (1990), Orlikowski (1993), and others. The theory-building process also included spurts of “intuition,” “serendipity,” and “creativity.” The overall process is explained next.

First, the sites where each control mode was observed were noted. Specifically, behavior and outcome control were observed at all four case sites; self control was found at M&M and Megamax; and clan control was implemented at Roseland and TAF. Second, for each control mode, the transcripts from the appropriate sites were read, and all stated reasons for implementing particular control modes were extracted and put into a table. Thus, four tables were generated at this point, one for each mode of control.

The next two steps involved coding the data. Initially, the reasons given for implementing each mode of control were coded using a technique called open coding (Strauss and Corbin 1990). Open coding is a process of analyzing the data to place conceptual labels on them, and it involves procedures for making comparisons and asking questions about the phenomenon. Some of the conceptual labels applied in this research—such as knowledge of appropriate behaviors and measurable task outcomes—are antecedent conditions suggested by the literature (see Table 1); others were suggested by the data (Strauss and Corbin 1990).

The end result of the open coding process was the assignment of a conceptual label to each explanation. Next, axial coding (Strauss and Corbin 1990) was applied, in which similar concepts are linked together into categories. Looking across the concepts generated during open coding, themes were sometimes found that suggested a common category. For example, several conceptual labels uncovered during open coding were: controller's desire to monitor behaviors; controller's desire to measure outcomes; norms and expectations of the organization and department; interest in developing collegial, trusting relationships; controller's lack of desire or time to oversee daily work of others; and controller's expectations that the project leader exercise self control. For these concepts, a common underlying theme is definitions or expectations about individual roles. Thus, the generalized category relating these explanations is labeled role expectations.

The steps described above culminated in Table 5, which shows, for each control mode, representative comments $^{4}$ about the reasons for implementing specific modes of control, conceptual labels applied to the reasons, and the generalized categories of explanations. Table 5 thus becomes the basis for generating a theory of why stakeholders build portfolios of particular modes of control. Strauss and Corbin (1990) describe this step in the analysis as selective coding, in which a core phenomenon (here, the construction of a portfolio of control modes) is selected and a story line is developed to describe this phenomenon of interest. In addition, categories identified through axial coding are integrated and related to the core phenomenon. The theory developed through this process, which is presented in the Results section, is intended as an initial understanding of the construction of portfolios of control modes. It remains for future research to validate and enhance the theory proposed here.

## How Do the Stakeholders Exercise Each Mode of Control?

The information from the data matrices (e.g., Table 3) is summarized in Table 6, which captures the mechanisms used by both IS and user controllers to exercise each mode of control.

The Exercise of Behavior Control. As seen in Table 6, a variety of mechanisms was used to exercise behavior control. Commonly used mechanisms included project plans, meetings, and progress reports. Armed with project plans, which detailed specific tasks and responsibilities, the controllers ensured project leader decisions and behaviors were consistent with the plans by attending meetings and reviewing progress reports. The user contact at Roseland supplemented these mechanisms by participating in walkthroughs and reviewing technical ISD documentation such as dataflow diagrams and data models.

In addition to viewing project plans, reports, and meetings as mechanisms of behavior control, three controllers (user contacts at M&M and TAF; IS manager at Megamax) found that the definition of organizational roles, with the articulation of clear responsibilities and procedures, was also a means to implement behavior control. For example, the IS manager at Megamax relied on the QA team to report on specific behaviors during the project, such as whether team members were incorporating appropriate standards into the design. Given this information, she could then evaluate and re-direct behaviors, as necessary. Similarly, the M&M user contact expected her subordinate to send her project status updates. Innovating, the TAF user contact designed and implemented an approach to ensure that he and the end-users were actively involved throughout the project: he instituted “user groups,” teams of four or five users, each charged with identifying specific requirements for particular areas of the system (e.g., inventory, sales), and approving requirements and system design. Parallel user groups were established in both locations. These user groups allowed the user contact to exercise behavior control by providing him with a means of influencing and directing the ISD process. For example, when a programmer realized he needed 80 hours to meet a specified requirement instead of the 40 hours budgeted, the decision to include the complexity or to limit the scope was made by the appropriate user group.

The controllers also used a variety of other behavior control mechanisms. For example, the Roseland IS manager “walked around” the organization to personally observe individual activities. Since project participants were located in both the United States and Europe, the IS manager and user contact at TAF used conference calls and travel as a means of exercising behavior control. Finally, the user contact at Megamax attended demonstrations of the system over the course of the ISD effort to assess and influence project progress.

The Exercise of Outcome Control. Table 6 indicates that, just as with behavior control, one of the most common mechanisms used to implement outcome control was the project plan, which typically specified when the system would be implemented and what it would cost. In addition, requirements documents (whether formally or informally constructed) were considered contracts, specifying functions that the users wanted implemented and functions IS agreed to implement. Thus, these controllers were able to evaluate whether the system met their needs as outlined on the requirements document, and whether it was implemented on-time and within budget, by comparing outcomes to goals set in the project plan.

Standards were also a vehicle for implementing outcome control in the sense that they represented desired goals for the new systems. For example, at Megamax, the completed system was checked by the QA team immediately before installation to ensure that it adhered to system standards (e.g., dataset naming conventions, job control language, sign-on procedures) and to verify that required technical documentation (e.g., structure charts, file layouts, a list of critical users, a disaster recovery plan) had been created. The IS manager was then able to determine whether outcomes had been achieved by reviewing the QA report. At Roseland, the user contact held the project leader responsible for maintaining standard features (such as report headings and function key definitions) from the existing system. When the final system was delivered, he verified that these features were in place.

There was also variation in the exercise of outcome control, as indicated in Table 6. For example, the IS manager at Megamax again relied on the role of the QA team: she knew whether required functionality was included in the system by determining if the QA team signed-off on the final test results. Both the user contact and the IS manager at Roseland knew whether the users were satisfied by observing their reactions to a formal presentation of the system made shortly before implementation. In addition, the user contact conducted extensive system testing at the end of the project to assess whether outcomes were met, and the IS manager walked around the organization to assess the extent to which the users were satisfied with the final product.

The Exercise of Clan Control. The mechanisms used to exercise clan control, shown in Table 6, varied across the two organizations where it was observed. At Roseland, clan control was exercised via organizational and departmental cultural norms. The organization assumed the role of the clan: here was a group of individuals who had internalized the organizational culture of meeting deadlines. Everyone interviewed commented on the importance of making the target dates. This focus on schedules was ingrained in the work habits of the user contact and project leader. There was a sense of urgency about meeting deadlines; both were committed to finishing on-time. The project leader commented:

From my standpoint, . . . we work overtime when we have to. We make a commitment to the date. Unless something really ridiculous comes up, we've made a commitment, we do what is necessary to get it done. That's how I look at it.

The culture of the IS department reinforced this emphasis on meeting target dates. While rewards were partly based on whether deadlines were met, individuals were expected to act in accordance with the culture: if the deadlines were in danger of being missed, requirements or standards were expendable, as were standard operating procedures. For example, when asked why she did not produce the “required” documentation for one of the subsystems, the project leader said: “I had six months to get it done.”

The IS department also functioned as a clan with respect to ISD standards in that there was a common set of values associated with their use. In particular, IS employees recognized the value of conforming to standards to facilitate long-term maintenance of systems. The data administrator, project leader, and IS manager all commented that IS employees are trained or socialized to incorporate standards when the situation warrants it, that is, employees are also trained to question their applicability in any given situation. Recognizing this, the data administrator referred to the standards book as "The Intelligent Programmer's Guide to Programming." Both the IS manager and project leader concurred; as the project leader put it:

If you find that you want to do something that's in violation of a standard, and you have a good reason for it, then the standard, so to speak, can be changed.

Despite the perceived importance of standards, there was no formal mechanism to ensure they were followed. Instead, the regulation of individual behavior was achieved through peer pressure, which eventually reached management's ear. As the IS manager commented: "Peers will put pressure on them, number one, and number two, it will get back to me."5

At the beginning of the TAF project, the IS manager established an environment conducive to clan control, envisioning that, in time, the project team would serve as the clan, with members embracing shared goals and values. He fostered a sense of common ground, mutual goals, and cooperative work through several mechanisms. First, he insisted that team members send pictures of themselves to each other so they could all "put a face to a name" and start to know each other. He also arranged for team members to travel to each others' sites, hoping to further engender camaraderie and cooperation. Finally, he encouraged the use of conference calls to strengthen and maintain a sense of shared purpose. In time, his efforts were rewarded. For example, early in the project, the IS manager recognized that team members were fighting over whether Europe or the United States owned the project. During this time, he stressed that it was a "TAF project," not a regional project. By the time this case study was conducted, the IS manager, project leader, and analysts all remarked that, though common goals and shared purpose were not present at the start of the project, they had all come to realize that they were creating a business solution that was best for the entire TAF organization. Thus, the IS manager socialized the individuals to believe in the importance of a global TAF solution, promulgating a set of shared values. The individuals, in turn, changed their behaviors as a result of embracing these values. Over the course of the project, they learned to trust each other and to work cooperatively: team members began to air grievances, discuss possible solutions, and listen to each other's suggestions. One example of this change in behavior concerns documentation tools. During the early stages, each analyst adopted her own approach to documentation, causing considerable rework and misunderstandings as the team tried to integrate various pieces of the system. But once the analysts acknowledged their common goals and learned to trust each other, they were able to address the documentation issue by openly discussing options and jointly deciding on a solution. One analyst explained it as follows:

When it comes to making a decision on documentation, what standards we're going to be using on the design, we'll sit and we'll talk about it. And we'll go back and forth on it.

The Exercise of Self-control. As seen in Table 6, evidence for self-control was found at M&M and Megamax, where both project leaders had considerable freedom in managing and defining their projects. This autonomy existed because the controllers involved viewed the organizational role of the project leaders to be self-managers.

As suggested in the literature, when self-control is operating, organizational rewards are based, in part, on an individual's ability to self-control (see Table 1). This reward structure was found at both Megamax and M&M. In particular, the Megamax IS manager based part of her performance evaluation on the project leader's self-control: the way in which she managed the ISD process, including how she led her team, communication skills, and relationships with clients. Similarly, the IS manager at M&M expected the project leader to manage the process. This view was reflected in the performance evaluation, which included assessments of the project leader's personal motivation and leadership ability. The culture of the IS department at M&M reinforced this exercise of self-control. The senior vice-president of IS, asserting that the company's culture was to promote and encourage individual initiative, noted that one of his important roles was "to facilitate the environment so that the people who work with you can do the best that they can."

Besides the establishment of organizational roles or culture by management, the exercise of self-control is found in the way in which the project leaders defined desired goals and behaviors for their projects, and in their self-monitoring and self-regulating. In both cases, the project leaders added their own goals to existing project objectives; they also defined appropriate procedures and behaviors. For example, the M&M project leader insisted on the use of a structured development methodology, even though its use was not required by his department. Not only did he himself adhere to the methodology, but he expected his subordinates to as well, and he evaluated their performance, in part, on how well they followed the methodology. The project leader was also instrumental in moving the project from a one-region voice response unit to an organization-wide standard solution. Rather than accepting the project as it was defined, he expanded it to find the optimal business solution. He commented:

I took a project [where] the concept was put a voice response system in [the local region] and that was it. I turned it into an [organization-wide] project ... I could have just put it in [locally], and that probably would have been fine. So because I got to see where all the benefits are and put it all together, I got to really shape a project into something.

He continually monitored progress toward achieving the project goals, considering himself successful when the old system became a "boat anchor" and the regions were "very happy."

The Megamax project leader was also instrumental in defining project goals and procedures. With respect to project goals, she recognized that the various end-user departments had a history of poor relations and little cooperation. Thus, she set a goal of forging a consensus from these departments on how they and their new system would work together. Even though no one directed her to address this issue, she felt it was imperative for the success of the project:

Everyone is so, still dreaming, to think you can make a system work across departments when the people don't work across departments. But for the system to succeed, for the project to succeed, you have to take on the business problem too, to some extent.

With respect to the development process itself, she defined roles and responsibilities for team members and key users; she scouted for potential team members; she devised reporting formats and frequencies; she initiated system demonstrations; she monitored the activities of the team and users. She also visited end-users daily ("I put on my tennies and I just ran"), asking questions and prompting them to participate in the ISD effort; she prodded the users to attend demonstrations and meetings where she helped them address their differences and come to some agreements.

Both project leaders regulated their behaviors according to internal standards. At M&M, for example, the project leader used a development methodology because it was his standard, not the department's. At Megamax, the project leader addressed the problem of bickering end-users because she felt it was important. She was intrinsically motivated to do a good job and was constantly self-monitoring, assessing her progress:

Is money my satisfier? No. Exciting projects is my satisfier. So I'm not going to wait to ratings time: I have to know, I want to feel all the way along how I'm doing.

The Exercise of Control by IS Managers and User Contacts. Table 6 displays the variety of formal control mechanisms implemented by both the IS managers and users. There are both similarities and differences in terms of the mechanisms used. For example, almost all stakeholders utilized project plans, meetings, and reports to exercise control; however, the use of other mechanisms such as walk-throughs, demonstrations, testing, and “walking around” seemed quite individualized. In some instances, a mechanism appeared to be institutionalized in the sense that it was used by both controllers in one organization, but not by any other controller (e.g., conference calls at TAF, presentations at Roseland). With respect to informal controls, neither the IS managers nor the users appeared to utilize a wide variety of mechanisms, although they both (particularly the IS managers) implemented some.

From Table 6 it becomes evident that the control of ISD projects is not just a function of what IS managers do, but what users do as well. Since there is generally a formalized reporting relationship between the IS manager and IS project leader, it is not surprising that these managers implement so many mechanisms of control. The user's level of participation in controlling ISD projects, however, is not so easily explained, since in only one case (Roseland) did the user contact have a job description that included responsibility for ISD projects. Another user assumed the liaison role as a temporary assignment (Megamax); for the other two (TAF, M&M), their ISD responsibilities represented additional work. What is therefore obvious from these cases is the heavy participation of users in controlling ISD projects, regardless of formal job descriptions.

Why Do ISD Stakeholders Structure Portfolios of Control Modes as They Do?

As described in the Analysis section, the data were coded to build an explanation of why stakeholders implement particular control modes. The coding resulted in the identification of four categories or forces that drive the selection of control modes (see Table 5): the availability of pre-existing mechanisms with which formal control can be exercised, task characteristics, role expectations, and project-related knowledge and skills. Each of these four forces is examined below. A theory is then developed to explain the process of constructing a portfolio of control modes.

Availability of Pre-Existing Mechanisms. The existence of mechanisms affected the controllers' implementation of formal modes of control. When procedures were in place—such as standard reporting practices and accepted planning approaches—the controllers used these as mechanisms of behavior control. Similarly, if outcomes were already in place and identified (e.g., development standards, performance goals, implied standards in existing systems), the controllers tended to implement outcome control with these outcomes as the desired goals. Specific instances are identified in Table 5. For example, the Roseland IS manager noted that he held the project leader responsible for meeting pre-established target implementation dates. Thus, he used an existing mechanism (i.e., pre-established targets) to exercise outcome control.

Task Characteristics. From Table 5 it is evident that the exercise of any mode of control is also partially a function of the characteristics of the task. This is consistent with existing frameworks of control (see Table 1). In particular, when appropriate task behaviors are known and observable, controllers are able to implement behavior control; when specific, measurable targets are identifiable, the use of outcome control is feasible. The TAF user contact, for example, specified an exact deadline and was able to measure whether the project team met the deadline. The exercise of clan control is also a function of task characteristics, in particular, whether the task presents obstacles for monitoring behaviors or measuring outcomes. For example, at TAF, it was difficult for the IS manager to observe project leader behaviors since the individuals were not colocated. Finally, the implementation of self-control is, in part, a function of the project size. For example, unlike the others, the Megamax and M&M projects were year-long or shorter, with clear discernible boundaries. Moreover, both projects were relatively small in terms of their budgets. Several examples are included in Table 5.

Role Expectations. The expectations or visions that individuals have about their own roles, as well as the roles of others, play a part in determining whether behavior or outcome control will be implemented. As shown in Table 5, the user contact at TAF exercised behavior control because he believed it was essential for him, rather than IS, to control the development process. He believed it was important to have measurable goals: "I want to be able to say that this project is over at a certain time. I want a definite end." Thus, he set up an environment conducive to outcome control by insisting on the specification of exact requirements, deadlines, and budget.

As seen in Table 5, role expectations also help determine the use of clan or self-controls; that is, if organizational or departmental norms exist, as at Roseland, then the norms become the source of expected behaviors. Role expectations may also be defined by an individual manager. For example, paving the way for clan control, the TAF IS manager desired and expected collegial relations to develop among all project participants, even though he found the process of building these relationships difficult. The IS managers at M&M and Megamax, noting they had neither the time nor the desire to oversee individual efforts of the project leaders, clearly expressed their expectations that the project leaders exercise self control. The Megamax user contact felt that he would be the focal point for upper level management and coordinate input from users, but he would leave the details of the project to the IS project leader. Thus, he too expected the project leader to exercise self control.

Project-Related Knowledge and Skills. The choice of behavior, outcome, and self-controls is also affected by the project-related knowledge and skills of the individuals involved. Moreover, the lack of specific project-related knowledge prevents the use of behavior control. For example, at M&M, the user contact's lack of knowledge of IS prevented her from fully utilizing existing mechanisms of behavior control: when she received IS progress reports, she ignored information containing IS jargon or references to IS-related issues. In contrast, as shown in Table 5, the IS manager noted that he was able to scan these reports and spot potential problems very quickly. Both the IS manager and the user attributed this differential use of behavior control to different levels of IS project knowledge. TAF provides another example. Prior to this particular project, TAF had used the Warnier-Orr systems development methodology. With this project, they switched to information engineering (IE) and its associated CASE tools. Because TAF had no prior experience with IE and its tools, the individuals struggled to define effective mechanisms, taking months to articulate appropriate steps and behaviors. In addition, because this project was the first TransAtlantic Foods global project attempted, there were no past experiences to guide them and no knowledge about how global projects ought to be managed.

The use of outcome control is also partially a function of the controller's ability to measure outcomes: the IS manager at Roseland was able to assess outcomes by "walking around" to learn whether the users were satisfied with the system; the user contact at M&M, who had previous experience with voice response units, was able to articulate and subsequently assess whether the project met her functional goals; and the Megamax IS manager, because she had access to QA reports, was able to evaluate whether the completed system adhered to departmental standards. Table 5 contains additional examples.

The ability of the project leader also affected the choice of control mode. The more knowledgeable and skillful the project leader, the more likely the controllers were to implement outcome control or to induce self-control. Representative comments are shown in Table 5. For example, the use of outcome control at Roseland was partially a function of the project leader's abilities: the IS manager, data administrator, and user contact all noted that the project leader is a highly skilled individual. In the two cases of self-control, the controllers repeatedly commented on the capabilities of the project leaders to handle IS projects, to work effectively with the users, and to interact successfully with the larger business community.

Constructing a Portfolio of Control. From the results of this study, a theory is derived that explains the process of constructing a portfolio of control modes. This process is portrayed in Figure 1 and discussed below. As other researchers have observed in their theory-building efforts (e.g., Eisenhardt 1989c), the theory derived through this inductive process is consistent with the data, though it does not completely and perfectly explain the cases.

To the extent that they can, the controllers rely on existing mechanisms to implement modes of formal control. When there are standard, accepted practices for controlling a project, those practices are incorporated into the portfolio of control modes. However, it is not enough that the practices are available and are accepted; controllers must also perceive their appropriateness for the task at hand. As Figure 1 suggests, there are several conditions affecting perceived appropriateness: characteristics of the task, role expectations of the participating parties, and project-related knowledge and skills of the individuals involved. For example, the Roseland user contact found that mechanisms such as walk-throughs and intensive system testing were appropriate devices for him to control the ISD effort because he, unlike many users, has an IS background (project-related knowledge). Similarly, the IS managers at both M&M and Megamax found it appropriate to monitor project status by reviewing detailed, technical progress reports because their extensive project management experience enabled them to quickly scan these reports and spot issues and concerns.

If the controllers perceived that existing procedures or targets on which to base formal control were in some way inappropriate, then the controllers designed or implemented other mechanisms with which to implement formal control. Several such incidents can be identified in the cases. The IS manager at TAF found the mechanisms designed for a local project based on a Warnier-Orr methodology inappropriate for a global project using IE. Two actions resulted. One was the development of new procedures for the IE environment. The second was the institution of conference calls and travel to exercise behavior control; as the IS manager commented: "We did spend quite a lot of time and money traveling around to make sure that people were on the same wavelength." The user at TAF found that IS-designed mechanisms (such as project plans inspired by IS methodologies and IS-written reports on technical issues) were of no use to him because he did not understand the language and nuances of managing systems development projects. Consequently, he conceived of and implemented the user groups. The Roseland IS manager gave two reasons why strict adherence to the pre-existing systems development methodology, complete with phases, reviews, and sign-offs, was an inappropriate control mechanism. First, he expected the project leader to tailor the methodology to a particular project, not to follow it verbatim. Second, he expected the project team to act cooperatively and professionally. Believing that sign-offs serve to justify finger-pointing and blame when problems arise, he feels they should be eliminated; instead of viewing the methodology as a mechanism of formal control, he practiced "management by walking around" to observe individual behaviors and project outcomes. He found this mechanism of control very effective:

If something was going wrong, I would have heard about it. From all levels, I would have gotten calls.... I'd be walking down the hall and the corporate controller would say: "What the hell's going on? Things are being held up."

As Figure 1 suggests, the definition of new mechanisms with which to implement formal modes of control is dependent on task characteristics, role expectations, and project-related knowledge and skills. For example, because his role expectations include the use of formal controls, the TAF user contact defined mechanisms that allowed him to exercise behavior and outcome control (user groups and precise project goals, respectively).

In most cases, formal controls were not used exclusively, but were complemented with either clan or self-control. At Roseland, for example, clan control played a role in ensuring that the project was completed on-time, even though this goal could be, and was, measured. As suggested in Figure 1, controllers' decisions to use clan or self control are driven by characteristics of the task, role expectations, and project-related knowledge and skills. For example, in the two cases of self-control, characteristics of the task (i.e., well-defined, bounded projects) and highly skilled project leaders influenced the controllers' decisions to induce project leader self-control.

Overall, what emerges from this study, as seen in Figure 1, is the following: the construction of a portfolio of control modes is a process that consists of the selection of appropriate mechanisms with which to implement formal control; the definition, if necessary, of new mechanisms with which to implement formal control; and the addition of mechanisms with which to implement informal control.

## Discussion and Implications

This section begins by discussing how control is implemented and why portfolios are structured with particular modes of control. Throughout this discussion implications for practice are highlighted. Extensions to current conceptualizations of control are then offered.

## How Control Is Implemented

Several implications can be drawn about the exercise of control in the ISD context. The first concerns the use of standard operating procedures as mechanisms of control. While it may seem that ISD methodologies and procedures for following standards are means of implementing behavior control (because appropriate behaviors have been identified), the cases presented here suggest these procedures are not always meant to be explicitly followed, but rather are intended to be judiciously tailored and refined as the project progresses. For example, at Roseland, the project leader and user contact viewed the long-term strategic project plan as a template from which to build detailed plans for each project phase. In addition, the IS manager and project leader viewed the systems development methodology as simply a set of guidelines. Thus, these procedures can be viewed by practitioners as templates from which a unique set of procedures can be developed for the particular task at hand.

A second implication concerns the exercise of outcome control, which involves the specification of desired outcomes. Various researchers have suggested that the targets may be interim milestones (Henderson and Lee 1992) or project goals (Kirsch 1996). Both were seen in this research: at M&M, deadlines and budgets were specified for each development phase, suggesting the use of interim milestones; at TAF, project targets were identified. However, evolving outcomes were also observed, in which targets were renegotiated over the course of the project. For example, both the project leader and user at Roseland stated that they met throughout the year to negotiate functionality, budgets, and schedules. Thus, not only might controllers implement outcome control with interim milestones and project goals, but they might also negotiate targets as needed throughout the projects. For the practice of ISD, this suggests that initially defined targets and procedures should not always be viewed as immutable, but as fluid and changeable. The dynamic nature of outcomes and behaviors has ramifications for performance evaluation schemes tied to producing specific outcomes or following particular behaviors as it suggests that prespecified goals may not be feasible; instead, interim milestones and evaluation criteria may be more appropriate.

A third implication concerns the role of IS and user stakeholders. The results indicate that both IS managers and user contacts play a role in controlling ISD projects. The implication for practice is that the control function should be located in both IS and user areas. One approach is to assign co-project leaders from the IS and functional areas, granting both the formal responsibility and authority necessary to manage the project. However, it is more difficult to prescribe how to co-locate control without formal job descriptions. Successfully sharing control in this case would seem to depend on the individual user (e.g., does the user want to control the project and is he willing to do so?) and on the nature of the relationship between IS and users (e.g., do individuals want to share control and are mechanisms in place to do so?).

Fourth, the findings suggest that controllers exercise multiple modes of control, and that each mode of control is implemented with a wide variety of mechanisms, e.g., implementing behavior control via status meetings, reports, and walking around the organization. Moreover, it appears that the same mechanism is often used to implement various modes of control, such as project plans serving as mechanisms of behavior and outcome control. For practitioners, this implies a need to identify relevant stakeholders and their control strategies. In particular, from the project leader's perspective, managing the development of a system seems to include sensing, interpreting, and reacting to the various modes of control implemented by multiple stakeholders: project leaders must recognize that different controllers exercise control in different ways.

## Why Portfolios Are Structured with Particular Modes of Control

As suggested by Figure 1, there is a process through which portfolios of control modes are structured, which involves choosing a mix of informal and formal controls. In general, the findings suggest that if mechanisms exist to exercise formal control, then controllers will attempt to utilize them. If they are not appropriate, controllers are faced with two choices: either forego the use of formal controls or define new mechanisms with which to implement them. In no instance did a controller in this study eliminate formal controls. A possible explanation for this is that the controllers were not comfortable relying solely on informal modes of control as a means to coordinate project tasks—they seemed to require a “comfort zone” of formal modes. For example, the M&M project leader remarked: “Without the formal mechanisms of control, the informal mechanisms of control are haphazard at best.” When asked if he could think of a situation when formal controls would not work, he replied: “Absolutely no. In my mind, no.... I really firmly believe that every project should have a statement of work defining these formal mechanisms of control.” When asked if he could rely on informal control only, the IS manager at TAF responded:

That would be too scary to do something like that. . . . There are many unknown things to allow for, margin of mistakes. If it was a team that had already worked together, or a team that we already know, or an environment that we already know, it would be different. But because everything was new, I think we need to anticipate and this is one way to anticipate problems. . . . These things go together. Let's call it informal team environment is key for the success, but also some formal tools to make sure we are heading in the right direction, that everybody is abiding by the same plan, otherwise we may even be drifting apart because . . . there is no formal way to bring everything to the same line of direction.

While formal controls were viewed as essential for coordinating task activities by monitoring specific behaviors and outcomes, the controllers seemed to view informal controls as a means of fostering relationships to ensure cooperation. At TAF, for example, the project leader noted that while she defined discrete, small tasks (some of which were just four hours in estimated duration) to track project progress, she exerted considerable effort to build trust among project participants. When asked why building relationships and trust were so important if task-level monitoring was possible, she remarked:

This project is very interrelated. Each analyst needs to know what the other analyst is doing. They need to trust each other, to respect each other's work, and to have good communication. Each analyst cannot do his design independent of the other analysts. If every analyst goes his own way, I am left with islands. It is crucial and necessary that the analysts communicate and talk to each other. Trust and respect are imperative.

This finding suggests that managers exercise control to coordinate specific activities, and to manage individual cooperation and contributions. Practitioners must recognize that control is more than simply calculating variances and taking correction actions (PMI Standards Committee 1996, Purba et al. 1995), and should consider how combinations of specific mechanisms of control can be utilized to achieve stakeholder objectives. One approach might be to balance the performance evaluation strategies of formal controls (Eisenhardt 1985) with the people or social strategies of informal control (Ashford and Tsui 1991).

## Extending Conceptualizations of Control

The results of this study have implications for understanding control in a context such as ISD, where the task tends to be ambiguous and involves stakeholders across organizational units. One implication concerns controller-controlled relationships. $^{6}$ In particular, this study points up the active role that users play in the control of ISD projects, even though they may not have formal organizational authority to do so. While ISD is an activity that demands highly specialized technical skills, and thus calls for management by individuals who understand those skills, it is also a means of delivering service to users, and thus requires active management by users. Indeed, in the cases presented here, the users did exercise considerable control. However, except for the Roseland user, they did so without job descriptions detailing such responsibilities, and without control activities formally located within user groups. Theories of control must therefore explain the exercise of control when individuals have or do not have formal organizational authority. Moreover, they must account for the exercise of control across horizontal, peer-to-peer relationships, as well as across superior-subordinate hierarchies. Role theory, which attempts to explain how organizational roles and positions constrain individual behaviors and interactions, may provide one means of exploring these issues:

A central notion in organizational role theory is that people tend to enter interactions with pre-existing expectations about how others categorized in certain ways are likely to behave. Over time these expectations become widely shared, stabilize into predictable patterns, and evolve into a collective structure of behavior.... These stable, shared patterns of expected behavior which are associated with positions become the basic fabric of organizational roles (Galletta and Heckman 1990, p. 171).

Another implication for conceptualizations of control concerns the purpose of control. As noted earlier, extant literature tends to view control as goal-oriented and purposive (Green and Welsh 1988), emphasizing its role in coordinating and monitoring tasks. This view is consistent with the practitioner literature's depiction of control as a cybernetic process. While coordinating task-related activities is obviously important, the results of this study suggest that individuals also exercise control to foster relationships in order to engender cooperation and elicit individual contributions. Thus, conceptualizations of control must take into account the varied reasons for implementing control.

## Limitations

There are several limitations to this study that should be pointed out. First, this study examined one type of project structure, in particular, projects in which an IS specialist assumed the role of project manager, and in which IS professionals did the bulk of the work and users functioned in liaison roles. While this structure is not atypical, it is also not the only way in which projects might be organized. Thus, the results of this study cannot be generalized to other forms of governance, for example, projects in which IS and client specialists function as dual project managers. Second, the study relied on retrospective accounts of ISD projects, which can result in some distortion of the facts. However, this study triangulated across data sources (Jick 1983), increasing confidence in the accuracy of the data. Third, the distinctions among the modes of control are sometimes blurry, resulting in potential difficulties in recognizing and classifying the various control incidents. This happens in part because, ultimately, all control is exercised to change behaviors (Ouchi 1979); thus, conceptual definitions overlap: for example, both self and outcome controls encompass the notion that the controller manages the day-to-day efforts of the project; both behavior and outcome control are based on the idea of formal documented procedures or objectives. The distinctions also become blurry because the same mechanism was often used to effect different modes of control. However, as discussed in the Methodology section, careful steps were taken to elicit and categorize control incidents in order to capture and accurately classify salient incidents. Finally, eliciting examples of informal controls proved to be quite difficult, as they typically are not documented and thus are more intangible than formal controls. Given the careful documentation of the case studies, however, the examples cited in this study seem to accurately reflect the use of informal controls in the ISD context.

## Conclusions

The study makes three substantive contributions to the literature. The first is an articulation of the mechanisms used by stakeholders to implement behavior, outcome, self, and clan controls. This articulation is important because it speaks to the issue of how multiple controllers implement a portfolio of control modes. In addition, it aids future research by providing much needed guidance for operationalizing modes of control. The second contribution is a proposed theory explaining why stakeholders construct portfolios with particular modes and mechanisms of control. Though this theory is an initial formulation of the construction process, it is a meaningful addition to the literature because little empirical work has tried to unravel the dynamics of multiple modes of control. The third contribution is an enlightened understanding of the critical roles played by users to control ISD projects. Few studies have examined how users' actions influence the control of projects, yet the results of this study suggest that their control activities are an integral part of the management of ISD projects.

There are at least four avenues that future work might pursue. First, it is not clear what constitutes a sufficient amount of formal control or what leads to the perception that an existing mechanism of formal control is appropriate. Future research is needed to investigate the construction of controllers' "comfort zones." For example, how much formal control is "enough" before it will be supplemented with informal control, and why might some controllers or situations call for more formal control before a "comfort zone" is established? A second area of needed research is a more in-depth study of the way in which modes of control are combined. Relatively little is known about why control modes are combined and the effects of those combinations. Moreover, there is little understanding about the way in which control is shared across IS and user organizations. Given the apparent importance of the users' control efforts, but their lack of formal responsibility for ISD projects, what are the consequences if users do not, cannot, or will not exercise control? Third, controllees such as ISD project leaders face a complex, dynamic environment, and the multiplicity of controllers and control modes contributes to that complexity. There has been little research examining how these controllees structure their work environment, how they decide which signals to pay attention to, and the effect of possibly conflicting goals or performance criteria on their behaviors and attitudes. Finally, the results are suggestive of changing control modes over the life of a project (e.g., the evolution of clan control at TAF). However, there is little substantive knowledge about how and why control modes change over time. By providing an enriched understanding of the control of systems development projects, the results of this study provide a firm foundation for future research. $^{7}$
