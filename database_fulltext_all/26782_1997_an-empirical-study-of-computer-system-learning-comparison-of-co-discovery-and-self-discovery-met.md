# An Empirical Study of Computer System Learning: Comparison of Co-Discovery and Self-Discovery Methods

Kai H. Lim • Lawrence M. Ward • Izak Benbasat

Department of Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Hawaii, Honolulu, Hawaii 96822
lim@dscience.cba.hawaii.edu

Department of Psychology, University of British Columbia, Vancouver British Columbia, Canada V6T 1Z4 lward@cortex.psych.ubc.ca

Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada V6T 1Z2 izak@unixg.ubc.ca

This paper reports a study that examined two types of exploratory computer learning methods: self-discovery vs. co-discovery, the latter of which involves two users working together to learn a system. An experiment was conducted to compare these two methods and the results were interpreted within a mental model framework. Co-discovery subjects were better than self-discovery subjects at making inferences about the capability and extended functions of the system. Furthermore, while working by themselves after an initial period of learning, they performed better in a similar, though more complex task than the one they encountered at the learning phase. Process tracing analysis showed that self-discovery subjects focused more on surface structures, such as detailed physical actions, for implementing the task. On the other hand, co-discovery groups focused more on relating lower level actions to higher level goals. Therefore, co-discovery subjects had a better understanding of the relationships between the physical actions and goals, and hence formed mental models with higher inference potential than self-discovery subjects.

(Mental Models; Verbal Protocols; Computer System Learning; Co-Discovery Learning; Process Tracing; Inference)

## 1. Introduction

This paper reports a study that examined two types of exploratory computer learning methods: self-discovery vs. co-discovery, the latter of which involves two users working together to learn a system. An experiment was conducted to compare these two methods and the results were interpreted within a mental model framework.

User training/learning has been suggested as one of the key factors in successful information system implementation and subsequent system utilization (Davis and Bostrom 1993). On the other hand, with the increasing availability of easy to use computer software, people seldom learn to use a system through formal training. Instead, they learn through self-discovery or help from peers. Several researchers have suggested that exploration learning is more effective than instruction-based training (Black et al. 1987, Carroll et al. 1985 and 1987, Kamouri et al. 1986). However, very little is known about why and under what conditions exploration learning is better. To design effective training programs, information systems managers and educators need to understand how people learn a system through exploration, and why exploration is an effective learning method.

Within the literature, one stream of research has examined the role of mental models in user learning. This research usually involves comparing a “mental model” group to a “no-model” group. Mental model groups are experimentally created by explicitly teaching a specific conceptual model of the system. The no-model group serves as a control group for comparing outcomes (e.g., Borgman 1986, Kieras and Bovair 1984, Santhanam and Sein 1994, Sein and Bostrom 1989).

Another stream of research suggests that individuals build mental models as they learn to use a system (Borgman 1982, Carroll and Mack 1985, Norman 1986, Carrol et al. 1987). These mental models have been argued to provide most of the user's subsequent system understanding and consequently strongly influence the user's task performance (Wilson and Rutherford 1989). The research described in this paper follows the latter approach. It does not teach the subjects a particular mental model, instead it investigates how different learning methods, co-discovery vs. self-discovery, influence the characteristics of the mental models developed. Moreover, rather than capturing the full mental model developed by a user, this study focuses on one aspect of a mental model that is considered to be one of its major functions, namely enabling the user to infer or predict the behavior of the system of which the mental model is a representation (Johnson-Laird 1981, Norman 1983). This feature, which we call the inference potential of a mental model, is presumed to be influenced by the learning method. In turn, inference potential influences user performance in subsequent tasks associated with the system that was learned.

Our study assigned subjects to one of two types of exploratory computer learning methods: co-discovery and self-discovery. Following the learning stage, we measured the inference potential of the subjects' mental models. We then asked the subjects to perform individually a more complex version of the task they worked on during the learning stage. Structural equation modeling was used to show the relationship between learning methods and inference potential, and inference potential and task performance.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Mental Models

It is commonly accepted that the concept of a mental model was first proposed by Kenneth Craik (1943). While Craik did not use the term “mental model,” he talked about an organism carrying “a small-scale model of external reality and of its own possible actions within its head” (p. 57). Mental models and their significance became prominent in the 1980s through the seminal works of authors such as Johnson-Laird (1981, 1983) and Gentner and Stevens (1983). There is a vast literature on this subject but its full coverage is beyond the scope of this paper. Interested readers are referred to two comprehensive review articles by Wilson and Rutherford (1989) and Staggers and Norcio (1993).

While one finds many attempts to describe what mental models are, very few formal or explicit definitions exist (Rouse and Morris 1986, Wilson and Rutherford 1989). Wilson and Rutherford (1989, p. 619) provide the following definition based on their integration of the different perspectives offered in the literature:

A mental model is a representation formed by a user of a system and/or task, based on previous experience as well as current observation, which provides most (if not all) of their subsequent system understanding and consequently dictates the level of task performance.

There are three components in the above definition of mental models, namely, representation, understanding, and performance. First, mental models represent aspects of tasks or systems formed by a user based on his/her prior experience as well as on current interaction with the system. In fact, almost all definitions of mental models characterize them as some type of representation. Second, mental models provide the basic structure whereby individuals can understand phenomena concerning the system or task being represented. A large number of authors have associated this understanding with the ability to make inferences and predictions about the task or system, e.g., infer how to operate a device (Johnson-Laird 1983, Williams et al. 1983, Kieras and Bovair 1984, Rouse and Morris 1986). The making of these inferences could be due to one's ability to “run” the model to understand the relationship between causes and effects (Genther and Stevens 1983). As a result, users with more developed mental models should perform more efficiently and effectively when interacting with systems or in task performance (Staggers and Norcio 1993).

In this study we investigate the relationship between the inference potential of a mental model and task performance. We will posit, based on the empirical and theoretical evidence presented in the next two sections, that different learning methods will lead to mental models that provide different levels of inference potential to the learner, and that this inference potential will affect performance in a new task. While we will explore some features of the mental models effected by the two learning methods in order to show how these features are related to inference potential, we do not purport or attempt to capture the "full" mental model of the learner. Though mental models, which have been used in psychology and human factors research extensively, have "face validity" and intuitive appeal that make them useful for explaining user behavior when solving problems and interacting with computer systems, a large amount of ambiguity still remains about them. Reviews of the literature (Wilson and Rutherford 1989, Staggers and Norcio 1993) indicate that there are a large number of unresolved issues concerning mental models, including different interpretations of the concept, how mental models differ from other schemes of representing knowledge, how mental models should be represented and if, and how, they can be captured. For these reasons, our approach will be to measure one important, and relatively more concrete, characteristic of a mental model, inference potential, and explore only some aspects of its complexity rather than attempting to capture and describe a fully detailed mental model.

## 2.2. User Training and Mental Models

Studies that examine the role of mental models in user training are often based on the assumption that users develop an unspecified model on the basis of training or information presentation format (Vessey 1991). Often a “mental model” group is compared with a “nomodel” (control) group. Differences in their performance are then used as an evaluation criterion.

For example, In Foss et al.'s (1982) study, students learned the use of a text editor using a file folder metaphor. The result showed that those who were provided with the metaphor learned more in less time. Rumelhart and Norman (1981) also studied the use of metaphors in text editor learning and found that subjects performed better with them.

Halasz and Moran (1983) taught subjects how to use a calculator using two training methods: (1) step-by-step instructions on how to perform standard tasks, or (2) instructions which included a verbal model of how the internal registers, windows, and stacks worked. Subjects' performances on the standard tasks were identical for the two groups. However, the group who learned the verbal model performed better on novel tasks.

Kieras and Bovair (1984) reported three studies investigating the role of mental models in learning to operate a simple control panel device. The mental model group learned the procedures faster, retained them more accurately, executed them faster, simplified inefficient procedures far more often, and inferred the procedures more easily than the rote group. Schlager and Ogden (1986) incorporated a mental model in the training materials for a database query system. The speed of learning improved for those queries that fit the model presented. Errors and difficulty occurred only when queries were different from the method taught.

While these studies provide convincing empirical evidence that incorporation of mental models in user training can improve performance, none of them focused on investigating mental models actually formed by their subjects. Rather, all assumed that their subjects formed the model taught by the experimental manipulation (Wilson and Rutherford 1989). No manipulation check was performed to ascertain that the postulated models were present, nor was an individual's performance compared with the characteristics of his/her mental model.

A more fundamental issue is the nature of active learning. Through their extensive laboratory observations on how people learn to use a word processor, Carroll and Mack (1984) concluded that people learn how to use computer systems actively by exploration. They pointed out that conventional training programs and instruction materials are limited and inadequate in several ways. For example, learners often unintentionally or intentionally sidetrack from the sequential instructions provided, and returning to the original track of learning, as intended by the training program, is often difficult. Instead of following training instructions, they learn by doing (trying things out), thinking (making sense of the system), and knowing (relating to their prior knowledge, e.g., using “metaphors”).

In essence, learners do not follow training instructions. Rather, they learn by actively exploring the system. With the increasing availability of easy to use computer software, this phenomenon is becoming more common. Several researchers (Borgman 1982, Carroll and Mack 1985, Norman 1986, Carroll et al. 1987) make the point that mental models are formed and evolve through a user's interaction with a particular system. Therefore, this study takes an ecologically more valid perspective by looking at learning as an active process and examines two learning methods involving exploration learning, self-discovery and co-discovery, instead of conventional training methods.

## 2.3. Co-Discovery Method

The co-discovery method is part of a comprehensive methodology developed at Bell-Northern Research labs (Kennedy 1989). It involves videotaping two subjects as they cooperate and work together to discover how to use a computer system. Usually, no user guidance or explicit training is provided. The experimenter simply gives the subjects a list of standard tasks to perform.

There are several theoretical perspectives which suggest that co-discovery learning may be beneficial. One perspective, delineated by King (1989), is based on Piaget's (1969) and Vygotsky's (1978) notions of social construction of knowledge. This perspective suggests that when learners interact with their peers, they are constantly faced with ideas and information that are inconsistent with their existing knowledge or beliefs. When they are asked to solve a problem or perform a task as a group, they are forced to reconcile their differences to come up with a unified view or approach. They either have to abandon their own perspective and take the point of view of others, or attempt to explain and convince the others that their perspective is better.

During these interactions, learners actively engage in cognitive restructuring (Bearison 1982). This includes adding details to their existing cognitive structures, correcting their misunderstandings, filling in gaps in their knowledge, and/or reorganizing their current knowledge structures (King 1989). All these potentially lead to improvement/revision of mental models that learners hold.

Several empirical studies have provided support for this perspective (e.g., Cummings 1985, Fletcher 1985, Webb 1982, Webb and Lewis 1986, Webb et al. 1988). For example, Webb (1982) found that students who gave explanations to other students showed higher levels of achievement than those who were not involved in group interaction. Further, students who sought and received help from peers in work groups also showed significant improvements in achievement. Webb attributed this to the fact that these students received more individualized and in-depth explanations than is usually available from teachers.

In a friendly, cooperative co-discovery learning environment, it is natural for the partners to question each other to clarify doubts and ambiguities. For example, if one partner does not understand a particular message on the computer screen, it is very natural for this person to say: "I don't understand what it is telling us," or "I don't know what is going on." Such statements or questions induce the other partner to think more carefully and attempt to come up with an explanation. In other words, the nature of co-discovery forces people to engage in deeper level thinking. This deeper-level thinking may thus enable people to improve/revise a mental model they are currently holding and formulate a more complete mental model of the task.

## 3. Research Model

Figure 1 presents the research model that guided this study. Two computer learning methods were tested: self-discovery and co-discovery. We hypothesized that the different learning methods would lead to mental models with different degrees of inference potential, which would in turn lead to different levels of task performance, measured in terms of time and errors made. In other words, the inference potential of mental models formed during exposure to a particular learning method would mediate the relationship between learning method and subsequent task performance.

The inference potential of a mental model refers to its potential for generating proper inferences about and predictions of a system's behavior. As was discussed in the theory section, the co-discovery learning environment facilitates individuals' engaging in deeper level thinking about the system to be learned. We would expect co-discovery learners to formulate mental models that generate better inferences and predictions than those of self-discovery learners.

Ten open-ended questions (see Appendix B) were used to assess the inference potential of learners' mental models. Learners were asked individually to answer these questions immediately after the learning stage. These questions referred to tasks that were different from the specific ones subjects performed in the learning stage. Each question presented a scenario, and learners were required to mentally "run" or "simulate" their mental models of the system that they had formulated during the learning stage to answer these questions.

The questions shown in Appendix B involved deciding whether or not it was possible to perform a specific task or whether a particular action was a valid one. These questions not only required subjects to decide if a particular task (or action) could be performed, but more importantly "if yes, how; if no, why not?" We would expect that learners who have mental models that provide high inference potential would correctly predict and explain the right outcomes. Therefore, a high score in answering these questions would indicate the existence of a good mental model that would provide high inference potential.

The model in Figure 1 also posits that the degree of inference potential of a mental model affects subsequent task performance. The literature on computer training suggests that learners who are trained to acquire a mental model do better in subsequent task performance (e.g., Kieras and Bovair 1984, Santhanam and Sein 1994, Sein and Bostrom 1989). Presumably, this is because those learners are applying the mental models they have been taught to perform these tasks. Therefore, we predicted that learners who had formulated mental models that provide higher inference potential would require less time and make fewer errors in performing the task.

Finally, a learner's experience with computers is expected to affect both the learner's proficiency in developing a mental model that provides high inference potential and his/her task performance (Norman 1983). A learner who uses computers extensively should possess more general knowledge about computer systems and/or specific knowledge about some other software programs. Although this knowledge may not be directly applicable to the current system, it serves as a starting point for understanding and predicting the behavior of the current system, i.e., this background knowledge helps the learner to formulate a better mental model of the current system. Therefore, a learner's prior experience with computers is expected to have a positive effect on the inference potential of the mental model developed. Furthermore, experienced users would perform a task quicker and make fewer mistakes because they can operate input devices more proficiently and interpret screen output more quickly. Therefore, computer experience was also expected to have a positive impact on task performance. Computer experience was measured using a questionnaire consisting of five scales (see Appendix D). The scores on these five scales were aggregated to form a composite score for statistical analyses.

## 4. Experimental Design

## 4.1. Experimental Setting

The experimental task was adapted from that of Lim et al. (1996; also see Benbasat and Todd 1992). The task involved using a simulated electronic mail system, which was implemented on an Apple Macintosh Ilci computer system, to set up a meeting date with co-worker(s). The interface design, as shown in Figure 2, follows the principles of Direct Manipulation (Shneiderman 1983), in which a user interacts with the computer as if he/she is working with real-world objects. For example, to write a memo, the user simply drags, using a mouse pointing device, a pen icon to a memo pad icon; to mail a memo, the user drags the memo icon to the mailbox icon. To effectively form a mental model of the task, and hence to complete the task, subjects need to make inferences from their real-life understanding of the functions of these objects. For example, a pen is for writing or drawing; a key is for opening. In essence, subjects need to infer the functions of the icons using real world analogies.

The learning task required the subjects to set up a meeting date with a designated co-worker. Instructions to guide the subject appeared at the bottom of the computer screen (see Figure 2). There were 46 separate instructions to be performed. These 46 instructions led a subject to set up a meeting date with one of the coworkers, whose behavior was simulated by the electronic mail system. The instruction told subjects what needed to be done, but not how to do the task. For example, the instruction would state “store the memo,” and subjects had to figure out how to do this task, which was to drag the memo icon to the file folder icon. If an error was made, an error message appeared and the subject had to correct the error by redoing the step.

To encourage subjects to put in their best effort, we promised to pay an additional \$20 to the person (or group) who finished the learning task in the shortest time. In both conditions, subjects were required to verbalize while performing the task and the entire learning session was videotaped. Subjects were told that another \$20 would be awarded to the person who provided the most complete and clear picture of his/her thinking process. The verbal protocols collected were used to provide insights as to the processes involved during the learning stage. We will elaborate on the protocol analyses in a later section. The computer tracked each subject's mouse movements in order to collect data on the time taken and the errors made.

The experimental task, similar to the learning task, required subjects to set up a common meeting date with co-workers. However, whereas the learning task involved one simulated co-worker, the learning task dealt with three. The major difference between the two was that instructions to guide subjects which were provided during the learning task no longer appeared on the screen. All subjects, including co-discovery subjects who learned the system in pairs during the learning session, worked individually. To complete the task the subjects needed not only a full understanding of the learning task concepts, but also the ability to apply them to a more general task, i.e., setting up a meeting with three co-workers rather than a single one.

## 4.3. Experimental Procedures

The experiment was administered to one subject at a time in the case of self-discovery subjects, and teams of two subjects in the case of co-discovery subjects. The session began with an overview of the experiment.

This included telling the subjects that the purpose of the experiment was to study how people learn to use computer systems, and asking the subjects to sign a consent form. Following the overview, subjects were given a short tutorial on basic mouse functions. The actual experiment consisted of the following three stages:

(a) Learning Stage. Two conditions were created for the learning stage: self-discovery vs. co-discovery. In the self-discovery condition, subjects were given the task instruction and asked to explore the system individually. In the co-discovery condition, two subjects were assigned as a team, working together to learn the system. The task instruction for co-discovery groups was the same as that for the self-discovery groups, except that the word "you" was replaced by the phrase "you and your partner" throughout the instructions. The entire session was videotaped.

(b) Inference Test. Immediately after the learning stage, each subject, including those in co-discovery groups, was asked to answer a set of inference questions (see Appendix B) individually.

(c) Experimental Task. Each subject was asked to work alone and to use the electronic mailing system to complete a simulated scheduling task, which involved setting up a common meeting date with three coworkers. Note that this task was a more complex version of the learning task which involved scheduling a meeting with only one person. In contrast, the inference test dealt with issues that were not specifically covered in the learning task. To successfully perform the experimental task, the subject had to utilize all the knowledge that he/she had learned about the system during the learning phase. Again, to encourage the subjects to put in their best effort, we awarded an additional \$20 to the person who finished the task in the shortest time.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Preliminary Analysis

The answers to the inference test were scored by two independent coders. The coders scored the test based on explicit instructions (see Appendix C for an example), which provided details on what specific points or explanations had to be mentioned for an answer to be scored as correct. The coders were told that the assignment of a score should not be based on the yes/no responses given by the subject; rather, scores should be assigned based on whether a good explanation had been provided, as detailed in the instructions. Any disagreements between the coders (r = 0.84) were reconciled before further statistical analysis was performed on the inference test scores. For the inference test, on average, self-discovery subjects scored 4.8 points (out of 10) while co-discovery subjects scored 6.3 points, which represents a 31% improvement in performance over self-discovery subjects.

For the experimental task, upon examining the data for time taken to complete the task, we noticed two outliers, one in the co-discovery and one in the self-discovery condition. Since both data points were more than three standard deviations from their respective means, they were dropped from further analyses. After dropping the outliers, on the average, self-discovery subjects took 3.17 minutes (21%) longer than co-discovery subjects (18.03 vs. 14.87 minutes) in completing the experimental task. Also, self-discovery subjects made more than twice the number of errors when compared to self-discovery subjects (4.9 vs. 2.0 errors). Instead, learning method affected the formulation of mental models that provided high inference potential, which in turn affected task performance.

This is consistent with our argument that inference potential of a mental model should mediate the relationship between learning method and task performance.

Figure 3 indicates that general experience with computer systems did not significantly improve task performance (Beta = 0.242) or inference potential (Beta = 0.268) for this specific task. Finally, the PLS analysis revealed that, of the two task performance measures, time to complete task is loaded substantially higher than the number of errors made (-0.960 vs. -0.641). This indicates that time was a better measure of performance than number of errors made.

Overall, our results show that co-discovery subjects performed better than self-discovery subjects. Recall that although the co-discovery subjects learned together, they took the inference test and performed the experimental task alone. Thus, differences between their performance and that of the self-discovery subjects cannot be attributed to immediate interactive processes, such as the stronger of each pair dominating task performance. It must have been the case that both members of each pair, on average, developed a better mental model of the system during the learning session.

5.2.2. Construct Validity. A methodological issue that needs to be addressed at this stage is the question of how the inference test and subsequent task performance differ in what they measured. As noted before, for performing the experimental task, the subjects could mostly apply directly the commands they learned in the learning stage. However, the inference test questions posed were outside the scope of the actions that the subjects had directly experienced during the learning task; to answer the questions correctly they needed to "run" their mental models and deduce the answers. Furthermore, recall that in scoring the inference test, we looked for specific evidence of the inference potential of subjects' models by asking them to describe the reasoning process that led to their answers.

Although we set forth to develop a questionnaire to measure the inference potential of mental models, it is possible that instead of capturing inference potential, the questionnaire might only have measured some aspects of task performance. This means that the inference test might only be an alternative measure of task performance, much like task completion time and number of errors made. Appendix E provides evidence on why the original model (which has time and errors made included as indicators for the performance construct and inference potential as a separate construct) is better than the alternative model (which has time, errors made, and inference potential included as indicators for the performance construct) in explaining the phenomenon observed.

## 5.3. An Exploratory Look into the Process of Mental Model Formation

Thus far, we have only demonstrated that co-discovery appears to be a better learning method than self-discovery, because co-discovery leads to the formation of mental models that provide higher inference potential. One key question that remains unanswered is: Why does co-discovery lead to mental models with higher inference potential? In this section, we attempt to provide a plausible answer to this question by examining the verbal protocols collected during the learning stage.

Recall that during the learning stage subjects were asked to verbalize their thought processes while they were learning the system. The verbal protocols of ten self-discovery subjects (out of the 20) were randomly selected to compare with those of the 10 co-discovery groups. This was necessary to equalize the number of groups in each cell. The verbal protocols collected were first broken into “chunks” of utterances. Each chunk had to be: (a) complete in meaning, and (b) independent from the previous and subsequent units. Second, these utterances were sorted into eight subcategories, as described in the next section. These subcategories were aggregated into two super categories, shallow vs. deep thinking, in order to explore the learning process and the complexity of the mental model formed. It seems reasonable to expect that deeper-level thinking would lead to the formation of more complex mental models.

5.3.1. Protocol Coding Scheme. Research in human problem solving shows that people process information through several stages or processes (Duncker 1945, Polya 1957, Newell and Simon 1972). Norman (1986) developed a model of such stages in the context of human-computer interaction. His model consists of four major stages. They are: translating goal, formulating strategy/action, interpreting output, and evaluating against goal. The translating goal stage is irrelevant to our experiment, because in the learning task subjects were provided on-screen instructions that were stated as goals. Therefore, only the last three of Norman's four stages were used as a basis for our protocol coding scheme. These became subcategories of the coding scheme.

Two further subcategories were used based on Norman (1986), namely describing physical action and reading screen output, as the stages subsequent to formulating strategy and prior to interpreting output (Norman, 1986). Two additional subcategories, reasoning/inferring and seeking understanding/clarifying, were added to describe general cognitive processes that could be associated with any one of the stages in Norman's model. Last, "other mental processes" was used as the category in which were placed protocol statements that did not fit into any other subcategories. These eight became the labels of the subcategories into which utterances made during the learning phase were placed. This coding scheme was developed prior to examining the video transcripts. The details of the scheme are described in Appendix A.

We anticipated subjects would make some irrelevant comments during the task. For example, “the mouse is sticking” or “the color looks terrible.” A comment of this nature is unrelated to the actual task, and was coded as “irrelevant” and not considered further.

Finally, we divided the eight subcategories, excluding “other mental processes,” into two supercategories, the surface-level processes and the deeper-level processes. These characterizations were developed to be concordant with the levels of processing theory of memory (Craik and Lockhart 1972). That theory postulates that the memorability of a stimulus or information (i.e., the ability to learn and store information as long-term knowledge) is determined by the depth to which it is processed. From the point of view of this theory, each of our eight categories can be seen to instantiate either surface level or deeper level processing. Surface level processing involves rote repetition of information without attempting to process the information more deeply and results in relatively poor recall; deeper level processing involves relating information more deeply to long-term knowledge and results in superior recall. Categories revealing surface level processing include reading screen output and describing physical action. Categories revealing deeper level processing include reasoning/inferring, understanding/clarifying, formulating strategy/action, interpreting output, and evaluating against goal. Since deeper level processing leads to better recall, and more instances of deeper level utterances implies more deeper level processing, we expected that protocols yielding more deeper level utterances would be indicative of the formulation of better mental models with greater inference potential.

Incidentally, the surface vs. deeper level process categorization is congruent with Norman's (1986) physical vs. mental level categorization of the stages. Norman characterizes execution (which corresponds to describing physical action in our coding scheme) as "the first physical action" (p. 39) in his sequence of steps and perception (which corresponds to reading screen output in our scheme) as "the evaluatory equivalent of execution" (p. 41). Therefore, the categorization scheme we have utilized in this paper is supported by both Craik and Lockhart and Norman's work.

5.3.2. Findings of Protocol Analyses. A total of 3,319 utterance units were obtained from the verbal protocols and analyzed. Two independent coders assigned each of these units into one of the subcategories described in Section 5.1.1 (including "irrelevant"). The initial interrater agreement for the subcategories was 0.72 (kappa = 0.65).

It was noticed that most of the disagreements between the two coders were in the categories "Evaluating Against Goal" and "Interpreting Screen Output." We believe that this was an artifact of the system used in the experiment. In a direct manipulation interface environment, screen outputs (feedback to users' actions) were designed in such a way that they provided users with direct comparisons against their goals. Therefore, utterances about this screen output were often difficult to code unambiguously as either "Evaluating Against Goal" or "Interpreting Screen Output." In view of this, we decided to combine these two categories into one. After this combination, the new inter-rater agreement for the subcategories was 0.80 (kappa = 0.73). The interrater agreement for the super categories (surface vs. deeper level processes) was 0.91 (kappa = 0.80).

Table 3 shows the detailed results of the protocol analysis for deeper-level and surface-level utterances (excluding “irrelevant”). Since the codings of the two coders were very similar at this level, except for “Evaluating Against Goal” and “Interpreting Screen Output” as explained above, the two coders’ averages are reported.

Two independent-sample t-tests were used to examine the relationship between learning method and type of utterance. First, co-discovery subjects generated more utterances overall (mean = 217) than self-discovery subjects did (mean = 102) and the difference between them was statistically significant $[t(28) = 4.80, p < 0.001]$ . Second, we computed the ratio of the number of deeper-level utterances to the total number of utterances (deeper level + surface-level utterances) for each subject. The average “depth” ratio for co-discovery subjects (0.60) was larger than that for self-discovery subjects (0.33), and the difference between the two ratios was statistically significant $[t(28) = 5.17, p < 0.01]$ . Thus, co-discovery subjects generated both more utterances and a larger proportion of deeper level utterances than did self-discovery subjects.

Overall, the protocol analyses suggest that the co-discovery subjects engaged in relatively more deeper level thinking about the task and hence probably formulated more complex mental models than the self-discovery subjects. However, this argument is tempered by the possibility that the phenomenon we observed might have been an artifact of the protocol elicitation method used. While co-discovery encourages sharing of ideas and challenging of unstated assumptions, and thereby forces subjects to formalize and state their thoughts, self-discovery subjects lack such motivation. It is possible that self-discovery subjects might have engaged in the same amount of deeper level thinking while they were engaged in the learning task, but that they were unable to, or failed to, report these deeper level thoughts. Therefore, we can only suggest that co-discovery may lead to more complex mental models than self-discovery. Further, we suggest that this higher complexity may lead to the formulation of mental models that provide higher inference potential, and ultimately lead to the improvement in subsequent task performance. We propose this line of reasoning as a possible explanation of the differences in the two learning methods that underlay the performance differences we observed.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

We found that co-discovery learning is more effective than self-discovery for learning a new computer system. Co-discovery subjects formulated mental models that provided greater inference potential than did those of self-discovery subjects. Further, subjects who developed mental models with greater inference potential performed better on a novel task. The latter finding is consistent with the literature on the role of mental models in new task performance.

Our exploratory analyses on the role of learning method on the development of mental models revealed several interesting issues. We found a dramatic difference between co-discovery and self-discovery subjects in terms of the way they thought about the learning task. While self-discovery subjects engaged relatively more in surface-level processes, such as describing the physical actions required to achieve the task, co-discovery subjects engaged more in linking the lower level actions to higher level goals.

Action identification theory (Vallacher and Wegner 1987) provides an interpretation for this phenomenon.

The theory posits that people think about actions at different levels of identification under different situations. While lower level task identities relate to the specific action, higher level task identities indicate more abstract understanding of the action, indicating why it is done. The theory states that during the learning stage, people tend to identify a task at a lower level, i.e., focusing on the details of implementing the physical components of the task. As they become more familiar with the task, they shift their identification to focusing on higher level goals.

Action identification theory provides a post-hoc explanation of the performance of the self-discovery subjects: during learning they focused on lower level details, such as describing their actions. However, the co-discovery subjects acted rather differently from what the theory predicts. Nevertheless, their behavior can also be characterized in terms of the theory. As argued previously, co-discovery learning creates an environment whereby partners have a natural tendency to question each other and to clarify doubts and ambiguities. This forces the partners to focus on the why and how of the task. In other words, the nature of co-discovery learning led the subjects to identify the task at a higher level of abstraction.

This shift in focus seemed to have a profound impact on the formulation, and ultimately, the quality of their mental models. For example, to understand the why and how of a task, subjects had to infer or draw analogies from their real-world experiences to the functions represented by the icons. As a result of this inference, the linkage or relationship between a particular goal and its corresponding physical action became clear. This resulted in the creation of a better mental model. The differential performances of the two groups on the inference test appeared to arise from the resulting differences in the inference potentials of their mental models.

Overall, our findings are consistent with those of Okada and Simon (forthcoming). $^{3}$ Okada and Simon examined the performance of pairs and singles subjects in a scientific discovery task. First, consistent with our findings, they found that the pairs performed better than the singles. Second, through the verbal protocols collected, they also found that their pairs engaged more in “explanatory activities” (entertained hypotheses, considered alternative ideas, and justified explanations), which roughly correspond to what we call “deeper level” processes.

Most importantly, they also attributed the observed performance advantage of pairs over selfs to the nature of co-discovery process, which forces the pairs to engage in explanatory activities (deeper level thinking). They argued that “explanatory activities help people to connect pieces of information into an organized theory. Having others as monitors encourages people to participate in such (explanatory) activities and helps them to construct their theories more actively and more deeply” (p. 33). Okada and Simon (forthcoming) not only support our findings but also extend our study. Their study indicates that our findings may be generalizable to domains other than learning to use a computer system.

Although our results indicate that there are clear advantages in co-discovery learning over self-discovery learning, one obvious question to ask is whether there is any cost associated with such benefits. One such cost could be learning time: does co-discovery take longer than self-discovery learning?

On one hand, since co-discovery subjects engaged in more discussion, explanation, and clarification, it would seem that they should require more time to complete the learning session. On the other hand, co-discovery subjects engaged in more deeper-level thinking. Consequently, they should be faster in solving impasses and figuring out the correct ways of performing the task. These two opposing tendencies may cancel each other's effects. In fact, our data on the total time taken to complete the learning task support this argument. On average, it took self-discovery subjects 14 minutes to complete the learning task, whereas co-discovery subjects took 13 minutes. Thus, it appears that to obtain the benefits of co-discovery one does not incur any significant additional learning time.

One contribution of this study is its investigation of some of the underlying processes involved in learning a computer system and forming mental models. Although other studies have shown that explicit learning of mental models leads to better performance, this causal relationship has often been equivocal, because of the lack of characterization of the mental models acquired. This study at least partially overcomes this problem by providing a way of "looking into the black box." In a larger perspective, this paper also responds to the criticism that research in Information Systems has generally ignored the explanatory process that transforms input to outputs (Benbasat and Nault 1990).

Another contribution of this study is to apply the cooperative or collaborative learning theories developed in education to computer system learning. We also extend the work on collaborative learning by investigating how the mental model concept in general, and inference potential in particular, mediates the relationship between co-discovery learning and performance.

One of the limitations of this study is the assumption made regarding subjects' verbal protocols. We assumed that subjects were able to express verbally most of their functionally relevant thoughts. It is possible that subjects might have processed some of their thoughts unconsciously, and thus were not able to verbalize those thoughts. This might especially be true in the case of self-discovery. The co-discovery subjects might have faced this problem, albeit to a lesser extent because the nature of the co-discovery environment required them to bring such thoughts into consciousness more often, thus making them verbalizable. This limitation precludes us from incorporating the verbal protocols collected (we used them as indicators of the complexity of mental models) into our research model and explicitly testing the relationships of that construct with other constructs.

Nevertheless, as discussed above, the score on the inference test and the time taken, and the number of errors made, in subsequent task performance show that co-discovery subjects out-performed self-discovery subjects. This suggests that there are clear differences in the mental models on which the performances were based that cannot be attributed solely to differences in subjects' abilities to verbalize their thoughts.

Another limitation of this study is related to the measurement of the inference potential. The measure that was developed for this study needs to go through additional testing to further evaluate its construct validity, including discriminant validity. At this stage, we cannot be certain that inference potential is a totally different construct than performance, which is formed by time and error measures. However, based on the qualitative and quantitative evidence supporting discriminant validity (see Appendix E), we believe that the test used is a good measure of inference potential and is different than the performance measure.

Finally, in future research it would be interesting to find out what kinds of learner pairs leads to better outcomes. For example, would learners of equal ability learn better together? When a slower learner is paired with a quicker one, are there benefits for both? Regardless of the answers to these questions, however, it is clear that on average the co-discovery method is superior to self-discovery for better learning and better task performance. $^{4}$

## Appendix A. The Protocol Coding Scheme

(1) Category 1: Reasoning/Inferring (R/I)

A protocol statement was coded as "R/I" when the subject made an inference, such as using an analogy based on his/her experiences, or explaining a logical and sensible plan/statement: e.g., 1: Pencil is for writing something or drawing picture; e.g., 2: It can't be this icon because we tried that earlier and it was for mailing.

## (2) Category 2: Seeking Understanding/Clarifying (U/C) $^{4}$

Protocol statements of this type occur when subjects attempt to seek understanding what needs to be done, or to clarify ambiguous points: e.g., 1: Am I (are we) going to be out of town on the 14th or the 15th?; e.g., 2: I forget what is this icon for.

(3) Category 3: Formulating Strategy/Action (F)

$^{5}$ There are two types of statements that are understanding/clarification in nature. One is related to the system (e.g., “How to mail a memo?”) while the other is related to the task itself (e.g., “How do we know if we are finished?”). We see both as integrated parts of the whole learning process. Therefore, no attempt was made to separate the two.

A protocol statement was coded as "F" when the subject was developing a strategy or action plan to accomplish the goal: e.g., 1: I (we) need to put the key to something else ... may be combining the key and the folder; e.g., 2: I think we better make a copy of the memo before we mail it.

(4) Category 4: Describing Physical Action (D)

“D” was coded for a protocol statement when the subject was simply stating his/her physical movement: e.g., l: I am clicking on the button next to “Helen”; e.g., 2: I am dragging the pen icon to the memo pad icon.

(5) Category 5: Reading Screen Output (RS)

A protocol statement was code as "RS" when the subject was simply reading out the instruction or the output messages appeared on the computer screen: e.g., 1: Retrieve incoming memo; 'e.g., 2: This is not a required action.

(6) Category 6: Interpreting Output (I)

Interpreting output occurred when a subject's statement was related to understanding the feedback appeared on the computer screen as a result of subject's action e.g., 1: Um, What does that mean?; e.g., 2: O.K., I see.

(7) Category 7: Evaluating Against Goal (E)

Goal evaluation occurs when a subject tried to compare the screen output against his/her intention to see if the goal had been achieved: e.g., 1: Good, I got it!; e.g., 2: Oh, this is not what I want.

(8) Category 8: Other Mental Processes (TO)

A protocol statement was coded as "TO" if it did not fit into any one of the categories described above, and the subject was clearly engaging in thinking about the task, at the time when the statement was made. This type of statement was usually expressed as a general comment, rather than a specific response towards a output appeared on the computer screen (see Category 6): e.g., 1: I am totally confused; e.g., 2: I think I had made a mistake along the line.

Appendix E. Quantitative and Qualitative Evidence for the Discriminant Validity of the Inference Test

This appendix discusses what the inference potential test represents—whether it is another indicator of outcome performance, as time taken and errors made, or as we have argued in the paper, a proxy for the inference potential of mental models.

Qualitatively, the variables for performance and inference potential were designed to measure two different concepts. The former was measured by asking the subjects to perform a more complex version of the same task they had learned, one they had direct experience with. The latter was measured by asking subjects to infer the answers to task related questions within the same domain, but ones with which they had no direct experience with during the learning task; furthermore, the inference score was based on the explanations the subjects provided on how they came up with the answer. Finally, temporally, the inference potential test was administered before the subjects performed the task which assessed their performance in terms of time and number of errors made.

Quantitatively, there are several ways of examining discriminant validity. One is to calculate the average variance shared between the constructs and their measures (indicators), i.e., the average variance extracted (AVE) and compare it to the variance shared between the constructs themselves (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The calculation of AVE involves taking the average of the loadings squared for all indicators that measure the construct of interest. $^{6}$ The loadings are interpreted as the extent to which the measure (indicator) is correlated with the overall component score (rather than with other measures).

Using this method, we derived two correlation-AVE matrices, one with inference potential score, time, and errors as the three indicators of the performance construct, and the second one with inference potential score forming a separate construct (as suggested in our original research model) and time and errors as indicators of performance. These two matrices are shown in Tables E1 and E2 below. Note that the diagonal elements are the square roots of AVE. Off-diagonal elements are the correlation among constructs.
