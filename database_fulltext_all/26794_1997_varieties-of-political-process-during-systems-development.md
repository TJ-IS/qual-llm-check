# Varieties of Political Process During Systems Development

John A.A. Sillince • Samar Mouakket

Management School, Royal Holloway, University of London, Egham, Surrey, TW20 0EX, United Kingdom
j.sillince@rhbnc.ac.uk

Management School, University of Sheffield, 9 Mappin Street, Sheffield S1 4DT, United Kingdom s.mouakket@sheffield.ac.uk

This study uses a longitudinal research design with multiple data collection methods on a systems development project. Five theoretical perspectives about power have been used to evaluate the case: zero sum, processual, organizational, structurally constrained, and social shaping/social construction. Our working assumption is that power is multidimensional and therefore that any attempt to understand systems development must simultaneously use several complementary perspectives. Studies of information systems development have in the past often been based on a view of power as zero sum (with winners and losers from systems development) and of power as based on information. We will attempt to show that expansion from this narrow definition of power has much to offer information systems research. The paper will attempt to identify what unique and essential insights about the relationship between power and systems development are surfaced by each perspective.

(Politics; Requirements Determinationy; Software Development Lifecycle; Political Roles of Systems Analysts; Organisational Factors; Power; Zero Sum Power; Processual Power; Organizational Power; Structurally Constrained Power; Socially Shaped Power; Project Trajectory; Organizational Design)

## 1. Introduction

We have taken a multitheory approach (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991, Pettigrew 1985), seeking to show that power is a complex phenomenon that can be understood through several complementary perspectives. We have chosen five such perspectives and will demonstrate that each one contributes new value added in terms of explanation of the case study we describe. First, these perspectives will be briefly introduced. Then a case study will be described chronologically. Finally, the theoretical perspectives will be applied to the case study material, and generalisations will be derived from this discussion.

Power has been defined as the ability to get someone to do something against their will (Dahl 1957). Functionalist theory (Selznick 1949, Miller and Rice 1967) was never able to account satisfactorily for this, because the theory assumed a normative consensus existed within organizations (Parsons 1951), enabling rational choices to be made, and has been criticised in these terms as unrealistic (Pfeffer 1981, Mintzberg 1983). The theory required the existence of an equilibrium position, yet this did not permit satisfactory explanation of conflict. Our five perspectives are put forward as advances over this earlier functionalist approach, recognizing that each in turn has its own limitations, which are compensated for in the perspective which follows. The sequence of presentation represents a progression in sophistication in thinking about power, and does not correspond to chronological development. Nonetheless, each perspective has a unique contribution to make, and this can be formalized into a working assumption.

ASSUMPTION 1. Power is multidimensional and therefore any attempt to understand the operation of power in systems development must simultaneously use several complementary perspectives.

## Zero Sum Power

The “zero sum” view of power (Clegg 1989, Fincham 1992) regards political conflict as a fight between individuals over an object, and the ability to overcome resistance (Weber 1947, Krackhardt 1990, Pfeffer 1992) and is often defined in terms of the control or ownership of resources (Pfeffer 1981). The object may be a reward, a punishment, a right, an identity, or expertise (French and Raven 1959). The object is a source-locus of power, which is then possessed by the individual. Because a locus exists, an absence of observable conflict means an absence of politics according to this view (Kling 1980, Kling and Iacono 1984).

The political “game” is therefore zero sum—when one party wins the other loses—since only one party can acquire the object. This raises the question of whether workers gain or lose when information systems are developed (a literature reviewed by Kling 1980) and what are the effects of information systems in increasing centralisation and decentralisation (Robey 1981, Mosco 1989, Hill 1981). Newman and Noble (1990) concluded that the conflict between users and analysts that they observed resulted in the users winning. Markus (1983) showed that a financial information system enabled the tightening of control in a formerly highly divisionalised company, despite attempts by the divisions to sabotage the system. Shostak (1987) showed that a new air traffic controllers’ system reduced the bargaining power of operational staff versus management. A favourite source-locus has been information, with the associated roles of store-keeper, guard and specialist-expert (Hickson 1971). Gottlieb and Borodin (1973), for example, argued that information is a source of power and related this to the concentration of information-gatherers at the top of an organization. Since politicians have to survive by serving competing interest groups, the practical implications were that systems analysts need to be better at politics (Markus and Pfeffer 1983) and that more user involvement was needed (Boland 1979, Ives and Olson 1984, Robey and Farrow 1982, Friedman and Cornford 1989).

Although it represents a major advance over previous functionalist theory, the zero sum view of power has several problems. It cannot explain why people accept reduced power when IT is introduced. This is partly because there is no distinction drawn between positive (socially responsible and altruistic) and negative (individual power hunger) motives for the conflict (McClelland 1970). For example, Coombs (1992) found that some doctors accepted new procedures and systems that reduced their power, when they also were given new information which enabled them to reason more fully about their patients' treatment. Willcocks and Mason (1987) showed that a new medical information system reduced the power of doctors versus other medical staff and so was resisted by doctors until it was discovered that the potential threat was nullified by continuing medical advances which only doctors could keep up with. The implication of the Coombs and the Wilcocks and Mason studies is that real cases rarely fit into a zero sum framework since participants' interests not only compete but also complement each other. It fails to account for the nature or the cause of systems analysts' rationalist ideologies and consequent inability to cope with political processes in systems development (Boland and Day 1989, Curtis et al. 1988, Hirschheim and Klein 1989). The equating of information and power treats information as unidirectional and deterministic in its effects and is, according to Boland (1986), a narrowly North American perspective. However, we shall see that centralisation-decentralisation was an issue in the case described below.

## Processual Power

The “processual” view regards power as a social relationship (Orlikowski 1989, Pettigrew 1973) rather than as an attribute of a person. The relationship involves an exchange of a reward, a punishment, a right, an identity, or expertise (French and Raven 1959). The relationship is created when demands are generated and when support is mobilised for these demands. The exchange is a resource-dependency one where the “dependence of Actor A upon Actor B is (1) directly proportional to A’s motivational investment in goals mediated by B and (2) inversely proportional to the availability of these goals to A outside the A-B relations” (Emerson 1962, p. 32). The value of the resources is a function of their irreplaceability and their centrality to the goals of those concerned. The relationship may also arise when two individuals have different types of power, such as when an individual in a higher organizational position is dependent on expertise possessed by someone in a lower organizational position (Pettigrew 1973). Important concepts are therefore the relations of reciprocity, dependency, and interdependency, the patterns of commitment toward these relationships that are developed over a long period of time, and the explanation of different types of power in terms of organizational structural variables such as complexity and heterogeneity (Pettigrew 1973, Clegg 1981, Mintzberg 1983, Ranson et al. 1980).

Because there is the possibility of exchange between holders of different types of power, the perspective takes a positive sum view of political “games”—both parties may gain from the political process when a mutually better solution is found (Fincham 1992). In the context of information systems, it is the technology which provides the negotiation focus. The systems analyst is a facilitator for both the negotiation process and in order to render the technology understandable (Klein and Hirschheim 1987). The force (Pfeffer 1992) or influence exerted by power comes partly from the roles that people play. At the simplest level, resources are exchanged between a giver and a receiver. These two roles enable a processual account of power to be constructed. However, since many aspects of role are organizationally determined, the processual perspective provides only a simplistic account of roles.

Because the processual perspective opens up the question of roles and role-playing competence and performance, other matters of subjectivity, such as responsibility, become relevant too. Again, many of these matters of responsibility are influenced by organizational rather than personal relationships, and so the processual perspective alone is unable to account for them.

The shortcoming of the processual view is, according to Knights and Murray (1994), that the parties are all seen as equals irrespective of their organizational position or their role with regard to organizational procedures and goals. Also, it fails to examine the inequalities which underlie the many interests of systems users and their effect on user resistance and mistrust.

## Organizational Power

The "organizational" model treats computerized systems as forms of social organization with important information processing, social and institutional properties. An example is the "web" model (Kling 1987, Kling 1991), which is a loosely structured model that can accommodate an organizational process approach (Allison 1971, Cyert and March 1963) or a negotiated order approach (Strauss 1963) to power relations within organizations, in which established distributions of power are confirmed or disturbed by the design of the technology (Pettigrew 1973, Markus 1981, Markus and Pfeffer 1983). An important dimension in Kling's (1987) case study of Printco was the dependence of the computer system on matters of social organization such as the need for cooperation from shopfloor workers (subject to pressure from superiors) for data entry of inventory records, and the pressuring by production planners of stockroom and purchasing managers to tighten their practices of recording and re-ordering. The practical implication for the role of the systems analyst is that he is required to mediate between senior management who want the system to work and other organization members who have no interest in the system working. The analyst should also attempt to move potentially zero sum outcomes toward becoming positive sum outcomes using conflict resolution (Robey et al 1993), with users learning how to exert influence towards conflict resolution as the development process proceeds (Robey and Farrow 1982).

In the organizational perspective, roles are different according to organizational or professional position, reputation, and resources of the role holder. Coombs (1992) investigated doctors' reactions to Resource Management information systems in the United Kingdom National Health Service. He found that despite the entrenched opposed views of the participants, "some doctors were, in a sense, 'converted' to the point of view that measures of relative efficiency were useful, and even compatible with received notions of clinical freedom.... Other doctors found themselves in situations where the features of the IT system they were offered included not only 'cost accounting' type functions, but also the ability to generate interesting clinical data about treatment patterns and results which could be useful for research and for medical audit procedures" (Coombs 1992, p. 11). Doctors had roles of resource allocators (a role reluctantly taken, and so one with a negative attitude towards information) and of researchers (with a positive attitude to information). Thus organizational roles contain much of the richness that helps to explain behaviour, and they raise the important practical question of what happens when particular roles are played badly or ignored. Dagwell and Weber (1983) showed that systems analysts do not fully recognise the nature of their role as a change agent on the various coordinating mechanisms in organisations such as reward systems, power structure, and leadership styles. We shall show that in the case study the process was influenced by how the different political roles were played, and how well they were played.

One of the problems in the relationship between user and analyst is often the absence of responsibility. Walsham (1993, p. 75–95) in his study of a Processing Company, explored the various organizational causes of implementation failure. In particular, he identified a responsibility vacuum between senior management and systems staff. Responsibility is one of at least three aspects of an analyst's work (Boland and Day 1989). The other important tasks are getting users to cooperate using persuasion, reassurance and openness, and making moral decisions (Boland and Day 1989). In the latter study an ethnomethodological approach was used to represent the world through a system designer's eyes—a method advocated by others (Dagwell and Weber 1983, Kling 1977). Boland and Day's important study raised questions about other systems development tasks, and about the implications for the education of systems analysts. Responsibility also crops up in another study—Franz and Robey (1984) discovered two important political patterns of analyst behaviour focussed on accountability: escaping responsibility and scapegoating. Inability to take or accept responsibility has implications for organizational control yet may be strongly influenced by academic training. Most of the analysts interviewed by Newman and Rosenberg (1985) had been programmers with no training in systems analysis, which accounted for the lack of knowledge in organisational politics. Little effort had been devoted to educating the systems analyst to being sensitive to organisational variables.

There is a large literature emphasising the importance of the organizational context in the adequate and satisfactory functioning of information systems. Examples of relevant aspects of organizational context are organization culture (Grote and Baitsch 1991, Davies 1991), the history of support and opposition among dominant and subordinate groups (Pettigrew 1973), and turbulence (Huber and Daft 1991). Even at the theoretical level, information system effects cannot be detached from questions of organizational structure, performance and adaptability (Huber 1990, Malone and Rockart 1993). Organizational information systems support communication processes, yet these are processes in which enduring and complex institutions are involved (Yates and Orlikowski 1992) and in which information systems are often deemed inappropriate (Daft et al 1987). The current case highlights the effect of organizational goals on systems development.

One clear theme emerging in the study of organizational power and systems development is the management of conflict. In a study of Sky Building Society, Walsham (1993, p. 96–117) examined the difficult and necessary balance between participation and direction. It was clear that participation can encourage conflict. The dynamics of this relationship between user and analyst and the balance between participation and direction and how it affects conflict has been studied by Newman and Robey (1992) who showed that there are key encounters during the systems analysis process that significantly affect the quality of outcome. Pettigrew (1973) conducted a longitudinal study of the effect of organisational politics on the development of installing a computer system in an organisation. Part of his study focused on the analyst's role and the impact of organisational decision making on his job. Organisational politics played an important role in the decisions taken during the four years studied in the developing and installing of the computer system. Curtis et al. (1988) studied requirements and design in 17 large software projects. They found that part of the problem of shifting requirements was caused by power shifts within the user organization. The ability to understand requirements and control their variability depended on how these power shifts were communicated. Orlikowski (1989) investigated organizational power shifts during systems development and implementation of CASE tools. She found project teams displayed marked patterns of division of labour, patterns of dependency, coalition formation among teams, acts of coercion and rebellion, displays of territoriality, resentment and stereotyping, as well as the enactment of subcultures, all behaviours recognizable as organizational in character.

Often systems analysts give insufficient time to users, and this problem can be compounded when systems analysts give less time to lower ranking users. Mouakket et al. (1994) studied the whole of a systems development project. The project was allocated 7.5 man months by the software company supplying the new bespoke system, and yet contact time between analyst and users was only 24 hours, a clear indicator of the low importance ascribed to users. More contact time was allocated to higher ranking than to lower ranking users, and the final system reflect the priorities of higher rather than lower ranking users, even though lower ranking users were going to be using the system much more often than higher ranking users. Despite the relatively short time given by the systems analyst to contact with users, there was considerable user influence on the requirements determination process, in business direction requirements, system outputs, and in design. This influence extended throughout systems development until after system delivery, when over half of all requirements were requested by users.

However, the approach treats organizations in isolation from their structurally constrained or cultural context. And the approach neglects specifying the conditions under which political behaviour is more or less likely to occur within systems development projects (Pfeffer 1981), and assumes a simple rational view of political interests (Newman and Noble 1990). Also, it forces a rigid separation between technology and social relationships, despite bringing to light (as in Kling 1987) the fact that computer networks are also social networks, because it has no theory explaining the similarities or differences between social and technical aspects of systems or the boundary between the two.

## Structurally Constrained Power

The “structurally constrained” perspective on power has concerned itself with how the goals and assumptions of the powerful or the powerless operate at a super-organizational level. It is important to look at this level for some of the structural influences on behaviour within organizations, including class perceptions and ideologies (Willmott 1987). The wider society shapes how organizations are and how they see themselves—as hierarchies or as markets for example (Fincham 1992). Studies of the labour process of capitalist production (Braverman 1974) have had considerable influence on the study of the structuration relationship between technology and organizations. The focus of such work has been the influence of culture and ideology on managers' thinking when using technology for deskilling, intensifying and controlling labour (a literature surveyed by Orlikowski 1988). CASE tools have also been seen as an organizational means of routinizing and hence controlling software development (Orlikowski 1989), although the attempt at control caused a reaction of resistance by the company's programmers. Foucault's (1980) work recorded the ways in which human subjects of power have been objectified as particular kinds of subjects through science, stigma, and self-identity definitions. Others have focused on the reverse side of the power equation and studied worker resistance. For example, Burawoy (1979) has shown how factory workers can behave in certain ways which recreate conditions of exploitation. Because of the ability of the perspective to investigate subjectivity, it is difficult to attach any zero sum label—"exploitation" is zero sum only when it is accepted as exploitation, and the perspective is sufficiently rich and ambiguous to suggest that this recognition does not always occur.

Although Braverman (1974) claimed that capitalist labour processes lead to fragmented and atomized subjects, he was unable to provide convincing detailed evidence of subjective perceptions of workplaces in order to show how the individualizing tendencies come about. Moreover, the claim that management's sole aim in information systems development is control is contradicted by Child (1985). Also, the perspective tends to portray management as omniscient, again contrary to evidence (Knights and Wilmott 1990, Murray 1989). And the deskilling hypothesis has been challenged (Burawoy 1979, Littler 1982). Moreover, several studies have shown that information technology does not have highly predictable effects on power distribution. For example, a study of rail freight found that whereas supervisors lost control within marshalling yards, they regained some control by being integrated into the management control system of area freight operations (Dawson and McLoughlin 1986). Another criticism has been that the approach reifies the distinction between the social (the wielder of power and resistance) and the technical (the means by which they are wielded). It is this criticism that the final perspective attempts to answer.

## Socially Shaped Power

The “social shaping/social construction” perspective attempts to look inside information technology, for example at technological “trajectories”—the life cycle of technical innovations (Law and Callon 1992)—and the implications on labour processes (Webster 1989), and to determine why information technologies emerge and are adopted at particular times. The starting point is the observation that people’s perception of information technology is socially constructed (Berger and Luckman 1971) and that information technology is as social as it is technical.

The purpose of social and political activity in each case is to integrate the problematic into the unproblematic and taken for granted (Berger and Luckmann 1971, Callon 1986, Latour 1987). This is an example of how human flexibility and plasticity, which enables an attitude of world-openness, must be transformed by a social and political process into an attitude of world-closedness (Berger and Luckman 1971) in order for the social environment to be rendered predictable. For example, in actor-network theory (Callon 1986; Latour, 1987) there are a number of steps in this process: definition of problems, arousal of social interests, enrolment of agencies and actors, and mobilization of network members. "Intermediaries" such as literary inscriptions (texts), technical artifacts (computers), skills (knowledge) and money are used by network members to define each other. Highly "stable" networks require "convergence" (agreement about the nature of a problem to which some potential technology could serve as a solution) and "irreversibility" ("translation" across some socially recognised boundary marking acceptance or commitment). Information systems development is the outcome of a process of translation which creates irreversibility by redefining problems in terms of existing solutions, and which reconfigures the boundaries and relationships between the social (people) and the technical (intermediaries).

An example is the use of data modeling in the U.K. NHS (Bloomfield 1992), which, far from being what it claims to be (a technical process), is in fact a means of implicit and explicit social control about the legitimate view(s) of organizations and information systems (Bloomfield 1992). The interesting issue becomes, why was something considered to be technical, and how was this view established and made to stick?

Figure 1 shows each of the five perspectives as increasingly sophisticated treatments of power, in which more sophisticated perspectives (toward the right side) sometimes subsume and sometimes replace less sophisticated perspectives. For example, the socially shaped perspective normally addresses structural constraints, and considers relations between groups (the organizational perspective) and between individuals (the processual perspective), but the emphasis on socially constructed meanings is antithetical to the zero sum perspective of power and information as “unproblematic” objects with understood meanings. However, on the less sophisticated side of Figure 1, the zero sum perspective says nothing about the other four perspectives, and the processual perspective says nothing about the effect of hierarchy, control, or the taken-for-granted.

## 1. Method of Research

The case study investigated inhouse systems analysis within the Housing Department of a large U.K. university. A longitudinal research design (Pettigrew 1990) was used. Multiple methods of data collection were employed including interviews with the systems analysts and the users, observation, and examination of existing documents. As far as possible, what people said in interviews and what the researcher observed, were compared with documents, for validation purposes. Formal interviews and demonstrations which took place between the systems analysts and the different users involved were attended. These people, including the systems analysts, were also interviewed individually and frequently. The following data collection methods were used.

## 3. The Case Study

## The Decision to Computerise

In November 1991 a large U.K. university approved the funding for development by in-house systems analysts of a computerised system for the allocation of student accommodation by the Housing Department. The coordinator in the Central Housing Services Office hoped for a fully computerised system of all the sections within the Central Housing Services Office. However, because of the lack of funding, and since the MAC Initiative (a large multi-university information system begun in 1988) was to be implemented in 1995, the system was to cover only the Central Housing Services Office and part of the sections which were the flats and the halls of residence only.

The two analysts started their investigation by interviewing several employees from the Central Housing Services Office. However, their main contact was the coordinator (central-user), since she was the main person in the Central Housing Services Office to be responsible for receiving student applications from Central Admissions and afterwards passing them on to the different flats and halls of residence. In this way the analysts played the role of securing their power base (Figure 2)—always maintaining a special relationship with senior management via the coordinator. Also, the two analysts interviewed the end-users in charge of the university flats section. This was simply because their office was located in the same building as the Central Housing Services Office.

Following interviews with the central-users in November 1991, and a few meetings with end-users (flats), one of the analysts (the analyst-designer) presented a proposal report (Proposal A) on a centralised accommodation system to the users whom she had interviewed. Proposal A was for all allocation decisions to be made by the Central Housing Services Office. The halls of residence were not to be involved in allocation decisions at all but were to use the new system for other purposes. The analyst listed what she saw as eight user requirements. In addition, the report stated that a system could be developed within eight months, (i.e., by about June 1992).

In February 1992 the two analysts started interviewing the six secretary-administrators (end-users) in charge of the halls of residence and their business manager. The analysts were surprised to realise that the seven end-users had not been informed of the development of the computerised system. The secretary-administrators of the halls of residence were very suspicious of the whole investigation, and the analysts felt that these secretary-administrators did not really want the new system. Each secretary-administrator was used to operating independently, which meant each one had her own manual method and forms for processing student applications. The two analysts felt that the secretary-administrators did not want the new system because they were not computer literate, and because they feared centralisation and standardisation by the Central Housing Services Office.

The business manager responsible for the halls of residence, at the request of the analysts, had to use the coercive power (Figure 2) of his position in order to get the halls secretary administrators to cooperate further with the analysts. End-users represented a potentially obstructive element (Figure 2). They could theoretically have refused to participate—it was they who would largely be using the system when it was finished. After meeting the secretary-administrators several times and getting more knowledge of the system and the flow of information, the two analysts realised that the system could not be developed without taking account of their requirements. They therefore adopted a consensus-seeking approach (Figure 2). That meant additional new requirements which the central-user had not requested.

The analysts were not prepared for this change since they had in mind that the system would be designed mainly for the Central Housing Office, but acknowledged the fact that the system needed to be expanded to include some requirements of the end-users (the secretary-administrators of the halls) if they wanted the system to be practical and to be accepted by the end-users. Therefore they produced another proposal (Proposal B) for the boundaries of a computerised system and an implementation timetable (delivery in parts by October 1993). Proposal B was for halls of residence to decide on accepting applications, but with the Central Housing Services Office having the power to question these decisions. The new proposal was presented to all the users and it listed several requirements which partly reflected what these end-users (halls of residence) wanted. The new proposal covered a more comprehensive system than the one first anticipated by the analyst. This was because the analyst felt the need to give something to the end-users (halls of residence) so that they would accept and feel responsible for the new system. The list of user requirements jumped from 8 requirements to 18 requirements due to new ones submitted by end-users. Some requests for user requirements were refused by the analysts, who thus exercised technical control (Figure 2) over the addition of new requirements—difficulty of implementation was not something that users could question.

After producing this proposal and presenting it to the users, the analysts asked for feedback. Due to the lack of constant communication between the analysts and the end-users, which was clear from the beginning of systems development, formal and written reports were the means of contact. The analysts received letters from several end-users stating their concern over some issues and asking for additional requirements. As a result the analysts agreed to provide the end-users with one extra requirement. Thus the systems analysts, exerting either financial control or technical control (Figure 2), had the final say on which requirements should be included in the system. They justified their response by reference to difficulty of implementation, human resources, lack of time, and lack of money.

## End-User Ignorance and Need for Reassurance

During the design phase, one analyst (the analyst-designer) was responsible for designing the screens and building the data base, while the other did the programming (the analyst-programmer). The analyst-designer started building the data base in March 1992. Later on, another two programmers were assigned to help in developing the project. Several demonstrations were held by the two analysts for the central-users including the coordinator. In the beginning, the demonstrations were mainly about the screen design. The central-user (coordinator) seemed satisfied but requested four new requirements (which the analyst agreed to add) during these early demonstrations.

The demonstrations for central-users were usually held twice a month, and there were eight of these during the design phase. Many queries from either the analysts or the central-user were answered by telephone calls. The only contact between the analysts and the end-users during the design phase was by means of two demonstrations. The first of these, attended also by the central user, was held to show the halls secretary-administrators the screen layout. During the demonstrations they were shown some screens, but according to the analysts the end-users were not able to give any feedback because they did not have any idea of what was going on. End-users complained to the analysts that everything was new to them and that they were not able to understand what the computer could do. The secretary-administrators (i.e., the end-users) were apprehensive and confused about the purpose of computerisation. As a result, some of the end-users produced a list of problems concerning the development of the new system to their business manager. The problems showed the confusion and lack of understanding which the hall Secretary-administrators felt regarding the new system. As a result, five training sessions were held for them to familiarise them with the new system. The analysts were therefore forced into playing an educational role (Figure 2). The greater familiarity with the system, and the extra attention being given them, provided symbolic reassurance to the secretary-administrators that their interests were being considered by senior management and analysts. The analysts were therefore involved in a process of symbolic reassurance (Figure 2)—small amounts of time were used to increase the confidence and sense of involvement of Secretary-administrators. No training sessions were needed for the central-user because she was more involved in system development. In March 1993, another demonstration for the end-users (halls of residence) was held, by which time they seemed satisfied.

End-users felt very confused during the first demonstrations. They did not know how to use the screen and enter data in it. In addition, during all the demonstrations the central-users and the end-users were not given any manuals or documents to guide them through the demonstrations. The analysts argued that in the proposal there was no mention of the analyst's responsibility for writing any manuals. The analysts therefore used persuasion to convince the users that this was so. Furthermore, they believed that writing such a manual was a time-consuming process, and that users should write such manuals. And in reality, this was what happened. In June 1993, the coordinator in the central housing office produced simple user documentation which aimed at guiding the users when inputting data.

## Sponsorship Invoked Again

In November 1992, the business manager, who was responsible for the six halls of residence in the university, noticed that nobody had thought about the need for a method to link the computers between the halls and the university network. The main priority had initially been given to the development of a system (Proposal A) for the Central Housing Services Office, which already had a computer link with the university network, and this had been changed to the more end-user oriented Proposal B without really considering how end-users were going to use the system. As a result, the business manager of the halls sent a letter to the senior assistant registrar in the university raising this matter. This prompted the academic secretary-administrator's office to play the role of sponsor (Figure 2) again and to act quickly to solve this problem, since the whole project could have collapsed if this matter had been ignored. In January 1993, agreement was reached to fund a link between the Central Housing Services Office and the halls of residence to solve this problem.

The need for more funding therefore involved some element of persuasion (Figure 2). The analysts felt that it was not their problem, whereas the Central Housing Office was mainly interested in the processing of application forms rather than day-to-day administration of halls of residence. The analysts had to ensure that the sponsor realised his responsibility and understood the crucial role as sponsor in funding the link.

Delivery—A Sudden Burst of New Requirements
The system was delivered to the housing services in parts starting from February 1993, and most of its parts were delivered to the users by July 1993. That was the time when the system went live. However, once the system went live 28 major new requirements were requested from the analysts that had not been requested during the analysis or the design phase. This shows the growing sense of possibility experienced by end-users, analogous to that experienced by a pressure group (Figure 2). Some of these requirements were not developed because the analysts believed that they were too complicated and required changing the data base itself, and that it was too late to change these (again their role of technical control and financial control; Figure 2).

The end-users (flats) asked for various kinds of reports that were not discussed during the analysis and the design phase. They asked for 11 different kinds of reports, such as a list of disabled students and an alphabetical property list of all properties, which the analysts agreed to study to decide whether they would be able to provide for the users or not. Some simple reports could be produced using a commercial package, while others needed special programming. Users now felt that they knew more about what they wanted and were asking for more and more requirements.

## Dissatisfaction After Delivery

The final delivery of the system was in the beginning of October 1993 as scheduled. Two requirements, which were included in the proposal following the analysis phase, had not been done. Following the final delivery of phase 1 of the system, the two analysts held feedback sessions with the different users involved in the project. During these meetings all the users asked for different changes in the system, but the analysts, exerting financial and technical control (Figure 2), were not able to make any commitments to do these requirements. The main reasons for this were the lack of financial resources, time resources, and human resources available. End-users expressed their satisfaction with the system in some areas, but they were facing some difficulties in other parts. They still believed that the system had been designed for the Central Housing Services Office essentially, and that what they received had been insufficient for them. The two analysts held a meeting with the central-user. The central-user expressed satisfaction with the system, but requested several additional requirements, some of them new. The central-user felt that adding these requirements would improve the system and make it more efficient to use.

A final meeting was held between the end-users (halls of residence) and the analysts, where the halls secretary-administrators listed the problems they were facing in the system they received. Since they knew that the system was not developed for them, and because they felt that the analysts had decided not to allow any more requirements from end-users to be added, they did not ask for any new requirements.

## Sponsor Asked to Fund Second Stage

The analysts were not able to promise the users anything regarding the problems which they pointed out during the feedback sessions. But they did ask the users to provide a prioritized list of properties of requirements which they thought would improve the system, and then presented it, with an estimated timetable, to the academic registrar section in the university to obtain approval for time to be allocated to do them. It was the role of the university's senior management, as sponsors of the project, to legitimate such a crucial step (Figure 2). The Housing Department got the approval to do so in January 1994, and the two analysts were appointed to complete a second phase of system development. The second phase was delivered to the users in parts and the final part was to be delivered in May 1994 which means that system development (both Stages 1 and 2 of Proposal B) took about two and a half years compared to the initial estimate of eight months for Proposal A.

After getting approval to continue working on the housing services system, the two analysts met the end-users informally a few times, since the analysts had knowledge of the problems which the users were facing with the system. Most of the queries were discussed on the telephone. Most of the requirements requested were for the end-users. After the system was completed the analysts met the users in June 1994 formally two times only in feedback sessions. In general, although the central-users wanted a more comprehensive system in the beginning, end-users felt satisfied with the system after the changes were made. End-users had therefore “accepted” the revised version of the system. The new system was able to save the department time, allowed for the standardisation of procedures, and strengthened centralisation within the department.

## 4. Analysis of Zero Sum Power

Zero sum power is relevant to the current case in the struggle between analysts and end-users to influence requirements (see Figure 3). Analysts believed that it was they who should control requirements determination, and this led them to use a particular language, which was technical and obscure to end-users. This contrasted with the end-users' language, which was concrete and short term, and likely to become out of date very quickly, and hence of limited interest to the analysts. The analysts' belief in their mandate to control requirements led to a patronising attitude to end-users and a characterisation of them as low in technical sophistication.

The conflict extended into the user organization, which contained not only end-users, but also senior management and its own users (called central-users here). These three types of individual had different interests. End-users wanted to avoid centralisation, standardisation and control. Central-users wanted to speed up the housing allocation process and increase efficiency. Senior management wanted to honour modularisation targets and avoid student homelessness at the beginning of the academic year. Moreover, within the analysts' organization there was pressure from its senior management to cut corners and carry out the analysis quickly.

Under the old manual system, when a student's application was passed to one of the six halls of residence, that hall had the right to reject the application without giving the central housing office any reason. If the application was rejected, it was passed on to the next hall of residence, meaning a very long wait for some applicants. The old system therefore allowed halls of residence considerable discretion over deciding applications for accommodation. For example, a student who played drums could be refused on the grounds that he/she might disturb neighbours, or a student in poor health could be refused on the grounds that the accommodation was unsuitable. Also, halls had the discretion to accept students even though they did not meet the university's requirements—for example, home based postgraduates (not normally offered accommodation) who had special difficulties. The new computerised system would link the six halls with the Central Housing Services Office, and in effect would enable the Central Housing Services Office to demand a reason why each hall had made a particular decision. Before computerisation, halls often held onto applications in case they had empty places later, causing applicants to wait longer than necessary. After computerisation applications remained 'in' the Central Housing Services Office. For all these reasons there was therefore a very real centralisation issue—the Central Housing Services Office wanted to use computerisation to centralise whereas end-users wanted things to remain as they were. This was therefore a zero sum situation.

Each secretary-administrator in each hall of residence did things differently prior to computerisation. This caused frustration and annoyance in the central housing office. Although the Central Housing Services Office represented the top level of the organizational hierarchy, in reality it had little power and control over the processing of students' accommodation applications. Each year there were many empty rooms because the Halls were rejecting suitable applicants. The Central Housing Services Office therefore wished, against the wishes of the halls, to standardise procedures.

Both standardisation and centralisation were prime senior management motives for change. But standardisation and centralisation were not what end-users wanted, and so they had to be disguised. The stated reason for computerisation (mentioned in the report by senior management) was not standardisation or centralisation. The report presented the reasons for computerisation as being technical (the large number of applications needing processing quickly) and not as being what they really were: political and contentious. The exercise in misleading information emphasises the zero sum nature of the situation.

Zero sum power has its own view of system success. Because the possibility of consensus is excluded, success goes to the agent whose interests are served by the system. It succeeds according to the agent whose interests are served, and is pronounced a failure with reference to the agent whose interests are not served.

Thus there is no real criterion of success offered by this perspective, rather the question “Success for whom?” Moreover, the existence of dysfunctional behaviour such as deceit and political misinformation are viewed as integral parts of the conflict resolution process, despite the fact that these elements may be harmful, and possibly even destructive to the project. However, the most difficult problem of the perspective’s view of system success is the fact that it does not offer an overall criterion of success—there is no normative respite from realist, partisan interest.

## 5. Analysis of Processual Power

There were 10 main users with whom the analysts had contact throughout the project. There was a personal assistant and secretary/administrator (flats), both end-users responsible for allocating students to self-catering properties. There were hall secretaries/administrators (one for each of the six halls of residence), end-users responsible for allocating students to halls. These end-users eventually would be using the system daily (and could withdraw their cooperation) and so represented potential obstructors. Another end-user was the business manager of all the day-to-day activities of halls of residence, who informed the analysts that Proposal B required a new link between the Central Housing Services Office and the halls. The coordinator in the Central Housing Services Office (termed a central-user rather than an end-user) was responsible for providing information regarding students applying for accommodation to any section and providing senior management with reports and was invaluable to the analysts in representing end-user and central-user views. Both the business manager and the coordinator, therefore, played a liaison role.

Although most end-users were unhappy with computerisation, their opinions did vary. The two analysts (an analyst-designer with formal programming but no systems analysis training, and an analyst/programmer with little experience of systems analysis) were therefore engaged in being political theorists. They sought to create a realistic model of the competing groups. This model included the fact that the end-users were divided about the issues of centralisation and standardisation (although most viewed it unfavourably), thus weakening them as a political force. Also the end-users were not computer literate. The conflicts within the end-user group were also present within the central-user group with senior management and coordinator in favour of computerisation and lower grade staff against. Thus the analysts' model was two dimensional. One dimension (applicable to end and central-users) was a conflict between those who accepted and those who opposed change. The other dimension involved centralisation and standardisation and was between Central Housing Services Office (central-users) and the peripheral offices such as halls of residence (end-users).

Figure 4 shows the case according to the processual power perspective. Sources of influence are not only agents themselves (analysts, senior management, etc), but also particular values (see Figure 4).

This richer view of the relevance of role for power attribution enables the technical status of the different participants to be explored more fully. For example, Pettigrew (1973) has characterised the status of the systems analyst as stemming from being able to present herself as an expert (to senior management or to end-users), acting as a technical gatekeeper controlling information (to suppliers of technology), and understanding the way things operate within a particular organization. This status underlies the technical and financial control exerted by analysts. Analysts are fundamentally change agents (Knight 1967) and this responsibility must be recognised and supported by their development organizations. A case supplied by Newman and Rosenberg (1985, p. 402) illustrates this: "The employees flatly refused to use the new stock control system so the analyst went in one weekend, took the stock control cards and burned them. The employees then had to use the new system." However, the crucial role during requirements determination is the elicitor role (learning from the user). Its complementary role, played by the analyst, is the educator role (learning from the analyst). Newman and Noble (1990) thought learning of equal importance to political conflict, and included it as one of their explanatory models.

Senior management, on the other hand, wish to limit the discretion of workers when this is against their interests without eradicating the beneficial effect of dis-

They wish to control operations in terms of balancing the demands of strategic and day-to-day considerations. Moreover, analysts see senior management of the user organization as their power base, whose interests must be considered paramount, who act as sponsor of systems projects, and who legitimate the various "signing off" rituals analysts seek to sustain. It must be admitted also that analysts have the special role of outsider (with management consultants, investment analysts, and others), enabling senior management not to be seen by insiders as doing its own “dirty work” and thus directing unpopularity onto external change agents.

One crucial role that end-users played was that of a pressure group. This occurred a number of times in a number of different ways. The process is similar to the pattern of demands made during the development of a pressure group—initial complaints and seeking of reassurance, concern about political representation, and the accepting of requests from end-users for new requirements encouraging yet more requests (Edelman 1960).

(1) Complaints about late delivery. Although the analysts delivered the system around July 1993 according to the timetable written in Proposal B, the users began to express their concern regarding the delivery time of the different phases of the system. Thus the users, as a pressure group, became more adept at switching their attention and the nature of their demands from one time to another. The users did not anticipate the workload they had and the effect that the computerised system had on their work.

(2) Complaints about misrepresentation by central-users. The users who had more contact with the systems developers during the analysis and the design phases were central-users. That is why end-users felt that there had been a communication problem between them and the analysts. They believed that the analysts had not spent enough time with them to know more about their needs and wishes during the analysis phase. This was corroborated by the business manager of the halls of residence. Moreover, the analysts privately admitted the truth of this complaint to the researcher. This explains why most of the requirements requested by the users during system delivery were from end-users rather than from central-users.

(3) Growing demands for new requirements. Users changed their requirements several times throughout the systems development process. They changed them during the analysis phase, during the design phase, and even during system delivery. Furthermore, user requirements seemed to take a more detailed form as the system developed and as users' knowledge of the system increased. This was particularly so during system delivery. As time went on there was a declining likelihood of the requirements changes being accepted, a rising level of detail, and no fall-off in the number of extra requirements requested.

End-users are necessary to the development and use of information systems. During the development of systems they may effectively resist by playing an obstructor role. Obstruction and coercion are complementary opposites. A more constructive role is that of pressure group—attempting to modify or add to requirements. A number of analyst roles connect with the pressure group role. The analyst responds by sometimes making tangible promises and sometimes giving symbolic reassurance. Another important role is the liaison between end-users, senior management, and analysts. The role is shown by the solid arrow in Figure 5, which shows the way in which the project was driven along a nondeterministic trajectory (Law and Callon 1992) by the liaison and other roles and by the driving effect of project management.

Processual power has its own view of system success, which is how well the system development process enables all stakeholder views to be articulated, exchanged, and evaluated. This is similar to DeBrabander and Thiers' (1984) position. They argue that system success depends on effective communication, and that threats and promises detract from a successful outcome. Similarly, Robey et al (1993) showed data that supported their claim that the ability to resolve conflicts between all parties underlies system success. The difficulty is how to establish what is the overall success in terms of some externally recognisable form of validation. This difficulty arises because all views are treated as equal, with no criterion for according priority to different agents. The perspective enables a complex research agenda to operate, evaluating the relationship between system success and how well roles are played. Lack of success is hypothesised to be a result of poor role playing or of gaps in the provision of roles. However, this is a rather narrow managerialist view based on the assumption that problems arise from poor management of process gains and losses (Frame 1987) rather than from conflicts of interest. Moreover while acknowledging power and a diversity of interests, “it leaves the inequalities underlying those interests unexamined” (Knights and Murray 1994, p. 16).

## 6. Analysis of Organizational Power

Bearing in mind organizations' most significant decisions involve change, the organizational power perspective is set within a context of strategic organizational change (Lewin 1951, Lippitt 1958). Power in this perspective is viewed in positive sum terms—the ability to mobilise resources to accomplish some end (McClelland 1975, Kanter 1979, Roberts 1986). Roles and symbolic actions (Johnson 1990) and different types of technological sensitivity to change (Bancroft 1992) identify the specific stage of organizational change reached. At each stage different roles will be important. Figure 6 shows stages of organizational change (Johnson 1990) and roles in the change process (Harrington 1991) as they apply to the case study data.

During the first stage of breaking down resistance to change or “unfreezing” (Lewin 1951), the crucial roles were taken by the coordinator in the central housing office (who provided the operational rationale for change), senior management (who expressed dissatisfaction at non attainment of macro level goals such as elimination of year start homelessness and speeding up the process), and the end-users’ business manager (who coerced end-users into meeting the analysts).

During the “information building” stage all actors contributed, although it was crucial that end-users shared process “ownership” with central-users. Senior management took an overall view, comparing progress with comparable computerisation progress elsewhere.

The experimentation (design) stage was dominated by the analysts, and the refreezing stage was dominated by the coordinator and senior management. However, these roles are taken from the “business process reengineering” literature (Harrington 1991), and the technical and linear are unfortunately emphasised at the expense of the symbolic and the nonlinear. Figure 6 also shows the symbolic acts (Johnson 1990) and

rituals at each stage.

Organizational power implies its own definition of systems success, which is linked to change in the organization. This is because the organizational implications of information systems are usually so extensive and important that it is natural to view systems development in the wider context of organizational change and organizational design. There is a similarity between models of organizational change, models of technological change within organizations, and various models of system development (see Figure 7).

The success of an information system (according to organizational power) depends on whether it helps or hinders organizational change and adaptation. Thus the stage of unfreezing requires an information system which is responsive to external information sources and multiple perspectives, the stage of information building requires an information system which is able to help group argumentation by encouraging conflicting views, modeling, theorizing and forecasting, the stage of experimentation requires design support and statistical sampling and testing, and the stage of refreezing requires group convergence techniques by means of propaganda and the development of shared views. Conflict and consensus are not, therefore, irreconcilable opposites. Rather they are appropriate at different stages in the change process. The perspective is therefore able to support an overall view of system success without falling into the normative trap of supporting a consensus view of the organization. However, it is instrumentalist—an information system has value only to the extent that it furthers some organizational goals, rather than having meaning or value outside that focus (Bloomfield and Coombs 1992). And it leaves unsolved the problem of defining what are the “optimal” organizational goals and adaptations. The perspective also leaves undecided the question of organizational power. Without any answer to this question, it is difficult to say whether organizational change has occurred deliberately or accidentally.

There is some evidence that the new primary care information systems used in the drive to fundholding in U.K. primary care in the early 1990s were not a logistical success. But more importantly, they forced doctors to go through the symbolic actions of fundholding during which they enacted upon themselves the discipline of keeping to a budget (Sillince and Frost 1994).

However, the centralisation implications are ambiguous. Computers may lead to centralised data processing and to decentralisation of responsibilities. Although senior management is enabled to maintain access to data about distant activities, evidence suggests that information systems also enable local staff to become empowered to organize themselves more effectively and autonomously (Child 1972, Linde 1988) particularly in the case of sophisticated systems (Bruns and Waterhouse 1975). Moreover the effects depend on circumstances. Blau et al (1976) found that greater centralisation results when computers are used in functional organizations and that greater decentralisation results when computers are used in divisionalised organizations.

Information systems have to be used by higher hierarchical levels in order to avoid the systematic distortion inherent in subordinates' verbal reporting (Galbraith 1976). This is because subordinates are unable to smooth data inputs for formal reports. This may in theory upwardly shift power (Mechanic 1962). However, during crises, and perhaps always in practice, Bariff and Galbraith (1983) found that managers tend to prefer verbal communication. On the other hand, in theory information systems equalize information-based power as everyone has access to the same data. In theory, for example, performance evaluation can be put on a more objective basis so that subordinates can expect fairness in their superior evaluations of them (Blau 1963). However, in practice Grant (1988) found that in his observed performance monitoring system, computerized monitors required supervisors to make more qualitative judgements. These studies leave contradictory impressions. What the structurally constrained power perspective adds is the ability to resolve this contradiction by saying that information is useless without the ability to use it to control (a subordinate, an agenda, a deadline, etc.).

The centralisation-decentralisation construct may not be the most relevant dimension. Three other dimensions will be mentioned here. Firstly, information flows have an effect. Kling and Iacono (1984), in a study of an MRP system, found that middle managers were more tightly controlled by the computerized system, but not by higher levels of management. Rather, control was exercised by information flows themselves which needed to be accurate and timely. Secondly, there is the content of information. Information belongs to certain categories, and reinforces a particular view of the world (Bloomfield and Coombs 1992). Thirdly, power is manifest within the forms of discourse enabled by information systems—“this view of power is not concerned with more or less naked threats, coercion and control . . . It is concerned with discourses and associated bodies of knowledge (disciplines) which constitute the dominant view . . .” (Bloomfield and Coombs 1992, p. 471).

Information systems development brings questions of task analysis, job design, and organizational development out into the open. The parties involved in those activities are forced to confront each other either directly or indirectly. The former discretion of the halls of residence secretary-administrators (the end-users) had to be jettisoned in the interests of organizational effectiveness. This was something the end-users had to come to terms with. Information systems within the housing office in practice made visible end-users' inefficiency. But also there was the dominant view, the conventional wisdom, that this was what information systems were expected to do.

Equally, senior management had to acknowledge its initial responsibility for reducing the formerly intolerable waiting time. Such former waiting times for accommodation (months in some cases) have been rendered archaic by new technology and social changes to the extent that the “customer” (in this case the student) was not prepared to be passive. In the face of such social change and raised expectations any senior manager refusing to modernise was risking his/her own future. The senior management of the university was faced with increasing numbers of students to house within a diminishing number of weeks at the start of the academic year. In practice there was no other choice for them but to go for computerisation. Also, the acceptance of the importance of information systems in the society outside the organization, because it represented the dominant view, made visible senior management’s slowness in adopting modern methods.

The analysts were faced by senior management with a simple goal (computerisation) but with an ambivalent subgoal (how much discretion to give to end-users), and by end-users who operated inefficiently and yet who gave considerable effort and goodwill to their jobs. Those end-users resisted computerisation and yet wanted to gain knowledge of it. In order to give “ownership” to end-users, analysts changed the system to take account of end-users’ needs, while at the same time maintaining the move towards centralisation required by the central-user (the housing coordinator). Despite the analysts’ delicacy in attempting to balance interests and seeking consensus, they did have a dominant view of how the system should be developed. This view assumed that a consensus existed on organizational goals, that hierarchy was of paramount importance (Dagwell and Weber 1983) and that the most important voice was that of senior management or at least its appointed liaison person, the Coordinator in the Central Housing Services Office.

The perspective is antinormative in the sense that it emphasises the structural constraints (such as the dominant view) acting on agents, rather than encouraging them to use their freedom in a particular direction. One attempt to reduce the perspective's determinism is structuration theory (Giddens 1984). The insight of relevance here is "duality of structure," that institutional or technological constraints and agents' actions are mutually reinforcing so that social structure is not just an object but something which becomes real through agents' actions. Orlikowski and Robey (1991) have applied structuration theory to information technology, to show how it is a product, a medium, and a condition of human action. An example they give is the way in which clients' problems are interpreted in a way that is allowed by the limited and selective knowledge embedded in CASE tools. The example demonstrates the potential of structuration theory for multiple, nested levels of explanation and for reflexivity of explanation.

Success in terms of the structurally constrained power perspective depends on our reaction to the changed patterns of visibility created by information systems. Increased visibility of the influence target by means of surveillance will increase reward and coercive power (French and Raven 1959) and thus lead to an increase in compliance (Kelman 1958). Increased visibility of the influence source will increase referent power (French and Raven 1959) and thus when the influence source is salient will lead to an increase in identification (Kelman 1958). If we favour greater efficiency, then increased visibility of work behaviour of end-users is a benefit, so long as any disbenefits (reduced goodwill and job commitment) are not also high. This potential for benefit is not unidirectional: there may also be (although this study did not discover any) occasions when increased visibility of superiors' work behaviour is beneficial to their subordinates.

## 8. Analysis of Socially Shaped Power

Because this perspective assumes that any social and political process is problematic (and wishes to identify what it is that “stabilizes” the situation to bring about “convergence” of interpretations to make it unproblematic) it challenges assumptions about the artificial boundaries created by people, for example between social and technical matters (Bloomfield 1992), and between the “inside” of the process (which the analysts did not want others to see) and the “outside” of the process (which the analysts intended only others to see). Even the distinction between systems analysts and programmers is in reality turned into a significant boundary. Newman and Rosenberg (1985) have shown that a socially constructed boundary exists between programmers (with dirty work and low status) and systems analysts (with clean work and high status). The communication across this boundary is by means of the program specification, by which the systems analyst gives details of the problem to be solved by the programmer. Categories on either side of such a boundary are used in the sense of “finite provinces of meaning” (Schutz 1967). The perspective focusses on the moments of “translation” when important changes take place, and when power is exercised, in particular the definition of problems, the arousal of social interests, and the enrolment and mobilisation of agencies and actors (Callon 1986, Latour 1987). Successful “convergence” occurs when networks of social interests are coherent (Law and Callon 1992).

Being human must be continually externalised in social activity. This is routinised by means of institutionalisation, in which rules are developed such as “actions of type X are performed by persons of type Y.” Only part of the self is involved in social and political action—the objectivisation of this part is a role. Institutions need to be continuously brought to life by roles. Because world-closedness is brought about by institutionalisation and roles, if many events in a society have shared meanings, the scope of institutionalisation will be wide because its rules can be widely understood. But in a modern society institutions become segmented and relevant only to a few. In such a situation there is a need for an “integration myth” (Berger and Luckmann 1971) to enable segmented social groups to believe they share something when in reality they do not. One integration myth in the case study was that end-users would benefit from the new system as much as would everybody else because they partly “owned” the system. Another integration myth concerned the analysts. In order for such a specialised professional group to maintain its legitimacy it required an integration myth of what systems analysis was and how it was special from other activities, so that it could only be performed by professionals (Hirschheim and Newman 1991). The myth keeps insiders in and outsiders out (Schutz 1973, p. 75–85), using academic credentials and sanctions.

In the current study, there were four major changes or translations. Each one concerned money and involved a key document and timetable. Firstly there was the initial decision by the housing office management to go ahead with computerisation, contained in a proposal document. This was part of and supported a larger decision by the university's senior management to computerise the entire administration system (which in its turn was part of a central government sponsored multiuniversity system). It was supported by the housing office coordinator, who had identified clear problems of lack of centralisation and standardisation. Thus the first translation involved a nesting of supporting pressure (and problems)—central government (value for money)—university management (not being left out of central government scheme)—housing management (speed of processing applications for accommodation)—coordinator (centralisation and standardisation).

The second major translation was the shift from Proposal A to Proposal B intended to give end-users a sense of ownership. Such proposals were marked by documents (lists of requirements), manpower needs and implementation timetables. The third major change or translation was the decision to create a link between the central housing office and the Halls of Residence. Proposal A had not needed a link, but Proposal B required one. The lack of funding therefore represented an oversight by the analysts, and as soon as it had been noticed, was assured of support by the analysts and their sponsors, the senior management. The fourth translation was the go ahead for stage 2 of the system. By now the end-users were committed to the system and their criticisms were merely constructive ones—it should be able to do more. There was therefore sufficient support for the analysts to put pressure on the sponsor for more money, by getting the sponsor to “sign off” the project in full knowledge that end-users were dissatisfied.

Looking back, the first translation involved making the initial proposal technical, covering up the political aspects, maintaining the fiction that there was an inside (analysis) and outside (politics) (Bowker 1992). This was not a deliberate piece of deception, but a sensemaking device to make the world more understandable—because sensemaking is partly driven by plausibility rather than accuracy (Weick 1993). Proposals are more understandable and hence more acceptable to cosponsors when they are thought of as being "simply" technical, without political uncertainties attaching to them. The sensemaking device is used not only by the proposer but also by the cosponsor: technical dimensions can be overlooked as outside cosponsors' competence or responsibility.

The second translation was represented as merely an internal adjustment to their technical activities rather than the major politics-induced change that it actually was. Thus an “outside” change was represented as not really a change because it was “inside.” The third translation was due to a part of the political changes from Proposal A to Proposal B not being well enough concealed inside (the analyst’s function) necessitating an outside person (the end-users’ Business Manager) doing this explicitly and momentarily disturbing the fiction of the inside-outside boundary. The third translation involved the analysts externalising political criticism from the inside to the outside. Instead of criticism being directed at their work—the inside—it was redirected into political pressure (for further money)—the outside.

In order to keep the inside and outside separate, the analysts had to tidy up, simplify, and make unchangeable their conception of inside and outside of the systems analysis project to make them easily distinguishable. The systems analysis project was therefore represented as a linear series of signed-off stages beginning with proposal reports and timetables, as recommended by systems analysis methodologies. The outside environment was represented as an unchanging, hierarchical, consensual organization. This suggests that the normative force of the perspective (its evaluation of system success) comes from examining the extent to which the tidying up and simplifying activity distorts communication or dogmatises analysts' thinking. In the case, communication was distorted by initial analyst condescension toward end users (initially characterised as outsiders) with infrequent, written communication, so that a liaison role (resented by end-users) became necessary. Dogmatic analyst thinking (together with slow learning end-users) contributed to the need for the analysts to revise the design repeatedly.

The systems analysts did not want outsiders to see inside the systems analysis project. Instead they strove to maintain a clear boundary between the inside and the outside. Stage 1 and Stage 2 were both signed off and both had timetables—intended to show outsiders that the systems analysis project was a self-contained, contractable, and controllable one. The reality was much messier. Stage 2 was needed because of Stage 1's failure to reassure users. Moreover the timetables were wild guesses—the analysts' initial estimate was eight months whereas the actual project duration was two and a half years. For the same reason, the change from Proposal A to Proposal B was explained as being about ownership rather than about consensus seeking. And the way in which extra requirements were negotiated long after the end of the requirements stage was explained as being "prototyping" rather than an inherent uncontrollability in software project management. Another boundary-maintenance activity by the analysts was saying what was, and what was not, their responsibility and thus part of the originally defined system. Neither the writing of manuals nor the halls to Central Housing Services Office link was a part of the original system (inside) and so belonged to the outside (requiring extra funding). The tidying up activity has also been observed by Murray (1989, p. 294): “as major systems developments approached their ‘go live’ dates increasing amounts of what was deemed to be ‘nonessential’ development work were timetabled into an innocuously labelled ‘post-launch’ phase of the project.”

It has been suggested that systems analysts display two behaviour types, rational and political (Miles 1980, Franz and Robey 1984). “In system development, the rational myth is exceptionally important because of the close association between computing and logical decision processes. Designing a system haphazardly or in an overtly political manner would violate most organizations’ cultural norms of rationality” (Franz and Robey 1984, p. 1208). The need to appear rational to outsiders is described by Murray (1989, p. 294): “Senior and middle managers may also expend vast quantities of energy covering up divergencies from the espoused strategy and shifting blame for shortcomings onto other divisions.”

We suggest that outsiders are actors who judge the project against general organizational goals. The roles of sponsor and legitimator are therefore ones which define the boundary between inside and outside. Insiders are actors who have resources and private interests available for exchange which can advance the project forward in time. Analysts are always insiders except when they transfer to new projects. But end-users occupy both inside and outside. As the ultimate reason for the act of sponsorship, as the beneficiary of the new system, and as the repository of task domain knowledge the end-user is an outsider. The roles of elicitor, power base securer and liaison are therefore across the boundary since they treat the user as an outsider. But as a participant in the messy and incoherent process of requirements determination, the end-user is privy to the uncertainties and technicalities (reasons for acceptance or non acceptance of their requests by the analysts) of the systems analysis process and in this sense are insiders. The analyst roles of educator, consensus seeker, symbolic reassurer, and promiser are therefore all boundary-dissolving ones. They are roles which treat the end-user as an insider. Treating users as insiders inappropriately is a major breach of rules. Franz and Robey found that users became angry when a systems department attempted to use a technical manual and methodology textbooks to dictate meanings to them. Insiders seek control over systems development through acquiring responsibility but when things go badly they may seek to become outsiders (thus escaping responsibility). Those who fail to become outsiders when things go badly become scapegoats (Franz and Robey 1984).

## 9. Discussion

Five theoretical perspectives have been used to draw different messages from the case study. Each perspective has its own advantages, disadvantages, and implications for practitioner systems analysts as shown in Figure 8. As one moves from zero sum towards socially shaped power, there is a transition from low explanatory but high prescriptive ability towards high explanatory but low prescriptive ability. For structurally constrained power, there is no advocated improvement strategy, rather the practical implications are that the systems analyst has a number of ethical choices (for example, how much to reinforce the dominant rationale and how much to enable dissenting voices to be heard). And for socially shaped power, the practical implications are phrased in terms of negative criticism of previous prescriptions (techniques and methodologies), the need for a normative theory, and the need for a research agenda rather than in terms of any constructive advocacy for improvement.

The zero sum power perspective would conclude that senior management “won” and that centralisation was the result of systems development. However, it is inadequate as an explanatory model because the end-users now have a less stressful time during the four-week housing allocation period (eight hours’ less work per week is spent on processing applications), and they do feel a sense of having mastered a new technological skill. Two practical implications flow from recognition of the existence of zero sum power: the need to move a situation away from a zero to a positive sum game (the analysts did this by changing from Proposal A to Proposal B), and the need to disguise the zero sum nature of the game (senior management did this in their initial report justifying computerisation).

Processual power is relevant to the extent that there was a complex set of roles which each actor group played in order to ensure that requirements were satisfactorily evoked and communicated. Yet it is inadequate as an explanatory model because it would be difficult to say whether or not such roles were adequately played, what the consequences would have been if they had not been adequately played, whether some roles were more important than others, and what the organizational constraints on those roles were (Murray 1989). Several practical implications are suggested by this perspective. Analysts should attempt to improve their role-playing skills; their own organizations have a change agent responsibility; senior management in user organizations often attempt to use the external nature of the analyst's position as a means of avoiding being held directly responsible for change (leading to delicate role negotiation); and role-playing and conflict resolution are centrally involved in the manipulation of user expectations and ownership and thus in system success (Ginzberg 1981, Robey et al. 1993).

Organizational power is able to provide an explanation of organizational change related to the project and is able to specify different stages when consensus and conflict were appropriate. But it is unable to supply any evaluation of system success. More information about which roles facilitate which stage in organizational change and in software development would enable more focussed attention by analysts on role-playing.

Structurally constrained power identifies the multiple external constraints working on the various actors, and the patterns of visibility created by the information system. Although visibility in this case has resulted in surveillance rather than empowerment, this is not the general implication of any system and must depend upon the particular circumstances. As an explanatory model, then, structurally constrained power is incomplete. The perspective raises a number of ethical choices which are open to the user organization, which often abrogates responsibility (Newman and

Rosenberg 1985, p. 399), leaving such choices to be made by the systems analyst. These choices concern control versus empowerment, centralisation versus decentralisation, mystification versus clarification, enabling versus disabling of dissenting voices, and covertness versus overtness.

Socially shaped power identifies very clearly the crucial moments (or “translations”—Bloomfield and Best 1992) when things changed significantly and explains them in terms of a socially constructed boundary between the project (the “inside”) and its environment (the “outside”). The inside and outside were kept separate requiring the analysts to tidy up, simplify, and make unchangeable (“irreversible”—Callon 1991) and agreed-upon (“convergent”—Callon 1991) their conception of the systems analysis project. The project was presented as a linear series of signed-off stages beginning with proposal reports and timetables. The outside environment was represented as an unchanging, hierarchical, consensual organization. The cognitive simplification and communication barriers this boundary created had important impacts on the success of systems development. The boundary served to reinforce users’ perception that the system was being brought from outside to be applied to (Bloomfield 1992, p. 194) their organization.

The socially shaped power perspective generates the least practical implications but also the richest research agenda of the five power perspectives. The definition of system success is the ability to generate meanings which project the range, subtlety and expressiveness of communication within a particular organization or community. Although this definition is adequate as a starting point, we acknowledge that it requires a normative theory of completeness, correctness, rationality or clarity of communication such as that suggested by Habermas (1973), but with a much sharper focus on information systems. Besides consideration of this issue, a research agenda would include an examination of the artificial distinction between the technical (the information system) and the social (the user organization) and the assumption that the relationship between the two can be managed by means of techniques such as methodologies and tools (Bloomfield 1992, p. 191). The approach is often, as for example with Floyd's (1988) method, to assume that the technical (the computer system) must be adapted to fit in with the social (the organization or task context). A useful research agenda would focus around the process of change, and would assume that any future increase in self awareness by analysts created by research will lead to improved systems. In particular, it suggests that the boundary between the software development project and its environment (user organization, developer organization, external environment) needs closer research attention. Particular items on this research agenda that have directly practical implications include the use of socially constructed intermediates (texts, software, skills, time and money) as boundary markers, and the relationship between roles and this boundary (roles for creating boundary markers, roles for spanning boundaries, and roles for strengthening or dissolving boundaries). An interesting question with normative implications is the circumstances in which boundaries and boundary-related roles are useful (keeping secrets, maintaining tight discipline and goal consensus, and purifying language—Douglas 1966—on essential projects) or harmful (preventing communication, increasing conflict, enabling blaming and the avoidance of responsibility, and enabling the external environment to reduce its support). The dual nature of such boundaries suggests that behaviour towards them (defining, naming, and classifying such boundaries and their associated roles) is ambiguous and complex. $^{1}$
