# Facilitator Influence in Group Support Systems: Intended and Unintended Effects

Terri L. Griffith • Mark A. Fuller • Gregory B. Northcraft

John M. Olin School of Business, Washington University, One Brookings Drive, St. Louis, Missouri 63130-4899 griffith@wuolin.wustl.edu

Hankamer School of Business, Information Systems Department, Baylor University, Waco, Texas 76798-8005 mark\_fuller@baylor.edu

Department of Business Administration, College of Commerce and Business Administration, University of Illinois, Champaign, Illinois 61820 northcra@uiuc.edu

his paper addresses facilitation, a developing area of Group Support Systems (GSS) research. The facilitator role is one of improving a group’s communication and information flow; facilitators are meant to enhance the manner in which a group makes decisions without making those decisions for the group. However, there is a paradox in facilitation: The influence required to facilitate a group changes the group’s outcomes. Additionally, strict impartiality for facilitation may be too much to expect because facilitators may unintentionally bias group outcomes, or because facilitators may have their own agendas. Acknowledgment, training, and standards for facilitators may prove useful ways for groups to retain the benefits of facilitation without incurring the costs of inappropriate facilitator influence. Implications are drawn for new research acknowledging the complexity of the GSS sociotechnical system, and the importance of sociotechnical facilitation in organizations.

(Facilitation; Group Support Systems; Electronic Meeting Systems; Groups; Power in Organizations; Sociotechnical Systems)

## Introduction

Groups and teams have emerged in recent years as an important vehicle for managing organizations (Barua et al. 1995, Bursic 1992, Goodman 1986). Product development teams (Ancona and Caldwell 1992a and 1992b, Bursic 1992) and semiautonomous work groups (Goodman et al. 1988), for example, offer organizations the benefits of diverse thinking and synergies in the way work is completed (Hackman 1982). However, organizational work groups may have “process losses” (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1993) in which group dynamics interfere with the effectiveness and efficiency of the group, resulting in outcomes that are less than expected given the available resources (Steiner 1972). Process losses may result from evaluation apprehension (Diehl and Stroebe 1987), groupthink (Janis 1982), and/or production blocking. Concurrent with the growing focus on groups in organizations, and perhaps not independently (Galbraith and Kazanjian 1988), have been rapid advances in the use of technology to enhance workplace performance, especially through improved communication (e.g., Fulk and DeSanctis 1995).

Group Support Systems (GSS) are one example of an electronic organizational communication technology which links technology and organizational participants in a sociotechnical system (Trist and Bamforth 1951). A sociotechnical system approach to GSS acknowledges that organizational and human outcomes can be improved by striving for fit between the social and technical elements of the organization (e.g., Katz and Kahn 1972, p. 701). In a GSS, the social components of an organization are combined with the technical components in an attempt to create a balanced and synergistic relationship. In a GSS, users interact with and through a computer-mediated communication system to do collaborative work (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987). Organizations can use GSS to enhance communication within and across groups. Research suggests that GSS can reduce some aspects of process loss inherent in using groups and teams in organizations (Benbasat and Lim 1993).

The components of the GSS sociotechnical system include group members, task, technology, and facilitators. Facilitators (both of the technology and/or of the group process), group members participating in the interaction (whether peers or part of a hierarchy), and technology (the group support system hardware and software) interact within the context of the task (e.g., decision making, information gathering). Much of the GSS research literature has focused on the effects of a specific GSS technology on group member participants (e.g., participants’ behaviors and attitudes), rather than on the dynamics of the GSS sociotechnical system. This paper addresses a developing focus of GSS research: facilitation. Facilitation within the context of a GSS usually entails operating the technology and/or facilitating the group’s discussion (Clawson, Bostrom and Anson 1993; Niederman, Beise and Beranek 1995). Facilitation is a key element to the success of groups and teams in a sociotechnical system. Facilitation helps the participants exploit the capabilities of the technology and their group, in the pursuit of their task (e.g., Anson et al. 1995; Wheeler and Valacich 1996). In this paper, we use a sociotechnical system approach to GSS in order to highlight the diversity of factors contributing to facilitator influence in GSS settings.

The role played by the facilitator in a GSS is integral to the objective of the sociotechnical system approach, which is: “. . . to find the social and technological arrangement that meets both the needs of the [group members] . . . and organizational goals” (Pasmore and Sherwood 1978, p. 41). The central paradox which this paper seeks to explore is that the very sources of influence that allow facilitators to enhance a group’s processes and outcomes—to facilitate the group—may undermine a facilitator’s intended impartiality (Anson et al. 1995, Broome and Keever 1989, Cooks and Hale 1994, Doyle and Straus 1982). This paper brings together research on power, information processing, and influence in groups.

As with most research on groups and sociotechnical systems, the issues raised in this paper straddle multiple levels of analysis. Influence effects necessarily trace their origins to the thoughts and behaviors of individuals—in this case, facilitators and group members. Thus, the primary focus of this paper must be the potential influence of facilitators on group members. However, the thoughts and behaviors of facilitators and group members become consequential by virtue of their impact on the group’s processes and outcomes, and their implications for the management (and possible redesign) of the sociotechnical system. Therefore, these inescapable considerations also are featured in this paper.

While this paper focuses on facilitation of a GSS, the issues raised are relevant to a variety of other sociotechnical systems (e.g., Computer Integrated Manufacturing, LotusNotes communication systems). The dynamics of any sociotechnical system that entail role differentiation (e.g., facilitator, group member) and differentially distributed power and influence can be informed by the following discussion. For example, the role (whether formal or not) of an applications designer for LotusNotes might be considered similar to the facilitator role in GSS; indeed, any roles for which expertise, power, and influence play a part in the sociotechnical system have clear parallels to the discussion that follows. The importance of our arguments to other sociotechnical settings is revisited in the conclusions of this paper.

## Background

Group facilitation existed well before the advent of GSS. (See Hirokawa and Gouran 1989 for a review.) However, the increased use, and complexity, of the GSS sociotechnical system may increase the importance of facilitation. A great deal of non-GSS organizational research may offer critical insights into GSS facilitation; past research on group roles, power, influence, and other group and communication processes all provide useful background for understanding electronic group facilitation in organizational settings.

Research on facilitation in the context of GSS is developing (Anson et al. 1995, Bostrom et al. 1993, Clawson et al. 1993, Dickson et al. 1989, Dickson et al. 1993, George et al. 1992, Niederman et al. 1995, Wheeler and Valacich 1996). However, while previous researchers (e.g., Bostrom et al. 1993) have catalogued important features of facilitation, how these features become influential in an organizational setting remains relatively unexplained and unexplored. For example, although Niederman et al. (1995) addressed the important features and relationships within facilitation, that work was “investigative” (p. 23) in that only the responding facilitators’ perceptions of relationships between facilitation features and meeting outcomes were considered.

The identification of relevant theories is a necessary prerequisite before a comprehensive model of facilitation can be developed. Having assumed a sociotechnical approach, this perspective is focused on the interactions and fit between the people, systems, and technology involved in a GSS session. This perspective can be used to predict the outcomes of the myriad directions a facilitated session may go. The goal of this paper is to highlight a way of understanding why techniques and activities result in influence, rather than to propose a particular technique (e.g. whether or not to use facilitation, Anson et al. 1995) or delineate facilitation activities (e.g., Clawson et al. 1993). This perspective provides the foundation for a detailed examination of the “facilitator paradox” described previously.<sup>1</sup>

This paper begins by reviewing the foundations of facilitator influence. Roles and expectations are addressed first. Given the sociotechnical nature of GSS, these roles and expectations derive from an understanding of both organizational norms and technical requirements. Next, the source of a facilitator’s ability to facilitate is elaborated through an analysis of power, information processing, and influence in groups. This elaboration reveals the “facilitator paradox,” given the intended and unintended origins of facilitator influence. Finally, with this paradox in mind, organizational strategies for the effective use of GSS are considered, future research issues are identified, and six research propositions are developed. Further development of these ideas in the context of a specific sociotechnical setting (GSS, organizational structure, etc.) will result in empirically testable hypotheses. The current set of propositions is not intended to be a complete list of all the relevant questions of facilitator influence. However, the propositions do provide a focal point that could be elaborated in a specific research stream.

## Foundations of Facilitator Influence

## Forms of Facilitation

Many researchers have demonstrated positive effects of GSS on organizational outcomes and group communication, including improved project time-tocompletion, and group member satisfaction (e.g., Nunamaker et al. 1991, Nunamaker et al. 1989), idea generation (e.g., Connolly et al. 1990, Jessup and Tansik 1991), and group processes (e.g., Zigurs et al. 1988). However, some researchers have reported opposing findings (e.g., George et al. 1990, Watson et al. 1988). The roles and behaviors of facilitators in groups (e.g., Bostrom et al. 1993, Dennis and Gallupe 1993, Dennis et al. 1991, Fuller and Trower 1994, Kraemer and King 1988, Limayem et al. 1993, McGoff and Ambrose 1991) may help account for these apparent contradictions.

The American Heritage Dictionary (1981) defines facilitate as “. . . to free from difficulties or obstacles; make easier. . . .” Most descriptions of the facilitation process establish the facilitator role as one of strict impartiality; the facilitator is meant to enhance the manner in which a group makes decisions—not make content decisions for the group (e.g., Anson et al. 1995,

Broome and Keever 1989, Doyle and Straus 1982). Bostrom et al. (1993) reinforce this view, describing facilitation as a “. . . set of functions or activities carried out before, during, and after a meeting to help the group achieve its own outcomes [emphasis added]. The essential characteristic of facilitation is to help make an outcome easier to achieve.” A general sense of facilitation drawn from these descriptions is that facilitators should be impartial; that is, facilitators should make only indirect contributions to the final solution through neutral enhancement of the processes of communication and information processing by the group. This assumed impartiality is a foundation of facilitation.

Implicit in the use of a facilitated GSS is the assumption that group member participation in the outcome is desired, and that the sociotechnical system is needed to make that participation effective. Vroom and Yetton (1973) note that group member participation in decision making is critical when (1) the group members have the information needed to make the decision, and (2) it is important that group members are committed to the decision. In such cases, facilitation needs to be impartial so that the outcome is determined by the group members (they have the information) and so that the group members feel that the outcome was theirs (resulting in greater commitment).

Eden (1990) has suggested that decision support can effectively link both process facilitation and the model building skills of a management scientist for better decision making (p. 48–50). While Eden (1990) uses the term content facilitation to describe the use of management science/operations research modeling, this form of facilitation seems focused on the method rather than the substance of a group’s deliberations, and is thus consistent with impartial process consultation (e.g., Rockwood 1993, Schein 1969). The boundaries of the approach used in this paper define facilitation as an impartial application of group process. This is distinct from content contributions (content consultation) which would provide information or direction to the group beyond the impartial enhancement of group process. (Please see Zorn and Rosenfeld 1989 for further discussion of process versus content consultation.)

In practice, GSS facilitation has two distinct components: technical facilitation and group process facilitation (Clawson et al. 1993). Both facilitation functions— technical and group process—may be assumed by a single individual (e.g., Clawson et al. 1993). The technical facilitation role is focused on enhancing group members’ use of the communication and information technology; the group process facilitation role is focused on enhancing group members’ interactions with each other.

To simplify the presentation of our approach, the following discussion focuses on facilitators who are outside third parties. However, the implications for situations in which a group member fills a facilitator role also are addressed. Throughout this paper we will use the terms technical facilitator and group process facilitator without any assumption as to the number of people used to fill these roles. Additionally, our discussion will focus on “same-time, same-place” facilitation, where all participants are located at the same facility during the meeting, as opposed to a teleconferenced or asynchronous mode. Although some aspects of facilitator influence may change in situations that are not “same-time, same-place” (indeed, group process facilitation may be non-existent in some asynchronous settings where “facilitation” is really no more than serving as a remote technology operator, or “technician”), these issues are left for discussion in future research.

Technical Facilitation. In order for a group to function smoothly and productively in a sociotechnical setting such as a GSS, someone has to operate the technology. A group that appreciates the advantages of using a GSS may not have the expertise to use the system without technical assistance. Clawson et al. (1993) asked facilitators for self-report descriptions of their activities and identified three facilitation dimensions specific to the use of technology: (1) Appropriately selects and prepares technology, (2) Creates comfort with and promotes understanding of the technology and technology outputs, and (3) Understands technology and its capabilities (p. 556). The fundamental role of the technical facilitator is to ensure that the technology’s advantages are made accessible to group members—perhaps by running the technology so that group members who could run the technology instead can concentrate on the group’s task content, and certainly by assisting those group members who are not themselves masters of the technology.

Group Process Facilitation. In a sociotechnical setting such as a GSS, the importance of group process facilitation may be less obvious than the need for technical facilitation; groups can meet—even effectively— without a group process facilitator. However, the fundamental role of the group process facilitator is to enhance (i.e., improve efficiency and effectiveness of) the communication and interaction of group members in order to help the group achieve outcomes that make better use of the resources available within the group. Clawson et al. (1993) also identified thirteen dimensions of facilitation that can be described as “group process” related, including [numbers from the original; p. 556], (1) Promotes ownership and encourages group responsibility; (4) Listens to, clarifies, and integrates information; (10) Presents information to the group (instructions, written materials); (12) Plans and designs the meeting process; (13)Manages conflict and negative emotions constructively; (14) Directs and manages the meetings. Interestingly, the identification of thirteen group process dimensions, in contrast to only three technical dimensions, highlight the fact that group process facilitation is a key component of facilitation in general (Anson et al. 1995).

The above descriptions of the two basic components of facilitation—technical and group process<sup>2</sup>—again highlight the intended impartiality of the facilitator role. A sociotechnical system such as a GSS is itself a communication tool by which groups can enhance their group processes and outcomes (e.g., Benbasat and Lim 1993). The facilitator role is intended to ensure that this tool does in practice enhance the group’s communication processes and outcomes. The facilitator role is intended to help the group achieve better or faster what the group wants to achieve. According to this characterization of the facilitator role, a facilitator’s own views regarding the outcome of the group’s task, or the facilitator’s own agenda (beyond that of good facilitation), should be independent of his/her facilitation of the group’s processes or outcomes.

## Power and Information Processing in Facilitation

How is a facilitator able to facilitate? Influence is the cornerstone of the facilitator role. The process of facilitation requires that the facilitator—whether technical, group process, or both—somehow influence (i.e., change) the communication behaviors of the group. Much of a facilitator’s ability to facilitate arises because sociotechnical systems are interacting social systems, and interacting social systems are susceptible to a variety of forms of social influence (Asch 1972). Examples of such social influence effects are those arising from power and information processing.

Power. Frost (1987) has noted that communication structure, channels, networks, and rules are avenues of power, and GSS facilitators have a great deal of control over the communication of the group. This control and its outcomes can be understood by a power analysis of facilitator roles. Many discussions of power in organizations revolve around an individual’s influence over others resulting from the ability to reward or punish them. Thus, an individual’s boss has power over that individual (and probably can influence that individual’s behaviors) because the boss controls important rewards and punishments for that individual— such as pay raises, performance appraisals, and job assignments. French and Raven’s (1959) classic description of power in organizations suggests that there are additional types of power which are not so directly tied to an individual’s control over rewards and punishments. Three such sources of power are legitimate power, expert power, and status.

Legitimate power is authority—the right to give orders. In formal organizational hierarchies, individuals accept and respect the dictates of legitimate authority figures because legitimate authority carries with it control over rewards and punishments. In a sociotechnical system such as a GSS, facilitators have legitimate power (e.g., Keltner 1989). In order to facilitate the group’s interaction, the facilitator must have some legitimate authority to give orders and to determine how things happen. A facilitator’s legitimate authority should exist to the extent that the facilitator is perceived by the group members as having the right to structure the group’s work. The technical facilitator may select the GSS tools that the group uses and/or determine how the selected tools are used by the group. The group process facilitator may determine when the group is ready to begin, switch tools, or quit. The facilitator’s legitimate “right” may be due to the facilitation role in the organization, and/or delegated to the facilitator by the authorities hiring or designating the facilitator.

Just as in formal organizations, deference to an authority figure in a sociotechnical system (the facilitator) can be traced to control over rewards and punishments (e.g., French and Raven 1959). Groups may use the technological tools that a sociotechnical system offers because the use of those tools (and the facilitator’s direction) promises the enhancement of group processes and outcomes—a reward. A group’s or group member’s unwillingness to abide by the dictates of the facilitator carries an implicit risk of not receiving the rewards available from the proper use of the technological tool.

A parallel argument can be made for expert power. Expert power arises when one individual possesses knowledge that other individuals do not possess. Facilitators may also gain expertise simply by being involved in the planning of the session—simply by having a headstart on the rest of the group. Knowledge provides power to an individual when it carries the implicit risk that not obeying the person who “knows best” (the expert with the knowledge) will result in negative consequences. Experts often are given authority (legitimate power) in groups precisely because they are expected to know best. Again, a facilitator (whether technical or group process) is an expert—an expert in the use of the sociotechnical system. Because the facilitator knows best, the group’s or any group member’s unwillingness to abide by the facilitator’s dictates should carry a risk that group processes and outcomes will suffer. Additionally, perceptions of legitimate and expert power may be affected by whether the facilitator is a member of the organization, or has been brought in from outside of the organization. Less experienced group members may perceive facilitators to have more expertise than more experienced group members would perceive. Outside facilitators may be perceived as having less legitimate power (given they have no formal role in the reporting structure of the organization), but more expert power.

Legitimate and expert power also imply a secondary determinant of influence: the power of status. Status is deference or esteem accorded to an individual because of that individual’s standing as an expert. Status can lend extra credence or weight to an individual’s opinions or actions (Northcraft and Neale 1990). Thus, a facilitator’s status might provide the facilitator power even in areas in which the facilitator is not an expert.

The foregoing discussion of sources of facilitator power leads to a first research proposition:

Propositon 1. The strength of a facilitator’s ability to influence—to control the group’s process—will be a function of the facilitator’s power.

Power makes it possible for a facilitator to facilitate successfully (i.e., to control the group’s process) because group members will be influenced by a powerful facilitator. Kelman (1958) has noted, however, that people will often be influenced by powerful others, even while not agreeing with them. In facilitation, group members may defer to a legitimate facilitator because they have to. In contrast, they may defer to an expert or high-status facilitator because they want to, because they believe that experts and high-status individuals are more likely to lead them to good decisions. This leads to a second research proposition:

Proposition 2. The strength of a facilitator’s influence will be greater if the facilitator has expert power or status than if the facilitator has only legitimate power.

Information Processing. Facilitator power provides an obvious path for influencing group members. Group members may do what facilitators tell them to do because facilitators hold a variety of forms of power. However, facilitators also have available to them much more subtle forms of influence, that derive from the information processing tendencies of human decision makers (Kahneman et al. 1982). Three examples of these subtle, information processing determinants of influence are framing, anchoring, and salience.

Framing would occur if the manner in which a facilitator introduces or poses (“frames”) a question or issue influences the way that group members respond to that question or issue (Tversky and Kahneman 1981). For example, individuals respond differently to the prospects of gains or losses (Kahneman and Tversky 1979); all other things being equal, decision makers will be risk-averse to protect gains and riskseeking to avoid losses. Significantly, whether an issue or question is about gains or losses is often a function of the reference point employed (Kahneman 1992; White and Neale 1991). (“Are we doing better than last year?” “Are we losing ground against our projections?” “Are we losing ground on the competition?” “Do we want to protect our market share?”) A facilitator who, in introducing an issue, chooses to highlight the prospect of gains to protect (rather than losses to avoid) may elicit different risk-preferences in group members, thereby yielding quite different decisions (e.g., Northcraft and Neale 1986). More generally, the overall context in which a facilitator leads a group to see a conflict or problem may influence the kinds of solutions the group considers and agrees to (e.g., Pinkley and Northcraft 1994).

Anchoring is a related mechanism by which the way a facilitator introduces or talks about a question or issue may dramatically influence the decisions a group makes. Anchoring occurs if a reference point that a facilitator uses to introduce a question or issue unduly influences group members’ deliberations about that issue (Northcraft and Neale 1987). For example, a facilitator who asks a group to set a profit goal for the coming fiscal year might anchor the group’s deliberations by first reminding the group of last year’s goal, last year’s achieved profit, or last year’s average profit within the industry; any of these figures would provide a strong “anchor” for the group’s deliberations and influence the goal which the group decided upon. Past research on individual judgment has shown that even random, irrelevant information can anchor deliberations and thereby dramatically influence final decisions (Tversky and Kahneman 1974).

Finally, salience refers to the extent to which information stands out, is particularly prominent, visible, eye-catching, or attention grabbing. Salience contributes to influence because information that is made more cognitively available has a greater opportunity to influence decision-makers’ deliberations (Tversky and Kahneman 1973). For example, the more vivid and concrete information is, the more likely it is to be recalled and so have impact on decision-makers’ deliberations (Wilson et al. 1985). In the case of sociotechnical communication systems such as GSS, the facilitator typically is more salient than any single group member, precisely because of the facilitator’s physical centrality as a legitimate authority running the session. This would be particularly true in those GSS designs where the facilitator is required to run the technology (Dennis et al. 1988), versus designs in which facilitation is optional. The facilitator’s greater salience means that the facilitator’s comments are more likely to be recalled and more likely to be considered than any group member’s comments. This also means that any comments made by the facilitator will have a greater opportunity to frame or anchor the group’s deliberations. Additionally, since facilitators control the flow of communication in GSS, they may be more likely to speak first. Such “first advocacy” behaviors have been shown to anchor groups’ thoughts, choices, or actions (Weisband 1992).

It is interesting to note that group members (or anyone else in the GSS setting) can themselves influence other group members through framing, anchoring, and salience. Thus, not only must a facilitator be concerned with his or her activities as they relate to influence, but facilitation also requires that the facilitator monitor and manage the activities of others as well. The more power a facilitator has, the more likely are facilitator activities to create information processing effects. Conversely, the more power a facilitator has, the less likely are group member activities to create information processing effects. This leads to a third research proposition:

Proposition 3. The effects of information processing on facilitator influence will be moderated by facilitator power.

Both the group process and technical facilitation roles provide opportunities for influence. The group process facilitation role is probably the most critical given its interactive nature. However, the technical facilitation role clearly holds power (legitimate, expert, and status) as well; the question is how the role is perceived by the group members, given its lower salience. The technical facilitation role may have less actual opportunity for influencing group members via information processing, though even here, technical facilitators may still have opportunities for influence. For example, technical facilitators may control the time taken to start new tools, choice of headings and heading levels in an outline, etc., and these may play roles for framing, anchoring, and salience.

## Use of Facilitator Influence

## Intended versus Unintended Effects

Facilitator power and information processing allow facilitators to help the group achieve better outcomes by influencing the behaviors and cognitions of group members. But facilitator power and group member information processing also are the foundation of an important paradox for sociotechnical systems facilitators. The paradox of facilitation is that facilitators are supposed to influence without being influential.<sup>3</sup> Facilitators are supposed to influence (or specifically, enhance) the group’s process (i.e., efficiency, effectiveness, and commitment) without affecting the content of the decision (Anson et al. 1995, Rockwood 1993, Schein 1969). Yet, by improving the process, facilitators are working with the GSS and the group to affect the quality of the outcomes (Nunamaker et al. 1991), and thus, ultimately, the content of the decision.

This paradox suggests that the facilitator in a GSS must walk a fine line. The facilitator must use available sources of influence to alter fundamentally—to enhance—the outcomes achieved by the group. Yet, it is apparently demanded by the foundations of facilitation that this influence be “impartial.” Unfortunately, impartiality may be too much to expect of facilitators, for three reasons: (1) facilitators may unintentionally influence group outcomes, (2) facilitators may have agendas that support their position as facilitators, and (3) facilitators may also be group members with their own individual agenda regarding the group outcome. Each of these three possibilities is discussed in turn.

(1) Facilitators May Unintentionally Influence Group Outcomes. One of the hallmarks of social influence is that it need not be intentional to occur. A well-meaning facilitator may not realize the extent of his or her influence over the group (e.g., the salience of his or her comments, his or her framing of questions and issues, and his or her anchoring of the group’s deliberations and decisions). Groups may turn to sociotechnical tools such as GSS when they are uncertain about what to do, and uncertainty makes groups particularly susceptible to influence (Latane and Darley 1970). On the power side, a technical facilitator may not fully grasp the weight given to his/her suggestions about which tools to use, particularly when the group is not technically knowledgeable. There may also be pressures for the facilitator to be pulled into the dynamics of the ongoing session, perhaps losing some perspective on the meeting (Keltner 1989, p. 24). Finally, a particularly conscientious facilitator may try to help a group by providing examples or making suggestions. Support for the use of these behaviors is even found in the description of Dimension 15 of facilitation behaviors (Clawson et al. 1993, p. 556): “Encourages/supports multiple perspectives . . uses techniques, metaphors, stories, examples to get the group to consider different frames of reference.” However, when facilitators do not provide balanced examples reflecting all sides of an issue (or without considering the impact of the particular example), unintentional influence may occur.

At heart, unintentional facilitator influence is still impartial; it does not intentionally move the group outcome in one direction or another. Nevertheless, it fundamentally alters the group’s process and outcome.

(2) Facilitators Have Agenda(s) That Support Their Role as Facilitators. Perhaps a more alarming possibility is that a facilitator’s influence can serve some agenda other than the group’s. For example, a facilitator may want to be respected by the group, to look like a good, competent facilitator (perhaps in order to be hired again). Kingsbury (1972, p. 110) suggests that a facilitator may be “concerned about his [sic] own learning in the group, his own gratification, and his standing with other members.” But how is the competence of a facilitator judged? Little research seems to have addressed this issue (Anson et al. 1995 provide a beginning), but the general performance appraisal and goal-setting literature suggests that whatever dimension is perceived as being measured and rewarded will be the dimension most focused on by the performer (e.g., Locke and Latham 1990, p. 95).

This may create a substantial (though possibly subconscious) role conflict (e.g., Katz and Kahn 1972, p. 204) for the facilitator. On the one hand, the facilitator is supposed to help the group find the best solution; on the other hand, the facilitator has a schedule to keep and personal goals to achieve. This conflict could lead a facilitator to (perhaps unintentionally) make decisions (e.g., tool selection, timing of breaks) or comments (e.g., framing, anchoring) that move the group toward completion of the task without necessarily moving the group toward effective completion of the task.

(3) Facilitators May be Group Members. The problem of facilitator role conflict becomes an even more compelling issue when the facilitator is also a contributing group member (Keltner 1989, p. 22). While the foundations of facilitation imply that the facilitator role is occupied by a dispassionate, impartial third-party, in some groups the facilitator may be a group member, complete with a group member’s biases about the preferred outcome. Just as the group leader of a traditional group who has a vested interest in the group’s final decision has many opportunities to influence the group’s processes and outcomes, the facilitator in a GSS has ample opportunity to drive the group’s deliberations in the direction of his/her vested interests (Keltner 1989). (And again, any influence exercised by a facilitator in such a situation need not be intentional to occur.) Even the ethics of facilitation provides no easy answers, Zorn and Rosenfeld (1989, p. 97) note, “In working for the ‘good,’ . . whose ‘good should be considered first?” Even when the facilitator’s own interests are set aside, the facilitator must still contend with the tensions between management versus group “good,” and group versus member “good.”

The above discussion of intended and unintended effects suggests the fourth proposition:

Proposition 4. Facilitator influence is more likely to be intentional and not impartial when facilitators have agendas that conflict with the agenda of the group.

## Limits to Influence and the Paradox

The central premise of this paper has been that the very sources of influence that allow facilitators to enhance a group’s processes and outcomes—to facilitate the group—create a risk that facilitators may violate the impartiality with which they are charged. It is inevitable that a facilitator must be able to alter the way a group thinks and acts. A facilitator who cannot influence the group cannot facilitate the group either. But a facilitator who can influence the group begs a difficult question: How is the line drawn between facilitator influence that facilitates the group and facilitator influence that unduly sways the content of the group’s decisions?

The extent to which a facilitator can exert inappropriate influence may be moderated by the task. Dickson et al. (1993) found that when a group’s task required flexible facilitation, tightly structured group and technical facilitation (lack of fit within the sociotechnical system) actually resulted in lower levels of consensus than did chauffeur-driven groups (technical facilitation only, where all activities were userselected). Additionally, Dickson et al. (1993) note that even manual groups (no electronic communication or facilitation support—e.g., Watson et al. 1988) have achieved higher levels of consensus than they found in their tightly structured facilitated GSS groups. These examples suggest that facilitation’s influence and effectiveness can be limited by the task at hand. The effectiveness of the overall sociotechnical system stems from appropriate fit between the task, technology, and social actors. Interestingly, particular facilitator characteristics also play a role. It has been suggested that novice facilitators may rely on heavy structure, resulting in lower effectiveness in some groups.<sup>4</sup> Thus:

Proposition 5. Facilitator influence will be moderated by task characteristics.

Additionally, the influence that facilitators hold over groups should be dependent upon the centrality of the facilitator role in a particular GSS setting. Technical and group process facilitators should have expert power over a group only to the extent that the group believes it will better accomplish its goals with the help of the facilitator. Technical and group process facilitators should have legitimate power over the group only to the extent to which the group believes they have the obligation to accept the facilitator’s influence (French and Raven 1959). Finally, the influence of technical and group process facilitators should result from group member information processing only to the extent that facilitators are active, central figures in the session; a facilitator who has no “air-time” has less opportunity to influence (see Proposition 2).

Benbasat et al. (1993) have suggested that it is the technology and the decision structure that enhance the group’s efficiency and effectiveness. The GSS technology enhances individual and group communication, information handling, data resources, and process structures (e.g., Poole et al. 1993). Facilitation is sometimes (but not always) necessary to physically run the tools, but facilitation itself need only be enabling, not directing. This is similar to the role played by some computer facility staff. They are necessary to open the doors, but not to achieve the benefits. To go a step further, some group process functions could be built into the GSS itself (for example, by having the computer track participants’ communication frequency and goad nonparticipants back into the discussion).

Limayem and colleagues (1996; 1993) have experimented with automated facilitation in which the GSS itself facilitates groups (versus a “user-driven” setting where, after training on the GSS, the users alone make decisions about the use of the tools, e.g., Gallupe et al. 1988, Zigurs et al. 1988). In Limayem et al.’s (1993) automated setting, the GSS included facilitation mechanisms aimed toward guiding the group through the decision making process. For example, the GSS explained the decision model, provided summaries and recommendations based on group outcomes. Such automated GSS systems would not beg the facilitator’s “paradox of impartial influence” since the GSS itself can be assumed to be impartial (or at least stable— particular designs ostensibly could favor one decision alternative over another—see below). Limayem et al. (1993) found that, facilitated groups (either human or automated) achieved better post-decision consensus and perceived decision quality than did non-facilitated groups; human and automated facilitation did not differ significantly on these dimensions.

However, depending upon the automation of GSS tools to resolve the facilitator’s paradox seems unfortunate for two reasons. First, it may only displace facilitator influence rather than do away with it. When systems are completely automated, the influential decision becomes which automated system (which form of automated facilitation) to use. As Limayem et al. (1993) and Kozar and Zigurs (1992) have discussed, facilitation is still being provided, but by the system. The variety of facilitation possibilities within such an automated system is limited only by the boundaries of human information processing and the programmer’s skill. For example, systems could guide (or bias) group process by making some issues more salient than others. Member contributions might be sorted by keyword, making salient the keywords with the most mentions. Other systems might note which issues were raised first, thus creating a situation where anchoring could be a concern. Deciding which automated system to use would require the ability to see and understand the particular biases of a given system. (The following advice for Creating Effective Facilitation raises issues important for both human and automated facilitation.)

Second, this use of automation to resolve the paradox “throws out the baby with the bath water.” The problem is not that facilitators are influential; facilitator influence carries with it the prospect of benefit in terms of group processes and outcomes. A technical facilitator is there to help the group get the most out of the technology; a group process facilitator is there to ensure that the group accesses everything the group has to offer. Automated facilitation is a form of scripted facilitation. Limayem et al. (1993) found no differences in the effects of human and automated scripted facilitation. They note, however (Limayem et al. 1993), that their results are from a particular type of task, a specific GSS, and a “one-shot” session. Some evidence suggests that scripted facilitation (at least when carried out by a human facilitator) is less useful than human facilitation when that human facilitation can be flexible (Dickson et al. 1993). The real challenge is making sure that a facilitator’s influence does not cross that invisible line that separates intended from unintended influence, and neutral influence—impartiality—from undue influence.

## Creating Effective Facilitation

The real answer to the paradox of impartial influence may be three-fold. First, GSS facilitators need to acknowledge and understand the sources of influence that they control. Even among those facilitators who are currently aware of the obvious ways that they can drive a group’s processes and outcomes, some of the more subtle forms of facilitator influence (such as framing and anchoring) may be important revelations (e.g., Northcraft and Neale 1987, Northcraft and Neale 1986).

There are several ways to achieve this awareness. GSS documentation, professional groups, user feedback, and a commitment to staying abreast of the literature are starting points for a detailed discussion of facilitator effects and experiences. A “reflective” approach is advocated by Zorn and Rosenfeld (1989) in their presentation of ways to improve ethical facilitation. They cite Paradise and Siegelwaks’ (1982) work, recommending that facilitators be encouraged to examine the rationale behind their judgments and expose themselves to higher levels of ethical reasoning. Historical accounts of facilitation scenarios and roleplaying exercises may be ways of supporting this reflection.

Second, facilitators need to be trained to facilitate in a way that minimizes unintentional influence. A group process facilitator who fully understands the issues of framing and anchoring can, for instance, be trained to avoid unbalanced introductions to topics or issues that unduly influence a group’s deliberations and conclusions. Experiential exercises (e.g., Hoover 1977) that give facilitators the opportunity to practice the construction of neutral questions and prompts, and to observe the effects of framing and anchoring may be especially helpful. Training methods similar to those for interview administrators (i.e., unbiased interaction techniques) may also prove helpful (e.g., Dunham and Smith 1979). Since the focus of this paper is on influence, we will not go into the broader skills that facilitators need to be able to plan and run effective sessions (e.g., what decision tools to use when). Interested readers are referred to Clawson and Bostrom (1996) for a broader discussion of facilitation training.

Finally, there need to be standards which spell out the obligations of facilitators with respect to the use of their influence. Disclosure statements similar to those used by some real estate agents may be appropriate. Facilitators could provide information regarding their relationship to the group (whether a vested interest exists, what form of facilitator evaluation is in place, the facilitator’s goals, etc.). This information could be requested by the GSS software at the beginning of the session, and then the facilitator’s response made part of the permanent record. Public statements are one way to engender commitment (Salancik 1977); thus, a public statement of neutrality may increase facilitators’ awareness and commitment to impartial proceedings.

Standards may also be developed from ethical foundations already in use for dispute mediators. Similar to facilitation, dispute mediators control the way that disputants interact, but do not control the agreement (if any) that they reach (Sheppard 1984, Thibaut and Walker 1975). Cooks and Hale (1994, p. 61) note four cornerstones of mediator standards-of-practice, including disputant self-determination, informed consent, mediator impartiality, and mediator neutrality. Further consideration may find these four cornerstones useful starting places, again, with the goal of increasing facilitators’ awareness and commitment to impartiality.

Through acknowledgment, training, and standards, facilitators can become sensitized to the scope of their potential influence over groups and begin an ongoing discussion concerning the appropriate management of that influence. Professional publications such as “Facilitator Central” (http://hsb.baylor.edu/fuller/fac/) or the publications of the International Association of Facilitators are areas where this type of discussion can take place.

Proposition 6. Facilitator awareness of power and information processing will moderate facilitator influence.

Unfortunately, the organizational effectiveness of facilitator awareness in turn may be moderated by the facilitator’s motivation and ethics (e.g., the facilitator’s agenda).

The above issues are important not only for GSS facilitators, but for organizations as well. As organizations increase the use of integrated groups, “managing the white space” (Rummler and Brache 1991)—situations where important organizational structure and relationships are left unspecified in order to maintain flexibility—becomes important throughout the organization. Groups are often drawn from across the organization, and group members themselves may be troubled by issues of intended and unintended influence. Additionally, facilitation training is being given in some firms as a standard part of teambuilding (Dyer 1987). Thus, it may be important to consider whether the concerns discussed here are important throughout organizations as empowerment gives more people more opportunities to influence with less initial training and experience.

All three of these dimensions of facilitator influence (strength, intentionality, partiality) are in turn moderated by awareness and task characteristics.

This paper is about what factors make a facilitator influential, what makes it possible for a facilitator to facilitate. As shown in Figure 1, those factors that contribute to a facilitator’s ability to influence (i.e., power, information processing) are not the factors that determine how a facilitator uses that influence (e.g., intentionality, partiality). Again, this reflects the paradox: the factors that make a facilitator influential—and thereby able to facilitate—cannot control whether that influence is used appropriately or not.

It is interesting to note that one of the reasons for creating technologies (like GSS) to support group communication processes (such as group discussion and decision making) was precisely to insulate beneficial group processes from inappropriate influence, for instance by providing an anonymous way to contribute comments (Connolly et al. 1990). It would appear, in retrospect, that the imposition of an additional component (such as a GSS) in group communication cannot remove the possibility of influence but only transfer it elsewhere in the sociotechnical system. In the case of group process, moving influence to a technology such as a GSS begs the question of who in turn manages the GSS and its potential influence over group process. At best, the use of a GSS may transfer that influence to sources—the facilitators—who may have less (if any) vested interest in the outcome, and whose use of that influence can be reasonably controlled via awareness, training, and standards. At worst, the use of a GSS may transform that influence into a more subtle form (for instance, framing and anchoring by a group member facilitator) that other group members may find difficult to recognize or resist.

## Future Research

Thinking about the separate components of the GSS sociotechnical system may provide new directions for future research. To date, relatively little GSS research has been published which focuses on facilitation. It is possible that the perceptions of GSS researchers have been too strongly focused on the overall system to have warranted separate attention for facilitation during these early stages of GSS research.

A study by Zigurs and Kozar (1994) demonstrates how both GSS researchers and users may have difficulty identifying the distinct and separate influences of facilitators in a GSS sociotechnical system. Both the researchers and the users in their study seemed to combine facilitation in with the GSS technical system. The researchers did not separate the facilitator from the GSS itself when they questioned users concerning the roles filled during the group process. And, although the GSS in the Zigurs and Kozar study recorded 100% of all text entries, only 55% of the participants stated that the system filled the group’s “Recorder” role. (Zigurs and Kozar note that this result could be due to either the participants’ reluctance to ascribe a role to the technology, or the participants’ concern that verbal inputs were not recorded by the system.) Also, in specifying how to tease apart participants’ role perceptions, Zigurs and Kozar suggest a technological change from a facilitator-driven to a user-driven system, rather than simply suggesting that participants be allowed to respond regarding their role perceptions for both the technology and the facilitator. This may be an indication of just how closely the GSS sociotechnical system is integrated. Fruitful areas of future research may be developed by continuing to disentangle the different aspects of the sociotechnical system.

Future research may also move beyond questions of whether or not facilitation has effects on group outcomes to how that facilitation influences outcomes. The current paper provides a blueprint for intended and unintended consequences resulting from the GSS communication and information sociotechnical system. The exemplar propositions presented here are an early step toward empirical research on influence in facilitation. An additional issue is how to avoid the negative consequences through either social, technological, or combined methods. Social strategies may include facilitator understanding, training, and standards for an awareness and avoidance of unnecessary influence. Future research may test the efficacy of the experiential exercise and bias training described earlier. Such research should document the way in which these different forms of training affect facilitator behavior as well as group outcomes.

Future work can build an understanding of how the components of the sociotechnical system work together. Technological strategies may include system design such that facilitators are not “designed-out” at the cost of losing flexible facilitation benefits. Continued work along the lines of Limayem et al.’s (1993) research will be important here. Just as a social component (the facilitator) can be studied within the overall context of the sociotechnical system, so can technical components be adapted to better suit the overall system. Technological decisions made with an understanding of the task (Dickson et al. 1993) are an example of using both social and technical understanding of the group process to guide research and GSS use. Future research which considers the role of facilitation within the context of distributed (both in time and place) groups may be especially interesting. What is the relationship between social and technical components as the “group” itself begins to change? In all cases, facilitation should be brought up to the level of our understanding of the other key GSS areas.

Future research should also consider the structural issues of facilitation. Several facilitation roles have been discussed here (technical, process, content). Some mention has also been made of facilitators who act as a member of the group, and others (Keltner 1989) have mentioned issues for facilitators who may be leaders of the group. Structural components of facilitation bring to light issues of role conflict and role overload. Although this paper has discussed problems when there are vested interests, role overload (e.g., French and Caplan 1972) is also a critical issue. Traditional group process facilitators monitor the group and facilitate communication through the use of manual group process techniques; GSS group process facilitators monitor the group and are concerned about which manual or technological tool to use. GSS facilitators may also take on the technical facilitation role and so may encounter even more role overload. The effects and costs of such overload should be considered for both research and in practical meeting planning.

Process-oriented research may be one useful way to create more efficient and effective research (Cummings 1982, p. 572). For example, the Benbasat et al. (1993) review of published work between 1981 and 1991 reports that multiuser GDSS studies typically show higher levels of user satisfaction when a facilitator works with the group during the decision-making process. This “outcome only” finding points to the need for more process-oriented research. What process is occurring as a result of facilitation that leads to better outcomes? Clawson et al. (1993) and Niederman et al. (1995) have delineated facilitation behaviors and Anson et al. (1995) have looked at different results for high versus low quality facilitators (as rated by users), but more distinctive work will study the effects of actual GSS facilitator behaviors with an eye for empirically studying how group process is improved, and whether group process is improved in an impartial manner. Both facilitator communication behaviors and group process intermediate outcomes (e.g., individual’s knowledge of the problem, breadth of alternatives considered, existence of strategic goals) need to be studied, along with the more traditional measures of final performance, group consensus, etc.

## Conclusion

Organizations are functioning in an environment in which groups and group communications offer the possibility of increased organizational efficiency and effectiveness. However, the use of groups and teams adds increased complexity to organizational functioning. GSS provide an electronic communication tool that, through changes in both communication channels and decision processes, may positively enhance organizational outcomes (e.g., faster decisions using a greater breadth of information). The key to achieving these benefits is acknowledging the importance of each of the component parts of the sociotechnical system. This includes the social and technical features of GSS groups that provide the technical and group process facilitators great opportunities for influence.

GSS are not the only sociotechnical system where facilitation becomes an important (if not critical) part of organizational functioning. Indeed, as organizations look to technology to enable new organizational forms, it is important to consider the implications of facilitator influence in the broader context beyond GSS. All sociotechnical systems by definition have a technical component—a component which will require (to a degree) the presence and participation of a technically knowledgeable individual to “run” the technology for system users. This necessity of having a technical facilitator to run the technology raises, for all sociotechnical systems, the specter of inappropriate influence (whether intentional or not) on the part of the technically knowledgeable individual. Paradoxically, sociotechnical systems often are designed (like GSS, for example) to use technology to prevent inappropriate or unwanted human influence on the final outcome of an organizational process (Bostrom et al. 1993); the inevitability of facilitator influence suggests that no technology can ever totally insulate an organizational process from human influence. Even in systems where the most intrusive form of facilitation has been removed (i.e., group process facilitation), human influence can still play a role through the initial design of the technology, from assumptions regarding the technology and its use (e.g., Adaptive-Structuration Theory, DeSanctis and Poole 1994), or through the activities and decisions of technical facilitators and operators. Sociotechnical system users must consider each of these areas in terms of their intended and unintended effects for the task at hand.
