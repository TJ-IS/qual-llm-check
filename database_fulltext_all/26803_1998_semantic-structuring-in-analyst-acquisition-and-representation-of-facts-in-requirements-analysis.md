# Semantic Structuring in Analyst Acquisition and Representation of Facts in Requirements Analysis

George M. Marakas • Joyce J. Elam

University of Maryland, Department of Information Systems, College Park, Maryland 20742 gmarakas@mbs.umd.edu

Florida International University, Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Miami, Florida 33199 elamj@fiu.edu

he determination of information requirements is one of the most crucial stages in the software design and development process (Montezemi 1988). It is during this stage that the greatest degree of interaction occurs between the analyst and the user (Lauer et al. 1992). Despite the system development method employed, the functional success of many aspects of requirements determination ultimately rests on how well the user(s) and analyst(s) communicate (Holtzblatt and Beyer 1995). The purpose of this paper is to report the results obtain from a laboratory experiment that investigated the effects of a semantic structuring process of inquiry on the process of interview-derived information acquisition and the subsequent overall correctness of the logical representation of the facts obtained. The study focused on the specific question types used by systems analysts and the role their semantic construction played in representing the information flows in a business system. Three underlying semantic patterns of questions emerged from the analysis. The results showed that certain question types were associated with increased accuracy of logical representations regardless of analyst experience level. Further, the semantic and process patterns that emerged were also directly related to accurate representation of facts and demonstrated an experience-level independence. The results indicate that disciplined questioning strategies are not necessarily learned from practice and they can be improved via structured training. Each of the patterns provide insight into the questioning process employed and the effectiveness of different strategies of inquiry. Implications for both the practitioner and the academic research communities with regard to analyst interview behavior are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Many of the mechanical complexities of information systems analysis and design have been lessened by the advent of computer-based development tools such as CASE (Computer Aided Systems Engineering). Despite this increased assistance, however, we still have much to do on the “people” end of the process. One of the greatest problems the system designer must face is the need to completely define the requirements of the information system under development (Zmud et al. 1993). The determination of information requirements is one of the most crucial stages in the software design and development process (Montezemi 1988), and it is during this stage that the highest degree of interaction occurs between the analyst and the user (Lauer et al.

1992). Regardless of which system development method is employed, the success of many aspects of information acquisition and system requirements determination ultimately rests on how well the user(s) and analyst(s) communicate (Holtzblatt and Beyer 1995).

Many information system (IS) failures can be traced back to a lack of specific and correct information requirements (Cooper and Swanson 1979, Davis 1982, Kydd 1989). Conversely, many IS successes can be traced to the proper identification of information requirements early in the design process (Mittermeier et al. 1982; Boehm 1976, 1981). The gathering and organization of system requirements, regardless of methodology employed, is a complex activity that is often prone to error. One study (Jenkins et al. 1984) found that over 50% of the systems reviewed had problems necessitating a return to the requirements analysis (RA) phase due to important requirements being missed the first time. Boehm (1973) found that the relative cost of correcting an error increases exponentially with the project phase in which the error is detected. A requirements determination error that is not found until the phases of development can cost 10 to 100 times as much to fix as it would cost had it been found during the specification phase.

While the IS literature is rich in prescriptions for structuring the requirements determination process, such prescriptions tend to be explicated at a more macro (coarse granularity) level with little focus or discussion on the more micro (fine granularity) issues such as just how, and when, to perform the steps outlined in the method. In addition, despite the plethora of approaches contained in the literature, two general characteristics seem to emerge. First, little empirical evidence is provided regarding the effectiveness of a particular method or its relative value over another. Second, the vast majority of methods, despite their categorical differences in ontological perspective, make use of some form of direct, verbal communication technique, such as interviewing, with the end user(s). Noticeably missing, however, is any micro-level focus on the process of questioning or question construction and subsequent answer interpretation during information gathering. Through an increased understanding of why a particular activity within the overall the requirements determination process, such as interviewing, may produce incomplete information, we might gain insight into how we can improve the delivery of quality information systems.

The purpose of this paper is to report the results of a study that investigated the effects of a semantic structuring process of inquiry on interview-derived information gathering and the subsequent overall correctness of a derived logical model. Semantic structuring refers to the application and use of specific question types for the purpose of obtaining specific Interviewderived information or realizing particular information-seeking objectives or goals in an interview context. Specifically, the study focused on investigating the particular question types used by systems analysts and the role their semantic construction played in understanding the various information flows in a business system. More generally, the results of this study provide insight into how analysts can improve their effectiveness in gathering interview-derived information from end-users necessary to develop a conceptual understanding of an information system. To gain such insight, we focused our research on observing the relationship between such conceptual understanding and one of the most widely used activities by the analyst during the process of arriving at conceptual understanding. Two research questions serve as the motivation for this research:

(1) What specific question types, from a semantic construction perspective, are most effective in gathering direct interview-derived information related to the construction of an accurate model representing the logical data flows within an organizational or business subsystem?

(2) Can an inquiry process composed of specific question types lead to obtaining greater accuracy in the collection of relevant direct interview-derived information related to business subsystem processes and data flows?

In this paper, we define requirements determination as a set of semistructured and related activities performed to facilitate the gaining of an understanding of a business problem. Banbury (1987) argues that the process by which requirements determination is satisfied is not a singular deterministic procedure carried out by following a tightly prescribed set of steps.

Rather, it is an emergent process that contains many underlying methods, some of which may be structured and prescribed. Turner contrasts the process of requirements determination via classical life-cycle versus evolutionary approaches such as prototyping. He points out that while the two approaches differ significantly in their component elements and underlying activities, the quality of the resulting system is nonetheless determined in part by the degree to which the designer understands the requirements or needs of the users. He argues that both approaches rely on some form of effective dialogue between the analyst and users to elicit an accurate and useful expression of needs. Further, Davis (1982) notes that “simply asking prospective users of the information systems to specify the requirements will not suffice,” (p. 5) because of the limitations that all individuals have with regard to information processing and dealing with the complexities often associated with multiple patterns of interaction between analysts and users. In keeping with these arguments, the conduct and results of this study are not intended to provide a fully explicated requirements analysis process or to even suggest that the useranalyst dyed is the only activity manifest in the data gathering process. The basic premise here is simply that the use of a semantic structuring process will enhance the ability of the analyst to elicit and organize user requirements, the user to explicate his or her requirements, and both parties to more effectively, efficiently, and accurately communicate with each other.

The organization of this paper is as follows. Section 2 provides a review of the relevant literature regarding the information requirements specification process with a particular emphasis on the activities of information seeking and validation contained therein. In addition, both conceptual and empirical literature related to the psychology of questioning is reviewed and used as an additional theoretical basis for the formulation of testable hypotheses. Section 3 presents the hypotheses tested in this study and details the experimental design and methodology employed. Section 4 presents the results obtained, and § 5 discusses identified limitations associated with the interpretation of these results, discussion of the findings, and implications for both research and practice.

## 2. Information Gathering in Requirements Determination

The literature regarding information requirements determination spans over three decades, beginning with the classic work by Ackoff (1967) and continuing to the present day. While significant attention has been paid to the acquisition of information requirements, until recently the effort has been primarily directed toward providing conceptual approaches to the problem— with little evidence, either anecdotal or empirical, being offered in support of the wide variety of methods presented. Banbury (1987) contends that there is inadequate knowledge regarding the interaction between the analyst and user during information requirements determination. Galliers (1987) argues that insufficient attention to the importance of the requirements determination process exists in both research and practice. Vitalari (1985) used protocol analysis in an exploratory study intended to define the various types of knowledge used by experienced systems analysts during the requirements determination phase. His findings positioned interviews with users as one of the top 20 knowledge categories identified as important by both high- and low-rated systems analysts. Keil and Carmel (1995) develop the concept of customer-developer links; those techniques and/or channels that allow users and analysts to exchange information. They identify the one-on-one interview as a common activity in the development of both inhouse and commercial consumption software. Despite the acknowledged problems associated with useranalyst interaction, direct interview of the user still remains as one of the primary means of obtaining the necessary information (Awad 1985, Holtzblatt and Beyer 1995).

Churchman and Schainblatt (1965) recognized the value of effective communication and mutual understanding between a specialist and a generalist. Our literature is replete with studies demonstrating the importance of effectual communication between analysts and users (Bostrom 1984, 1989; Cronan and Means 1984; Guinan 1988; Kaiser and King 1982; Thomas and Carroll 1981; Vitalari and Dickson 1983; Guinan and Bostrom 1986; Agarwal and Tanniru 1990; Fazlollahi and Tanniru 1991; Byrd et al. 1993). Notwithstanding this apparent trend toward more empirical work, a great deal of understanding is yet to be gained regarding the specific techniques and activities associated with the information gathering activity within requirements determination.

In its simplest form, information gathering can be performed in one of two fundamental manners: (1) scanning (Aguilar 1967), browsing through information without a particular goal in mind or question to answer; or (2) focused search (Huber 1991), where the investigator is looking for specific information or the answer to a specific question. Both forms of information gathering are prevalent during the requirements determination phase of design. Information gathering can be viewed as a multimodal activity in that a wide variety of sources such as documents, forms, manuals, memoranda, observation, and direct interviewing, among others, are used to begin to paint a picture of the both the current system and the requirements of the proposed system.

Edstrom’s (1977) work found a negative correlation between ineffective communication and system success. Salaway (1987) trained system developers using an organizational learning approach to communication. In her analysis of transcripts of conversations between analysts and users, Salaway found that unstructured, ad hoc communications between the two parties were often error-prone and manifest of dysfunctional characteristics that could result in costly errors in system designs. Zmud et al. (1993) offer mental imagery, or the development of pictures in the mind’s eye, as a scenario-based technique for information generation and gathering in the requirements analysis process. Their results suggested that this approach may be useful in situations where requirements determination involves future thinking. Curtis and his colleagues (1988) conducted interviews of key personnel involved in large software development projects. Through their analysis they identified communication bottlenecks and breakdowns at all levels as one of the three major problems affecting software productivity and progress. They point out that such problems are not new and have been chronicled over several decades (c.f. Weinberg 1971, 1982). Recently, Vandenbosch and Higgins (1996) looked at the relationships between information acquisition activities and mental models.

Weinberg (1982) laments the fact that systems analysts “spend a lot of time exploring the behavior of others, but very little time analyzing their own behavior” (p. 71). He points out a number of pitfalls and dysfunctions that can manifest themselves through improperly constructed questions or questioning patterns. Kennedy and Mahapatra (1975) noted that “any significant gains in the overall management of projects intended to analyze information requirements must begin with a better understanding of the detailed processes by which individual analysts can learn the needs of individual users. Understanding the building block is requisite to designing the wall” (p. 72).

The classic work of Boland (1978) identified the interaction process between users and analysts in terms of protocols of interaction. He proposed that “the designer and the manager have different skills, role perceptions, and mental schemas which are combined through their protocols of interaction into a single design statement,” (p. 887). His work compared protocols couched in traditional approaches to information gathering such as one-sided interviews to those involving an alternative approach of initial information sharing and critique. His findings suggested that the alternative protocols of interaction produced higher quality designs with important advantages to implementation. He concluded that the process in which user involvement is effected is as important, if not moreso, than simply ensuring that user involvement takes place. Further, the structure of the information gathering process can have a material effect on the quality of the information gathered and thus, the quality of the derived system. Different interaction processes produced requirements that differed significantly In both quality and potential for implementation.

Dorsey and Koletzke (1997) argue that while a skilled interviewer may be able to elicit a great deal of information from a user, without some type of format or structure the value and accuracy of the information may become greatly diminished. They offer a simple framework of three question types that can be used by the interviewer to structure a “tree of information” (p. 189): (1) validation questions, e.g., “Is this what you are saying?”, (2) horizontal questions, e.g., “Are there other areas about X that we need to discuss?”, and (3)

vertical questions, e.g., “Tell me about X. Is X important?”.

In this framework the analyst gathers and structurally organizes data by moving in either a horizontal or vertical direction thus gathering information in a manner that it can be easily organized into a tree structure. The validation questions are used to confirm the various relationships modeled during the interview. While the simplicity of this framework appears initially appealing, no empirical evidence to date is available to support or deny the claims of success offered by its creators.

Waldron (1986) argued that despite the fact that interviewing skill is an essential element in the determination of the completeness, accuracy, and reliability of collected knowledge, most interviewers are rarely trained in specific, practical interviewing methods. Turner (1987) asserts that while we clearly can design information systems, we often do not know exactly what we did to effect a successful outcome, or vice versa. As such, Turner posits that this lack of knowledge stands as the single greatest barrier to improving our ability to increase system development productivity and improve the quality of the information requirements activity:

“It is symptomatic of our lack of understanding of the process of design that the most useful skill in accomplishing this activity, interviewing, is not included in most courses or textbooks” (p. 110).

We agree with Bostrom (1989) that “communication processes should be regarded as an integral part of systems design.” Such processes are not intended as a replacement to system design methodologies but rather as a complement to them. To this end, Bostrom has suggested that effective communication patterns can be used to enhance frameworks, such as Davis’ (1982) contingency approach for selecting information requirements methodologies.

One method proposed in an attempt to improve the quality of information gathered during questioning is called structured interviewing. While no consensus exists among researchers as to the precise definition of the term structured interview, a general set of characteristics have been identified that serve as an adequate definition. Structured interviewing involves the deliberate application of systematic and predetermined rules for observation and evaluation (Motowidlo et al. 1992). Further, these rules must be applied for all interviews conducted in the same or similar contexts.

Agarwal and Tanniru (1990) conducted empirical work in this area motivated by the need to develop more effective structured interviewing methods to gather information requirements. They conducted a laboratory experiment to determine the effectiveness and efficiency of a structured method over an unstructured approach. They hypothesized that the structured interviewing technique would be more efficient, allow novice knowledge engineers to perform better, extract more subjective and qualitative knowledge, and provide the interviewee with more assistance in recall and anticipation than the unstructured technique. The results provided support for all the hypothesized effects except improved assistance in recall and anticipation by the interviewee. Further, the subjects using the structured technique outperformed both novice and expert analysts using an unstructured approach to knowledge acquisition. Agarwal and Tanniru point out that the results of a single experiment cannot be interpreted as conclusively demonstrating the value of a particular technique, but argue that their results provide an empirical basis for a more focused investigation of the existence of an underlying normative model for elicitation of information system specifications.

Despite this call for a more micro-level research approach toward information gathering techniques during requirements determination, the literature does not reflect a unanimously positive response. In fact, several efforts have been made to “avoid” the interview altogether by utilizing techniques that either do not require, or are minimally dependent upon, direct elicitation of responses from the user (Montazemi and Conrath 1986, Gutierrez 1989, Zmud et al. 1993).

Regardless of position for or against direct-interview information gathering it appears that the general assumption is that the questioning process is a natural, instinctive one and that the ability to generate a series of questions intended to supply the information necessary to model a problem environment is acquired simply through repetitive domain experience and not through any specific processual design. While the IS literature provides little empirical evidence to either validate or refute this assumption, additional insight can be obtained from a literature base contained within the field of cognitive psychology.

## 2.1. The Psychology of Questioning

Dillon (1982) has argued that semantic patterns of questioning evolve and tend to prevail over time in many well-defined knowledge domains and structures. He offers courtroom questioning and psychotherapy, among many others, as examples of knowledge domains where the type of questions and the structure of asking and answering have become highly domain-specific and Institutionalized. This suggests that a domain-specific, or possibly even a subdomainspecific, questioning structure exists or tends to develop over time in response to the need for deriving certain types of information.

To facilitate the study of inquiries and interrogatives from a semantic rather than lexical perspective, taxonomies have been developed for classifying questions (D’Andrade and Wish 1985). Graesser et al. (1988) extended the work of Lehnert (1978) by developing a semantic taxonomy of questions identified by 18 unique inquiry dimensions (Table 1). Through their taxonomy, researchers have a tool for the analysis of both question generating mechanisms and techniques.

The focus of recent psychological research on questioning has been to discover the relationship between the questions asked by a person and that person’s cognitive representations and processes (Lauer et al. 1992). The commonly held belief concerning question asking is that questions are tools that are intended to effectively extract information from a respondent (LaFrance 1992). This notion has been recently challenged by a number of question theory researchers (Axley 1984, LaFrance 1992). LaFrance argues that questioning does not extract existing, fully formed knowledge but rather generates an explicit invention of it that can be subsequently transformed into the rules comprising a knowledge base or the flows of data in a process. Complementary to this perspective, Winograd and Flores (1986) argue that viewing questioning as a process of information extraction misses a critical facet. This assumption, which they refer to as “a rationalistic representation hypothesis,” suggests that the information needed by the interviewer already exists in the respondent’s head in a fully formulated model configured in a precise manner. In contrast, they suggest that “meaning always derives from an interpretation that is rooted in a situation” (p. 111):

“. . . the essence of language as a human activity lies not in its ability to reflect the world, but in its characteristic of creating commitment. When we say that a person understands something, we imply that he or she has entered into the commitment implied by that understanding” (p.123).

This argument speaks directly to the importance of employing a sound, structured interviewing process during requirements gathering. The primary objective during the interview is to transfer facts from one model form (the respondent’s recollection) to another model form (the DFD). Because the two forms of the models do not necessarily represent a good fit and because meaning is often a subjective function of the situation rather than a more objective situation-independent interpretation, an ad hoc inquiry approach may lead to unexpected or inaccurate outcomes.

As an example, Loftus and Palmer (1974) found that the modification of a single word within the body of a question can significantly alter the respondent’s recollection of an event. In their classic experiment, subjects viewed a film of a traffic accident and were then asked a series of questions related to what they saw including the following: “About how fast were the cars going when they hit each other?” Other subject groups received identical questions except certain words were altered within the body of each question. In the previous example, the verb hit was replaced with either smashed, collided, bumped, or contacted. Despite the fact that all respondents witnessed the same accident at the same time, the wording of the question dramatically affected their reports. Subjects given the word smashed estimated the highest average speed of the vehicles while those receiving contacted estimated the lowest. One week after the experiment, all subjects were called back for additional questioning. It was found that the wording of the questions previously posed caused subjects to uniquely reconstruct their memories of the accident. When asked whether or not they recalled seeing broken glass at the accident scene, significantly more subjects in the smashed group said they did than those in the hit group. The findings of this study indicate that the results obtained from a question are inextricably tied up with how it is being asked, and possibly even when it is being asked in relation to other queries and replies.

## 3. Hypotheses Tested

Lauer et al. (1992) clearly demonstrated the importance of questioning in the gathering of data flow information. The primary results of their study showed a significant relationship between the number of questions asked and the accuracy of the resulting logical DFD (r 4 0.54, p , 0.01). More important, however, their results also suggested the existence of a pattern of questioning that appeared to be strongly associated with the degree of correctness of the DFD score. Therefore, it appears that the four stage process and semantic structuring pattern that emerged from their exploratory work provides a fruitful starting point from which to begin a focus on fine granularity methods of interview-derived information acquisition during requirements determination.<sup>1</sup>

Thus, a general hypothesis was stated, Hypothesis H1.

Hypothesis H1. The use of the semantic structuring model of inquiry will result in greater accuracy in the construction of logical representations over the use of unstructured inquiry.

The literature suggests that a measurable difference can be expected between the questioning patterns and types of questions employed by low domain experience questioners versus those with a relatively high degree of domain experience (Miyake and Norman 1979, van der Meij 1990).<sup>2</sup> Turner (1987) argues that experience can act both to define an initial acceptable solution set and influence the interpretation of facts and other sensory data. He suggests that a process of addition or subtraction based on experience is used by observers to construct a perception of the phenomenon before them. As such, a wide and rich range of experiences is needed if the important elements of the design problem are to be recognized. Building upon this premise, the inexperienced analyst must be given alternative mechanisms for derived essential elements of an existing design if he or she is to ever gain the necessary experience to internalize such skills.

As such, it seems reasonable to expect the effect of using the specific process model of inquiry to vary across experience levels. If, as we tend to implicitly assume in our texts and literature, information gathering techniques, such as interviewing, are skills that are developed and refined through experience, then the effect of a particular strategy of inquiry should not be as large in high experience analysts than in the lower experience analysts. Thus, three separate subhypotheses are offered that are derived from the general hypotheses stated in H1:

Hypothesis H1a. The use of the semantic structuring model of inquiry will result in greater accuracy in the construction of logical representations by low experience analysts over the use of unstructured inquiry.

Hypothesis H1b. The use of the semantic structuring model of inquiry will result in greater accuracy in the construction of logical representations for high experience analysts over the use of unstructured inquiry.

Hypothesis H1c. The relative degree of improvement resulting from the use of the semantic structuring model of inquiry in the correctness score of a logical representation will be greater for low experience analysts than for high experience analysts.

It has also been proposed that high experience may serve to exacerbate the likelihood of guessing or inferring facts rather than inquiring about them (Miyake and Norman 1979). As such, an experienced analyst may be more likely to “imagine” facts rather than explicitly ask for them during information gathering. In the context of logical representation construction, such guessing may result in the mapping of theoretically feasible, yet nonexistent elements or data flows. A theoretically feasible element or data flow is one that could logically exist within the context of a particular system, but in fact does not. The proposed semantic structuring model of inquiry serves to facilitate the acquisition of specific facts as well as the individual and collective confirmation and verification of those facts and their relationships. If successful in its support of these activities, the use of the model should serve to reduce the potential for guessing or inference of factual information. Thus, it is hypothesized:

Hypothesis H2a. The number of feasible but nonexistent elements mapped in the construction of a logical representation will be significantly greater for those using no specific inquiry strategy than for those making use of the semantic structuring model of inquiry.

Hypothesis H2b. The number of feasible but nonexistent elements mapped in the construction of a logical representation will be significantly greater for high experience subjects than for low experience subjects, regardless of the inquiry method employed.

Extending the argument from Hypotheses H2a and H2b, recollection of similar business process analyses previously experienced by an analyst could conceivably result in the ability to correctly ascertain the existence of some elements or processes without actually inquiring directly about them. Logically, it also seems reasonable that if a question is explicitly asked about the existence of a theoretically feasible but nonexistent element, such items will not be included in the final logical representation. Given this, it appears fruitful to investigate the extent to which this ability to correctly infer or derive data elements and flows based on past experience may, or may not, contribute to the accuracy of the logical representation. If this ability is salient, then we should find evidence of accurate representation of one or more elements without a specific question being asked to support it. If it is not manifest, however, then a systematic relationship should be found between the explicit presence of a question inquiring about a specific data element and the probability of correctly representing it.

Hypothesis H3. The presence of a question inquiring about a specific data element or flow will be significantly related to the probability of identifying it correctly regardless of the inquiry method employed.

Should the semantic inquiry strategy under investigation prove to be more effective than an unstructured method, one additional area of investigation must be explored. It is possible that certain subjects will be successful in generating a highly accurate logical model despite the fact that they are not, consciously or otherwise, employing the specific semantic structuring model of inquiry under test. The strength of the underlying semantic pattern that emerged in the Lauer et al. (1992) study suggests that a common pattern of questioning is manifest when seeking information related to component elements of a logical model. Further, given the findings regarding the relationship between level of experience and propensity to infer, it is possible that experience level may not be the primary operational determinant of accuracy in a logical representation. Given this, subjects that accurately construct a logical model without employing the method of inquiry under study may tend to exhibit patterns of questions similar to other such subjects. In addition, given the hypothesized effect of the proposed inquiry process combined with the finding of Lauer and his colleagues, these unconscious but nonetheless effective patterns of inquiry may be similar to those subjects who used the structured approach and successfully created an accurate representation of the system. To investigate this further, two exploratory hypotheses are stated.

Hypothesis H4. Subjects using no specific inquiry strategy that are successful in constructing an accurate logical representation will exhibit semantic questioning patterns similar to other successful subjects.

Hypothesis H5. Subjects using no specific inquiry strategy that are successful in constructing an accurate logical representation will exhibit questioning patterns similar to the semantic structuring model of inquiry.

## 3.1. Experimental Design and Methodology

A laboratory experiment was conducted. The design consisted of two control groups and two experimental groups of 10 subjects each. The two control groups were composed of low experience and high experience analysts respectively using an unstructured (no specific imposed strategy) elicitation approach (CLx and CHx). The two experimental groups were composed of low experience and high experience analysts, respectively, using the specific semantic model of inquiry (ELx and EHx). The low experience subjects were recruited from students nearing completion of the undergraduate MIS program at a southern university, and the high experience subjects were recruited from professional system analysts and software designers employed at several major corporations. Figure 2 contains a graphical representation of the experimental flow.

A pretest questionnaire collected subject demographic data including age group, level of education, and years of system analysis or software development experience. In addition, the instrument required the subjects to respond to a series of questions intended to measure their abilities regarding basic logical data flow diagramming methods and techniques and to quantitatively determine their individual level of expertise within the knowledge domain. A correct response rate of 80% or greater (determined as acceptable during pilot testing) on the skills portion combined with an assessment of term of practical experience in systems analysis work was required to qualify a subject for assignment to one of the four treatment groups. Following completion of the pre-test instrument all subjects were randomly assigned to one of two subject groups based on their experience level.

Subjects assigned to groups ELx and EHx participated in an approximately 30-minute training session in the application of the semantic structuring inquiry process and were instructed to apply this strategy during the course of the experiment. They were also given about 30 minutes to perform some brief (non-ISrelated) practice exercises intended to clarify the steps and question types contained within the process. During this practice session the subjects were allowed to question the research coordinator for clarification of any point regarding use of the inquiry process or specific question type but were advised that no questions pertaining to the pending task would be answered.

All subjects received a copy of an abridged description of an existing business system (see Appendix A). The information contained in the abridged description was such that the subjects were able to discern only the process elements for the business system (verified during pilot testing). The subjects were told that this abridged description represents some information provided to them during the early stages of requirements determination and that their task was to develop a thorough understanding of this portion of the system and then represent it in the form of a logical data flow diagram. The subjects were further instructed that the primary means by which they would be able to develop this understanding was through interviewing a “key employee” of the organization that has detailed knowledge of the system under study. This person would be able to answer any questions relating to either the business system’s processes or data flows. Further, the subjects were told they would be given as much time as they considered necessary to conduct the interview but they would not be able to conduct more than one interview with the company employee.

The “key employee” of the organization under study was one of four external associates who thoroughly studied the complete system description and successfully completed both the nomenclature skills portion of the pretest instrument and the construction of the logical DFD representing the system. In addition, although the associates were made aware of the need for their responses to the subjects’ questions to be as accurate as possible, they were not aware of the specific research questions under study, the experimental design, the hypotheses being tested, or the treatment group assignment of any subject. In addition, the key employees were assigned such that each was interviewed an equal number of times per group. This was to ensure that no bias was introduced by the employee as a result of any individual characteristic or response style. The subjects in this experiment were tested on an individual basis and were not grouped with any other subjects other than during administration of the pretest questionnaire for all subjects and the inquiry process training for ELx and EHx subjects. A post hoc regrouping of subjects by key employee revealed no significant differences across the four respondents with regard to any specific variable of interest. The entire manipulation lasted no more than 2.5 to 3 hours for any subject.

All subjects were given whatever time they desired to review the abridged business system description, the task instructions, and prepare their questions before conducting the interview with the employee. Though no record of the time spent by each subject on each review activity was kept, the total preparation time for each subject was recorded. During this review period, the inquiry process subjects were allowed to compare their questions to the taxonomic construction of the recommended question types in each phase of the inquiry process. They were also provided a folder of materials that included a check list for using the model of inquiry, a pad of paper, and a mechanical pencil to be used during the interview.<sup>3</sup> The control subjects received the identical folder of materials with the exception of the inquiry model checklist. Both folders were made to appear identical so as not to bias the key employee’s responses by alluding to any differences amongst the interviewers. In addition, physical conditions during the interviews were such that the key employee would not be able to directly observe the materials being referred to by the subject. Following completion of the interview, each subject was provided with graphing paper and a symbol set template and was asked to construct the logical DFD from the information obtained.

To ensure the specific inquiry process under study was used by the experimental subjects, a manipulation check was conducted. The same method used to map the questions asked by all subject-analysts to the semantic taxonomy (described in the next section) was used to determine the extent to which the experimental subjects made use of the inquiry process model. To be considered a usable response a subject had to have made use of the model, both in terms of question type and sequence of steps, at least 70% of the time. This level of use was established from information obtained during the initial pilot study. It was found that all pilot subjects receiving the semantic structure training, regardless of experience level, were able to adhere to the process at least 70% of the time. Several pilot runs were conducted at levels of adherence ranging from 50% to 100%. Pilot subjects mandated to adhere to the process at either 90% or 100% reported extreme cognitive stress and an inability to concentrate on the respondent. The pilot subjects at the 80% adherence level did not report any significant cognitive overload but were only able to perform consistently at the 70% level. Pilot subjects at the 70% adherence level confirmed this consistency, thus this level was chosen as appropriate. All pilot subjects, however, expressed their belief that the 100% adherence level was readily attainable given enough time to gain comfort and experience with the process.

A review of the transcribed interviews and subsequent taxonomic coding of questions asked revealed only one experimental subject used the model less than the required 70% of the time (approximately 60%) and was therefore disqualified from inclusion in the data analysis. An additional subject was recruited, tested, and included with that group’s data.

## 4. Analysis and Results

The initial phase of the data analysis required the questions asked during the interviews to be mapped onto a matrix formed by one of the four inquiry stages of the model under study<sup>4</sup> and one of the 18 semantic categories of the Graesser et al. (1988) taxonomy. Three independent judges were used to perform the classifications, none of whom had any knowledge of either the experiment or the source of any individual response or diagram.

Following the independent classification process a comparison of the categorizations of the three judges was performed and any disagreements regarding a particular question were resolved, wherever possible, through discussion. The results of a test of interrater agreement (Kendall’s W) indicated a high degree of consensus among the judges (W 4 0.923, p 4 0.0027), suggesting that the mapping of the questions into the matrix formed by the inquiry stages and semantic taxonomy was successful.

The correct solution consisted of 9 elements and 19 data flows (see Appendix B). A score was calculated by awarding 2 points for each correct component of the DFD, making a total of 56 points possible. The awarding of two points for each element was necessary since some of the flows transfer two items of data. If the subject identified only one of the two, a single point was awarded. No points were either awarded or deducted for any nonexistent processes or data flows included in the diagram; however, a separate analysis of those elements was later conducted. All subject diagrams were compared to the correct solution diagram and were graded by an independent judge. Any issues requiring clarification were referred to the principal researcher for resolution. A random check of 20 of the 40 scores assigned by the independent judge was performed by a second judge with no discrepancies from the solution diagram detected.

Analysis of the various demographics associated with each treatment group revealed no unexpected significant differences among them that could materially or adversely confound the results obtained. Further, tests of normality revealed no significant deviations in the data collected.

Support for the two exploratory hypotheses, H4 and H5, was determined using a form of discriminant analysis known as a K-means cluster analytic classification (Aldenderfer and Blashfield 1984, MacQueen 1967, Wishart (1982). Unlike the more common hierarchical agglomerative methods (e.g., Ward’s 1963 method) which create a dendrogram of nested relationships, Kmeans clustering is classified as an iterative partitioning method. The essence of the procedure is depicted in Figure 3.

More specifically, the reassignment of each case to the cluster with the nearest centroid was based upon an inclusive combinatorial approach. Following the assignment of each case to the cluster with the nearest centroid, the case was not removed from further consideration (inclusive) and the centroid of the cluster was recalculated after each change in its membership (combinatorial). This iterative evaluation and assignment process continued until all cases had been assigned to one and only one clustering.

The logic behind the iterative partitioning methods is derived from the statistical theory associated with multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) (Aldenderfer and Blashfield 1984, p. 47). In effect, the method is concerned with detecting the homogeneity of clusters represented in a multivariate space. That is, the K-means procedure attempts to minimize the variance within each cluster. In addition, although sample size is an issue of concern with regard to obtaining meaningful or interpretable clusterings, the mathematical requirements for such procedures are relatively simple. Further, it must be noted that the clusters created are the result of iterative comparisons amongst all the cluster members with regard to the classification variables. As such, statistical noise is not an issue. The technique will always produce clusters that best represent the primary similarities amongst the cluster items. The researcher is charged with the responsibility of determining whether the differences amongst the clusters formed are interpretable. The basic underlying assumptions are that the number of groups is at least 2 or more, there are at least two cases per group, and that the number of discriminating variables is less than the total number of cases minus two (Klecka, 1980). Table 5 and Figure 4 contain the comparative results of the final clusterings for semantic and stage patterns for all subjects.

In analyzing evidence of support for Hypothesis H4, three distinct clusterings emerged. Subjects grouped on the matrix formed by the number of questions asked in each of the 18 categories within the Graesser et al. (1988) semantic taxonomy and the number and type of questions asked within each of the four stages of the process model of inquiry under test. In addition, two distinct clusters formed based on the subjects’ individual DFD scores. Because of the wide disparity associated with the total number of questions asked by the subjects, the number of questions was normalized by dividing by the total number of questions asked by the subject into the number of questions asked in each taxonomic category and each process model stage before any analysis was conducted. This was intended to minimize problems associated with matching patterns of questioning between subjects who did not ask a large number of questions with those who did.

As can be seen from the graphs in Figure 4, the three unique patterns are differentiated by the frequency of use of a given semantic category and the frequency of focus with regard to the information being gathered (stage of inquiry). Pattern A subjects focused their attention almost equally between verification of known facts and identifying specific concepts and procedures (39% verification versus 43% concept completion plus instrumental/procedural inquiry). Further evidence of this strategy is found by looking at the relative percentages of inquiry within each stage of the process model. Pattern A subjects concentrated the majority of their effort (69%) toward identifying specific processes and identification of entities with the remainder almost equally divided between their associated subtasks (16%) and confirmation of known facts (15%). This approach appeared to be the most balanced approach among the three clusters and was, therefore, labeled as the balanced pattern.

In contrast, Pattern B subjects spent better than half of their effort (55%) in some form of verification and only slightly more than one-third of their focus (34.08%) in gathering information of a specific conceptual, procedural, or causal nature. The percentage of stage-specific frequency spent also reflects this questioning strategy. As did Pattern A subjects, Pattern B subjects concentrated more effort in the identification of specific entities (37%) than in any other stage of the inquiry process but focused equal effort in identification of specific processes (24%) and confirmation of all known facts (25%). This pattern suggests that the method of inquiry employed concentrated on the confirmation of individual facts as they were collected from the respondent but did not focus on the confirmation of the relationship of those facts and their collective contribution to the overall logical model of the system. The logic of this conclusion stems from the high overall frequency of verification combined with the relatively low frequency of confirmation of subtasks. A review of the transcripts for Pattern B subjects further confirmed this characterization. This evokes a scenario of the subject-analyst “focusing more on the trees than on the forest” and, thus, was labeled the micro-inquiry pattern.

In Pattern C, subjects made twice as much use of verification of known facts than all combined inquiry related to identification of concepts, procedures, and causalities (64% versus 30%). This same pattern is found in a review of the relative areas of focus by Pattern C subjects in the process model stages. Active confirmation was employed slightly more than half of the time (50.79%), while identification of entities, specific processes, and associated subtasks was performed during the remainder (49.21%). This pattern reflects a generally cautious, confirmatory approach to the inquiry and can, therefore, be best described as a confirmatory strategy.

## 5. Limitations

While the results obtained here appear encouraging with regard to furthering our understanding of the role of semantic questioning patterns in information gathering, as with any empirical endeavor, limitations must be taken into consideration when interpreting them.

The first area of limitation lies with the decision to employ a laboratory setting for the study. It was felt that given the lack of strong prior research in this area, the laboratory provided the most effective setting for isolating and controlling for possible confounding variables while simultaneously effecting the desired treatment. Despite this, it should be noted that the laboratory setting does not allow for certain realities to exist that may serve to affect the subject’s actions. Most notably, is the fact that all subjects were aware that this was a simulated interview with a simulated employee of a fictitious company. Further, the subjects were told that the key employee would never knowingly provide a false answer to any question. This eliminates any concern on the part of the interviewer with regard to the potential for the respondent to either lie or knowingly leave out relevant information. This condition, therefore, may serve to reduce or inhibit rigorous confirmation and cross-checking activity by the subject conducting the interview.

The sample size of 10 subjects per cell must also be discussed within the context of possible limitations. The decision to choose this cell size was made due to the potential difficulties associated with obtaining a large number of high experience subjects. The significance levels obtained during the pilot study indicated an effect size of moderate to high suggesting that 10 subjects per cell would be sufficient. In addition, it should be noted that Hypotheses H1, H2a, and H2b were tested by comparing the combined results of the two control (CLx and CHx) and two experimental groups (ELx and EHx). This resulted in a sample size of 20 subjects per cell. The chi-square analysis for Hypothesis H3 used all 40 subjects as data points. Further, the k-means cluster analysis, despite its relative insensitivity to sample size as explained in the previous section, used all 40 subjects in the initial creation of clusters. Finally, tests of normality were conducted to determine the satisfaction of such underlying assumptions and no significant deviations in the data were detected. Given the strength of the results it can be assumed that the power of the tests conducted were of sufficient strength. Nonetheless, it must be noted that a larger sample size would have served to further strengthen the experimental design.

The subjects used in this study, while representing the required characteristics as dictated by the experimental design, nevertheless must be acknowledged as imposing certain limitations on the outcomes. While clearly possessing the skill set expected of an entrylevel analyst (as evidenced by the performances obtained in the pretest skills test), the student subjects may not possess the same motivations as an actual analyst in the early stages of his or her career. As such, they may not approach the task with the same rigor that a real analyst might. In addition, since we do not yet know at what point in an analyst’s career the formation of some type of personal interviewing technique begins, the students serving as low experience analysts may actually have an experience level that is lower than reality. While this situation did not appear to manifest itself in the results it must nonetheless be acknowledged.

The high experience subjects also represent a point of possible limitation. They were all obtained as volunteers from several large corporate concerns and were made aware of the request for participation by their supervisor or department head. As such, their motivation to participate was not driven by the same forces that affected the student participants who were recruited by the principal researcher from a pool of students that had successfully completed their undergraduate MIS coursework. While the students may be motivated to perform well for a former professor the professionals may not feel the same need to give serious attention to the project. While no instance of lack of seriousness or attention was encountered by the principal researcher with any subject, this possibility must, nonetheless, be accounted for in interpreting the results.

Another possible limitation is the constraint placed on the experimental (ELx and EHx) subjects to make use of the prescribed inquiry process at least 70% of the time during the interview portion of the experiment. While it was initially felt that this constraint could result in the discarding of a number of subjects’ responses, only one such situation occurred. Nevertheless, it must be noted that the retraining of an individual in skills that place heavy cognitive burdens on that individual can result in many behavior patterns that otherwise might not be manifest. Since this was the case here, the imposition of such a constraint may have additional effects on the outcomes that we do not yet fully understand. Given this, future research should be cognizant of both the need for and the problems associated with such constraints on analyst behavior and should strive to gain a greater understanding of their effects on the analyst.

An additional area of limitation lies with the decision to use a single method of logical system representation. The use of a logical DFD may have induced certain biases in the outcomes that would not be manifest (or that manifest themselves in a completely unpredictable manner) in other forms of logical representation such as an object-oriented approach. Future research should investigate the effects of semantic structuring on alternative methods of representation and on information gathering activities in other stages of development. In addition, the “translation” of the semantic structuring approach must be investigated in other settings such as JAD and with other design methodologies such as object-oriented approaches. Until such investigation is conducted, the generalizability of the technique beyond the analyst-user dyad remains unknown.

Finally, the exploratory nature of the study must be recognized. Although each hypothesis advanced was built upon existing theory, the various theoretical foundations used were developed outside the information systems requirements knowledge domain. While the theories suggest applicability across a wide variety of situations and domains, one must nevertheless view the results obtained here in more of an exploratory sense rather than in any definitive sense. It is felt, given the encouraging results, that IS researchers should view the subject of questioning and semantic patterns in interview-derived information requirements analysis as an identifiable and separate stream of research, such that theories developed in other knowledge areas may be ratified within the IS realm and new theories that serve to explain phenomena found uniquely within the IS environment may be developed.

## 5.1. Discussion

The principal objective of this study, to provide insight into the answers to the two stated research questions, appears to have been realized and, as such, should lead to a greater level of understanding into the activity of interviewing during information gathering for requirements determination. Several implications, both theoretic and applied, arise as a result.

The general Hypothesis H1 and its associated subhypotheses were focused on answering the second research question regarding the relationship between a specific semantic structured method of inquiry and the accuracy of the logical representations derived from the information gathered through the process. Support for the general hypothesis was strong, indicating the value of applying a specific semantic and stage inquiry process to information gathering activities. Prior research (e.g., Agarwal and Tanniru 1990) has successfully shown the value of structuring the interview content over unstructured ad hoc approaches. Most academic and corporate-sponsor training programs in systems analysis acknowledge and emphasize the importance of interview structuring from a content perspective. The results obtained in this study, however, provide evidence of value in structuring the inquiry from both a semantic and a process perspective as well. Further, the results provide evidence in support of the theoretical underpinnings of questioning theory regarding the effect of the semantic content of a question, in addition to subject content, on the quality of information derived from a given respondent. This suggests that the assumed common skill set of an analyst associated with “interviewing the user” so often prescribed in the IS literature may not be so common after all. Thrusting an analyst of any experience level into an information gathering activity without a priori structuring from a content, semantic, and process standpoint may actually serve to increase the potential for erroneous requirements specifications, thus increasing the likelihood of less than the desired level of system performance, effectiveness, and overall success. Further evidence of this can be found by reviewing the results obtained in the high experience treatment groups (CHx and EHx). Despite the demographics of these subjects with regard to experience as system analysts, they were unable to achieve a mean accuracy level in excess of 75% rather than the 90% or 95% level wisdom would suggest.

The three subhypotheses each provide additional support for the need to apply semantic structuring to the inquiry process but through more narrowly focused lenses. Support for both Hypotheses H1a and H1b suggest that the application of semantic and process structuring to the inquiry process has measurable value that may be experience resilient, or somewhat independent of the experience level of the analyst. The implication of these findings should not be understated. Often the application of structure to a pending interview is viewed as both context specific and individual in nature. The more senior analysts often believe they have developed certain reliable, though empirically untested, “techniques” that can be regularly incorporated into their Information gathering activities but cannot easily be explained or borrowed by others. Conversely, the more junior analysts may spend as much or more time in attempting to “discover” or develop their own style of inquiry than in actually gathering the information necessary to complete the requirements specification. In either case, the potential for incomplete or inaccurate specification is increased.

The potential for error by both low and high experience analysts can be seen from the results obtained in this study. While the results indicate a clear and statistically significant 50% improvement in accuracy, regardless of experience level, when employing a semantic structuring approach, only 86% mean accuracy levels were achieved (high score centroid of 48.15 out of a possible 56). Further, the high experience analysts who did not employ any specific inquiry approach (CHx) were only able to achieve a mean accuracy level of slightly over 70% (39.40 out of a possible 56); only 6% better than their low experience counterparts (CLx). One should note that the information gathering task provided to all subjects can clearly be categorized as “textbook” and not overly complex compared to many analysis scenarios faced in today’s organizations. Implicit in these findings, then, is the intrinsic possibility of error associated with the direct interview process commonly found in a user-analyst dyed. The application of an inquiry structuring technique that is experience independent or, at the very least, experience resilient could contribute significantly to reducing the risks associated with highly individualized or ad hoc inquiry processes.

The semantic structuring process appears to further serve as a source of reduction for errors associated with guessing or inference based upon past experience or logic. Hypotheses H2a and H2b investigated the relationship between the presence of logical errors in the information gathered and the use of semantic structuring. The results suggest that the application of semantic and process structuring serves to reduce the number of technically feasible errors associated with the construction of a logical design specification. This issue is not of a trivial nature. The increasing complexity of today’s globally integrated information systems allows for a myriad of physical solutions to the design of a system. The design of the logical model, however, is constrained by the need to accurately determine what is actually occurring in an implementationindependent manner. If data flows or processes are included in the initial logical model that do not exist in reality, the chances for a flawed physical design increase significantly. Regardless of design methodology employed, the need for direct verbal communication between one or more analysts and one or more users is both essential and inevitable. As such, the proliferation of technically feasible but non-existent data flows or processes in the initial logical model is likely. It is important to note that the need for extended validation of all requirements through methods such as structured walkthrough is not eliminated by semantic structuring, but rather the results indicate that there is less to be validated if analysts conduct their direct interview information gathering activities using this technique. While the elimination of errors associated with either misinterpretation or omission can never be completely eliminated, it appears that semantic structuring may serve to mitigate the proliferation of such errors.

The results obtained in the testing of Hypothesis H3 may initially appear to be somewhat intuitive. It seems quite reasonable to assume that one must ask a question if one is to obtain an answer. Counterintuitively, however, the results also suggest that accurate logical representations of business processes may be more closely related to the method of inquiry itself than with any given level of experience. Thus, the formal training of analysts at all experience levels within the organization in the process of semantic structuring combined with an organization-wide set of standards for direct interview structuring and conduct should make a measurable contribution to the quality of system design.

From this it appears that the skills associated with both structuring the interview and the actual interviewing of the user should command more attention and finer granularity of method than has been previously afforded the activity in most of the systems analysis literature or texts.

In many ways, the results obtained in testing the remaining two exploratory hypotheses serve as the most interesting and informative of the study. Upon initial review, it appears that there is a definite relationship between certain semantic constructions and the accuracy of logical representations created from the derived information. Beyond this, however, a closer analysis allows for several additional insights to the answer to emerge.

Recall from Table 6 that the majority of the highscore subjects were found to have employed the balanced inquiry approach (17 balanced, 4 micro-inquiry, 5 confirmatory). The same analysis using the low-score subjects shows that only four were found to have used the balanced approach, while seven used the microinquiry strategy and three employed a confirmatory inquiry process. From this, a relationship of measurable proportion appears to exist between the type of question employed and the accuracy of the derived logical representation. This adds further support to the interpretations offered from the preceding hypotheses that semantic structuring of the inquiry process plays an Important part in obtaining accurate interviewderived information during the requirements specification process.

Looking to the frequencies associated with the stage of inquiry across the three strategies we can see the coupling of derived subject matter to the semantic constructions employed. In other words, we find that the type of question or its frequency of use is related not only to the accuracy of the logical representation but also to the need to acquire various types of information during the process as well. Both the balanced and micro-inquiry approaches focused a similar amount of effort on the identification of subtasks; probably the single most important stage of inquiry in preparing a logical representation. The confirmatory subjects concentrated twice as much of their efforts in confirmation as did the micro-inquiry subjects and 3.5 times as much as their balanced approach counterparts. Further, they focused nearly twice as much attention upon confirming predetermined data elements than they did in identifying the various subtasks associated with the logical process. Finally, they spent almost four times as much attention in the confirmation stage than in identifying the specific context processes that served to make up the system under study.

In contrast, both the balanced and micro-inquiry approaches focused almost twice as much attention upon specific process identification as did the confirmatory strategy. While the balanced approach appears to be the most successful given that the majority of high scoring subjects tended to cluster in that pattern, more high-scoring subjects appeared in the confirmatory cluster than in the micro-inquiry pattern (5 of 8 versus 4 of 11). Admittedly, these classifications cannot be considered comprehensive in nature. Nonetheless, they suggest the possibility that, in the absence of any proactive structured identification of specific data elements such as that found in the balanced approach, intensive confirmation may serve to increase accuracy. This exploratory portion of the study was intended only to begin the examination of these issues and, as such, should and did generate more questions for research than it answered.

## 5.2. Conclusion

Future research into the underlying semantic patterns identified through this effort should focus on several key areas of investigation. First, additional examination is needed with regard to refinement of the model proposed in this study. In addition, it should be noted that this study focused on but one of several information gathering activities necessary for the complete requirements determination for an information system. Further, the context within which the information-seeking activity is performed, and the associated dynamics of user-analyst interaction will logically dictate the degree to which a semanticstructuring approach can, or should, be applied. The advent of modern design methodologies such as joint application development (JAD), rapid application development (RAD), or object-oriented development (OOD) have created a number of contexts in which the process of information-seeking may be better left unstructured or more fluid that a semantic-structuring approach would allow. Nonetheless, effective verbal communication with the user will continue to play an integral part in the successful development of information systems, regardless of design methodology, for some time to come and new methods of capturing the information within these contexts should be investigated. Given this, a number of questions must be addressed regarding the applicability of semantic structuring across various information gathering contexts. For example, is the semantic structuring of an interview effective in the gathering of physical data elements for the construction of an entity relationship diagram? If so, what is the characteristic structure of the inquiry model? Can semantic structuring be understood such that once the context of an information gathering activity is established a model can be designed to maximize the interviewer’s efforts? What are the similarities or differences in underlying semantic patterns across the various information gathering steps within a particular development methodology? Or across methodologies?

To answer these questions, research must be directed toward identifying the context or condition(s) under which a particular question type should be employed in gathering interview-derived information. This type of knowledge has been acquired in other knowledge domains and would appear to be equally useful in an IS setting. Specifically, each inquiry needs to be analyzed so that it can be identified not only by its semantic construction but by the type of information that can be derived and the expected form of response to that type. From this, both semantic and process structuring of the information gathering activity for a given system could be computer-assisted through the design of expert systems intended to provide initial construction of the interview based on certain analystspecified parameters. Such expert system facilities could then be incorporated into modern CASE platforms as additional tools for use during the requirements determination activities. Further, the applied community could be provided with prescriptive information regarding the content of training programs intended to facilitate introduction of the interview method into their information gathering activities. The education process for professional analysts should be restructured to place greater emphasis on the formal delivery of interview training and techniques rather than assuming the existence of inert communication skills. This restructuring should extend to all phases of information gathering and requirements analysis with an eye toward improving upon existing techniques that are based upon assumed skills rather than actual demonstrable skills. To that end, both research and the applied communities should work together on the development of instruments that can be useful in both evaluating and developing communication skills. Finally, the outcome of this stream of research could serve to Inform both the DSS and database design communities in their efforts to improve the design and usefulness of such systems.

## Appendix A Complete Description of Music-By-Mall Business System

The Music-by-Mail Record Club advertises cassettes and CDs in a variety of magazines. Most orders are submitted by magazine subscribers who complete and send coupons to the mail order company. All mail arrives at the receptionist’s desk. The receptionist sorts and distributes the mail to the appropriate departments. Mail orders and letters requesting order cancellations are forwarded to an orderentry clerk in the sales department.

The order-entry clerk initially checks the availability and the price of the ordered items, and, if necessary, mails a back-order notice to the customer. This clerk also takes orders from customers directly by phone and forwards all fillable orders to the credit-clerk.

When the order entry clerk receives a letter requesting an order cancellation (or a cancellation by telephone), the status of the order is first determined. If the customer order has not been invoiced, the order-entry clerk informs the warehouse that the order should be canceled and then informs the customer that the cancellation has been completed.

When fillable orders are received by the credit clerk, the customer’s credit status is checked. Orders are approved and an orderconfirmation letter is sent to those customers with good credit standing. Customers with bad credit standing are sent a payment-overdue notice requesting prepayment. The credit clerk forwards approved orders to the warehouse.

The warehouse fills the approved order and updates the inventory availability. A packing slip is sent with the packaged order to the customer, and a shipping notice is sent to the accounts receivable department.

Accounts receivable bills the customer for the products shipped. This department also maintains the invoice data files, updating them to reflect charges or payments received. Payments are received in the mail and delivered to accounts receivable by the receptionist. A payment receipt is sent to the customer.
