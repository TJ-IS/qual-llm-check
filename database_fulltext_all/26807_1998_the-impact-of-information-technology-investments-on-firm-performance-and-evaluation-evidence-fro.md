# The Impact of Information Technology Investments on Firm Performance and Evaluation: Evidence from Newly Industrialized Economies

Kar Yan Tam

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Hong Kong kytam@usthk.ust.hk

he impact of information technology (IT) investments on firm performance has been the subject of active research in recent years. However, findings of almost all studies are based on data collected in the United States. Little work has been done elsewhere to validate these results and to see if they are applicable across national boundaries. In this study, we fill this gap by comparing four newly-industrialized economies (NIEs) with regard to the impact of IT capital on business performance. Secondary data collected from various sources are used to assess the impact over the period from 1983 to 1991. Findings based on four business measures and a market valuation model based on Tobin’s q are reported. While the current results are consistent with work done in the United States in general, discrepancies among the four NIEs are observed. Combined with findings from previous work, three pieces of evidence seem to emerge that are generally observed across country boundaries. First, IT investment is not correlated with shareholder’s return. Second, there is little evidence that the level of computerization is valued by the market in developed and newly-developed countries. Third, there is no consistent measurement of IT investment as indicated by the mixed results across different performance ratios. Modeling and measurement concerns expressed in previous U.S.- based studies are also observed in our comparative study. Our findings provide a starting point to accumulate a body of comparative studies for the development of a theory that links IT investment, firm performance, and macro factors such as national technology policy in an integrated framework.

## 1. Introduction

The business value of information technology (IT) investments has received considerable interest from both academics and the business community in recent years. The central question is whether the tremendous amount of IT capital invested in the last few decades has had any impact on the performance of the investing firms. So far, empirical findings reported in the literature are mixed.

These discrepancies, in many cases, could be traced back to the inconsistency of performance measures. This is clarified by Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994), who argued that IT value can be measured in three dimensions: productivity, business performance, and consumer value. Drawing on theories from various disciplines, they argued that these three dimensions are not necessarily causally related. Based on IT spending data collected in the United States, they showed that while IT capital is correlated with firm-level productivity, it has little correlation with business performance. On the other hand, IT capital has created tremendous value that is passed on to consumers (Brynjolfsson 1996). Classifying IT value into different dimensions and assessing its impact accordingly will eliminate much of the ambiguity in comparing results from existing studies.

Another means to validate findings is to compare IT impacts across different countries. So far, almost all empirical studies were conducted in the United States. It remains unclear whether findings such as those reported in Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994) are applicable to other countries, especially those that have been spending heavily on IT in recent years. As Rosenzweig (1994) suggested, the external validity of a study depends on the extent to which the theorized or observed relationship among variables can be generalized to other settings. A key dimension of external validity is international generalization. In view of the size of the worldwide IT market and the rapidly changing share distribution at the global level, it is important to expand the scope of investigation to include other countries. As Dedrick and Kraemer (1995) have observed, few studies compare a number of countries systematically.

A review of articles in major IS journals indicates no firm-level empirical work has been done outside the United States on this subject. There are only a few comparative studies on IT investment so far—mainly pioneer work on the relationship between IT investment and economic growth (King et al. 1992, 1994, 1995; Kraemer, Gurbaxani and King 1992; Kraemer and Dedrick 1994, Dedrick and Kraemer 1995). However, these studies reside at the country level with a focus on national IT policy and economic development.

In this study, we attempt to fill this gap by empirically examining the impacts of IT capital on business performance in four newly industrialized economies (NIEs) in Asia: Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaysia, and Taiwan. Recent work by Tam (1995) has reported IT impact varies in these four countries with Singapore and Taiwan showing significant productivity gains from IT investments. This report will focus on the impact of IT on business performance and market valuation. In order to make the results comparable, the variables and methodology used here will be compatible with previous studies conducted in the United States.

## 2. Firm Performance Measures

As suggested by Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994), productivity improvement and business performance are not necessarily correlated. Improvements in productivity will not necessarily translate into gains in profit measures and increase in the stock market value of the firm. Clemons argued (1991) that IT has become a competitive necessity rather than a source of competitive advantage. This is premised on free market entry. Following Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994), we hypothesize that the same argument could be applied to countries with a reasonably open market for IT products, implying the rejection of the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis H1. IT investment is correlated with business performance ratios of the firm.

Hypothesis H2. IT investment is correlated with the stock market return of the firm.

Hypothesis H1 relates to the ex-post evaluation of firm performance, while H2 is concerned with the market valuation of returns from investing in IT. In an efficient market, securities should be valued to yield the required return. Since there is little evidence that IT investments are more (or less) risky than other investments, they should generate similar returns.

For H1, three commonly used performance ratios are used: (1) Return on Equity (ROE), (2) Return on Asset (ROA), and (3) Return on Sales (ROS). For H2, the Total Shareholder Return (TSR) is used. These measures are well understood in business and are widely used to evaluate the quality of an investment. Furthermore, they have been used in previous IT impact studies (Alpar and Kim 1990, Cron and Sobol 1983, Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1994, Strassmann 1990, Weill 1992), thus creating a basis for comparison. Definition of each variable can be found in Table 1.

To estimate the impact of IT capital on these ratios, a regression model is developed as shown below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} B P R _ {i t} = \sum_ {t} \sigma_ {t} D _ {t} + \sum_ {k} \theta_ {k} I _ {k} \\ & + \alpha \mathrm{ln} (C _ {i t}) + \beta \mathrm{ln} (\mathrm{BV} _ {i t}), \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathrm { B P R } _ { i t }$ is one of the four ratios, i.e., TSR, ROE, ROA, or ROS; $C _ { i t }$ is the computer capital, $\mathrm { B V } _ { i t }$ is the book asset value of firm i at time $t , D _ { t }$ and $I _ { k }$ are dummy variables used to control for year and industry effects with t[[1983, . . 1991] and $\mathbf { k } \in [ 1 , \dots , 5 ]$ representing five different industries. In a recent IT investment study, Harris and Katz (1991) reported a relationship between firm size and IT investment. The book asset value is used to control for firm size effect,<sup>1</sup> which is a common practice in empirical studies investigating the relationship between accounting variables and financial ratios (Banz 1981, Dimson and Marsh 1986).

## 3. IT Capital Measurement and Data Sources

## 3.1. Measurement of IT Capital

In this study, we treat computers as a class of capital stock. The stock includes major processors and peripherals that provide the underlying computing infrastructure of a firm. Data are obtained from annual issues of the Asia Computer Directory (ACD), which publishes descriptions of hardware installations of major firms in Asia. ACD is the leading source of IT information for vendors and users in Asia with a publication history of more than 15 years. An example of a typical inclusion is shown in Figure 1.

Since the acquisition date and price of a computer system are available in ACD, computer capital stock can be derived by using the perpetual inventory method (Jorgenson 1989). The method requires aggregating different flows of services obtained from computers of different ages. The service provided by a computer in each year after its purchase is proportional to the initial investment in the system. Each system is referred to as a vintage of capital. The flow of capital services is a quantity index of capital inputs from different vintages. The fact that computers are general-purpose machines allows the assumption of perfect substitutability to be made. This enables us to define total computer capital stock as a weighted sum of past investments. The weights are given by a schedule indicating the decline in relative efficiency of each vintage as given below:

$$
C _ {i t} = \sum_ {\tau = 0} ^ {\infty} C _ {i t \tau},\tag{2}
$$

where $C _ { i t }$ is the total computer stock of firm i at time $t ,$ and $C _ { i t \tau }$ is the corresponding computer stock with age s at time t. Equivalently,

$$

$$

where $d _ { \tau }$ is the relative efficiency index for a computer with age $\tau ,$ and $A _ { i t }$ <sub>1s</sub> is the amount of computer investment for firm i at time $t - \tau , \tau = 0 . . . <$

Depending on the nature of the capital goods, $d _ { \tau }$ can be constructed using either a straight line or a declining balance method. To reflect the rapid advancement of computer technology, a declining balance pattern efficiency is used:

$$
d _ {\tau} = (1 - \mu) ^ {\tau},\tag{4}

An annual depreciation rate of 20% is consistent with the generally accepted depreciation method for computer assets in the four economies.<sup>2</sup> While a computer may not depreciate in the physical sense, it will become obsolete as system software and applications create an increasing demand for greater processing and storage capability over time. The depreciation rate attempts to capture the rate of obsolescence of a computer in relation to the expected services it delivers. Replacements are treated as new vintages in our calculation.

## 3.2. Adjusting the Quality and Price Effects of IT Capital

d represents a general rate of depreciation and should be adjusted for quality improvement and price decline of computers over time. These adjustments can be incorporated by multiplying the computer stock by a price deflator derived from published price indices for computer capital, e.g., the computer price index published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA).<sup>3</sup> Note that the depreciation rate represents the rate of obsolescence in meeting the users’ needs over time, while the price deflator reflects the declining price of the acquisition of computer capital of the same type. While we have reasons to expect that the depreciation rate and price deflators may vary among the four economies, it is more likely that variation of the rate of obsolescence $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \mu )$ is more uniform than the rate of price decline of computers. The price deflator is related to factors such as distribution channel, tax incentive, pricing structure, import/export policy of IT products as well as the price elasticity dynamics of IT users (Tam 1996) in each economy. These factors are likely to impose a trend of price decline which is unique to each economy. To address variation in price deflator, four annual price deflators are used in our analysis: 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, and 0.3. When combined with the 20% depreciation rate, the total depletion period for computer capital ranges from 5 to 7 years. This is in line with the life cycle of most computer models and is consistent with our observations on the change in computer installations in the firms listed in ACD over time.

## 3.3. Data Sources

Although small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) dominate the four economies, we will focus on large firms so that comparisons can be made with U.S. studies that were based on large enterprises. We select firms that were publicly listed in the stock exchanges as the potential pool of firms. Each firm is then matched with the entries reported in annual issues of ACD which contain information about hardware installations for major firms in these four countries. As shown in Figure 1, it includes information about the firm’s business as well as the model and cost of major hardware and peripherals. However, not all firms in ACD are publicly listed. To obtain related financial information, each firm in ACD is matched with entries in two financial databases: PACAP and GV. Firms appearing in both sources are then retrieved. As shown in row 3 of Table 2, the sample so collected represents some of the largest firms in the Asia Pacific region in terms of market capitalization. These firms also represent heavy users of IT in the four economies.

Each firm in the dataset is assigned to one of the five industries: service, industrial, trading, transportation and utility. Classification is done by first grouping firms according to their line of business as stated in ACD. For firms operating in more than one industry, they are classified to the industry that generates most of its revenue. Almost all firms in our sample have a distinct line of business which falls into one of the five industries. The classification is then validated by matching the firms in each group with the listing classification in their local stock exchanges. Inconsistencies are resolved and firms are reassigned in consultation with local experts. It is found that over 95% of the firms are correctly classified in the first step. The dataset is nonbalanced, with the number of firms increasing between 1983 and 1991. Multiple occurrences of the same firm during the investigation period is accounted for by using year dummies in the regression model. Only firms with all the information required to estimate Equation (1) are retrieved. Others with missing data are discarded. Summary statistics of the data sample are shown in Tables 2 and 3.

## 4.1. Market Value of the Level of IT Capital

As indicated by the small adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ for some NIEs, there may be factors which affect share return and business performance that are not captured by the model. To fine-tune our analysis, we adopted the Tobin’s q theory proposed in Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994). The theory postulates that the long-run equilibrium market value of a pool of assets held by a firm equals its book value. Any discrepancies between the two values suggest either the market is in disequilibrium or that there are sources of rent-generating assets that do not appear on the books. Tobin’s q has been suggested for use in evaluating firm performance (Wernerfelt and Montgomery 1988) and many have adopted this method to evaluate such intangible corporate assets such as R&D (Cockburn and Zvi 1988).

We extend the use of the Tobin’s q to the study of IT investment. In particular, we hypothesize that computer capital measured as a percentage of book value is valued by the market. A number of rationales are given for the market to value IT investment. IT is considered as a means to save costs by automating labor-intensive operation processes. Also, IT is generally regarded as an enabler for business process reengineering whose aim is to streamline the structure and operation of a firm. ## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The impact of IT investment on firm-level performance in four NIEs is empirically investigated in this paper. Using previously adopted methodology, we test the correlation between computer capital and a number of performance ratios and determine whether the market value IT investment by firms. The findings reveal differences across NIEs and across performance measures.

When compared across performance measures, one can observe that results on shareholder return are very consistent while results on other ratios are mixed for the four NIEs. The finding of null impact on shareholder return does not contradict with the mixed results of the accounting ratios. Such a difference could be explained by the general belief that shareholder return represents the market’s expectation of the future profitability of a firm while the other three ratios (ROE, ROA, and ROS) are mainly historical measures. The current work indicates that this is indeed supported empirically across the four NIEs.

The advantage of cross-national research is to allow researchers to establish the generality of findings and the validity of interpretations across different nations (Kohn 1987). Given the increasing amount of IT investment worldwide, results based on a single nation need to be tested and confirmed in different national settings to establish external validity. Cheng (1994) defines two types of research findings with universal applicability that can be obtained from cross-nation studies. The first type is called a context-embedded relationship, which uses societal context as a predictor in the relationship. Confirmation of a contextembedded relationship automatically implies universal acceptance of the findings because societal variables have been explicitly accounted for. The second type is a context-excluded relationship, which employs data from a single nation. Its confirmation is restricted to the nation studied. To investigate its universal generality requires testing the relationship across different nations. So far, almost all empirical research on IT investment is based on a single nation and can be classified as context-excluded. The validity of the findings as universal knowledge needs to be evaluated in different national settings. A key findings of the current study is that market expectation of firm performance is not correlated with IT investment. Thus, when combined with findings by Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1994), there is strong evidence that the relationship could be established with universal applicability. In other words, the level of IT investment is not an explanatory variable in shareholder return when the market is efficient. The latter condition implies the ability of investors to discern discrepancies in national settings and incorporate them in calculating the expected return. In an efficient market, investors will consider discrepancies in accounting procedures and evaluate a firm based on its future profitability rather than on its historical performance. The short-run competitive advantage such as first mover advantage of new IT applications cannot be sustained for a prolonged period. Eventually, the novel applications will become routine operations and will be copied by competitors in the industry. Thus, all gains in performance will disappear eventually. In a forward-looking market, this information is properly discounted in the security’s price. An insignificant impact is expected and this is demonstrated in our findings in the four NIEs

On the other hand, our findings suggest that the relationship between IT investment and accounting ratios could be confounded by institutional factors and are societal specific. The findings on ROE, ROA, and ROS, when combined with empirical studies conducted in the United States, indicate the impact of IT investment on firm performance is not a direct one and is likely moderated by the different management orientation and financing decisions unique to a national setting. For example, Lee and Blevins (1990) reported determinants of accounting performance vary considerably between ratios and between industrialized and newly industrialized countries. They found that performance depends very much on the debt ratio, capital intensity, export activity, firm size, plant and equipment, and level of diversification. These moderating variables are in turn affected by deeper cultural and societal factors. This is consistent with a study by Child (1981), which reports that macro-level variables, including IT, have become more similar across countries, whereas micro-level variables related to culture and social behavior have become dominating factors that limit the generalizability of findings of studies across social systems. The effects of these variables may even dominate other IT factors such as information system management and development. Recent work has attempted to incorporate variables in the value chain as intermediate links between IT investment and accounting-based performance ratios (Barua et al. 1995). To the extent that societal factors are adequately represented, findings of these studies could have limited generalization to other countries. The mixed results obtained for ROS, ROE, and ROA in the current work indicate the impact of IT on firm performance should not only be moderated by intermediate variables but should also be investigated in conjunction with any societal factors that may have an impact on firm performance.

Furthermore, impact of IT investment can be affected by the amount and effectiveness of IT deployment. An important factor is the type of IT deployment. A closer look at the IT applications, which are also published in ACD, reveals that the majority can be classified as transactional systems that are noninnovative in nature. This is consistent with a study by Dos Santos et al. (1993), which found that the financial market values IT investments selectively. Using U.S. data, they reported that the financial market only values IT investments that are innovative in nature. Other less innovative IT investments will have no or even negative effects. The dominance of transactional oriented systems is of no surprise since all four NIEs still lag behind the United States in developing strategic and innovative applications. As transactional systems are well understood and their impacts on the bottom line better known, investors should expect the same returns as on other capital investments.

While the learning effect of firms will likely introduce a lag on firm performance (Brynjolfsson 1993), its impact may not be uniform over time. This may explain the discrepancies between the one-year lag and no-lag results of H1. As suggested by Attewell (1992), a large knowledge barrier needs to be overcome in the adoption of business computing. Once an IT application is implemented and institutionalized, the lag will gradually shorten or even disappear. As suggested by Peffers and Dos Santos (1996), a one-year lag may not capture all of the performance impacts because firms may not realize all of the benefits of such investments for several years due to the organizational learning effect. Following this line of reasoning, we suspect that the lag effect may still exist for IT capital. However, it may not be easily detectable especially when the analysis is restricted to a constant lag period (e.g., one year) and aggregated IT captial is used in the current study.

Similar to U.S. firms (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1994), market valuation of IT capital as a rent-generating source does not exist in three of the four NIEs. In these NIEs, IT is treated no different than other assets. The potential gains from investing in IT are fully recognized and valued by the market. On the one hand, the significant results observed in Malaysia, though very small, indicate the potential of IT may not be fully realized. Such a potential has value and is reflected in the share price. We speculate that the significant result observed in Malaysia could be attributed to the different state of IT development in the country. The economic development measured by GNP still lags behind the other three NIEs. Using the IT maturity model reported in a survey of a number of Asian countries (APO 1994), which include the four NIEs studied here (Table 12), Malaysia was still in the initial stage of IT development during the period investigated in this study. The other three NIEs were already in the Growth and Maturity stage. Thus, it is hypothesized that Malaysian firms may not have completely institutionalized their IT applications and the benefits may not have been fully realized. Furthermore, it is observed that a large percentage of Malaysian firms in the sample are industrial firms with a heavy concentration in agriculture and plantations. The combined effect may be that a substantial gain in business performance is expected when these companies automate. Thus, an increase in the level of computerization is matched with a corresponding increase in the valuation of Malaysia firms.

Our findings also provide implications on the continuing debate regarding the effectiveness of marketdirected vs. plan-directed strategy on the development of the IT industry at the national level. The impact of government policy on IT investment can be addressed from two perspectives. The first focuses on the need of an IT policy to facilitate IT production and infrastructure development at the national level. The second focuses on the relationship between national IT policy and IT investment impact at the firm level. While previous work has documented the importance of national IT policy in IT production in a number of Asian Pacific countries (Dedrick and Kraemer 1995), little research has been done to investigate whether the existence of such a national policy will benefit firms to have better leverage on their IT investments to improve their performance. As indicated in the current study, there is no clear evidence that this is indeed the case. According to Dedrick and Kraemer (1995), Hong Kong and Malaysia are labeled as market directed, while Taiwan and Singapore are labeled as plan directed. It is interesting to note that although the four economies have different governmental technology policies, IT investment has no significant impact on shareholder return for all four NIEs. Also, the results of ROE, ROA, and ROS are mixed within the marketand plan-directed groups.

A basic premise for establishing a nationwide IT policy (i.e., plan-directed) is that a centralized effort led by the government will better facilitate the diffusion and application of IT for economic growth, implying implicitly that a positive impact will be realized at the firm level. However, as indicated by our results, there is no solid evidence that government-led IT policy has any significant impact on firm performance.

Unlike IT production, the impact of IT policy on firm performance, even if it does exist, is likely to be indirect. Many objectives of a national policy such as creating a nationwide telecommunication network, enhancing computer awareness, facilitating technology transfer, providing R&D training, expanding and improving the quality of IT education certainly play key roles in the advanced application of IT by firms. However, their positive impacts on firm performance may be undermined by other factors including tight regulation of key industries such as telecommunications and banking, outdated legal and copyright measures, and import restrictions commonly found in developing countries.

Although it is a first attempt at considering the impact of IT in a cross-national context, this paper has several limitations that need to be recognized in interpreting its findings. First, the current study represents the empirical testing of IT capital on performance, and no attempt is made to provide a theoretical framework. Yet, the empirical findings can be used to guide and facilitate the development of such a theoretical model. Second, our definition of IT capital includes only computer and peripherals without accounting for software and input from IT labor. This may underestimate the contribution of IT capital and consequently overestimate its impact. This problem is addressed by using different computer deflators in our analysis. For example, a 15% price decline factor, which underesti mates the actual price decline, would offset the underestimation problem in IT capital. As our findings indicate, the results are not sensitive to changes in the computer deflator. Nevertheless, there is a risk that the amount of IT capital is not exact but an approximation. Third, the data sample is compiled from a relatively large number of secondary sources. Although consistency checks are conducted to ensure the data are of acceptable quality, data problems may still exist. This in fact is a recognized challenge for researchers doing comparative studies in developing countries. For example, no consistent deflator for capital investment (e.g., by industry, assets) exist for the four economies. Thus, we have to resort to the GDP deflator in our study. While IS researchers may not be familiar with the data sources used here, the sources are publicly available. In fact, the Global Vantage and PACAP databases are published by U.S. institutions. Unlike many IT impact studies that employ proprietary firm data, it is possible for others to replicate and extend the current study in the future.

Our findings also reveal similar measurement problems and modeling concerns cited in previous studies. One concern is the unit of analysis. Some of these firms have multiple business units and their use of IT could be quite different. For these firms, the impact of IT on business performance will be averaged out because our data set is limited to aggregated IT investment at the firm level and performance measures at the business unit level are not available. Similarily at the industry level, effects of specific industries are averaged out because our classification of firms into five industries may be too coarse for some firms.

## 6. Conclusion

Our objective is to document empirical findings of the relationship between IT investment and firm performance in a way which facilitates systematic comparison with studies done across national boundaries. The main contribution of this study to IT impact studies is to investigate IT investment at the firm level in a crossnational setting. Our findings add to the validity of observed relationships between IT investment and firm performance reported in previous studies. While it is important to explain the difference between NIEs as they relate to environmental factors and government policy, this is beyond the scope of the current study. However, work in this area is far from complete. More work needs to be done in theory development and empirical analysis, especially through comparative studies at the country level. Our study provides a starting point for accumulating empirical findings across national boundaries for the development of a theory that links IT investment, firm performance and macro factors such as national technology policy and environmental factors in an integrated framework.<sup>4</sup>
