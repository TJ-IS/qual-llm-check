# Firm Characteristics and Investments in Information Technology: Scale and Scope Effects

Sanjeev Dewan • Steven C. Michael • Chung-ki Min

University of Washington Business School, Mackenzie Hall, Box 353200, Seattle, Washington 98195 sdewan@u.washington.edu

Department of Business Administration, University of Illinois, Urbana Champaign, Illinois 61820 smichael@uiuc.edu

Department of Economics, Hankook University of Foreign Studies, Seoul, Korea cmin@maincc.hufs.ac.kr

his paper conducts an empirical analysis of the link between the scale and scope of the firm and information technology (IT) investments, emphasizing the role of IT in coordination and control. We extend the economic production function framework to include variables related to the boundaries of the firm, including related and unrelated diversification, vertical integration and growth options, and we estimate the resulting model on a data set based on annual surveys of IT spending by large U.S. firms, conducted by Computerworld during the period 1988–1992. Our results suggest that the level of IT investment is positively related to the degree of firm diversification, perhaps reflecting the greater need for coordination of assets within diversified firms. We further find that related diversification demands greater IT investment than unrelated diversification. Firms that are less vertically integrated have a higher level of IT investment. Finally, firms with fewer growth options in their investment opportunity set tend to have a higher IT investment, consistent with an agency perspective which predicts excessive IT investment by managers with “free” cash flow. Put together, these empirical relations between IT investments and firm characteristics help us better understand the role of IT in coordination and control and the choices firms make in information systems and strategy.

(Information Technology Investment; Information Systems Strategy; Scale and Scope; Coordination; Diversification)

## 1. Introduction

The fundamental choices of management in any firm are to select products and quantities that the firm will offer to which markets. These choices determine the scale and scope or boundaries of the firm. The scope of the firm can extend into related products or markets (related diversification), unrelated products or markets (unrelated diversification) or into input factor sourcing and distribution (vertical integration). The boundaries of the firm affect the need for coordination and communication, and therefore they are likely to also influence the firm’s demand for information processing and IT. The purpose of this paper is to see if there exist robust empirical relations between IT investments and firm characteristics related to the boundaries of the firm, including size, diversification, vertical integration, and growth options.

Previous researchers have analyzed the coordination requirements of alternative organization structures and examined the implications for the use and impact of IT (see e.g., Malone and Smith 1987, Malone et al. 1987, Gurbaxani and Whang 1991). In addition, organizational researchers have noted that control mechanisms increasingly employ IT (Eisenhardt 1985, Jones and Hill 1988). While this prior research has generated interesting testable implications, the analyses are largely theoretical in nature.<sup>1</sup> We empirically examine the relation between IT investments and variables that measure the scale and scope of the firm. Some of our specific research questions are: How do diversification and vertical integration affect the demand for coordination, control and IT? How does the relatedness or unrelatedness of diversification affect the need for information processing and IT? Do firms with more growth option assets require more or less IT?

The academic literature appears to have been more successful in explaining the time pattern of IT adoption and growth (see e.g., King and Kraemer 1984, Gurbaxani and Mendelson 1990), than in elucidating firm characteristics that drive IT investments. The few studies that relate IT investments to firm characteristics focus on firm size or growth in isolation and use specialized data sets based on a single firm or industry. For example, Harris and Katz (1991) examine the relationship between IT investment intensity (defined as the ratio of IT expenses to total expenses) and firm size for a sample of life insurance firms. They find that IT intensity is relatively higher for smaller firms, but it does not change systematically with the level of IT expenses itself. Other studies that link IT investments to firm size include DeLone (1981), Turner (1982), and Gremillion (1984). Sobol (1993) looks at another firm characteristic, growth, and identifies the types of information systems expenditures that are sensitive to fluctuations in the short-term financial performance of the firm.

Our integrative analysis of IT investment incorporates both scale and scope variables, uses firm-level data from a broad cross-section of the economy, and emphasizes the coordination role of IT within and across the boundaries of the firm. We adapt the economic production function framework to yield an estimation model that relates IT investment to firm diversification, vertical integration, and growth options, in addition to various input and output production variables. We test the model on a data set consisting of U.S. firms that were included in the Computerworld surveys over the period 1988–1992, covering a range of manufacturing and service industries. This survey data has been previously analyzed by Dewan and Min (1997), among others.

To preview our results, we find evidence consistent with the proposition that the relatively greater need for coordination on the part of diversified firms calls for higher levels of IT investment. Related diversification appears to demand greater IT than unrelated diversification because of the higher level of coordination required when sharing resources across related lines of business. IT investment is negatively related to the level of vertical integration, suggesting that the savings in internal coordination requirements from greater focus are more than offset by the increase in external coordination needs. We also find that firms with most of their assets in place (i.e., low growth options) tend to have a higher level of IT investment, possibly a manifestation of the agency costs of free cash flow. These results are robust to different proxy measures for key variables and to alternative specifications.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we develop hypotheses linking diversification, vertical integration and growth options to IT investments. Section 3 presents an estimation model for IT demand that combines scale and scope effects. Section 4 describes our data sources and the construction of variables. Section 5 presents empirical results and robustness checks, while Section 6 offers concluding remarks.

## 2. Firm Scope and IT

A basic premise of our analysis is that organization structures that are more coordination or control intensive also have a higher demand for information processing and IT. While non-IT mechanisms for coordination and control exist, such as face to face meetings and travel budgets, they are not perfect substitutes for IT and therefore the demand for IT will rise with increased coordination and control requirements. In our analysis, we find it useful to distinguish between internal and external coordination (Gurbaxani and Whang 1991). Internal coordination deals with the processing, sharing and communication of decision information within and across levels of the management hierarchy, as well as with the monitoring and performance evaluation required to deal with the agency problems arising from incentive conflicts in delegated decision settings. External coordination consists of the writing and enforcing of contracts with customers and suppliers as well as search, communication and transportation activities associated with acquiring input factors or distributing product. In the rest of this section, we present theoretical arguments dealing with how changes in scope variables affect coordination and control requirements and therefore the demand for IT; we take up each of the variables in turn.

## 2.1. Diversification

Diversification refers to the extent to which a firm operates in multiple lines of business. Firms diversify in response to “excess capacity” in resources or productive factors, resources which cannot be transferred across the boundaries of the firm without incurring high transaction costs (Penrose 1959, Teece 1982, Montgomery 1994). Examples of such resources include proprietary technology, market information, management expertise, or a specialized physical asset. Diversification creates new internal coordination requirements associated with the sharing of such resources across multiple lines of business or organizational units. As an example, the leverage of research and development efforts across related products, calls for teamwork, collaboration and sharing of results and findings. As another example, the diversified firm must allocate capital across operating units, which requires management committees and coordination. The increase in coordination requirements depends on the extent to which decision making in separate organizational units are contingent on one another (more on this below).

While diversification increases the need for internal coordination, it has little effect on external coordination activities. Diversification might lead to some savings in external coordination costs. For example, contractual costs can decline due to diversification because multiple legal contracts (with suppliers, buyers, etc) can be combined into one contract. Search costs might be lowered when several independent search processes can be replaced by one search process. It should be noted, however, that the vast literature on diversification has not identified savings in search or contractual costs as motives for diversification.<sup>2</sup> More importantly, much of this external coordination is reduced only at the expense of new internal coordination in the form of transfer pricing, information sharing and communication, and other internal coordination mechanisms. Applying existing resources into new lines of business creates new interdependencies within the organization. Diversification should be viewed as expanding the firm’s activities, not internalizing previously external transactions (see e.g., Teece 1982 and Montgomery 1994). This contrasts diversification with vertical integration, where a firm is internalizing activities which previously required external coordination. For a given firm size and level of vertical integration, the sharing of resources across multiple business units will not alter the external coordination requirements of those business units, but will entail additional internal coordination. Accordingly, we hypothesize that diversification leads to a net increase in coordination requirements, and therefore a higher demand for IT investment:

Hypothesis 1. The higher the level of diversification the greater the demand for IT investment.

We turn now to the useful distinction between related and unrelated diversification (Palepu 1985, Hill 1988), grounded in the nature of the resources used for diversification. Firms pursuing related diversification attempt to exploit economies of scope through the sharing of physical and human resources across similar lines of business. Unrelated diversification seeks to achieve economic benefits by being able to allocate capital and other financial resources in an internal market more efficiently than public exchanges can. Related and unrelated diversification have different needs for coordination and control, which we examine next.

Thompson (1967) argues that the purpose of coordination is to enable joint action by interdependent organizational units when decisions of one unit affect another. In Thompson’s model, there are three types of interdependence: pooled, where each unit renders a distinct and direct contribution to the whole; sequential, where the output of one major unit is the input for another; and reciprocal, where the output of each major unit is the input for another. Thompson’s analysis indicates that the three types of interdependence are increasingly difficult to coordinate. The firm engaging in unrelated diversification incurs pooled interdependence while the related diversifier incurs reciprocal interdependence (Lorsch and Allen 1973). As a result, the need for coordination increases with the relatedness of the units of the diversified firm. Using the same theoretical basis, Hill and Hoskisson (1987) construct a model of the information processing needs of the related and unrelated diversifier and they also argue that information processing needs are greater in the related than the unrelated diversifier.

The demand for information used for control also depends upon the nature of diversification. Two control mechanisms to deal with agency problems are monitoring and incentives (Eisenhardt 1985), where the former clearly involves more information processing and therefore more IT. Top managers in unrelated diversifiers often have little firsthand knowledge of the operating affairs of a particular division’s industry, technology, or geographic region (Dundas and Richardson 1982). Therefore monitoring is much more difficult, and top management of unrelated diversifiers focus almost exclusively on financial results reported in a standardized format, employing incentives rather than monitoring as a control mechanism for agency in the firm (Lorsch and Allen 1973, Kerr 1985, Hill and Hoskisson 1987). By contrast, relatedness among business units blurs the relationship between contributions of each individual unit and overall firm performance, making accountability more difficult and incentives less effective for the related diversifier (Hill and Hoskisson 1987, Jones and Hill 1988).<sup>3</sup> Top management in those firms must employ monitoring by observing behaviors and emphasizing more subjective and “richer” modes of performance evaluation based on knowledge specific to the business or industry (Hill and Hoskisson 1987). “To control the divisions of a related firm, corporate executives must process more information and undertake more subjective performance evaluations than their counterparts in conglomerate [unrelated] firms (Hill 1994, p. 312).” Considerable empirical research supports the theoretical work outlined here, including Lorsch and Allen (1973), Bettis and Hall (1981), Kerr (1985), and Hill (1988).

To summarize, related diversification, with reciprocal interdependence and monitoring-based control, is more coordination and information intensive than unrelated diversification, characterized by pooled interdependence and incentives-based control. The higher demand for coordination and control will in turn increase demand for IT, and we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. Related diversification demands greater IT investment than unrelated diversification.

## 2.2. Vertical Integration

Vertical integration describes the extent to which successive activities in a value chain are conducted within a firm. When firms increase their level of vertical integration, by moving some production processes inside the firm, the effect is to decrease external coordination, but at the expense of greater internal coordination requirements (Gurbaxani and Whang 1991). IT has been shown to be an effective means of implementing both internal and external coordination in vertical relationships (Malone et al. 1987, Gurbaxan and Whang 1991). Whether vertical integration increases or decreases demand for IT is an empirical question dependent upon costs and capabilities of technologies in either external or internal application. Theory does not offer an unambiguous prediction. There is some empirical evidence (Brynjolfsson et al. 1994) that greater IT investment is correlated with a decrease in firm size, which in turn suggests that IT might have a greater effect on external coordination than on internal coordination. With the caution that existing theory is less supportive of the following claim than the others, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. The higher the level of vertical integration, the lower the demand for IT investment.

## 2.3. Growth Options

The market value of a firm is a combination of assets in place and future growth options, the value of which derive in part from future profitable investment opportunities. Growth options represent the future potential of the firm to grow in scale or scope. Examples of growth options include new product introductions, capacity expansions, acquisitions of other firms, investments in brand name, and basic research. A wellknown characteristic of high-growth-option firms is that they reinvest their earnings in positive net present value (NPV) projects, and therefore have relatively low cash on hand for dividend payout (Smith and Watts 1992). By contrast, firms with more of their assets in place, and low growth options, are likely to have “free” cash flow; i.e., cash flow in excess of that required to fund positive NPV projects when discounted at the relevant cost of capital. Agency considerations predict a negative relation between the level of growth options and IT investment. As Jensen (1986) analyzes, managers have the incentive to use the free cash flow to increase the resources under their control, rather than pay out the cash to the shareholders. Given the relative ease of justifying new investments in their information systems—the “empire-building” syndrome—managers with access to free cash flow might be inclined to make excessive investments in IT. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4. The lower the extent of growth options in a firm’s value, the higher the level of IT investment.

Smith and Watts (1992) predict that the ratio of the book value of assets to the market value of the firm reflect the extent to which the value of the firm consists of assets in place, and the ratio is therefore negatively related to growth options. Since a firm’s balance sheet normally does not include intangible assets like growth options, the larger the proportion of firm value determined by tangible assets (as opposed to growth options), the lower will be the firm’s asset to value ratio. Accordingly, we use assets to value ratio as a measure of assets in place and as an inverse measure of the extent of growth options; the construction of this variable and others is described in Section 4.

## 3. A Model of IT Demand with Scale and Scope

The scope variables described in the previous section—diversification, vertical integration and growth options—are often correlated with the scale or size of the firm. For example, larger firms tend to be more diversified, more vertically integrated and have more of their assets in place. The effects of variation in the scope variables might be confounded by simultaneous variation in scale. Therefore, it is important to control for scale in order to isolate the effects of changes in firm scope on the level of IT investment. There are a number of proxies for the scale of the firm, including sales, value added, market capitalization, number of employees, or total asset base. The choice of the scale measure depends on the broader choice of the estimation framework. Viewing IT as a factor of production, as in Dewan and Min (1997), a natural choice for the estimation framework is an economic production function, which relates the output of the firm to various inputs. Both sales (gross output) or value added (net output) can be used to measure scale in suitably designed firm-level production functions.<sup>4</sup> To be consistent with our sales-based measures of diversification, described in the next section, we employ sales as our primary scale measure. Sensitivity of our results to this choice is examined in Section 5.

In our empirical model, the dependent variable is IT capital, while the explanatory variables are the scale (annual sales) and scope (related diversification, unrelated diversification, vertical integration, and growth options) of the firm, along with non-IT factors of production: non-IT capital and labor. In particular, our estimation equation is specified as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln I T _ {i t} = \text {constants} + \alpha_ {1} \ln S _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} \ln K _ {i t} \\ \qquad + \alpha_ {3} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {1} (R D) _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} (U D) _ {i t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {3} (V I) _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} (A V) _ {i t} + \epsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where S 4 annual sales, IT 4 IT capital, K 4 non-IT capital, L 4 labor and other expenses, RD 4 related diversification, UD 4 unrelated diversification, $V I =$ vertical integration, and AV 4 assets to value ratio (an inverse measure of growth options). i, t index the firm and year, respectively, and $\epsilon _ { i t }$ are i.i.d. error terms. The “constants” term aggregates the effect of the year and industry dummy terms we include in the regression. In particular, we include industry sector dummies (four for manufacturing and five for service) in the above estimation model to allow broad industry groups to have different mean levels of IT investment. We also employ indicators for each year in the sample period, to account for possible macroeconomic trends that might influence the demand for IT.

Equation (1) is derived by extending (and rewriting) the Cobb-Douglas production function to incorporate the scale and scope variables discussed above.<sup>5</sup> Standard production function reasoning suggests that a higher level of output would require greater IT input, for a given level of other inputs. Further, for a given level of output, non-IT capital and labor inputs are individually substitutable with IT capital.<sup>6</sup> To summarize, the predicted signs of the coefficients in Equation (1) are as follows: $\beta _ { 1 } , \beta _ { 2 } > 0$ (Hypothesis 1); $\beta _ { 1 } > \beta _ { 2 }$ (Hypothesis 2); $\beta _ { 3 } < 0$ (Hypothesis 3); $\beta _ { 4 } > 0$ (Hypothesis 4); and $\alpha _ { 1 } > 0 , \alpha _ { 2 } , \alpha _ { 3 } < 0$ (production function logic).

A number of considerations suggested that we employ a more sophisticated technique than ordinary least squares (OLS) in the estimation of Equation (1). First, the output sales and the inputs IT capital, non-IT capital, and labor, are likely to be jointly determined in the firm’s production process, so our specification of Equation (1) introduces simultaneity bias. Similarly, the scope variables are also jointly determined, because IT investment affects the costs of coordination and control.<sup>7</sup> Therefore their direct inclusion also might introduce simultaneity bias. As is well known, OLS estimators are inconsistent in the presence of simultaneity bias. In addition, the above discussion regarding the measurement of diversification and vertical integration suggests that RD, UD, and VI may be imperfect measures of their respective (inherently difficult to measure) constructs, creating an errors in variables problem. The solution to both simultaneity bias and errors in variables is the same: instrumental variable estimation. Two methods commonly used to implement instrumental variables estimation are Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML) and 2-Stage Least Squares (2SLS). Although both are consistent and have the same asymptotic distribution, the LIML estimator approaches normality much faster than the 2SLS estimator and is strongly preferred (Anderson, et al. 1982). Therefore, we choose to use the LIML estimator for our empirical analysis. Our set of instruments includes one-year lagged values of the endogenous variables plus all other exogenous variables.

## 4. Data Sources and Variable Measures

We analyze the set of large U.S. firms included in the Computerworld annual surveys over the period 1988 to 1992. Most of the firms in this survey are drawn from the top half of the Fortune 500 Manufacturing and Fortune 500 Service listings; about two-thirds of the firms are from the former source while the balance is from the latter. IT-related data from the surveys are matched with non-IT data from Compustat. All variables are measured in dollar terms, converting current dollars to constant 1990 dollars using suitable deflators.

The reader is referred to Dewan and Min (1997) for details on the construction of the production input and output variables, S, IT, K and L. Here, we briefly describe the dependent variable in this study, IT capital, the total installed base of computer hardware, software, peripherals and services used by a firm to produce revenue. We use the terms IT investment and IT capital synonymously to represent the total stock of IT capital, net of depreciation. While annual information system expenses are likely to be sensitive to short-term fluctuations in the firm’s financial performance, IT capital is a more stable indicator of the production technology available to the firm. Some types of IT expenses, however, such as outlays on software development, create assets of long-lived value that affect future revenue and are rightfully classified as investments. Our measure of IT capital combines computer-related investments with a capitalized value of some IT expenses.<sup>8</sup>

Next, we describe our scope variables. For diversification, we use the entropy measures introduced by Jacquemin and Berry (1979) and employed by Palepu (1985), among others. A key property of the entropy measure is that it allows the distinction between related and unrelated diversification, which other measures do not permit. The Appendix presents detailed definitions of the various sales-weighted entropy measures, based on Compustat business segment data. Briefly, the variable RD measures the extent of related diversification arising out of operation in several industries of the same two-digit SIC code. UD is a measure of unrelated diversification, due to businesses in different two-digit SIC codes. Both RD and UD are nonnegative numbers and TD 4 RD \` UD is a measure of total diversification. Table 1 presents some examples of companies with different types of diversification. For each company, the table displays the values of RD, UD, and TD. J. C. Penney is an undiversified company, with a single business segment accounting for all its revenues. At the time of this study, Harley Davidson operated in two different industries (motor cycles and motor homes) in the same two-digit SIC code, indicating related diversification, but no unrelated diversification. By contrast, 3M has unrelated diversification with three business segments in different two-digit SIC codes. For growth options, we adopt the proxy measure used by Smith and Watts (1992): growth options are predicted to be inversely related to the ratio of book value of assets to the market value of the firm. The market value is estimated to be equal to the market value of equity plus book value of assets minus book value of equity. Assets to Value ratio is admittedly a noisy (inverse) measure for growth options, since it is impossible to account for those growth options known only to management, and which are not reflected in publicly available data. Section 5 considers the sensitivity of our results to these measurement problems.

The secondary data used in this study has limitations.<sup>10</sup> Most importantly, the survey does not pick up the entire range of IT Investments; omitted categories include software, communications equipment, and outsourced services. This omission is partially mitigated by our inclusion of a capitalized value of IS labor expenses in our measure of IT capital. If undercounting of IT assets does not systematically vary with the scale and scope variables, then it should not bias our results. Also, our measures of diversification are conservative estimates of the true levels of diversification (see Appendix). The problems associated with measuring vertical integration and growth options have been mentioned above.

Note in Table 2 that the average firm in the sample is quite large, having an annual value added of \$4,004 and non-IT capital of \$8,521 in millions of 1990 dollars. The size distribution of firms is skewed: the mean of the value added distribution is almost as large as the third quartile. The median level of related diversification declines and is equal to zero over 1990–1992. In other words, in the period 1990–1992 half the firms have zero related diversification. On the other hand, unrelated diversification increases and then decreases. The trend in vertical integration and assets to value ratio is more uneven. The summary statistics are consistent with the fact that the Computerworld survey was broadened substantially in 1990 to include smaller undiversified firms.

## 5. Empirical Results

We estimated the model of Equation (1) using the limited-information maximum likelihood (LIML) estimation method and our results are presented in Table 4. The variables as a whole explain 57% of the total cross-sectional variation in IT investment. The signs of all the estimated coefficients are consistent with those predicted by theory. The coefficient of sales is positive and significant, as greater firm scale is associated with a higher demand for IT input. The coefficients of the inputs non-IT capital K and labor and expenses L are negative and significant, as one would expect.

With regard to the diversification variables, the signs of the coefficients are as predicted. The coefficient of RD is positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , consistent with our hypothesis that related diversification raises the demand for IT capital. The coefficient of unrelated diversification is positive and marginally significant (p $< 0 . 1 0 )$ under a two-tailed test. The signs of the coefficients for RD and UD are both positive, and the two coefficients are jointly significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , providing empirical support for Hypothesis 1. Finally, theory predicts that the demand for coordination will be higher in firms with related rather than unrelated diversification. The coefficient for related diversification is higher than that for unrelated diversification, and the difference between the coefficients is statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , supporting Hypothesis 2.

The coefficient of the vertical integration variable VI is negative and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , consistent with Hypothesis 3. This suggests that lower levels of vertical integration, or greater focus on a smaller set of value chain activities, goes hand in hand with higher levels of IT investment. As a check on this result, we run the model on simply the firms whose primary activity is within the manufacturing sector (SIC 20-39). This restricted data set controls, at least to first order, for “position” in the value chain. The negative relation between vertical integration and IT investment is even stronger in the manufacturing subsample of our data set. These findings regarding the effect of vertical integration suggest that information processing needs and IT demand are greater when the firm used external suppliers rather than internal production.

Finally, the coefficient of assets to value ratio AV is positive and significant. Recall that growth options are negatively related to AV, so firms with high growth options tend to have a lower level of IT investment. Earnings from existing lines of business are more likely to be channeled into expenditures associated with the growth options than into expanding the existing base of IT. By contrast, for firms with most of their assets in place (i.e., firms with low growth options), our results suggest a higher level of IT investment. This finding supports the agency perspective on free cash flow. Hence, our results suggest that managers of firms with low growth options, and more of their assets in place, tend to make excessive IT investment. An alternative explanation for this result is simply that assets in place require more coordination and therefore more IT. Our empirical tests clearly cannot distinguish between these explanations.

Next, we describe a number of robustness checks. First, we conducted a test for normality of error terms and confirmed that our specification does not violate this key OLS assumption. To check for heteroskedasticity, we reestimated the model using White’s (1980) covariance matrix estimation. Use of the heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance matrix estimator resulted in no material change in the basic results reported in Table 4.

We considered the sensitivity of our results to a number of alternative proxy measures for the key scale and scope variables; the results are reported in separate columns of Table 5. The estimates for the baseline model (Table 4) are reproduced for convenience in Column (0). Column (1) reports the results obtained using value added to measure scale. Recall from our discussion in §4 that the scale measure is tied to the production framework. When using value-added for scale, the variable L is simply labor cost, as opposed to labor plus other expenses in the baseline model, due to the fact that value added is deflated sales less deflated material costs (see Dewan and Min 1997). The parameter estimates are qualitatively similar to those in Column (0), though the $R ^ { 2 }$ and all t-statistics are somewhat lower than the baseline case.

Next, we examine two alternative diversification measures, with the results reported in Columns (2) and (3). In Column (2), the diversification measure is the Herfindahl index. The Herfindahl index at the fourdigit SIC level is defined as $H E R F = 1 - \Sigma _ { j = 1 } ^ { M } s _ { j } ^ { 2 } ,$ where $s _ { j }$ is the sales share of 4-digit SIC j. Column (3) uses the entropy measure for total diversification, TD, which is equal to $R D + U D$ (see Appendix). It should be noted that there is a high level of correlation (0.97) between HERF and TD. The coefficient estimates for both HERF and TD are positive and significant, concurring with our hypothesis that more diversified firms have a greater demand for IT investment. The signs and significance of the other variables in each regression are consistent with each other and with the results in Column (0).

Columns (4) and (5) use the value-added to sales measure for vertical integration. Column (4) uses sales and Column (5) uses value-added as the scale measure. The coefficient estimates are by and large consistent with the baseline case, except for the coefficient of VS, which is positive and significant in Column (4) and insignificant in Column (5). Why the discrepancy? The value-added to sales ratio appears in the same equation with its denominator (S) in Column (4) and with its numerator (V) in Column (5). This introduces multicollinearity between the measures for scale and vertical integration, leading to unreliable estimates. We conclude that VS is not a good choice for measuring vertical integration in our model, and we therefore rely on the VIC measure for vertical integration.

Finally, in Columns (6) and (7) we consider two different proxy measures for growth options. Column (6) uses Research and Development to Total Expense ratio (RDE), while Column (7) is based on Equity Market to Book Ratio (MBE). Growth options are positively related with both variables, unlike the assets to value ratio in Column (0) which is negatively related with growth options. Including each of these alternate measures leaves unaffected the other estimated coefficients. The growth options variables themselves have the right sign and are significant. Overall the signs and significance of the variables in each regression are consistent with each other and with our baseline results.

We considered finer controls for industry using indicators for each two-digit SIC industry. This would allow for possible differences in mean levels of IT required by industry-specific technological or market factors. The results using this alternate specification are not materially different from the baseline results using sector dummies. In our cross-sectional time-series model, the regression errors are assumed to be independent across years. This might result in autocorrelated residuals. However, it is difficult to apply timeseries models and sophisticated lag structures to the residuals because the effective data period covers only four years from 1989 to 1992. Alternatively, we examined the robustness of our results to possible autocorrelated regression errors by running pure crosssectional regressions, separately for each year in the sample period. The significance of the estimated parameters was somewhat lower, on account of a smaller number of observations in each year, but the overall results by year were consistent with the pooled baseline model. We get similar results partitioning the data into two subsamples corresponding to even and odd years. On balance, we believe that autocorrelation is not a serious problem for our data set.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

This paper addresses the question: “What types of firms make greater investments in information technology?” In brief, our answer is: firms that are more diversified, especially in related lines of business, firms that are less vertically integrated, as well as firms whose market value consists mostly of assets in place.

Our results shed light on how the scale and scope, or the boundaries, of the firm affect the derived demand for IT, a key resource that enables coordination and communication. Diversification increases the need for coordination of assets across multiple lines of business and therefore, as our results confirm, increases the demand for IT. The demand for IT is relatively higher in firms that are diversified into related lines of business, because these firms require coordination of more resources than firms with unrelated diversification. We find that IT investment is negatively related to the degree of vertical integration. Our result that firms with high growth options have lower IT investment supports the notion of excessive IT investment by managers with free cash flow at their disposal.

These results should be of interest to management scholars and practitioners as well as IT researchers. Penrose (1959) characterized the expansion of the firm as an extension of the firm’s capacities, presumably requiring coordination for resource-sharing. Previous empirical research, while thoroughly examining the effect of diversification on performance, has not extensively considered how coordination might be achieved. The results of this paper, showing that diversification creates a demand for greater IT investment, suggest that IT may be a powerful tool for coordination within the diversified firm. A useful area for future research would be to conduct field studies of specific diversified corporations to identify specific IT resources and strategies that enable coordination of shared resources of different types. Results from such a study would help managers identify at least one ingredient to successfully implementing a strategic diversification.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, we do not consider non-IT coordination and control mechanisms, such as face to face meetings or travel budgets. While such alternative instruments of coordination and control certainly exist, they are not perfect substitutes for IT. If IT facilitates coordination and control, then in the absence of perfect substitutes for IT, a rise in demand for coordination and control will raise demand for IT. Moreover, the existence of alternative coordination mechanisms biases against our hypotheses. If control and coordination demands rise, they could flow into alternative instruments, which would weaken the power of our statistical model. We do achieve significant results despite this handicap, which makes our results even more convincing.

We do not consider IT price effects, which are an important factor in models of IT growth (see Gurbaxani and Mendelson 1990). We believe, however, that firms in a cross-sectional U.S. sample have equal access to information technologies at similar prices. Another omitted variable is the distribution of sales among multiple products in the same 4-digit SIC code.<sup>11</sup> While within-business-segment diversification might be an interesting variable in its own right, its omission (due to data limitations) should not bias our results on diversification, since it clearly affects all cases (i.e., no diversification, unrelated diversification, and related diversification) in a comparable manner. Finally, our data set and variables have measurement problems, including narrow definitions of IT capital and vertical integration, some missing data, and some other measurement problems. On balance, however, this is the best available data set at the firm-level.

There are a number of directions for future research. First, this study has focused on the effect of the scope of the firm on IT investment. It would be interesting to relate these variables to firm performance, seeking to uncover possible complementarities between firm scope and IT. For example, one could examine the extent to which IT investment enhances the benefits, or mitigates the costs, of related and unrelated diversification. Second, future research could also focus on the relation between IT and the internal organization of the firm, examining issues such as centralization versus decentralization, span of control, standardization, and the hypothesized flattening of the managerial hierarchy on account of the adoption of IT. Third, this paper has examined one dimension of corporate information systems policy: IT investments. One could conduct a similar cross-sectional analysis to explain variations in other dimensions of IS policy, including chief information officer (CIO) compensation, outsourcing, choice of standards, client/server versus mainframe computing, etc. These and other IS policy choices can form the basis for much further research at the intersection of information systems and strategy.<sup>12</sup>

## Appendix: Entropy Measures of Diversification

Suppose a firm is active in N four-digit industries, indexed by i. These industries in turn aggregate to M two-digit industry groups $( N \geq M )$ , indexed by $j .$ Let $N _ { j } =$ the number of different industries in group $j , s _ { i } =$ share of industry i in total sales, and $s _ { i } ^ { j } =$ share of industry i, of group j, in total sales. Total diversification is measured as follows:

$$
T D = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} s _ {i} \ln {\frac {1}{s _ {i}}}.\tag{2}
$$

<sup>12</sup>The authors thank the anonymous reviewers and seminar participants at WISE’95 in London, Eastern Academy of Management, Purdue University, Boston University, and University of California at Irvine for their helpful comments and suggestions. They are also grateful to Mr. Paul Gillin at Computerworld for providing essential survey data used in this study. This work was started when all three authors were at George Mason University, and further developed when the first author was visiting the University of California, Irvine.

In other words, the entropy measure of total diversification is a weighted average of the sales shares of the different four-digit industries, where the weight for each industry is the logarithm of the inverse of its share. Unlike simple product count measures of diversification, the above entropy measure accounts for both the number of different industries and the distribution of sales among the industries. Related diversification, arising out of operation in several industries of the same two-digit group, is measured by:

$$
R D = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {M} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N _ {j}} s _ {i} ^ {j} \ln \frac {s ^ {j}}{s _ {i} ^ {j}}.\tag{3}
$$

Finally, unrelated diversification, defined as extending the business into different two-digit SIC codes, is measured by:

$$
U D = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {M} s ^ {j} \ln \frac {1}{s ^ {j}}.\tag{4}
$$

It follows that $T D , U D , R D \geq 0$ and that $U D + R D = T D ;$ that is, a firm’s total diversification (TD) is a weighted average of the firm’s diversification within groups (equal to RD) plus the firm’s diversification across groups (equal to UD). Thus, the entropy measure is additive in its application at different levels of industry aggregation. This additive property and the ability to distinguish between related and unrelated diversification are not shared by the other measures of diversification.

For the firms in the data set, we compute the entropy measures for related and unrelated diversification, defined by Equations (3)– (4), using Compustat business segment data. Firms for which segment data are unavailable in Compustat are excluded from the analysis. Our measures underestimate the true level of diversification for two reasons. First, Compustat reports business segment data for up to ten business segments per firm. Thus, our methods underestimate the degree of diversification for each firm where this limit is binding. Second, for a number of firms, Compustat does not provide the four digit SIC codes for all the business segments. Fortunately, this occurs in less than 5% of our observations. We assign the SIC code “9999” to business segments whose SIC code is not available in Compustat. Clearly, there are other ways of dealing with missing segment data. We believe that our method provides a conservative estimate of diversification, while maximizing the use of the available data.
