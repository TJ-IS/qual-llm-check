## 1. Introduction

Singapore has achieved remarkable economic growth since political independence, averaging 8.4% real GDP growth per annum over 1960–1996. With an average per-capita GNP of US\$26,260 in 1996, Singapore’s income level exceeded the OECD average and ranked next to only Japan in Asia. Singapore has also been consistently classified as among the most competitive nations in the world in recent years by the World Competitiveness Report (IMD 1997).

This paper argues that the economic success of Singapore owes much to her ability in carving out a significant share of the rapidly expanding global information economy. As can be seen from Table 1, Singapore’s share of the world production of information and communications technology (ICT) goods has risen from 1.3% in 1985 to 4.4% in 1994, ranking fourth largest in the world after the United States, Japan, and Korea. In terms of share of global ICT goods export, Singapore’s share of 6.9% in 1996 ranks third in the world (WTO, cited in DIST, Australia 1997). For a city-state with a population of only about 3 million, this is indeed a remarkable feat of achievement.

Singapore’s performance in terms of adoption and usage of ICT is equally impressive. As can be seen in Table 2, the intensities of usage of telecommunications and computer technologies in Singapore as of the mid– 1990s have surpassed the average of OECD countries in most cases.

This paper seeks to show that the rapid development of the ICT goods industry and the high rate of informatization in Singapore represent two interrelated aspects of a larger, integrated strategy of the Singapore government to leverage the IT revolution for economic development. To do this, the paper first develops a comprehensive value chain model of the information economy that integrates four key components that have been identified in the existing literature (§2). The paper then applies the framework to Singapore to develop an overall profile of Singapore’s information economy (§3). We show further that the development path of Singapore’s information economy can be conceptualized as evolving through four key stages, each involving a strategic public policy focus on one of these four components (§4). We derive relevant policy implications for other developing countries from our conceptualization of Singapore’s information industry experience in §5.

## 2. Conceptualization of Information Economy

Despite much hype in the recent popular press about the impending global shift from industrial economy to digital information economy (see, e.g., Tapscott 1996 and Martin 1997 for recent popular reviews), no dominant analytical model has emerged on how to define the overall structure of this new digital information economy. Instead, most recent empirical studies continued to draw upon conceptual frameworks that predated the PC and Internet revolution.

The early conceptual foundation for measuring information economy was laid in the pioneering work of Machlup (1962) and Lamberton (1971). These had led to a body of empirical works in the late 1970s and early 1980s that sought to measure the information economy in terms of a primary information sector comprising information goods and services transacted through markets, and a secondary information sector consisting of “in-house” production of information services (see, e.g., Porat and Rubin 1977, Rubin and Huber 1986, Engelbrecht 1988). These early empirical works sought to regroup existing industry classifications of production value-add into the primary information sector and made informed guesses or approximations of the secondary information sector using, e.g., compensations for information occupation employees. However, these works tended to yield a rather broad definition of information economic sectors and had been criticized on various grounds (see, e.g., Engelbrecht 1997 for a recent review). More recent works have focused on a more narrow definition of information industries, emphasizing mainly those sectors most directly related to the digital electronics revolution, including electronics manufacturing, software and IT services, telecommunications, and broadcasting/cable TV services (see, e.g., U.S. Department of Commerce 1998, AEA 1997). However, despite the much hyped digital convergence of computer, communications, and media industries in recent years, most empirical research has continued to be segregated along traditional industry boundaries, e.g., the OECD’s annual industry review continue to treat the IT and communications services industries separately (OECD 1997a, 1997b), and neither have much coverage of the information content industry, the definition of which is only beginning to be formulated (see, e.g., OECD 1997c). While some recent empirical studies (e.g., U.S. Dept of Commerce 1998 and DIST, Australia 1997) had made attempt to include coverage of some content industries in their estimation of the size of national information technology industry, such coverage remains incomplete (e.g., the U.S. Dept of Commerce 1998 study includes broadcasting and cable TV, but not print media publishing and distribution).

There is also a significant body of research literature focusing on the pattern of diffusion, adoption, and usage of IT on one hand, and the diffusion of mass media communications on the other. However, with few exceptions (e.g., King et al. 1994), most existing empirical research makes no attempt to integrate analysis of information industry development with the study of the diffusion of IT/content usage. Moreover, with few exceptions (e.g., Engelbrecht 1986, Dedrick and Kraemer 1998, Jussawalla et al. 1998, and Hanna et al. 1996), most studies of information industries continued to focus on the advanced countries, with few empirical works on the newly industrialized economies (NIEs). The bulk of the available studies on the information industries of the Asian NIEs focused narrowly on the rise of their electronics manufacturing industries (e.g., Ernst and O’Connor 1992, Hobday 1996).

Synthesizing from the existing literature, we propose in this paper an analytical framework that integrates the four distinct components of the information economy that have hitherto been treated largely as separate industries (see Figure 1): (1) the production and distribution of information and communications technology (ICT) goods or appliances; (2) the production and distribution of information contents; (3) the development and operation of enabling network infrastructures; and (4) informatization, or the usage of ICT appliances and network infrastructures to access and process contents. As depicted in Figure 1, these four components of a national information economy are linked to one another as well as to the external regional and global economic environments through physical movement of ICT goods/contents and network connectivity. For instance, both ICT goods and content can be produced for domestic use or for export, while informatization can draw on domestic supply or import of ICT appliances and contents.

The generic vertical value chain relationships within and among these four components of information economy are also defined in Figure 1. Basically, our framework identifies three generic value-adding stages for both the ICT goods and content industries: creating, making, and moving. The output of these 2 sectors serve as inputs to informatization, which involves the generic value-adding activities of transacting and using. The network infrastructures provide the physical conduits as well as the interoperable standards that enable the production, distribution, and use of contents and ICT appliances. Finally, the output of informatization affects all sectors of the economy, including the three information industries themselves. While the importance of the first three components in the overall economy can be measured in terms of their direct contribution to GDP, the last component’s contribution to economic growth is indirect, either through enhancing productivity in various ICT using sectors (see, e.g., Brynjolfsson and Yang 1996 for a recent review of empirical evidence) or through the multiplier effects of an increase in final consumer infotainment demand.

We believe the above framework provides a parsimonious high-level conceptual grouping of more detailed conventional industry classifications. Following OECD (1997a,b), we define the ICT goods industry to cover computer and office equipment, consumer electronics, communications devices, industrial electronics, and the electronic components and subassemblies that go into all categories of ICT end products. We adopt a broader definition of content industry than the U.S. Department of Commerce (1998) to cover software, print, audio, and video publishing/distribution, as well as the advertising industry and various IT services industries. We define network infrastructure services to cover both postal and telecommunications services, with the latter including not just the basic carrier services, but various value-added services as well.

We define informatization to include expenditure by domestically-based enterprises and households on ICT goods, software, telecommunications, and IT services to access and process content. Following Porat and Rubin (1977), we include in-house IS expenditure as well as externally sourced IT services. Conceptually, the expenditure on various information content used (computer databases, printed publications, audio and video programmes, etc.) should also be included in the measurement of informatization. However, empirical difficulties in getting reliable data as well as conceptual difficulty in defining the value of contents produced in-house prevent coverage of these items.

We believe that the above integrated framework serves as a useful analytical-descriptive tool to study the changing role and structure of the information economy of any country. First, the framework provides an integrated measure of the overall size and contribution of the information economy in the national economy as a whole by incorporating not only the direct GDP contributions of the respective ICT goods, content, and network infrastructure sectors but also the indirect contributions of informatization. Second, by providing a high-level conceptual classification scheme the framework offers a convenient measure of the structural composition of the information economy, enabling comparative analysis between countries and changes over time. Third, by providing a systematic scheme for horizontal grouping of industry sectors and vertical grouping of generic value chain stages, the framework can be used to describe the strategic routes by which a country can upgrade her information industries. Basically, the framework suggests that a country can move up the value-add ladder in the production of ICT goods and contents in three basic ways: shifting from mature, low-end products to new, high-end products within the same sector and value chain stage (e.g., from low-end hard disk drives to high-end drives, or from basic-feature to feature-rich software products); shifting from lower to higher value-adding stages within the same sector (e.g., from manufacturing into R&D, or from content distribution to original content creation); and shifting from lower to higher value-adding sectors (e.g., from commodity ICT goods to proprietary contents, or from consumer electronics assembly to semiconductor wafer fabrication).

Fourth, by integrating information industries and informatization, the framework highlights the fact that a country can plug into the global information economy not only on the supply side but also on the demand side; i.e., a country can increase her overall national competitiveness through more effective informatization in all economic sectors (including the information industries themselves). Moreover, more effective informatization can also stimulate consumption of new infotainment and interpersonal communications, which will in turn induce growth of the content/ICT goods production industries and network infrastructure development.

Last, but not least, by explicitly highlighting the interrelationships among the four components of the information economy, the integrated framework also serves to focus analytical attention on the importance of policy coordination in the development of information industries. Traditionally, in most countries, the four different components of the information economy fall under separate ministerial or departmental jurisdictions—Ministry of Trade and Industry in the case of IT goods production, Ministry of Telecommunications in the case of telecommunications network infrastructure, and Ministry of Information in the case of content industry and broadcast network infrastructure. The exception is informatization, which typically is not recognized as a coherent policy area and as such is either neglected or involves overlapping programmes or initiatives from different departments. The capability of government to achieve overall policy coherence and implementation coordination across these different functional domains has been identified as a key challenge for government in industrialized nations (Hanna et al. 1995).

In the next two sections we apply the above analytical framework to examine the structure and growth dynamics of Singapore’s information industries, based on a synthesis of available empirical data on the size and characteristics of each of the four components of information economy.

## 3. Overview of the Structure of Singapore’s Information Economy

Table 3 summarizes our empirical estimates of the structure of Singapore’s information economy as of the mid-1990s based on our proposed conceptual framework. Overall, the information industries in Singapore are estimated to have contributed directly over 14% to the national GDP in 1994. This is considerably higher than the estimate of 7.5% in the case of the United States in 1996 (U.S. Department of Commerce 1998). Even allowing for underestimation of the U.S. figure (due to the noncoverage of print media publishing and distribution), the Singapore economy appears to be more dependent on information industries than is the United States.

Our empirical estimates show that the structure of Singapore’s information economy is characterized by a high degree of dominance by the ICT goods production and distribution industry, which accounted for close to 70% of the combined value-add of all information industries. This is a considerably higher share than is the case of the information economy in the United States (U.S. Department of Commerce 1998). Indeed, ICT goods-related manufacturing represented the most important manufacturing sector in Singapore, accounting for over half the total manufacturing valueadd of the Singapore economy, and about 45% of the total manufacturing employment in 1995 (EDB 1995). The content industry sector as a whole (including software publishing/distribution and IT services) ranks a distant second (17%) to the ICT goods sector, followed closely by the network infrastructure sector (14%).

The composition of each of the components of Singapore’s information economy can be found in Table 4a-c. In the ICT goods manufacturing industry, computer and peripherals assembly represent the largest cluster (47% by value-add), followed by the semiconductor wafer fabrication, assembly and test cluster (19%) (Table 4a). Within the computer and peripherals cluster, magnetic hard disk drive assembly represents the largest subsector, followed by computer assembly, and other peripherals (mainly printers and soundcards). There is also a sizable contract manufacturing sector in the form of printed circuit board assembly (PCBA) activities (6.0%). The telecommunications equipment cluster still remains small (6.6%), though rapidly expanding, while consumer electronics had been declining in importance in recent years.

In the content industry sector, the largest subsector in terms of value-add was print media publishing and distribution (47.8% by value-add), followed by IT services (16.1%) and software publishing and distribution (12.0%) (Table 4b). Audio-visual media production and distribution came next (11.5%), followed by advertising (10.9%) and news reporting services (1.6%). Network infrastructure services consist predominantly of telecommunications services, and hence no separate breakdown table is provided.

While comparable data for other developing countries are not available, the high degree of concentration of Singapore’s information industries in the ICT goods production sector is probably characteristic of other high growth East Asian NIEs as well (Dedrick and Kraemer 1998). The possible exception is Hong Kong, which has a more developed movie production and video programming industry, but a substantially smaller presence in ICT goods production (mainly consumer electronics and contract manufacturing). Both Singapore and Hong Kong are also likely to have above-average share of value-added contributed by the network infrastructure sector, as a result of their respective regional business hub roles in Northeast and Southeast Asia.

Turning to informatization, the largest expenditure item was found to be IT hardware purchase by enterprises and households, which constituted nearly half the total estimated informatization expenditures (Table 4c). Next in importance were telecommunications services and consumer audio-visual electronics. ## 4. Key Stages in Singapore’s Information Industry Development

By extending our analytical framework backward in time, we can trace the historical development of the structure of Singapore’s information economy as far back as data availability allows. Correlating our analysis of the industry development trends with our observation of the changing role of government policies towards the four key components of the information economy (see Table 4), we were able to identify four distinctive stages in the historical development of Singapore’s information economy, with each of these stages corresponding to a dominant public policy thrust in one of the four information economy components. This staged development pattern is summarized in Table 5 and depicted graphically in Figures 2(a)–(d). A brief description of the key development features in each of these four stages is provided below.

In the first stage, spanning the period from political independence in 1965 to the late 1970s, the main strategic thrust of the government was to establish Singapore as an offshore electronics manufacturing base for multinational corporations (MNCs) from the United States, Japan, and Europe. As analyzed in greater details elsewhere (see, e.g., Goh 1996 and Wong 1998), the electronics manufacturing industry began in Singapore in the second half of the 1960s with the arrival of MNCs such as Philips in consumer electronics and Texas Instruments in semiconductor assembly. The comparative advantages of Singapore as an offshore location at that time were the abundant supply of unskilled labor and a government that was eager to attract MNC investments and willing to invest in basic infrastructures to support their offshore production facilities. Largely on the back of MNC investments in consumer electronics and semiconductor assembly, Singapore’s electronics manufacturing output increased nearly twentyfold, from S\$283 million in 1970 to S\$5,344 million in 1980 (see Tables 6 and 7).

In this second phase, coinciding with the rapid growth of the personal computer market worldwide, Singapore shifted quickly to become a major manufacturing site for computer and peripherals (mainly magnetic hard disk drives) by American MNCs. Japanese electronics MNCs also expanded their manufacturing presence in Singapore in this period, partly due to significant appreciation of the Yen. This period also saw the emergence of local electronics supporting industries, especially precision engineering and printed circuit board assembly (PCBA), as well as rapid upgrading of semiconductor assembly to more complex products and to include automated testing (Wong 1992, 1998). Overall, a definite shift from laborintensive to skills-intensive manufacturing operations can be observed: the average labor productivity of Singapore’s electronics industry more than doubled from S\$27,740/worker in 1980 to S\$56,260/worker by 1990 (both in 1985 constant prices), resulting in a real labor productivity growth of 7.3% p.a., in contrast to a mere 1.3% p.a. improvement between 1970 and 1980.

While this shift in the IT goods manufacturing industry structure is certainly important, an even more significant development during this period is the rapid pace of diffusion and adoption of IT across many key sectors of the economy. As can be seen from Table 8, the penetration of computer among business enterprises in Singapore rose sharply from 13% in 1982 to 68% by 1989. This rapid computerization was driven in part by the PC revolution, which made computer technology increasingly affordable, and in part by the aggressive promotional activities of the National Computer Board (NCB), which was established in 1981 to spearhead IT diffusion and software industry development in Singapore (see, e.g., Gurbaxani 1991, Wong 1996). A National IT Plan was formulated in 1985, with a strong focus on facilitating IT diffusion through promotion of IT manpower development, IT culture and awareness, and development of Singapore-based IT vendors. In particular, the government played a leaduser role in IT adoption. Large-scale computerization of the public sector was initiated in this period under the Civil Service Computerization Program (CSCP). Under this program, the annual IT expenditure of the public sector increased from S\$14 million in 1985 to S\$200 million in 1990. A particularly notable example of the lead-user role of the government is the development and implementation of an EDI system for external trade customs declaration and clearance. Called TradeNet, the system achieved dramatic improvement in the speed and cost of trade transaction processing (King and Konsynski 1990, Neo 1994). Advanced computerization efforts have also contributed to Singapore’s seaport and airport becoming the most efficiently run in the world (Tan and Loh 1992, Tung and Turban 1996).

The rapid computerization of the economy in turn stimulated the development of a software vendor and IT services industry in Singapore (see Table 9). The revenue of Singapore-based hardware, software, and IT services vendors increased sevenfold from S\$208 million in 1982 to as much as S\$1491 million by 1990. By the end of the decade, most of the world’s major IT vendors have established their presence in Singapore, with many using Singapore as their regional sales, training, and technical support hub. A significant pool of IT professional manpower has also developed, with the estimated number increasing from less than 900 in 1980 to 4,000 in 1984 to 14,300 by 1991. In 1991, more than half of these are employed in the MIS departments of user organizations, although an increasing proportion was being employed by IT vendor firms to do software development or provide systems integration and other IT services, such as systems consultancy and IT training. A cluster of software R&D activities also emerged around newly created public IT research institutes, local universities, and some of the larger IT MNCs. Aside from this, however, the overall content industry remains relatively underdeveloped during this period. While TV viewership, newspaper circulation, and music/video/book distribution expanded steadily, there was little growth in local content publishing. Broadcasting remains entirely in the public hand, while the local newspaper industry consolidated into essentially one major state-controlled player (SPH).

In contrast, the network infrastructure experienced rapid upgrading in the form of digitalization of network exchanges and deployment of new telecommunications technologies such as packet-switched data communications and ISDN (Wong 1993). Investment in networks and equipment by Singapore Telecoms, the PTT monopoly, expanded steadily in this period with cumulative capital expenditure exceeding S\$3 billion over the decade of the 1980s, or more than S\$1,000 per inhabitant (Singapore Telecoms, various years), an investment intensity close to the norm of advanced countries (ITU 1997a).

This period saw the further technological upgrading and expansion of Singapore IT goods manufacturing industry from skill-intensive sectors to technologyintensive sectors such as semiconductor wafer fabrication, computer printers, and telecommunications equipment. Meanwhile, the existing hard disk drive assembly sector underwent rapid automation and output expansion, making Singapore the world’s leading HDD manufacturing site and accounting for over half the world’s HDD production (Wong 1997b). Local supporting industries continued to strengthen. At the same time, with rising costs, many of the ICT goods manufacturing firms started to redistribute the laborintensive part of their operations regionally. A “growth triangle” arrangement linking Singapore to the southern state of Peninsular Malaysia and the Riau Islands of Indonesia was created to facilitate this regionalization of Singapore-based manufacturing firms. Overall, the transition to technology-intensive manufacturing is accompanied by an average real growth in labor productivity of 5.4% over 1990–1995 (EDB 1996b). A significant shift towards R&D activities can also be observed during this period. Between 1990 and 1996, R&D expenditure by electronics and IT firms in Singapore more than tripled, from S\$177.6 million to S\$707 million, and easily accounted for the largest share of all private sector R&D in Singapore (63%) (NSTB, various years). Although MNCs remained dominant in ICT manufacturing, significant local players such as Creative Technologies, Aztech, and the Singapore Technologies Group have also emerged.

The diffusion of computer continued strongly in this period (computer penetration rate among enterprises increased from 68% in 1989 to 90% in 1994, see Table 8), but the primary focus has shifted from computer diffusion in the earlier period to network infrastructures development in this period. In anticipation of the impending paradigm shift towards “information superhighway,” a new strategic IT plan, called IT2000: Vision of an Intelligent Island, was released in 1992. Several major policy changes were introduced as part of the plan (Wong 1996). The monopoly PTT, Singapore Telecoms, was privatized, and a progressive schedule of liberalization of the telecommunications sector was announced. This schedule was subsequently accelerated so that complete liberalization will be achieved by the year 2000. A major investment program to build a broadband National Information Infrastructure (NII) with fibre-to the-curb was launched, and a dual local access policy was implemented, with the existing local telephony access network being complemented by a program to lay coaxial cable TV lines to all residential homes. The National Computer Board was restructured to take on the new mission of evolving the NII architecture and promoting new applications deployment over the NII. After some initial hesitation, the government also came to embrace the Internet, launching major initiatives to promote Internet diffusion to homes and schools. The percentage of households with internet access rose to 8% by 1996, and further doubled by late 1997 (Table 10).

In conjunction with this NII development strategy, a decision was also made to revamp public broadcasting policy. The public TV and radio stations were corporatized in 1995. A new, public-owned cable TV company (SCV) was launched in 1995 with a mandate to spend S\$500 million to deploy cable TV to all homes in Singapore by the end of 1999. By mid-1997, more than half of Singapore’s residential units were installed with coaxial cable access. Around the same time, the proportion of Singapore homes that came under “optical fibre-to-the block” coverage has also risen to over 90%.

The emphasis on NII development in this period is reflected in the significantly higher capital expenditure by Singapore Telecoms compared to the earlier period: In the 6 years from 1991–1996, the cumulative capital expenditure of S\$4.15 billion exceeded its total capital expenditure for the preceeding 15 years.

In tandem with the NII development program and communications liberalization policy during this period, the government also sought to strengthen Singapore as a content packaging and distribution hub in the Southeast Asian region by targeting leading content MNCs to invest in Singapore. Over a short fouryear period from 1992 to 1996, Singapore attracted 15 major satellite programmers to use Singapore to customize and broadcast their programmes to the region. These included HBO, ABN, ESPN, MTV, Walt Disney Television, The Discovery Channel, and Sony Entertainment TV. These investments, in turn, had stimulated the development of a number of indigenous satellite uplink/downlink services companies and preand post-production content packaging houses. Singapore also became the Asia-Pacific VAN hub for organizations like SITA and ABACUS, as well as attracted global online business information services companies like Reuters, Dow Jones Market, and Bloomberg to use Singapore as their regional technical development and support hub.

In the current, still emerging stage of development of Singapore’s information economy, the Singapore government’s vision is for the island to develop into a total information business hub by the early part of the next millennium. Besides consolidating Singapore’s premium position as the logistics hub in Southeast Asia, the total information business hub strategy seeks to broaden Singapore’s role as the regional hub for the creation, distribution, and transaction of contents.

With the physical installation of the broadband network infrastructure nearing completion and with PC penetration of households already reaching over 40% (Table 10), Singapore has now entered the NII applications deployment stage. Public policy focus in this emerging stage is shifting from network infrastructure building to content industry development. As of mid-1998, several major nationwide electronic commerce applications have been commercially deployed or in an advanced stage of market trials. These include the deployment of a nationwide network of public information kiosks, the Secure Electronic Transactions (SET) protocol-based Internet electronic commerce market trial, the Singapore ONE broadband multimedia applications market trial, and the Singapore Telecom’s Video-On-Demand (VOD) market trial (Wong 1997a). To facilitate the commercialization and deployment of user applications, the National Computer Board has organized a number of industry consortia, including the Digital Media Consortium (DMC), the Electronic Commerce Hotbed (ECH) Consortium, the Online Technologies Consortium (OTC), and the Singapore ONE Pioneer Club scheme. These are designed to facilitate communications and cooperation between technology suppliers, applications developers, policy makers, and users. Leading MNC IT and multimedia software publishing companies are involved in these consortia activities. Incentive schemes are also in place to encourage companies to enter into Internet publishing and electronic commerce. As of July 1997, Singapore has already achieved a ratio of 1.96 WWW hosts/ 100 population (Table 11), the highest in Asia, and the penetration of electronic commerce is expected to rise steeply with the impending commercial deployment of SET and Singapore ONE.

To accelerate the shift towards commercial deployment of broadband, multimedia applications, the Singapore government has increasingly recognized that the next big impetus to growth of Singapore’s information economy will have to be in the area the development of NII-based contents and applications. Creation of new contents, as well as R&D and innovative applications of advanced technologies, have been identified as the next frontiers in which Singapore must develop core capabilities.

In line with this push for creative content development and technological innovation, the Singapore government’s MNC investment promotion efforts are increasingly focusing on positioning Singapore as a regional hub for innovation and creativity. With respect to the ICT goods industry, the strategy is to encourage MNCs to establish world product mandate charters (like HP in the ink jet printer), R&D centres, or lead manufacturing plants in Singapore. With respect to the content industry, the strategy is to encourage the major media companies that have already established their distribution presence in Singapore to move upstream into content publishing. Another strategy is to encourage foreign creative talents to start up new content-related businesses in Singapore.

It is in this context that the role of broadband network infrastructure is considered critical by the Singapore government. By providing a technically advanced infrastructural environment and a ready commercial testbed, the Singapore government is hoping that it will confer a locational advantage to Singapore that can be used to offset her other disadvantages.

In summary, we can see from the application of our analytical framework that Singapore’s information economy development went through four distinct stages, each corresponding to a strategic thrust in one of the four components identified in our framework. It is important to note, however, that as policy focus shifted from one component to another, existing policies were continuously refined to strengthen what had already been built up. Moreover, throughout the four stages, even as policy focus shifted the strategic intent of Singapore remains one of seeking to plug into the global information economy and to leverage it for technological learning and economic growth. The information industry development path undertaken by Singapore appears to have evolved in a spiral up the value-adding chain, leveraging the capabilities developed in an earlier stage to build new and more advanced capabilities in later stages.

## 5. Policy and Research Implications

Our interpretation of Singapore’s experience in developing her information economy raises several interesting policy implications for other developing countries. First, our analysis suggests that small nation-states can indeed leverage the rapidly expanding global information economy as engine for economic growth. With virtually no natural resources, Singapore has been relatively successful in riding the technological waves of the IT revolution through a strategy of welcoming MNC investments and investing in the infrastructure and human resources that attract such investments. We believe that this strategy of plugging into the global ICT and content production network is still replicable by other developing countries, even though they would not be able to enjoy the first mover advantage that Singapore earlier had by being among the first developing countries in the world to pursue this strategy in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Indeed, the rapid growth of ICT production in Malaysia and Thailand in the last decade has been largely based on a similar strategy of promoting MNC investment. Besides political stability and efficient government administration, a key factor in Singapore’s ability to attract increasingly high-tech MNC investments has been the significant public investment in human resources development and infrastructure, especially network infrastructures (Wong 1998). These policies can be imitated by other developing countries. Indeed, some emerging economies may even be able to use their abundant supply of skilled labor to offset their disadvantages in infrastructure to leapfrog into the newer information industries, e.g., India in software development and Philippines in multimedia animation.

Second, Singapore has demonstrated the importance of promoting network infrastructure development and informatization besides ICT goods production. Singapore has embarked on computerization diffusion and NII development at an earlier stage than other highgrowth East Asian NIEs such as Taiwan and Korea. Although lagging behind the United States, the United Kingdom, and Hong Kong, Singapore has also embarked on telecommunications liberalization earlier than most developing countries. In this regard, Singapore’s information industry strategy appears to strike a balance between the approach of Hong Kong, which focused more on developing network infrastructure to support her regional trading and business services hub role, and those of Korea and Taiwan, which focused more on growing the ICT goods production sector. To fully leverage the global information economy, other developing countries should avoid the bias on ICT goods production and give more attention to informatization (Dedrick and Kraemer 1998). The “walking on two legs” analogy of Schware (1992) is apt here.

Third, our interpretation of Singapore’s information industry development suggests a deliberate, staged process of learning or capability building, whereby the capabilities developed (and demonstrated) at one stage provided the foundation for attracting and acquiring more advanced know-how at the next stage. Although the specific sequencing of capability development adopted by Singapore need not be applicable to other countries, it does suggest the need to develop a staged, leveraged, learning strategy in industrial catch-up (Mathews 1996). In this regard, it is interesting to apply our conceptual framework to conduct comparative research into the information industry development stages of other countries that have similarly achieved rapid information industry growth. This could enable us to discover other viable generic routes to rapid information industry growth that can serve as models for other developing countries. For example, India appears to have succeeded in growing a vibrant software industry without having first established a strong ICT production base, while Hong Kong has emphasized network infrastructure and content industry development (especially motion picture/video programming). Regardless of their initial areas of specialization, however, we believe that all developing countries must ultimately seek to leverage their competitive advantage in one component of the information economy to other components to achieve a more balanced capability to compete in the global information economy that is increasingly driven by digital convergence.

The issue of capability building strategy leads necessarily to the larger issue of the role of the state and the issue of policy coordination. It is by now generally acknowledged that market failures are prevalent in information economy (especially in network infrastructure and informatization), and that there is a role for the state to enhance the working of the market, not necessarily to replace it (see, e.g., Aoki et al. (1997) for recent contributions). While recognizing the possibilities of failures of state policies designed to ameliorate market failures, recent literatures on East Asian IT development (e.g., Kraemer et al. 1992, Mathews 1996, Dedrick and Kraemer 1998, Borrus and Haggard 1998, and Hanna et al. 1996) generally concluded that the state has played a positive role in promoting ICT industry development in these countries.

The experience of Singapore in information industry development clearly suggests that the role of the state has been pervasive. While the precise impacts of the various government policies can only be ascertained with further research, and while there have certainly been mistakes (see more detailed discussion below), there is little doubt that many of the information economy-related policies and programmes introduced by the government have contributed to the rapid growth of Singapore’s information economy up to now.

Effective policy coordination has been a major contributing factor to the successful implementation of various information industry policies in Singapore in the past. Indeed, the establishment of a special agency (the National Computer Board) to coordinate informatization and IT industry development policies has been an important institutional innovation that helped to overcome compartmentalization of government efforts. The establishment of high-level coordinating mechanisms such as the National IT Policy Committee is also instrumental in facilitating policy coordination (Wong 1996). Achieving similar degree of policy coordination will be a major challenge for other countries seeking to follow Singapore’s approach.

However, the pervasive role of the state in Singapore’s information industry development also appears to have a number of adverse consequences. First, in taking a top-down approach to pushing new technology deployment, the Singapore government has made a number of mistakes in the choice of technology. For example, in the early 1990s, Singapore Telecoms (at the time still a public monopoly) pushed for the deployment of proprietary videotext systems, which was later overtaken by internet WWW technology. Although the meteoric rise of Internet WWW technology could not have been foreseen, withdrawal from the obsolete system might have been slowed because it was perceived as a “national” project. Similarly, while the government was very successful in pushing the early development and deployment of EDI system for export/import documentation (TradeNet), subsequently the government-controlled company that operated the system might have been somewhat slow to shift to Internet technology, perhaps because of sunk investment consideration and the fact that it had such a market dominance. Second, strong state involvement together with the strategy of encouraging investment by foreign MNCs might have the effect of stunting the growth of indigenous technopreneurship. Third, the Singapore government has erred on the conservative side in the pace of liberalization not only of the telecommunications market, but also of the financial services markets. Greater deregulation of the latter is becoming increasingly necessary to stimulate innovation in new electronic commerce intermediary functions, and it is only belatedly that the government has begun to make bolder move towards greater deregulation of the financial services sector. Fourth, the desire of the political leadership to enforce censorship over pornographic and political contents could conflict with the economic goal to promote Singapore as a regional content publishing hub.

Some of these adverse consequences of state involvement might well be unavoidable (e.g., given her small size and lack of resources, Singapore probably had no other option but to rely on MNCs to achieve rapid economic growth), while others could be the result of deliberate trade-offs by the political leadership. Other countries seeking to draw lessons from Singapore’s approach to information industry development will thus need to understand the political and social contexts under which such an approach can work, and the trade-offs involved.

The experience of Singapore also suggests the need for continuous policy reforms in all countries to respond to the rapidly changing nature of the global information economy. Indeed, as value-add increasingly migrates from ICT goods production to content production (Dedrick and Kraemer 1998), a key challenge for the East Asian NIEs is how to avoid being stuck at the fiercely competitive end of ICT goods manufacturing and to shift towards the high-margin end of content publishing such as software, video programming, and new Internet enabled services. In the case of Singapore, the shift from ICT goods industry and computerization diffusion to NII development has necessitated significant telecommunications liberalization and policy reforms. It is likely that the next phase of NII applications deployment and content industry development will necessitate further significant policy and institutional reforms, including deregulation of financial services, liberalization of broadcasting and other information control, as well as the establishment of new policy guidelines, legal frameworks, and coordinating institutions for electronic commerce and Internet-based publishing. The mission and structure of existing institutions like the National Computer Board will also need to be redefined over time. Past success certainly does not guarantee the same in the rapidly changing world of information industries.

Last, but not least, the experience of Singapore raises interesting issues about the role of IS professionals in information industry development. While IS professionals have always played a critical role in the growth of IT firms and the deployment of ICT in public and private enterprises, the experience of Singapore also suggests that they have played a pivotal role in the visioning, formulation, and implementation of public policies related to information industries. From its inception, the National Computer Board has been largely, though by no means exclusively, led by IS professionals and staffed mainly by IS professionals. IS professionals in industry have also played an important role in advising and influencing the government on various IT policies. Yet few of these IS professionals had adequate exposure to the discipline of public policy in their IS professional training, and they largely had to learn on the job (although a fair number later attended postgraduate programmes in public policy or MBA courses). This observation raises interesting implications for IS educational institutions: While most IS educational programmes nowadays include a large dose of exposure to business and management practices, few IS educational programmes in the world provide a technology policy option. Another issue confronting IT policy making in Singapore is that as the government policy thrust increasingly shifts towards content industry and electronic commerce, policy makers and implementers similarly need to become knowledgeable about entirely new industry domains such as Internet publishing, digital content distribution, and new forms of trade and financial intermediation. Unless they can re-tool quickly, traditionally trained IS professionals risk becoming ill-suited for such jobs, and as such might come to exert a less influential or less positive role in shaping the future development of information economy in Singapore.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper developed an integrated value chain framework to analyze the structure and growth dynamics of Singapore’s information economy. Our analysis shows that Singapore’s information industries are concentrated in ICT goods production rather than content production, a pattern characteristics of high-growth Asian NIEs with the exception of Hong Kong. However, Singapore does differ significantly from other Asian NIEs in achieving much higher level of informatization.

Our analysis of the growth dynamics of Singapore’s information economy identifies four distinct stages, each corresponding to a strategic focus on a different component of the information economy: establishing ICT goods production capability in the first stage, promoting rapid computer diffusion in the second, proactively investing in NII development in the third, and deploying NII applications to develop content industry in the fourth.

Our interpretation of how Singapore developed her information economy raises several interesting policy implications for other developing countries and suggests the need to have more comparative research among NIEs as well as between NIEs and advanced countries.
