# An Information Company in Mexico: Extending the Resource-Based View of the Firm to a Developing Country Context

Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa • Dorothy E. Leidner

Management Science and Information Systems, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712-1175 sjarvenpaa@mail.utexas.edu

INSEAD, Boulevard de Constance, 77305 Fontainebleau Cedex France dorothy.leidner@insead.fr

he information industry assumes that information is seen as a valued resource that enables organizations and individuals to improve their effectiveness, efficiency, and overall competitiveness. For the information industry to bridge rather than divide further the global economy to information rich and information poor, we need to understand how firms, particularly local firms, can pioneer or participate in the information industry in emerging economies that do not inherently embrace information as a valued business resource. This research examines how one local firm shaped the external environment to pioneer a local information industry in Mexico and how it competes in a newly opened local market against foreign competitors. In doing so, the paper extends the resource-based view of the firm to a developing country context. The dynamic capabilities of strategic foresight and flexibility, coupled with a core competency of trustworthiness, are found to be critical in effecting internal and external change in an unstable environment.

## 1. Introduction

The information industry consists of (1) information content providers (newspapers, online databases), (2) information service providers (access providers, consulting firms, outsourcing companies), (3) software providers, and (4) computer and telecommunications equipment manufacturers (Raphael 1989, Chakravarthy 1997). The different sectors are interrelated, and a lack of advancement in any one sector can curtail growth in the other sectors (Allen 1988, Cassiolato and Baptista 1996). Our focus is on a firm in the content provider sector because, in this sector more than in others, the sources of differentiation and wealth creation are tied to the acquisition and use of information resources.

The context of the content provider is Mexico, an emerging economy. The fundamental difference between managing in a less developed and in a developed country is that in the less developed country, the environment tends to be much more challenging and carries more significant managerial implications (Austin 1990, Montealegre 1994). One well acknowledged barrier militating against the rise of the information industry sector in less developed economies is the lack of basic information technology (IT) infrastructure (Roche and Blaine 1996, National Research Council 1996, Roche et al. 1992, Hanna 1991). The lack is attributed to such causes as political, economic, security, and education issues; low teledensity rates; and resistance to imported technology (Danowitz et al. 1995, Pitroda 1993).

A second barrier, much less written about, is the lack of an information culture in many less developed economies. The information industry rests on the assumption that information is a valued resource and that the use and management of information improves individual and organizational effectiveness, efficiency, and overall competitiveness. Information technology is embedded with assumptions about effective management and control practices, about individual power and privacy, about what sources of information are valued and trustworthy, and about appropriate forms of communication, among others—values associated with management in western, industrialized nations (Danowitz et al. 1995). Moreover, technologies tend to assume that members of a culture are rather sophisticated in terms of their ability to absorb and utilize context free information. Difficulty arises in cultures that emphasize context-dependent sources of information (Kedia and Bhaget 1988, Danowitz et al. 1995, Hamelink 1983).

Using the resource-based theory of the firm as an interpretive lens, this paper will explore how a pioneering firm in an information content industry in Mexico achieved its competitive advantage in an environment lacking in basic technological infrastructure and supportive information culture. Our study began with two broad questions: (1) What resources and capabilities underlined the firm’s success?, and (2) How did the environment constrain the development of those resources and capabilities, and how did the firm overcome these constraints? The article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews literature on the resource based theory of the firm and its extensions and discusses unique characteristics of the environment facing less developed nations. Section 3 presents the methodology used. Section 4 presents the case and its analysis. Sections 5 and 6 discuss the case analysis results and their theoretical and practical implications. Section 7 concludes the article.

## 2. Conceptual Frameworks

## 2.1. The Resource-Based View of the Firm and its Extensions

The prevailing paradigm of understanding how and why firms gain and sustain competitive advantage is the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Schendel 1994, Mahoney and Pandian 1992). Focusing on a firm level analysis, the resource-based view emphasizes the resources possessed, developed, and deployed by an organization and understanding the relationship of those internal resources with performance and competitiveness (Penrose 1959, Andrews 1971, Wernerfelt 1984). The theory focuses on how and why some resources and capabilities are valuable, yet rare or idiosyncratic, difficult to imitate or nonsubstituable by allowing those firms that have them to accrue superior economic returns (Barney 1997). The competitive advantage arises from the heterogeneity of valuable resources and capabilities $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , strategic assets) across firms (Nelson 1991, Peteraf 1993). The sustainability of the advantage depends on isolating mechanisms, including causal ambiguity, social embeddness, path dependence, complementarity, and asset specificity of resources and capabilities (Peteraf 1993, Reed and DeFillippi 1980) that make them immobile, nonimitatable, or rare.

Resources and capabilities have some notable differences. Resources can be physical capital resources (e.g., financial assets and technology), human capital resources $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , managerial skills), or organizational resources (e.g., reputation, culture) (Barney 1986b, 1991). Capabilities, on the other hand, refer to a firm’s capacity to deploy valued resources, usually in combination or in co-presence (Amit and Schoemaker 1993, Schendel 1994). Capabilities include such competences as trustworthiness, organizational flexibility, rapid response to new customer trends, and short product life cycles. The capabilities are firm specific and are developed over time (Barney and Hansen 1994, Collis and Montgomery 1995). The theory assumes economic rationality in managerial choices for resources, notwithstanding the common cognitive biases, limitations, and organizational inertia that strategic decision makers face (Amit and Schoemaker 1993). By contrast, individual managers have little discretion over capabilities or the pace of their development although capabilities can not be disentangled from the firm’s past experience and the overall skills and knowledge of the management team (Amit and Schoemaker 1993, Mahoney and Pandian 1992). Investments in capabilities tend to be irreversible and highly path dependent, placing firms on the so called trajectory of capability development (Teece et al. 1997).

The information technology (IT) field has long linked IT with competitive advantage. IT is a resource that can generate competitive value (Ginzberg and Venkatraman 1992), but only if deployed so that it leverages or exploits pre-existing business and human resources in the firm via co-presence and complementarity (Clemons 1986, 1991, Clemons and Row 1991, Ross et al. 1996, Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997). In terms of sustainability, IT’s competitive advantage resides in the organization’s managerial skills related to IT, not in the technology (Mata et al. 1995). This last point underscores the skills of continuous assessment, learning, and transformation in reconfiguring IT resources with human, business, and relationship resources so that the firm can achieve new forms of competitive advantage under changing environmental conditions (Feeny and Willcocks 1998).

Increasingly, RBV is viewed as a paradigm rather than as a theory as scholars use it to integrate different viewpoints and frameworks from strategy, economics, organizational science, public policy, etc. (Conner 1991, Porter 1991, Mahoney and Pandian 1991, Coff 1997, Hart 1995, Ingram and Baum 1997, Levinthal and Myatt 1994). Three extensions of the theory are particularly relevant in the context of the developing country: (1) the dynamic capabilities framework, (2) institutional influences, and (3) the network analysis.

2.1.1. The Dynamic Capabilities Framework. The traditional resource-based view was limited to relatively stable environments. According to Barney (1997), “if a firm’s threats and opportunities change in a rapid and unpredictable manner, the firm will often be unable to maintain a sustained competitive advantage” (p. 171). Tushman and Anderson (1986) and Leonard-Barton (1992) noted that under major environmental jolts, a firm’s core capabilities risk becoming “core rigidities.” Miller and Shamsie (1996) found that knowledge-based resources help firm performance during unstable and unpredictable environments, whereas nonknowledge-based assets help performance during stable and predictable environments.

Teece et al. (1997) extended RBV to consider the situations of rapid technological change. They developed the notion of dynamic capabilities: “the firm’s ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments” (p. 516). The more rapid the technological change, the more dynamic capabilities are the source of sustained competitive advantage (Teece et al. 1997). The development of such capabilities is limited by the firm’s existing base of capabilities, and is shaped by the firm’s current market position and past history of developing capabilities (Teece et al. 1997, Grant 1996).

The difference between the traditional conceptualization of the resource-based view (Wernerfelt 1984, Barney 1986a, 1991, 1997, Grant 1991) and the dynamic capabilities view (Teece et al. 1997) is that whereas under the traditional view, current firm resources and capabilities are matched to the opportunities in the market place; under the dynamic capabilities view, the firm needs to develop dynamic capabilities to identify new opportunities and respond quickly to them. Under the dynamic capabilities view, strategizing (aligning the company to its environment) is secondary and can lead to overinvestment in current competences and underinvestment in integration, learning, and reconfiguration processes that underline dynamic capabilities (Teece et al. 1997).

The work of Hamel and Prahalad (1994) reinforces many of the concepts of the dynamic capabilities framework with one significant addition: A firm must not only build dynamic capabilities that allow it to respond to the rapidly evolving environmental changes, but it has to have the capacity and management repertoire to influence and even control those environmental changes as well. Shaping the environment and capitalizing on those changes requires strategic foresight, strategic intent, and strategic architecture (Hamel and Prahalad 1994). Foresight requires deep understanding of technology, demographics, regulation, and revolving customer needs as well as imagination, creativity, and perceived discretion to change that which is beyond one’s ownership (Hamel and Prahalad 1994). The strategic architecture is analogous to Collis’ (1994) “higher-order capabilities,” Volberda’s (1996) “the repertoire of flexibility increasing capabilities,” or Grant’s (1996) “architecture of capabilities” that allow rapid reconfiguration of lower level internal and external capabilities to a higher level of complex capabilities. Lastly, the firm requires strategic intent that provides the direction for the long-term market and competitive position.

2.1.2. Institutional Influences. Oliver (1997) combined the resource-based theory that focuses on rational motivations with the institutional theory that focuses on social and institutional motivations. The institutional theory assumes that organizational decisions and activities are influenced by the social context, including the firm traditions, network ties, regulatory pressures, and societal expectations (Zucker 1987; Scott 1987, 1995). According to Oliver (1997), firms are likely to acquire valued resources only when the resources do not depart significantly from resources acquired in the past and do not threaten the current valued resources. The firms are also likely to deploy those resources only if the institutionalized processes, culture, and routines in the firms, industry, and societal level nurture competency in the resource. Hence, resource and capability development requires not only an internally, but also externally, supportive environment.

2.1.3. Network Analyses. Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven (1996) integrated the network analysis theory with RBV. The network analysis theory (Granovetter 1992, Uzzi 1997) complements the resource based view of the firm by focusing on firm strengths that are embedded in the human relationships or social structures within the firm and across firms. Social networks are particularly important in societies that emphasize interpersonal relationships and strong group affiliation (Hofstede 1980). Social networks provide many information benefits in terms of access to information that might otherwise be unavailable, early exposure to the information allowing preemptive action, better filteration of irrelevant information and the legitimization of information (Burt 1997). Because of their position in the network, individuals are able to identify opportunities and get the right people together to develop the opportunities (Burt 1997). Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven (1996) found that the ability to form strategic alliances was dependent on the social and personal networks of the firm’s top management. And we can imagine that to carry out Hamel and Prahalad’s strategic intent will require external “maneuvering capability”—the use of social and political connections and the associated power to shape the environment.

The three extensions of RBV—dynamic capabilities, institutional effects, and external network analyses— have moved the resource-based view of the firm from what was originally a highly internal and rational view of the firm to take into account a broader range of contexts and situations. Porter (1990) emphasized the importance of trying to explain competitive advantage under different local situations and doing so by integrating new perspectives to RBV. We will build on this tradition by extending the RBV to consider the developing country context.

## 2.2. The Developing Country Context

Traditionally, strategy and management models developed in the West have tended to ignore the environmental factors that are so influential in the less developed countries (Austin 1990, Thomas 1996). Hence, it is no surprise that many administrative theories have been found to have a poor fit to the developing countries because of differing cultural values (e.g., corruption, extended family ties), differing economics (lack of competition, immobility in factor markets, lack of skilled labor and professionals), and differing institutional practices (e.g., political motives supersede economic motives). Also, while many Western models are based on the assumptions of stability, developing countries are in the midst of great change and constant instability requiring dynamic models (Kiggundo et al. 1983).

The IT literature describes many environmentally imposed obstacles that managers face in implementing information technology in their organizations (e.g., Goodman 1991, National Academy Press 1996, Roche and Blaine 1996, Nidumolu et al. 1996, Saraswat and Gorgone 1991, Palvia et al. 1992, Deans and Kane 1992). Although specific obstacles are different from one country to another, several common themes emerge. First, the government often controls or exercises influence over technology development and information industries. Second, there is a scarcity of managerial, technical, and/or financial resources to implement IT innovations so critical for the information industry. Third, the inadequacy of information infrastructure prevents network-based technology options. Lastly, societal culture, the economics of labor versus technology, and political instability might totally eliminate any benefits from IT investments and make information a nonvalued asset.

2.2.1. The Culture of Mexico. Several aspects of the Mexican culture run counter to the acceptance of the information industry. Two cultural dimensions are particularly relevant: collectivism and uncertainty avoidance. Hofstede (1980) assigned Mexico a score of 70 out of 100 on collectivism (the United States had a score of 9 out of 100) and 82 out of 100 for uncertainty avoidance (the United States had a score of 46 out of 100).

As a collectivist (also called high context) culture (Hall 1976, Hofstede 1980), Mexicans tend to develop extensive personal and professional networks which consequently reduce the need for detailed objective background information when decisions are made. Accustomed to relying on their networks, Mexicans are uncomfortable accepting information from sources not personally known (Hall 1976, Wiseman et al. 1989, Moran and Abott 1994). Quantified information coming from personally unknown sources (such as government forecasts or information providing firms) might be seen as having negligible value and lacking in trustworthiness.

As a culture high in uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede 1980, 1985, 1988), Mexicans prefer structure, clear rules, and standard operating procedures that limit their perceived exposure to uncertainty. One less obvious behavior associated with high uncertainty cultures is an avoidance of long-term plans in favor of short-term plans, since the long-term plans by nature involve a greater willingness to accept the uncertainty of future conditions. Such cultures often avoid excessive detail in planning as well, since detail in planning likewise creates uncertainty as to the ability to carry out the precise plan. Another behavior associated with uncertainty avoidance is restricted environmental scanning and information sharing as well as more frequent interpretation of environmental issues as a threat rather than as an opportunity (Hofstede 1980, Schneider and DeMeyer 1991). Under “threat,” organizations tend to restrict the flow of information to the organization, reduce participation in decision making, and focus on internal changes rather than shaping the environment (Dutton and Jackson 1987, Dutton and Duncan 1997, Carpenter and Golden 1997). Finally, uncertainty avoidance is associated with an orientation towards the past, as the maintenance of past traditions carries less uncertainty than the pursuit of future objectives. Managing change and capturing people’s enthusiastic support for change in the past-oriented cultures requires celebrating the greatest glories of the past. This helps manage continuity and identity (Tompenaars and Hampden-Turner 1998).

2.2.2. Environmental Adaptation Versus Shaping. The cross-cultural IT literature emphasizes the importance of adapting information practices to the core values of a nation and its user subcultures (e.g., Lachman et al. 1994, Robey and Rodriguez-Diaz 1989, Robey and Azevedo 1994). The implication is that if the societal culture does not promote the values embedded in modern information and communication technology, prospects for information industries are limited.

The more recent literature on IT and developing countries presents an alternative viewpoint to the local adaptation perspective. Two studies specifically examined how a local firm, rather than adapting the technology to the local conditions, shaped the conditions external to the firm and gained advantages from using information technology. A longitudinal study by Montealegre (1994) on a Guatemalan company examined how agricultural information systems were successfully implemented in an environment characterized as hostile for IT innovations. Resourceful company managers not only shaped their company but the broader economic and social system around them. Another study of a Jamaican insurance company similarly emphasized the resourcefulness and the visionary leadership of management in shaping both the future of the firm and its industry as well (Barrett and Walsham 1995). The above two studies challenge the importance of adapting information practices and IT implementations to local cultures. This echoes the call by Lehmann (1996), who advocated new IT management models for developing countries based on Hamel and Prahalad’s (1994) strategic intent that looks for opportunities to shape the environment.

Although the recent writings of the resource-based view emphasize environmental change, the change is a result of technological changes and global competition. By contrast, change in the developing country is often due to large societal and economic upheavals (e.g., the overthrow of governments, hyperinflation, ethnic clashes, natural disasters) that are rather difficult to shape by even the most foresighted executives. Hence, the question arises how an organization can build the dynamic capability to begin to shape a marketplace and succeed in a journey to fulfill its strategic intent in a constraining and unstable environment. The years of 1984 through 1997 have been described as the most volatile years of Mexican history with numerous economic and political crises (Kaufman and Rubio 1998). The case of the Mexican information company is revelatory because it provides an opportunity to extend the resource-based view of the firm to a less developed country. The case illustrates how an information company, by building on dynamic competencies, gained and sustained strategic advantage in an information industry, despite many environmental constraints—including a poor IT infrastructure and a societal culture that did not promote information as a valued resource.

## 3. Research Method

The case study method was chosen because we were interested in broad issues of “how” the firm pioneered the local information content industry and “why” it had succeeded in doing so despite a seemingly poor fit with the local environment for launching an information, and particularly a real-time information, business.

## 3.1. Data Collection

Data collection took place in the spring of 1995 and the summer of 1995, and then again in the spring of 1997.

The majority of the data were collected through semistructured interviews with open-ended questions focused on what, how, and why innovations had taken place. We interviewed 12 employees in Monterrey and 20 employees in Mexico City, 5 academics with collaboration/consulting relationships with the company, and 3 former residents/business people of Mexico City who had been customers of the company’s products. Within the Mexican information company, our interviewees represented a mix of levels and functions: We talked to business unit heads, the R&D manager, the head of advertising, editors, reporters, a production manager, a circulation manager, an information technology manager, delivery staff, as well as several members of the clerical staff.

Two-thirds of the interviews were conducted in English, and the rest in Spanish via a translator. Many of the interviews (mostly with the senior management) were scheduled in advance. These interviews lasted from one to three hours and were tape-recorded and transcribed. The interviews started with broad questions on the Mexican business environment, industry, competitors, the interviewees’ personal history and experience at the company, and their roles in the key innovations at the company since the mid 1980s. The specific questions varied by the person’s current position as well as their past roles.

In addition, other interviews (for example the ones with reporters, production staff, delivery, and clerical staff) were conducted as informal discussions on an ad-hoc basis as the researchers toured various facilities. Additional data were gathered from annual reports, newsletters, marketing and other promotion information on products and services, senior management speeches, strategy plans, mission statements, trade journals, local newspapers, the Internet, newswires, and electronic mail messages. There was a high degree of congruence among interviewees and documentary and bibliographic sources. The case description is short of financial information as the company continues to be privately owned and does not disclose financial information.

## 3.2. Data Analysis

The analysis of the data was conducted in several steps. First, the transcripts were read through while listening to the tapes to verify their accuracy. Then notes were taken that enabled a broad picture of the organization’s background, key products, and major decisions over the past several years. Notes were taken on themes that arose during the interviews, and as other interviewees made supporting comments these were added under a general theme. From these themes the initial case analysis was written. Finally, transcripts were analyzed for specific factors that supported, refuted, or extended the resource-based theory of the firm. In particular, we looked for comments pertaining to resources, strengths, or assets that were (1) part of the firm’s historical conditions or events (rare), (2) causally ambiguous, (3) socially complex, and/or (4) related to information technology innovations that were perceived as enabling opportunity and success in the marketplace. In addition, we looked for other factors that were not captured in these four factors that were thought to enable sustainable competitive advantage.

In summary, the research method used could be broadly classified under an interpretive epistemology (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991, Walsham 1993, Barrett and Walsham 1995) using a single case study. Next we provide the case description.

## 4. The Mexican Information Company

## 4.1. The Case Description

The Junco family has three businesses in Mexico: (1) El Norte, the number-one newspaper in Monterrey, (2) Reforma, one of the leading newspapers in Mexico City and the only global Mexican newspaper, and (3) InfoSel, a Monterrey-based provider of online, realtime information for businesses. Collectively, we will refer to these three companies as the Mexican Information Group or simply the Group. The managers in the Group consider the three businesses to comprise “an information company with different channels to distribute the information.” When asked what the Group’s strategy was, a senior manager responded, “our expertise is to gather, process, distribute, and present information. The different businesses represent different channels, but the fundamental expertise is the same.”

4.1.1. The Birth of the Information Company. In 1973, at the age of 24, Alejandro Junco de la Vega gained control of El Norte from his grandfather. El Norte had first begun publishing a Monterrey-based newspaper in 1938. By 1993, El Norte held between 90 and 95% of the newspaper market in Monterrey, a city of roughly five million inhabitants and with the most advanced industry in Mexico (Kras 1994). Unlike other newspapers, El Norte had developed its own private distribution network to deliver its paper, bypassing the unionized newspaper vendors. As the economy began to show signs of change in the early and mid-1980s in the form of deregulation, privatization, and the removal of barriers to technological development,<sup>1</sup> Alejandro Junco made a decision to redefine the company—to become an information company rather than a newspaper company. He concurrently decided that technology would be the most important competitive weapon, adopting the motto: “Information technology provides the way for market advantage.” An executive explained:

I came to work here in 1983. Alejandro Junco was only a bit more than 30 years old. But he had a clear vision, determination, perseverance to make it happen. To carry it out, he saw technology as a competitive tool, not just as an administrative auxiliary.

As early as 1981 when the IBM PC appeared on the market, Junco started thinking about the PC as a potential media to deliver information. According to the head of technology development and operations, “That’s why we call Alejandro a visionary because there was no information company back in 1981 thinking about PC becoming a tool to deliver information.” In 1985–1986, Junco established InfoSel (Informacio´n Selectivo—selective information) as the company that would handle the electronic information aspect and would work very closely with the IT department in developing offerings. The first project was to make a large electronic database of everything that was being published on paper around the world about Mexico. This database also included government-based statistical information on the economy. The electronic archive project was started on January 1, 1986. By April of 1987, InfoSel launched its first online service, InfoSel En Linea, consisting of online access to the archived information and to El Norte. The service allowed subscribers to dial into InfoSel’s system and perform searches of the information. The products were marketed to professional, legal, accounting, and financial markets because these groups were regarded as the most willing to reconsider and to perhaps change their views on the value of objective information.

Soon after, Junco began pushing for a real-time financial information product, in part as a reaction to the financially paralyzing stock market crash of 1987. The top managers at InfoSel believed that one of the reasons for the crash in Mexico was simply a lack of information: “It was a market operating without information,” said the head of the InfoSel business unit.

In November 1990, InfoSel launched InfoSel Financiero even as it canceled the InfoSel En Linea project. Although the need for online access to information was burgeoning, technical problems had vitiated the InfoSel En Linea product. Inadequate telephone lines in Mexico introduced so much static and noise that customers quickly became frustrated and the project eventually failed. By contrast, InfoSel Financiero was created as an online financial information service that did not rely on telephone lines. Instead, the company partnered with FM broadcast stations throughout Mexico. A head of strategic planning recalled, “This was an enormous undertaking.”

Unlike InfoSel En Linea, InfoSel Financiero was very successful. By 1993, InfoSel was earning \$8 million in revenue and launched a network product which would allow subscribing organizations to have one subscription but many users. By 1995, revenues were \$12 million and the product contained information from NYSE, NASDAQ, the options and equities markets, the fixed income, commodities, and futures markets. As of 1997, InfoSel had some 3,500 organizations using its system both nationally and internationally. A key executive reported,

InfoSel Financiero today is the standard for Mexican financial news and information. All the banks, all the brokerage houses, all major corporations are subscribers of our service. There is no single bank in Mexico that does not have our product.

During 1995, InfoSel developed the first interactive electronic trading system, LINCE, for settlements and clearing so that subscribers could not only receive financial information, but could anonymously post bids, buy and sell financial instruments, engage in one to one dealing or automatic matching, participate in auctions of instruments, etc. By 1996, LINCE was on 95% of the Mexican fixed income trading desks, and over 3 billion dollars worth of daily transactions flew through the system. The international competitors’ products had failed to crack the market of providing Mexican information. The international competitors remained viable in niches that provided international market information to Mexican-based firms. A manager noted “When we started, Reuters had 80% of the market share of (financial information). Now we have 80 percent” (in Alves 1997).

Also in 1995, InfoSel launched an Internet access dial-up service. InfoSel formed a close partnership with Microsoft and most PCs sold in Mexico came with an icon for an InfoSel Internet connection (Alves 1997). By 1997, InfoSel provided local Internet access in 36 Mexican cities through the largest information network in Mexico, to some 25,000 clients. It competed against some 150 companies. Despite its dominant position as an Internet access provider, management sees InfoSel as a value added Internet solutions’ provider to the professional market place, “The information we have is our competitive advantage.” A manager explained, “our slogan is that anywhere in the world, if you want information on Mexico, go to the InfoSel Web site.”

4.1.2. Reforma. Even as Junco’s vision of the electronic distribution of real-time information was progressing with InfoSel, he made the decision in 1993 to launch a new newspaper in Mexico City. Mexico City, a city of 25 million people, had a plethora of existing papers (25 to 30) and low per-capita readership. Many people suggested that Junco was “crazy” to enter into the Mexico City market. But Junco’s vision was about change: “changing the nature of information delivered in Mexico so that the information was trustworthy and so that the presentation of the information was superior.” Junco invested heavily in technology to enable Reforma to be the most colorful and graphical newspaper in Mexico, to enable Reforma to maintain the image created by El Norte of objective information, and to enable Reforma to invest in human resources and training. Although the values would be the same as with El Norte, Reforma was going to have a strong local flavor of the capital city so that, as an executive explained, “customers would call the paper their own. The name, Reforma, was picked because it represented tradition but also invited a reform.”

On November 20, 1993 (on the eve of the anniversary of the Revolution), Reforma produced its first newspaper. By 1995, Reforma had a daily circulation of 86,000 newspapers and 39,000 subscribers. Reforma has been called “the newspaper of record” and a paper that offers “the kind of independent reporting that is in short supply in Mexico’s authoritarian political system” (Advertising Age 1994). In early 1995, in conjunction with InfoSel, Reforma opened a web site to distribute news from its Reforma newspaper on the Internet and received 16,000 inquiries the first day. By 1996, the paper had 20,000 online subscribers, many from overseas, beginning to realize Junco’s vision of a global newspaper. In the spring of 1997, Reforma was first in advertising revenue and second in circulation among Mexico City newspapers and had the largest market share in the upper segments of Mexico City, which are the most important for advertisers. Reforma engages in regular customer surveys, encourages letters from readers, and has editorial boards composed of individuals from the community, as does El Norte. Also by 1997, Reforma’s national sector was published in six other newspapers in Mexico. Reforma and El Norte have strategic alliances with Business Week, Forbes, Dow Jones, and the New York Times. For example, Reforma publishes weekly or biweekly abstracts of the key articles appearing in Business Week and Forbes.

## 4.2. Environmental Challenges and the Group’s Solutions

The Group’s rise to the summit of the information business in Mexico has not been without challenges. Interviews with managers in the Group revealed several key constraints imposed upon them by their environment that had to be carefully managed (Table 1).

4.2.1. Overcoming Cultural Constraints. Among the cultural constraints facing Reforma, El Norte, and InfoSel were: (1) a work ethic among journalists that was inconsistent with the Group’s aspirations to nonbiased information, (2) a lack of information culture, and (3) a lack of a market for online information. According to the general manager of Reforma, “the biggest challenges we had with Reforma were first the people in Mexico City who would come to work for us because we saw their values and culture so different from those at El Norte.” It had become common practice in Mexico City for journalists to partake of “the ongoing system of selling favors and expecting something in return.” If no money was offered at a news conference, it was likely that the story would be negative. Many journalists also worked as advertisement salespersons to complement their income (Alves 1997). Hence, the challenge was to replicate the trustworthy image of El Norte in Monterrey with Reforma in Mexico City.

Reforma decided that it was not possible to “erase this journalism culture” so they opted to take “fresh, new people that had never been journalists and train them so they could work within our own ethics code and our own standards.” Reforma went to universities to recruit, a practice very rare in Mexico, and solicited applications. Twenty-eight hundred applications were received for the first 100 positions. The 100 graduates were sent to a 6-week training program, taught by U.S. journalism professors, where they would learn about company culture and ethics, writing styles, aggressiveness, and techniques. By carefully selecting graduates and training them, the Group was able to create a culture that adhered to strict ethical rules, encouraged innovation, inculcated hard work, pushed technological limits, and promoted loyalty. Reforma also deliberately sought female journalists. According to Junco, it was “very important that women were entering our newsroom. Women journalists were less susceptible to the old practices (of corruption) than men” (in Alves 1997).

A second cultural roadblock was the general lack of interest in information based on facts rather than rumors or opinions. Mexican journalism is said to be “rich in adjectives, poor in facts” (Trevino 1997). Percapita readership was strikingly low. Mexico City, with 18 to 20 million people, had the same circulation as the city of San Diego with 1.1 million people. The 3 pesos it cost to buy a copy of Reforma equalled about a fifth of the daily minimum wage, making it a luxury for the majority of Mexicans (The Dallas Morning News, April 9, 1995). One manager referred to Mexico as “an information poor country.” The challenge posed by this constraint was encouraging readership of a new style of journalism. Reforma’s investigative style of reporting was perceived as aggressive by people in Mexico city. The editor-in-chief recalled (in Alves 1997, p. 7), “In Monterrey, the public were more accustomed because we have been doing this for years. But in Mexico City we were criticized in the beginning and it took some time for the public to understand that were just practicing independent journalism.”

Through aggressive marketing and setting up its own distribution network, El Norte had raised per capita readership in Monterrey to the highest level in Mexico. These methods increased readership of Reforma as well. In addition, Reforma published numerous specialty supplements by topic, by age group, by the region in the city so that “each person received a [highly customized] paper that they could call their own.” Moreover, the mission of the papers was to report on reality and facts. An executive explained:

Our instructions to reporters specifically encouraged avoidance of adjectives. We strive to be direct, objective, well quoted, and void of any fabrication. Of course, we have our critiques who think we violate the Mexican tradition of flowery language, politeness and genteel etiquette.

InfoSel also faced cultural constraints relating to information. The business community lacked a tradition of third-party-based objective information. Moreover, the Mexican culture was not ready to use online information and “the people were not accustomed to pay for information.” Here the challenge was to teach the potential users about the benefits of real-time information and why it was worth paying for. The general attitude, according to one InfoSel customer, was that “one paid a peso or two for a newspaper—why would anyone pay \$500 for an information product.”

In response, InfoSel focused its real-time activities on the financial industry because it was seen the easiest to penetrate. These markets were deregulated and liberalized in the late 1980s and were becoming increasingly global. To survive, firms needed trustworthy, real-time information. The general manager of InfoSel summarized the challenge and the response:

Professionals have no history for using information and even less for paying for it, particularly paying for services on a monthly basis. Our business also assumes that people base decisions on information. Yes, the culture was a real enemy. We first focused our real-time products on financial market because it is easier to show bottom-line impact from having information. One does not pay for a product that they don’t understand how to use, or don’t know how to take advantage of.

4.2.2. Overcoming Technical Constraints. The Group faced several technical roadblocks, including: (1) a weak national technology infrastructure that prevented effective rollout of InfoSel’s information products, (2) customer technology lagging behind that of InfoSel, and (3) a shortage of qualified technical staff. These technical roadblocks challenged the Group’s ability to sell technologically advanced products. The initial InfoSel online product was canceled because the technical infrastructure could not support point-topoint operations. The second product used a broadcast wireless transmission wherein information was sent to FM radio stations and distributed via local stations. This was a major accomplishment because the organization had to form partnerships with numerous local operators. Over time, InfoSel built the largest private satellite-based information network in Mexico allowing release of wide area network versions of its products. It was this private network that it leveraged to become an Internet access provider.

The second major technical constraint was that as InfoSel continued to innovate and expand, they constantly found themselves ahead of their customers technologically. When they first introduced CD-ROM products for legal information, their clients did not have CD-ROM drives. A marketing manager at InfoSel remarked:

It is a real struggle to bridge the gap between what technology our clients are comfortable with and the technology that we think is the best one for delivering them our information. Coupled with this is our desire to be always one technology step ahead from our competitors.

To overcome this barrier, InfoSel Financiero worked closely with the leading banks in Mexico to initially launch its products. So close was their collaboration that some institutions felt that InfoSel had created a customized product for them. InfoSel also provided nearly unlimited training at the customer site and made customer service the highest priority. Similarly, InfoSel reached out when it first introduced material on the Internet. Clients had not yet heard of the World Wide Web. InfoSel provided free seminars to educate its clients. It also began an Internet access provider service to create a community of Internet users. According to one executive:

In the U.S., the public press is doing much of the education of Internet. In Mexico, access providers have to inform the public and get people to use electronic mail.

InfoSel also faced the challenge of finding technically qualified employees. The organization developed a close alliance with the Monterrey Institute of Technology, the leading Mexican engineering and technical university. For example, the head of technology development and operations had previously been the president of the university’s alumni association. The Group invested heavily in the technical training of its employees.

4.2.3. Overcoming Political Constraints. Both the newspaper union and the government presented barriers for the Group. All single copy newspaper stands in Mexico City were unionized. The Newspaper Vendors Union was one of Mexico’s traditional monopolies (Advertising Age 1994). A year after Reforma was launched, the vendors had grown unhappy because Reforma was sold through other outlets, including an efficient home delivery service and sales in restaurant chains and convenience stores, because Reforma was developing a network of independent street sellers, and because Reforma would be printed and sold every day of the year except at Christmas and New Year’s Day (all the other newspapers did not print on official holidays, of which there are many in Mexico). Moreover, because Reforma was defined as an independent newspaper, conservative PRI members were opposed to criticism coming from outside their traditional means of control and used the unions as a means to attempt to suppress sales of Reforma. The Union eventually withdrew Reforma posters and tried to stop distribution of Reforma altogether, initially plummeting Reforma circulation by 40%. During this crisis,

Reforma reporters and editors took to the streets to distribute the paper as did many of Reforma customers. After failed attempts to negotiate with the unions, Reforma made the decision to create its own distribution channel. It sought and was granted permission from the newly formed Commission of Competition. The investment was a costly decision but eventually worked in the favor of Reforma contributing to the growth in sales.

The second barrier was that “There is no country in Latin America where the press has been so openly involved in corruption. It has become part of the regime itself” (Alves 1997, p. 4). As a recently graduated journalism student in 1970s, Junco did not hesitate to go head to head against the Mexican president of the time, Lois Echeverrı´a, calling him a corrupt demagogue, despite the fact that a key raw material—paper—was controlled by the government. El Norte’s newsprint allotment was reduced by more than 80% but the spirited Junco refused to ebb his criticism. In 1989, Junco was still fighting for greater freedom and hosted a meeting of the Inter-American Press Association in which he openly called for the liberalization of the newsprint market. Soon thereafter, Junco argued in an article in the Wall Street Journal that imports to newsprint should be freed. In January 1995, Junco’s papers printed a report that Mexico’s new education secretary lied about having a graduate degree from Harvard. The secretary resigned (Bogart and Giner 1997). On April 21, 1997, Junco’s papers criticized the government for the lack of access to information. One of the country’s most powerful drug lords was arrested after a chartered jet ran out of gas and crashed. The government refused to release information about the jet ownership. Reporters searched U.S. databases to find the name of the jet owner.

Although the Group’s newspapers were the first to seek political independence from the government, others have followed. In 1996, El Universal, an 80-year-old major Mexico City newspaper that had been firmly pro-government, hired a young editor-in-chief, reporters, and columnists who were known for their criticism of the current Mexican president. Also, the leading financial paper, El Financiero, in Mexico City is considered an independent paper along with a few newspapers in the countryside and a paper in Guadalajara (Siglo21) (see Alves 1997).

The government contributed directly to problems for InfoSel in two ways: (1) government control of information, and (2) government resistance to allow private firms access to advanced technologies. The government has promoted secrecy, corruption, misleading and partial information, particularly when the information involves official government data. Even basic information is kept from the public and journalists with such excuses as it is “privileged information,” “it’s strategic for national security matters,” or “someone else has it.” For instance, basic economic statistics that are available to anyone in the United States are treated in Mexico as state secrets.”

The government first refused to cooperate with InfoSel Financiero and yield information on the Mexican stock exchange—the Bolsa—which threatened to preclude Infosel from obtaining the very information needed for a successful financial product. Moreover, it regarded InfoSel as a competitor. The negotiations took three years to complete. In the interim, InfoSel established what they referred to as the real-time newsgroup. This group collated daily information on markets as well as relevant stories for business readers. This group would therefore gather the information that the Bolsa refused to deliver. InfoSel encountered a similar problem when it tried to create a wide area network based on satellite technology. The government wanted to maintain control over satellite access. Eventually InfoSel prevailed and was granted the first state license to rent bandwidth on one of the government’s satellites.

In summary, the Group has experienced high growth and innovativeness in a market that did not always initially value the products it had to offer. We next interpret the case from the resource-based view of the firm, putting particular emphasis on the dynamic capabilities that allowed the Group to achieve its goal of the information company despite the various environmental obstacles in a highly volatile business environment.

## 5. Case Analysis: Key Resources and Core Capabilities

In 1986, over ten years ago, Junco and his management team made a large wager that the local markets would be deregulated, particularly the financial sector, and that the local firms would require new information services in the new competitive environment. The Group acquired and harnessed a number of key resources. Among the physical resources were IT investments; human resources included Junco’s leadership, but also technical and managerial training and development. Among the organizational resources were the history of political independence, the corporate culture, external networks, the search for synergy, and the longterm view. Via these resources, the Group developed the dynamic capabilities of strategic foresight and strategic flexibility on top of the capability of trustworthiness that in combination allowed them to achieve and sustain the position of an undisputed leader in the local information provider sector. Through shaping the environment, the Group has created a combination of resources and capabilities that are rare, valuable, and difficult to imitate. Moreover, the capabilities of foresight and flexibility keep the bundle of resources and capabilities a continuously moving target for the group’s competitors. Table 2 summarizes the key resources and capabilities.

## 5.1. Leadership of Junco

The leadership of Alejandro Junco has been the crucial basis of managerial foresight for the Group. Junco has a journalism degree from the United States and a rich web of U.S.-based business connections, both of which have contributed to his deep understanding of industry and technology trends. From his local Mexican basis, he was able to foresee emerging economic events as well as the resultant new markets and new needs that triggered the innovations that allowed the Group to pioneer the local information industry in Mexico. Junco has been an intellectual and spiritual leader. El Norte, Reforma, and InfoSel are all presided over by strong general managers. To them, Junco leaves the running of the business although as one executive remarked, “he is always there intellectually challenging how things could be improved.”

## 5.2. History of Political Independence

El Norte’s and Reforma’s tradition of fact-based journalism and political independence have contributed to the core capability of trustworthiness. Both leadership and political independence are resources where history, or path dependence, matters. Despite having had numerous fights with the government, with private companies, and with academia about the editorial line, El Norte and Reforma have continued to present the facts. This political independence has been a competitive asset to the Group as it has positioned itself as among the first providers of trustworthy, reliable, and uncompromised news, including real-time financial news, in the Mexican market. Junco “proved that it was possible to build a news company in Mexico, profit oriented and independent from the government, with a firmly declared commitment to serve the community” (Alves 1997, p. 7).

## 5.3. Culture

Although the Group’s three businesses have different local cultures, they share some common underlying values. First, Mexican traditions are celebrated. For example, the headquarters of Reforma, although newly built, represent Mexican traditions. “The headquarters of [Reforma] newspaper is probably the most monumental and architecturally innovative . . . The facade is neoclassical, resembling a European building of last century, but inside there are two other styles of Mexican architecture . . . representing the pre-Columbian construction . . . and a colonial house with a big patio” (Alves 1997, p. 6). Architecture that celebrates the past is highly prized in Mexican culture (Kras 1994); it represents the symbolic harmony of the past and present. Such connection to the past is critical in effecting change in the past oriented society. Harnessing this strong aspect of Mexican culture has been a key resource enabling a unique advantage. It has raised barriers of foreignness for non-Mexican firms wishing to enter the Mexican market.

Second, as somewhat of a contrast to the first value, employees carry forward the values of economic competition and profitability. Employees feel passionate about the mission of the businesses to disseminate information about Mexico to Mexicans in order to enable them to compete against large multinational companies. One manager explained, “what motivates me is to make Mexico competitive.”

The third value, integrating the first two, is the emphasis on human resources. This value is practiced by offering pleasant working conditions, high pay, continuous training, participatory management style, etc. The focus on human resources has been a barrier particularly to local competitors. One competitor source reported pay scales of 35% and higher at Reforma compared to other newspapers in Mexico City (in Alves 1997). A manager at El Norte explained, “If our new hires stay here for two years, they become so acclimated to our way of thinking and our superior working conditions that it becomes very difficult for them find a match at other firms.”

In summary, the Group has created a profitoriented, socially responsible and caring company culture. The Group has selectively combined U.S. values with those of Mexico. Although the three businesses share global aspects of the culture, each has implemented the culture honoring the local traditions and past history. For example, Reforma successfully transferred the high level of Monterrey-based professionalism through work practices and norms, but through the careful use of traditional Mexico City symbols and rituals.

## 5.4. External Networks

Junco has relentlessly pursued partnerships with other firms and organizations that share the same values. Above we have described partnerships with the Monterrey Institute of Technology, Microsoft, Dow Jones, Business Week, and Forbes. The Group pursued partnerships with the government as it did to ensure access to Bolsa data. Besides strong business and governmental alliances, networks exist with customers.

Strong social networks promote capabilities of foresight and strategic flexibility. An example is the customer boards. Wishing to have a paper for the people, El Norte established “editorial boards” composed of individuals from the community who serve free of charge for nine to ten months at a time. These boards provide important feedback to the editors of the respective newspaper concerning what types of topics need to be addressed, issues that need to be surfaced, special reports that would be valuable. El Norte News has over 2000 alumni of their editorial boards who serve as sources of information within their respective industries. Reforma has a similar network of boards. According to Junco (in Bogart and Giner 1997): “In Mexico there is a culture of nonparticipation. People are unconsciously bombarded with the idea that social problems are too big and complicated to manage, when the truth is that they can be solved. The community participation is very important to the life of the newspaper. It’s our central paradigm.”

In terms of InfoSel, developing a human network with the government and the financial sector was critical. All InfoSel products were designed with the help of lead customer groups. A manager explained, “Even before we launched InfoSel Financiero, we established a customer feedback group involving people from both the seller and buyer side.” This human network which transcends the organization’s boundaries contributes to a sustainable advantage over larger, multinational firms. A marketing manager at InfoSel explained:

Compared to Reuters, we have a little bit of an advantage because no one has more accurate information than the people who live it and breathe it. We have some ventures with official governmental entities that puts up ahead of the game. Our reporters have close communication ties with government and bank officers who are typically the first to be aware of important news in the financial sector and this network has given InfoSel an advantage over Reuters and other US-based firms trying to tap the Mexican market.

## 5.5. Information Technology

The Group believes fundamentally in the leverage of technology with business and relationship resources to provide the strategic flexibility to maneuver in an unstable economic climate. The reporting relationships reflect the strategic significance placed on IT as well as the institutional processes that allow IT to be leveraged within and across the Group’s businesses. The chief technology officer is the head of InfoSel and reports directly to Junco. The general managers of Reforma and El Norte also report to the technology officer on all technology development and operations issues. Technical experimentation and innovation are firmly embedded practices. The Group spends 10%–15% of its sales on technology development. IT is also at the heart of its product strategy. The Group offers different product lines by different technology options: Internet, CD-ROM, online services, etc. IT also provides operational and structural flexibility in the Group’s internal operations. For example, it operates a virtual newsroom between El Norte and Reforma. In fact, some have called the Group as a “multi-media company” (Alves 1997).

## 5.6. Long-Term View

Strategic intent requires a long term view and commitment to that long-term view. It took from 1986 until 1994 for InfoSel to become profitable. The problems with their original online service was an example of how they were able to learn from their setbacks and redesign some of their ways of doing business.

Perseverance in developing a private IT infrastructure to combat the underdeveloped national infrastructure also required a long-term view. Prior to 1990, there was no information made available on U.S. markets about Mexico because there simply was no demand for such information. The long fought battle to establish InfoSel’s infrastructure turned into a competitive advantage as the well-established U.S. information companies, including Knight-Ridder and Reuters, who were beginning to pursue the Mexican market required a large shift from their normal infrastructure to operate efficiently in Mexico. InfoSel was in an enviable position of having already developed a low-cost option that allowed them to be more expansive. This telecommunication infrastructure was heavily leveraged in the new Internet-based products.

## 5.7. Synergy

Another aspect of institutional fabric that has been critical in providing strategic flexibility is the cultivation of teamwork at every level. Every product is managed by a crossfunctional team. Where possible, processes (such as the editorial process, or technology development) are managed by a team spanning all three businesses. For example, editorial staff of Reforma and El Norte meet daily via a virtual newsroom. The head of the financial newsroom described his view:

A lot of things that we do is to create synergies between the newspapers, the real time services, and the information database products. This enables us to be there first and have better stories and better information. To achieve the synergy, we communicate and communicate and communicate.

The group’s goal was to reach seamless operations across its businesses where it made sense. For example, to promote synergy, minimize publication of effort, and ensure that the work was assigned to the most fit person regardless of the location, the group began to physically exchange the managers in the newsrooms every other week. One manager remarked, “Seeking synergy seems to be against a human nature. You have to work very hard to erase association.”

In summary, the leadership and connections of Alejandro Junco has enabled the Group to distinguish itself by its foresight of the coming trends. Its tradition of fact-based journalism and political independence has enabled the Group’s new online products and services to leverage the core capability of trustworthy information with its customers. Its culture, external networks, information technology, long-term view, and pursuit of synergy have been the bases of building the dynamic capability of strategic flexibility.

## 6. Discussion

The purpose of this study was to uncover how a local information firm in a less developed country can mold its environment and create sustainable advantage when the very foundations of the industry—information culture and information infrastructure—did not exist. Our initial analysis framework was the resourcebased view of the firm and the recent extensions to it.

## 6.1. Theoretical Implications

The primary contribution of the paper is the extension of the resource-based theory to the developing country context. Teece et al. (1997) extended the resource-based view to what was called “environments of rapid technological change.” We are further extending their view to environments where the firm has to shape the environment itself, not just proactively respond to it. Needless to say, our contribution is complementary to the original resource-based view and the dynamic capabilities framework.

Hamel and Prahalad (1994) maintain that for a firm to influence the trajectory of industry development, the organization needs (1) to have the capability to build and manage coalitions that leverage resources and capabilities across firms, (2) to be the market leader in the capabilities that will provide customer value, (3) to continually accumulate market learning through experimentation faster than competitors, and (4) to have a dominant market position and perhaps more importantly the “share of mind” among its future customers.

Clearly, the Group did not possess all requisite resources to materialize the vision of the Mexican information company. Section 5.4 described the many partners of the Group. These partnerships combined, focused, complemented as well as co-opted and protected valuable resources and capabilities. For example, when InfoSel entered the financial market, the Group began negotiations with the Dow Jones Corporation, and in fact Dow Jones became interested in legal ownership of InfoSel and Reforma (Alves 1997). But the Group was unwilling to cede editorial control and the partnership was instead formed around close collaboration and joint production of the Wall Street Journal Americas as well as InfoSel becoming the Mexican distributor of Telerate.

The second condition for shaping the evolution of the industry is developing unique and valuable capabilities that will provide future customer value, so called “gateways to the future” (Hamel and Prahalad 1994). The development of these capabilities can not be justified financially because they are more related to creating and perfecting “a class of future benefits” than any product or service lines. When Junco created the vision of an information company, there was no financial industry to speak of. All financial services in Mexico were government owned and regulated. The trade liberation process was introduced in 1985, which eventually lead to NAFTA in 1990s. The financial sector liberation started in 1987 including the commercialization of commercial banks; the government regulations of financial instruments were largely eliminated by 1990. These changes led to the emergence and rapid growth of financial intermediation services (Gonzales

1998). InfoSel was there when these changes in industry were occurring. InfoSel had as much understanding of these changes as the financial industry itself. Moreover, the Dow Jones partnership was critical for the Group to preempt the competition in accumulation and learning about online distribution of quotations and financial information to the newly emerging customer base.

The third condition for shaping the industry is market learning. This was mastered through close working relationship with the lead customers, hiring the best local talent often educated abroad, investing in technology and technology skills, and forming preemptive, exclusive relationships with partners that possessed related skills and capabilities (e.g., Dow Jones and Microsoft). Given that the financial sector itself was being reformed and now for the first time faced competition, there was room to create opportunities and the Group was able to develop the resources and capabilities faster than the domestic and the multinational firms.

The final condition for shaping an industry is the current market positioning. Although Mexico has experienced economic growth in the last fifteen years, the growth has occurred disproportionally in the North, especially Monterrey, Mexico (Gonzales 1997), the home of 60 year old El Norte, where the newspaper dominates the market. The Group has been able to leverage El Norte’s position as a foundation for testing its online information products. It was the strong market position of El Norte and later Reforma that likely helped Infosel attract businesses and individuals to try the new online products.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

The case study reported in the paper aims to provide a view of the environmental contingencies and constraints under which one information industry firm operated and how it influenced those contingencies and constraints and in fact turned them into sources and methods of competitive advantage. Hence, environmental factors can play both the roles of constraints and drivers in less developed countries (Nee 1992). For example, the Group found that the government posed certain constraints, even viewing the companies, particularly InfoSel, as a competitor at times. However, the relationships that the Group built with government officials also proved to be advantageous over the long term. They were able to get a lead on important information that foreign-based firms would have no access to and were able to eventually negotiate important deals that foreigners would have been precluded from even considering. Similarly, while the Mexican culture itself posed constraints on the Group’s ability to achieve its desired goals, the culture also provided an asset internally (in terms of motivating employees that their work was tied to the success of Mexico) and externally (in terms of forming strong ties with the community). And while the technical infrastructure of Mexico posed an initial constraint on InfoSel, this weak infrastructure also provided an advantage over foreign firms as InfoSel was able to readjust their project to the country’s capabilities and later negotiate concessions from the government. Thus, the environment has a profound effect on the operations of a local firm in a less developed country, both in imposing constraints as well as in providing opportunities if the firm is able to manage those constraints and contingencies. The dynamic capabilities (strategic foresight and flexibility) were critical in transforming constraints to opportunities. The dynamic capabilities model with an added emphasis on shaping the environment, provides some promise to the developing country context.

## 6.3. Limitations

The study has a number limitations that need to be considered in making any conclusions. First, any strategic analysis is situational and hence can not provide generalizable results, only direction. The purpose of the study was not to provide generalizability of empirical results to other firms, but rather explore the applicability of RBV to the developing country context. Second, the primary data were based on interviews that provided retrospective accounts of events. We took precautions against retrospective biases and errors as suggested by Golden (1992, 1997). We used multiple informants and consulted extensive bibliographical sources on the Group and the Mexican information industry and found over 30 articles (in Spanish or in English) from the period of 1990 to the summer of 1997. The events described in those articles were consistent with the interview data. The case study was also read by four different experts of Mexican and

Latin American studies for reporting errors who made no major corrections to the case. These individuals had no personal stake in the three companies, although they were familiar with Alejandro Junco’s enterprises. Third, another limitation of the study is that we analyzed the case data via a theoretical lens developed in the United States. The emphasis was not on understanding the events using Mexican’s own management thinking, terms, and constructs. Our approach is justified as long as one believes that there may be universal management principles (Early and Singh 1995). Finally, we were unable to gather financial information on the Group and on the direct competitors of the Group.

## 7. Conclusion

The information industry is in its infancy in Mexico and in many developing countries. The case reported here illustrates the ability of local managers to shape their external environment to pioneer an information industry where no industry exist. The findings bring forward the power of strategic foresight and strategic flexibility in effecting change in its own operations, in its customers as well as in the industry overall, while honoring the cultural and institutional traditions so critical in environments that celebrate the past.

We invite future researchers to build on and/or challenge our extensions to the resource-based view of the firm. The current study raises questions regarding the degree to which the resource-based view presents a culturally ladden view of strategic analysis and the degree to which it applies to firms in emerging versus established industries. From the information technology point of view, this study also raises questions of the role of social and political relationships outside the firm in enabling firms to gain sustained competitive advantages from IT, not just how IT is organized and managed within the firm. Finally, much research is needed using longitudinal research designs that explore the evolution of capabilities, both IT and non-IT related, of firms in emerging industries in less developed countries.<sup>2</sup>
