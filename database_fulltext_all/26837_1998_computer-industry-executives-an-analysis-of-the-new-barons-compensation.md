# Computer Industry Executives: An Analysis of the New Barons’ Compensation

Eli Talmor • James S. Wallace

Graduate School of Management, University of California, Irvine, California, 92697, and

Recanati School of Business, Tel Aviv University, Israel 69978

etalmor@uci.edu

Graduate School of Management, University of California, Irvine, California 92697 jswallace@uci.edu

n this paper we study the compensation determinants for CEOs in the computer industry I and compare these findings with a large sample of firms from other manufacturing and service industries. We examine whether superior performance is rewarded by higher levels of compensation and find cash-based compensation, such as salary and bonus, is influenced by performance. Depending on the growth prospects of the company, pay is tied either to accounting measures of performance or to stock return. In contrast, stock-based compensation, such as options and restricted stock awards, is not reflective of performance but depends upon the firm’s growth. Two other interesting findings are that the prevalent use of stock-based compensation in the computer industry does not appear to be the result of computer firms being “cash starved.” In addition, stock-based compensation does not appear to lead to larger executive stock ownership, as is widely believed.

(Executive Compensation; Pay-for-Performance; Cash-Based Compensation; Stock-Based Compensation; Stock Options)

## 1. Introduction

The emergence of the computer industry to stardom is one of the most remarkable phenomena in modern times. The transition of technological leadership from the giants of the post-industrial revolution era to newly formed enterprises has redefined many basic concepts of business management. If capital investment was the core of the industrial revolution, it is human capital that plays the central role in the rise and fall of computer companies. The dynamic environment of the computer industry has presented management with some unfamiliar challenges. The need to attract and retain top talent in a fiercely competitive and freeminded environment has become an ongoing challenge to most computer companies.

Any company, whether a mining business, a garment manufacturer, or a supermarket chain, needs to assure a high degree of employee retention and prevent recruitment by other, similar companies. Computer companies, however, also need to worry about the possibility of executive departures to join or form a start-up enterprise. Both the intellectual stimulation and the monetary reward associated with a successful new enterprise are enormous in the computer industry. This has made it essential to transform human resource management practices, adapted from established industries, to better fit the unique and intense needs faced by computer companies. The structure of executive compensation is a critical component in that scheme. Other, more specific characteristics of the computer industry are the level of risk and often the lack of a long-proven managerial track record when recruiting a new executive. Although the overall corporate goals remain the same, the design and implementation of executive compensation in the computer industry can significantly differ from practices in other industries.

This study analyzes the structure and determinants of executive compensation in the computer industry. We compare a large sample of computer companies with companies from the manufacturing and service sectors over the period 1992–1995. A main concern of the analysis is the effect of the form of CEO compensation and company characteristics on the pay-forperformance relation. The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

n For CEO cash compensation (mainly salary and bonus):

L Cash compensation is tied to company performance.

L Growth-oriented computer companies pay more.

L Cash compensation in high-growth companies is tied to performance in terms of stock return, i.e., to a performance measure that reflects expectations. On the other hand, for computer companies where growth is more modest, cash compensation is influenced by accounting measures (i.e., delivered performance).

L Managerial share ownership results in a nonmonotonic relation between cash pay and performance.

n Stock-based compensation (primarily option awards) is neither related to measures of performance nor to managerial ownership. However, its level depends on firm growth orientation.

n The use of stock-based compensation does not seem to be related to conventional measures of either short-term or long-term liquidity shortage.

n Executives appear to sell shares already owned when awarded new options or when exercising previously awarded options.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In § 2 we discuss the design of executive compensation and the variables that have been found in the literature to be determinants of executive compensation. Section 3 provides a description of the data in this study. We discuss our empirical results relating to CEO compensation in § 4. In § 5 we discuss findings that contradict widely held beliefs about stock-based compensation. Section 6 summarizes the paper.

## 2. The Economic Determinants of Compensation

Since managerial inputs, especially talent, are too complex to define or quantify in a meaningful way, shareholders prefer judging management on the basis of outcomes. As management output is also unobserved directly, researchers attempt to define measures that proxy for managerial productivity. The overall goal of the performance evaluation process is to choose output measures that minimize the noise relative to the information of the signal (i.e., signal-to-noise ratio), given risk aversion (Lambert and Larcker 1987). Both accounting and market return measures have been considered for top executive compensation; however, no conclusion has been reached regarding which type is more appropriate. From the shareholders’ perspective, it seems appropriate to define return as shareholder return rather than accounting return. Also, accounting numbers are ex-post measures and, as such can relate to performance only in terms of attained utilization of assets in place. In high-growth industries such as the computer industry, most of the assets are in the form of human capital and are not reflected in financial accounting statements. Hence, performance proxies that are based on book values are a rather inadequate measure of economic value. In contrast, market-based performance, being an ex-ante measure of value, may reflect achievements by managers such as technological innovations that will produce profits in years to come. Measuring delivered performance by shareholder return does suffer a few serious limitations, however. Most importantly, it is subject to significant marketwide fluctuations that are not controllable or accountable by the manager.<sup>1</sup> In addition, share price can reflect market speculations on such items as the likelihood of the company to be taken over. These speculations subject share price to large fluctuations that do not necessary reflect the productivity of the manager. In the presence of a risk averse agent, share price may not provide the best signal-to-noise ratio. Because earnings-based incentives do not suffer from these problems, they serve to shield managers from much of the noise associated with stock market fluctuations that are beyond the control of the individual manager.

The empirical literature has identified a strong linkage between accounting measures of return and top executive compensation.<sup>2</sup> A significant relation has also been found between CEO compensation and the stock market rate of return (e.g., Coughlan and Schmidt 1985 and Murphy 1985). Ely (1991) provides evidence that the relative weight set on alternative measures of performance varies by industry. The current study utilizes both the accounting measure of return on assets (ROA) and total shareholder return (RET).

In addition to performance measures, there are other factors included in the study. Numerous empirical studies document a strong positive relation between company size and executive compensation (see, e.g., Ciscel and Carroll 1980, Kostiuk 1990, and Schaefer 1998). Early justification was that size reflects management responsibility and job complexity and so requires a larger compensation. Lazear and Rosen note, however, that “On the day that a given individual is promoted from vice-president to president, his salary may triple. It is difficult to argue that his skills have tripled in that one-day period” (1981, p. 847). Lazear and Rosen’s “tournament theory” offers a rationale for this puzzle. The corporation is viewed as a hierarchical control structure, paying according to the person’s ordinal rank rather than by marginal contribution. The excess compensation for the top executive provides an incentive to executives lower down the corporate ladder who effectively participate in a rank-order tournament for the top trophy. We account for size of the company by the natural log of its total assets for that year, denoted LNASSETS.

From a theoretical standpoint, the CEO’s share ownership is probably the most intricate determinant of compensation. A recent body of literature, starting with Morck et al (1988), rely on both agency theory and empirical evidence to argue that the relation between firm performance and the degree of managerial share ownership is nonmonotonic. When managers own only a few shares, increasing their share ownership provides a better congruence of interest with outside shareholders. As their share ownership increases, they gain a tighter control and may engage in nonvalue maximizing activities. However, above a certain ownership level such activities become too costly for them, and a closer realignment of objectives with outside shareholders is achieved once again. The consequences of such a nonmonotonic impact of ownership on the pay-for-performance relation are not obvious. On one hand, the literature in organizational theory emphasizes the increase in the power of the CEO, as ownership can be viewed as a proxy for influence over the board. If a manager has enough shares to control the board, there may be no significant external forces to impose an effective pay-for-performance relation, and managers might engage in entrenchment. Yet, on the other hand, Stoughton and Talmor (1999) show that managerial share ownership interacts with the bargaining power and materially influences the optimal mechanism-design compensation schedule. Moreover, they show that incentives provided by share ownership and option awards act differently and therefore are not substitutes. Additional factors, which have been shown in the literature to affect the relation between ownership and control, are personal wealth and risk aversion.

The empirical literature on the CEO ownership-pay relation has largely indicated a negative association between the two variables (e.g., Core et al. 1997). The results have been dependent, however, on whether the CEO is part of the group which control the board or is a professional manager hired by the board (Hambrick and Finkelstein 1995 and Bar Yosef and Talmor 1997). We consider the percentage of the CEO’s common stock ownership as reported in the annual proxy statement, denoted OWNERSHIP. The variable excludes unexercised option grants.

There are several arguments that call for a positive relation between tenure and compensation. First, in terms of information asymmetry, the passage of time on the job allows the manager the opportunity to accumulate a track record. Information can work in either direction: It might either prompt a departure, if negative information is transmitted, or it might increase the CEO bargaining position should the information be positive. A positive relation is therefore expected between tenure and compensation. Second, Gibbons and Murphy (1992) argue that young persons need less formal incentive pay structure to be motivated, yielding a positive effect of tenure, especially on cash pay. Third, from a bargaining standpoint, the longer the CEO serves, the more influence he/she is likely to accumulate over the nomination of new board members, acting toward further entrenchment (see Hill and Phan 1991 for further discussion).

While we predict a positive effect of tenure on compensation, there are additional considerations that may make the tenure-compensation relation subtler. As has been lucidly pointed out by Hambrick and Finkelstein, long-tenured CEOs may have a lower mobility, lowering their bargaining power. Moreover, “If the long-tenured CEO becomes very committed to established policies and strategies and gives little consideration to new directions, then the person’s worth to the organization is diminished . . . As long as the firm experiences ‘satisfactory’ experience, the executive will not be replaced . . . However the CEO’s compensation may start resembling the figurehead role he or she may have evolved into” (1995, p. 181). In the context of our study, we predict that the effect of tenure on the fixed component of compensation will be positive. However, the predicted direction of the effect of tenure on the contingent compensation components such as option grants is unclear and might also depend on the ownership structure, i.e., be more pronounced for cases where the CEO ownership, and hence control, is higher.

The level of firm risk can also influence the design of the compensation structure. Executives’ wealth is typically concentrated in their human capital, and they cannot easily reduce their personal portfolio risk via diversification. Thus they may tend to make less risky choices than what would be optimal for diversified shareholders. We use two risk measures: the standard deviation of ROA, r(ROA), to reflect the degree of operational risk, and BETA for the stock’s risk. Based on the mechanism design literature we predict, for the fixed salary component of compensation, more risky firms compensate managers more to make up for the excess risk. The impact on the stock-based compensation is expected to be similarly positive, as risk sharing calls for more stock-based compensation. This will also influence the manager to undertake higher risk projects, thereby mitigating the lower risk tolerance conflict. Finally, risk sharing has been shown to be optimal for addressing the moral hazard problem.

Achieving a high growth rate is an important objective for numerous computer companies. Although actual growth has been occasionally used by previous studies, it would be quite misleading to do so for the computer industry, since an unusual portion of actual growth is obtained via acquisitions rather than through internal growth. Three measures will be used to assess the growth potential of a company. The first is the firm ratio of market to book values (e.g., Lewellen et al 1987). The more the shareholders are willing to currently pay for a given book value, the more they are confident that this book value does not correctly represent the growth potential of the company and its future earnings potential. For statistical convenience we will use the reverse ratio, i.e., the book to market ratio (denoted B/M), for that measure. The other two proxies for growth are the extent to which the corporation spends its resources on improving its technology and increasing its market share. Research and development is aimed at achieving a technological advantage, while advertisement is aimed at increasing market demand. Both these activities commit current resources for the purpose of increasing future profitability. The particular combination of the two can vary greatly between a technology-oriented company on the one extreme and a mass retailer on the other. Both measures are consistent with the informationprocessing demands theory, which argues that managers are paid according to their job complexity and for the information processing demands that are placed upon them (Henderson and Fredrickson 1996). Each measure has been used by other researchers (e.g., Mehran 1995, Kole 1996, and Hambrick and Siegel 1997). The research and development proxy, denoted R&D, is defined as R&D expense divided by sales.<sup>3</sup> The advertising proxy, denoted ADV, is defined as advertising expense divided by sales.

Finally, indicator control variables for separate years are added into the analysis to allow for possible changes over time in industry-wide compensation levels.

## 3. Sample Data and Descriptive Statistics

In October 1992, the Securities and Exchange Commission issued new rules regarding the disclosure of top executive compensation in the annual proxy statement (Schedule 14a). Item 402 of SEC Regulation S-K establishes guidelines on the detail and format by which corporations are required to disclose their top executive compensation. Compensation disclosure is required for the CEO and the other four most highly compensated executive officers (providing that their total annual salary and bonus exceeds \$100,000). Schedule 14a lists separately all major cash compensation: salary, bonus, payout from long-term incentive plans, and other annual compensation (such as perquisites, payments to cover taxes, etc.). Disclosure is also required regarding unit amount, terms, and grantdate values of stock-based compensation: restricted stock awards, stock options grants, Stock Appreciation Rights (SARs), and repricing of previously granted options. Finally, Schedule 14a states the number and value of options and SARs exercised by the executive during the fiscal year.

Compensation data for the top five executives are compiled by Standard and Poor’s (S&P) and are available in its ExecuComp database. The database includes all the compensation items from the proxy statements outlined above for approximately 1,500 companies. Option grants and restricted share ownership data are adjusted for stock splits occurring during the sample period. We supplement the ExecuComp compensation data with additional company financial variables obtained from Standard and Poor’s Compustat.

For the purpose of our analysis, we define computer companies as those with industry SIC codes of 3570– 3577 (hardware), 3674 (chips) and 7370–7376 (software). The control group includes all other manufacturing and service companies (SIC 2000–3999, 7000–

8999). A total of 39 control companies and one computer company are removed because they lack significant sales (R&D expenditures exceeded 50% of sales and often 100% of sales).

Compensation reported around events of succession appears distorted. Compensation for executives who served as CEOs for part of a year is not distinguished from those who served a full year. Clearly, if a CEO was appointed in October, the disclosed compensation is seriously biased downward. On the other hand, turnovers are occasionally associated with huge upward biases since the compensation at that time may include unusual items (such as golden parachutes) of extraordinary magnitude. We identify all cases of CEO succession and remove the entire company year from our sample data. A total of 32 CEO firm-year observations for the computer firms and 248 CEO firm-year observations for the control firms are deleted due to partial year information.

We identify all cases for which the company was not a public company during the entire period. This includes companies that had their IPO during the sample period or companies that were acquired or taken private during the period. We delete 15 computer firms (13 hardware and 2 software) and 99 control firms due to a lack of a complete four year data.<sup>4</sup>

After applying the procedures above, we obtain data for 108 computer companies (44 software, 39 hardware, and 25 chips) with 396 CEO firm-years, and 686 other manufacturing and service companies with 2429 CEO firm-years.<sup>5</sup>

The reality, however, is that the mean CEO share percentage ownership is larger outside the computer industry than within.

## 4. Multivariate Analysis of CEO Compensation Determinants

To track the factors affecting the structure of CEO compensation in the computer industry, we conduct pooled time-series cross-section regression analysis.<sup>8</sup> The analysis is performed for separate compensation components. Since some of the components (LTIP and restricted shares) are negligible instruments in the computer industry, and might serve a role similar to other compensation mechanisms, it is more useful to aggregate total compensation into two main forms of compensation. The first is CASH, which includes salary, bonus and LTIP payouts. The second is STOCK-BASED, which combines options and restricted share awards. A similar procedure is used for the noncomputer manufacturing and service companies. Table 3 presents the results of the four regressions. We turn next to take a closer look at the computer industry. We partition the sample into two equal size groups based on their growth prospects. We define growth by the book to market ratio and use the median of 0.29 as the split point. Computer companies with a B/M ratio lower than 0.29 are defined as high-growth, whereas companies with a B/M ratio above that level are labeled as low-growth. Separate regression results for CASH and STOCK-BASED compensation for the two groups are reported in Table 4.

CASH is significantly related to performance for both groups. However, the particular measure of performance differs. For high-growth computer companies, cash compensation is related to stock return (RET), while for low-growth companies it is related to ROA. This result has a certain appeal since the accounting metrics measure delivered performance and the utilization of assets in place. Strategic decisions have long-term consequences, which may not be reflected in current period accounting numbers. The limitation of conventional accounting numbers is particularly acute for high-growth companies, as earnings from existing operations does not reflect an adequate portion of the tasks faced by top management. Other responsibilities such as technological leadership and market penetration may be dominant considerations. This explanation is supported by the fact that the R&D variable is highly significant in explaining both of the compensation components for the high-growth companies. The stock return (RET) captures performance dimensions beyond those reflected in the current financial statement. In line with the above reasoning, RET has a significant positive effect on cash compensation for the highgrowth computer companies. As for the low-growth companies, the sign of RET is positive but not significant. Apparently, the focus when designing compensation packages is more on delivered performance as is evidenced in ROA. Finally for CASH, the operational risk variable, r(ROA), which is significant for the computer industry as a whole, is now only significant for the low-growth companies. One possibility is that operational risk is already captured through R&D and growth for the high-growth companies, thereby not requiring additional compensation.

The most important result concerning STOCK-BASED compensation is the lack of correlation to either measure of performance, for both groups. R&D, on the other hand, plays a significant role in explaining stock based compensation for the high-growth companies. This result is consistent with Balkin and Gomez-Mejia (1985) who documented a linkage of R&D to performance-based pay for the high-tech companies they surveyed.

In summary, the main results of Table 4 are that performance affects cash compensation in low-growth companies through the accounting performance measure, while stock market return affects the cash compensation in high-growth firms. Stock-based compensation does not seem to be performance sensitive for either group. The results are largely consistent with Lambert and Larcker (1987), who find that highgrowth firms (measured by actual growth rate in assets) tend to place more emphasis on RET than on accounting measures of performance. However, we find no other evidence in the literature to the perplexing lack of relation between options (and restricted shares) compensation and performance.<sup>11</sup>

Table 5 presents six pooled time-series cross-section regressions based on CEO percentage ownership and the form of compensation. As in previous regressions, the adjusted R<sup>2</sup>-scores for the CASH variable are higher than those for STOCK-BASED. The columns for CASH display an interesting pay-performance relation. Cash compensation is found to be significantly related to the ROA measure of performance both for the high- and low-ownership cases, however there is no such significance for the mid-ownership level. This result is strongly consistent with the economic agency literature. As discussed in §2, when CEO equity ownership is low, managers are essentially employees whose ability to exert influence on board members is limited, thereby allowing the board to implement a compensation package that relate managerial pay to firm performance. For CEO mid-ownership levels, between 1% and 5% of all shares outstanding, board independence and ability to effectively discipline the CEO is pared down. As the data show, no pay-forperformance relation is documented for such a case. Finally, the class of CEO high-ownership reflects a situation where there is much more at stake in the manager’s equity position than in the job itself. Hence there is a predisposition of interests between management and outside shareholders, resulting in the positive pay-for-performance relation for salary and bonus pay.

The results of Table 5 are consistent with Morck et al. (1988), who document a similar nonmonotonic relation between managerial ownership and firm market valuation as measured by Tobin’s Q. They are also consistent with Bar Yosef and Talmor (1997), who report a tighter relation between cash pay and performance in companies for which the CEO is also a block shareholder, vis-a-vis a hired professional.

The relation between STOCK-BASED compensation and performance is more clear-cut, as none of the two performance measures seems to have any influence. Regardless of the level CEO share ownership, compensation in the form of restricted stocks and options awards is not associated with performance. This result has stayed robust for our entire analysis of the computer industry and is not impacted by any of the partition criteria used.

## 5. Common Beliefs About Stock-Based Compensation

In this section we explore two commonly held beliefs about the use of stock-based compensation. The first belief is that options are so prevalent in the computer industry because these firms are unable to afford the cash compensation needed to attract and retain highlevel employees. The argument is usually framed in such a way as to consider stock options a substitute for cash, with the implication that the computer firms are younger and not able to generate sufficient cash. The second belief we explore is that stock-based compensation serves to increase managers’ ownership stake, and thereby reduces agency costs.

The rational for the prevalence and more extensive use of option grants in compensation packages in the computer industry has been the subject of some speculation. An often-heard argument is that computer companies are more cash hungry, thereby preferring the use of stock-based contingent payments to cash bonuses. To examine whether computer companies lack funds more than their counterparts, we compare certain measures of solvency and liquidity (see Table 6). Both the current and quick ratios are higher for the computer companies than for the other firms in the manufacturing and service industries (the difference in medians between the quick ratios is sizeable: 1.85 vs. 1.08). In terms of indebtedness and solvency, the mean debt-to-equity ratio is 19% for the computer industry vs. 67% for the noncomputer sample. We also include two measures of cash flow, free cash flow, and cash flow from operations divided by employees. The computer firms, even though they are roughly half the size of the noncomputer firms, generate more free cash flow (median amounts of \$11.4 million versus \$8.1 million). On a per-employee basis, cash flow from operations is also nearly twice as high for the computer firms (median amounts of \$23.6 million versus \$12.4 million). Apparently, neither immediate cash rationing nor long-term indebtedness may rationalize the more systematic use of options by computer companies. Tax arguments also seem unlikely since taxes should play similar roles in all industries.

The third column of Panel C of Table 7 displays estimates of how executive stock ownership changes when the executive is awarded new stock options. Since the executive does not actually receive any shares at this time, the null hypothesis is that a zero change will occur in total stock ownership for the executive during the year. The coefficient on the options parameter is negative, indicating that for every 1000 new options awarded, the executives on average sell 14 shares. This value, in contrast to the findings of Ofek and Yermack (1997), is not significantly different than zero. Executives in the computer industry, although appearing to sell some shares when they receive new option awards, are not as likely as their noncomputer industry counterparts to sell current stock holdings.

The next column estimates the effect of stock option exercises on executive stock ownership. Since each option exercised represents an additional share owned by the executive, the null hypothesis is that the executive will retain the share leading to a coefficient on the exercise parameter of 1. Not only is the coefficient not 1, it is negative.

Next we estimate restricted share awards. Although these are not nearly as common as options, the effect on total share ownership appears consistent with option exercises. The coefficient on the restricted stock parameter is again negative, indicating executives actually sell current shares such that total ownership goes down when restricted shares are awarded.

The final column represents a test of robustness by including each of the three independent variables in the regression. All observations in which any of the three events occur are used in this estimate. The values of the coefficients are all quite similar in sign, magnitude, and significance from the prior regressions.

The overall findings from this analysis is that when executives in the computer industry are awarded stock-based compensation, or when they exercise previously awarded options, they appear to hedge the additional risk exposure by selling shares they already own. This is in contrast to the belief that stock-based compensation will lead to larger ownership holdings and reduced agency costs. This is consistent, however, with our two previously reported findings. On one hand, executive share ownership is lower in the computer industry than in other manufacturing and service industries. Yet, average stock-based compensation is larger for the computer firms, relative to noncomputer firms, both in absolute dollar terms and as a percentage of total compensation.<sup>12</sup>

## 6. Summary

This study analyzes the structure and determinants of executive compensation in the computer industry. We compare 108 computer companies with a large group of control companies from the manufacturing and service sectors over the period 1992–1995. The major findings of this study are:

n During our sample years, computer firms outperform the other manufacturing and service firms both in terms of accounting measures (e.g., ROA) and market measures (e.g., shareholder return).

n Computer firms appear to be approximately twice as risky as the other manufacturing and service industry firms are.

n In terms of both dollar level and percentage of total compensation, cash salary and bonus is lower for

CEOs in the computer industry than for the other CEOs in our sample.

n In contrast, stock-based compensation is substantially higher in terms of both dollar level and percentage of total compensation for computer industry CEOs.

n Surprisingly, we find CEO tenure in the computer industry to be about the same as in the other industries. Share ownership for CEOs in the computer industry is actually lower than in the other industries during our sample period.

n With respect to the pay-for-performance relation, we find that executive cash compensation is tied to performance, although the particular performance measure that relates to compensation differs depending on the growth prospects of the computer firm and the CEO share ownership.

n In stark contrast, stock-based compensation is not associated with either measure of performance. A major determinant of stock-based pay is R&D, but only for high-growth companies.

n Contrary to common beliefs, the prevalent use of stock-based compensation in the computer industry does not appear driven by a lack of cash. Instead, we find computer firms with superior liquidity and solvency measures in addition to superior cash generation.

n Finally, the use of stock-based compensation in the computer industry does not lead to increased executive stock ownership. Executives appear to be following modern portfolio theory and hedging the additional risk exposure by selling shares they already own.

While cash compensation seems to follow patterns that are at least in part consistent with alignment of interests, very little positive is found with respect to stock-based compensation. This presents a challenge to one of the holy poles in the computer industry shrine, as the use of options has been celebrated and perfected in this industry. As reflected in the data, other industries have been largely followers on the use of stockbased compensation. Unfortunately, with respect to executive stock options, the cross-sectional patterns exhibited in the study substantiate the popular view that executives are rewarded generously and largely independent of their performance.<sup>13</sup>
