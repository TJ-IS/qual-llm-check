## 1. Introduction

The Information Technology (IT) industry is playing a special role in the transition into the Information Age: It is the industry that creates the machine tools of the new age. Not only does the IT industry enable other industries to make the transition—it is also a leading user of its own tools.<sup>1</sup> Because it is global, highly competitive, and extremely dynamic, effective information processing and quick response are critical for success in this industry, where both the external business environment and firms’ internal processes march to a fast clockspeed. As the same traits increasingly characterize many other sectors of the economy, the IT industry serves as a model for other industries to follow as they complete their transition into the Information Age.

The IT industry studied in this paper is a large and important component of the information industries that are the subject of this Special Issue. These industries participate in the information value chain that describes the transformation of data inputs into information outputs. For our analysis, we define the IT industry as the industry that develops and manufactures the physical devices that perform the functions of the information value chain, including computers, peripherals, consumer and industrial electronics products, and office equipment. While these products vary in their functionality, all follow a similar processing cycle and use similar technologies: Data are captured in digital form, processed by integrated circuit chips, and presented for some application. The results can then be recaptured and stored, or they can leave the digital loop. Though specific formats and applications vary across the different products, similar processes underlie both the basic functions of all devices manufactured by the IT industry and their development and manufacturing processes.

The IT industry is characterized by fast clockspeed (Fine 1998). The rate of change in the industry’s external environment, including developments in technology, consumer preferences, and market conditions, by far exceeds that of other industries. The result is a remarkable decline in price/performance ratios and compression of product life-cycles. Figure 1, for example, shows the compression of product life-cycles in the Personal Computer segment of the industry over the 1988–1997 period.<sup>2</sup> These life-cycles have been declining at an average compound annual rate of 16.7%. Figure 2 shows the compression of life-cycles for our entire sample of IT companies, with an annual decline rate of 9.4% on average.<sup>3</sup> As a result of this time compression, firms in the IT industry have been strained to adjust the speed of their internal operations to the accelerating external clockspeed. In an earlier paper (Mendelson and Pillai 1998), we have shown that, indeed, organizations adjust the rhythm of their internal operations to the dynamics of their external business environment, and we introduced a measure of “clockspeed” that captures the rate of change both on the supply side and in the product market. Speed of operations, however, is only one dimension of organizational activity; in this paper we study firms’ informational response to the clockspeed of their business environments.

Dynamic business environments are informationrich by their very nature. A higher frequency of change creates larger volumes of information that need to be communicated, processed, and ultimately translated into decisions. Thus, dynamic environments impose demanding information processing requirements on the organizations that operate in them. On one hand, dynamic environments generate more information that the organization must take into account in its decisions. Hence, one response to increasing dynamics is to increase the organizational “bandwidth” through the use of better information and communication technologies. On the other hand, decision-makers are bombarded with large volumes of data they must cope with, and human information processing capacity is becoming a critical bottleneck affecting organizational effectiveness. As Robert R. Walker, Hewlett Packard’s Chief Information Officer, remarked in an interview with us, “while we’ve seen a great revolution in bandwidth and computing power, unfortunately the input/ output capability of human beings hasn’t changed much over the last decade.” Because of bounded rationality (Simon 1982), decision makers are compelled to follow strategies that reduce information overload and enable them to focus on smaller, but more relevant, sets of data. This necessitates a reduction in the scope of activities that they engage in, in turn reducing the number of information signals that impinge on them.

The view of organizations as information processing systems was put forward by Simon et al. of the socalled “Carnegie School” (cf. March and Simon 1958, Cyert and March 1963). This approach characterizes individuals by limited information processing capacity or “bounded rationality,” and views organizations as information processing systems that are faced with fluctuating streams of data presented by the external environment. A key role of organizations is to absorb these data, put them in context, and reduce them into manageable information and decisions. Because their information processing capacity is limited, organizations try to reduce uncertainty and information overload using a combination of filtering and uncertainty avoidance strategies. March and Simon (1958) argue that organizations use rules of thumb and routines or “programs” to cope with complexity and reduce the need for information processing. March and Simon (1958) define a program as a sequence of activities that are evoked in response to an external stimulus. Some of these programs have little or no discretionary content (e.g., the program evoked in response to a customer order), whereas others give decision-makers substantial discretion (e.g., formulating next year’s budget). Further, organizations learn from their experience and adapt their programs or routines in response to changes in the external environment. Their approach thus views the organization as an information processing system that assimilates inputs and uses its evolving programs to make decisions—and to gradually improve the code that constitutes these programs.

Some of these views were taken further by the contingency theory of organizational design (cf. Lawrence and Lorsch 1967, Galbraith 1973), which focuses on ways in which organizations respond to the contingencies presented by the external environment. Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) argued that organizational design has to be responsive to environmental uncertainty. Tasks with varying levels of uncertainty require different types of organizational structures. As companies are compelled to handle functions with a greater range of uncertainty, they must develop a variety of structures while still sharing information across the organization. For example, product development might be carried out by temporarily constituted project teams that work together intensely on a single project, while the accounting function might be organized in a more traditional permanent structure. The two, however, still need to share information and interact with each other. Galbraith (1973) developed these ideas further, relating task uncertainty to information processing requirements. He suggested a combination of strategies that increase the organization’s capacity to process information, and strategies that reduce the need for information processing.

In this paper, we examine the use of specific strategies to accomplish these complementary goals in the context of the fast-moving IT industry. We consider both integration (or communication) and the reduction of information overload (or “focus”). First, following Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) and Galbraith (1973), we argue that, as the clockspeed of the firm’s external environment increases, the bandwidths of both internal and external communications become more important. This implies that firms that operate in more dynamic business environments should expand their use of communication technologies to improve information flows, both internally and with their business partners. Further, more dynamic business environments overload decision-makers with more data. We argue that firms counter information overload by using “informational-focus” strategies that reduce the amount of data decision-makers have to process, and hypothesize that the use of informational-focus strategies is positively related to the clockspeed of the business environment.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly discusses origins of business dynamics in the IT industry, our data sources, and the clockspeed measure employed in our analysis. Section 3 studies the impact of business dynamics on the use of communications technologies. Section 4 examines alternative model specifications, and §5 studies responses to information overload. Section 6 closes the paper with our concluding remarks.

## 2. The IT Industry

Our data come from business units in the IT industry. The total worldwide output of this industry exceeded half a trillion dollars in 1996 (cf. Electronics Business Today 1997). The hardware products manufactured by this industry support a variety of information processing tasks using integrated circuit chips. Thus on one hand, our sample consists of business units that operate in environments with a variety of clockspeeds; and, on the other hand, all of them engage in the development and manufacturing of information processing devices that have at their core the same basic technology:

semiconductor devices mounted on a collection of printed circuit boards.

Business units that operate in the IT industry experience high rates of change in technology, customer choices, and market conditions. Indeed, this industry is among the most dynamic sectors of the economy, as evidenced by its relatively short product cycles and rapid price/performance improvement.<sup>4</sup> The unprecedented rate of change in the IT industry is largely a result of the rapid development of the underlying semiconductor technology. The dramatic decline in the cost/performance ratio on the supply side enables manufacturers to continually hone their existing products and processes to best use the newly available chips, and to come up with breakthrough products that open new markets and create innovative applications.

Rapid developments on the supply side are echoed by changes on the demand side. Among the drivers of demand-side changes are the evolution of consumer preferences, the effects of complementary products and the threat of substitute products. In addition, changes in customer demand are often related to the rise of new technologies following performance breakthroughs. In the innovation-driven IT industry, supply can create its own demand.

## 2.1. Data

We studied the IT industry in a comprehensive survey that was conducted jointly by Stanford University, McKinsey & Company, Inc., and the University of Augsburg in Germany in 1994–1995.<sup>5</sup> The survey covered manufacturers in the IT industry worldwide—including business units in North America, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region. Since many of the firms in the industry produce a variety of products, the survey was administered at the business unit level. The sample consisted of 67 business units and covered both the business unit’s overall structure and performance, and a product group that was examined in detail. Frequently, this product group was responsible for most of the business unit’s revenue.

A wide range of products were represented in the survey sample, including computers, peripherals, office equipment, consumer electronics, and industrial and other electronic products. All these products support information processing tasks along the information value chain using integrated circuit chips. Despite this similarity, there is considerable variation in environmental conditions across the different industry segments.

The survey instruments were comprehensive and covered basic financial, managerial, and human resource data; data on manufacturing and product development; and data on marketing and sales. Early in the process, a comprehensive list that covered all significant players in the industry formed the universe of potential participants. These business units were selected based on industry publications, lists of large players in each industry segment, and general industry knowledge. The list was pruned<sup>6</sup> based on the likelihood of response and the desire to have a representative sample within each industry segment. Then managers at the highest corporate level were contacted to secure participation, resulting in a sample of 67 business units.<sup>7</sup> There were multiple informants, typically including the business unit controller, the human resource manager, the functional vice presidents, and their respective staffs. Each business unit was in contact with a team of research assistants who answered questions and reviewed the business unit’s responses. Following the completion of each survey, two members of the team conducted a set of on-site interviews (over a one- to two-day period) to validate the responses. The names of all firms were suppressed following this process to ensure confidentiality.

## 2.2. Measuring Clockspeed

In an earlier paper (Mendelson and Pillai 1998), we developed and validated a measure of clockspeed (or dynamics) that captures the speed of change both on the supply side and in the product market. This measure was based on three drivers: (1) the rate of decline in the prices of input materials; (2) the duration of the product life cycle; and (3) the “freshness” of the product line. We review each driver briefly below and then outline the method by which the composite clockspeed metric was computed.

Change in Input Prices. Our supply-side driver is the overall annual percent rate of decline in the prices of electronics items that were inputs to manufacturing. As already discussed, the prices of key electronics inputs fall sharply over time. This variable correspondingly measures supply-side turbulence.

Similar measures of dynamics and change have been used in earlier studies, both on the supply side and on the product market side. Williams (1992) classified industries as “slow-cycle,” “standard-cycle,” and “fastcycle,” depending on whether product price change was positive, close to zero, or negative, respectively. By this classification, almost all segments of the IT industry studied in this paper are “fast-cycle,” whereas cars and household appliances are “standard cycle,” and the health industry is “slow-cycle.” In our sample, more than 95% of the business units reported a decline in input prices, indicating that the IT industry is moving faster than most other industries.

Product Life. Product life, or the duration of the product life cycle, was defined as the time interval (in years) from the point when the product was first shipped to the point when the firm stopped shipment of the product.<sup>8</sup> This variable is a direct measure of product obsolescence. Since a shorter lifespan indicates a faster clockspeed, we used the negative of the product life value as our variable, to maintain a consistent direction. Norton and Bass (1992) estimated that the time from the beginning of the life cycle to the point of peak sales was about 5 years for drill-bits, 8 years for diapers, 11 years for a class of pharmaceuticals known as “blockers and inhibitors,” and 23 years for diuretics. In our sample, the total duration of the life cycle had a mean of only 3.2 years for the IT industry as a whole, and the more dynamic segments of the industry (like personal computers) had product life values below 1 year. Thus, product life cycles are clearly much shorter in the IT industry than in many other industries.

Product-Line Freshness. This was measured by the share of total revenues that came from products introduced within the previous 12 months. Whereas the product life variable measures the span of the product life-cycle, this variable gives an indication of the shape of the curve that depicts sales over time: higher demand for new innovative products shifts the peak of this curve towards the early portion of the lifecycle. The “vintage charts” used by Hewlett-Packard (Packard 1996) play much the same role in the company’s analysis of its product innovation. A similar chart was used by von Braun (1990) to represent companies’ innovation. Von Braun notes that 3M Corporation, a highly-touted innovator, set a target of 25% for the share of revenues coming from products that were developed in the prior five years. In contrast, the average value of product freshness (i.e., the share of revenues from products introduced during the last one year) in our sample was 37%, with values as high as 81% in the more dynamic segments of the IT industry, like the Personal Computer segment. Even allowing for some increase in clockspeed in the other industries over time, the IT industry is clearly much more dynamic and innovation driven than many other industries.

Examining the correlations between the three measures of clockspeed, we found that the two that are primarily market-related measures—product life and freshness—are very highly correlated (correlation coefficient 0.80), as one might expect (Mendelson and Pillai 1998). The correlation between these and the supply-side measure of input price change, although not as strong, is highly significant (0.60 with product life and 0.71 with freshness, both significant at ${ \sf p } =$ 0.0001). This suggests that firms in the IT industry do not just react to price/performance improvements by passing input price reductions through to customers. Rather, they take advantage of these improvements to develop new products that deliver greater value to the customer, and sometimes create entirely new customer segments.

Aggregate Clockspeed Metric. Our aggregate clockspeed metric was based on the three variables described above. Since clockspeed reflects the firm’s business environment, it was computed at the industry (or product) segment level, rather than the individual business unit level.<sup>9</sup> A principal-components analysis of the three indicators showed (Mendelson and Pillai 1998) that a simple average of the three would yield a satisfactory composite,<sup>10</sup> which explained about 80% of the variation in the sample.<sup>11</sup> The three variables were first standardized to a common scale (zero mean and unit standard deviation) over the entire sample of business units. The composite clockspeed metric was then calculated and standardized as the mean of the three scaled variables.

In Mendelson and Pillai (1998), we validated the composite clockspeed metric both for internal coherence and for consistency with data external to the surveys. In addition, we showed that the pace of the firm’s internal operations was largely driven by the clockspeed of its industry segment, further confirming the validity of our metric.<sup>12</sup> Figure 3 shows the values of the aggregate clockspeed measure, and its three (standardized) components, across the major segments of our IT industry sample. Clearly, the computer segments have the highest clockspeeds, followed by consumer electronics, while the specialized industrial electronics segments are the slowest.

As the dynamics of a firm’s business environment increases, there is more information to be analyzed, and more decisions need to be made. The proverbial horse-and-buggy system of communications will not do in a world where decisions must be made rapidly, with as much accurate and timely information as possible, and by individuals who are separated by large geographical distances. Dynamic environments require more advanced communication technology solutions to support rapid communications. Further, firms that operate in fast-clockspeed environments have to combat information overload by limiting the scope of activities and number of data signals they contend with. Thus, organizational contingency theory gives rise to the hypothesis that business units that operate in more dynamic business environments will respond by expanding their use of electronic communications and by pursuing focus strategies to combat information overload. We study the adoption of both strategies and their dependence on clockspeed in the next three sections.

## 3. Use of Communication Technologies and Portable Systems

Traditionally, communications between individuals and organizations have been conducted through faceto-face meetings, through telephone conversations, and by the use of paper memoranda. The availability of modern communication technologies has increased the feasible bandwidth and speed of communications. Affordable videoconferencing options make it possible to conduct meetings without physically transporting people to the same room at the same time. The problem of people not being permanently present at their office is addressed through the use of voice mail and/or pagers, and the transportation of paper messages is being replaced by e-mail.

Indeed, companies are increasing their IT spending, with much of the increase going to connectivity and networking (Violino 1997). In 1998 and beyond, companies are “pouring dollars into networks” and into “facilitating the sharing of information” (Asbrand 1998), with the IT industry leading the way (Gillooly 1997). According to Information Week’s (1997) annual IT spending survey, companies in the IT industry spent on average \$15,480 per employee in 1996—more than twice the survey average of \$6,918. IT spending was 4.5% of sales in the IT industry, compared to an overall sample average of 2.9%. Further, companies in the IT industry spent on average 14.2% of their IT budget on online and Web-based infrastructure in support of communications—more than twice the sample average (6.1%). Ron Maheu, chairman of Coopers & Lybrand’s national high-tech group, attributes this to the faster clockspeed of the IT industry: “Compared to other industries, for these high-tech companies, the pace of change is so fast, the life cycles are so much shorter, everything is so much more intensive . . . Even the smallest issues have a much greater impact.” (Gillooly 1997). Johansen et al. (1991) list “pace—fast and getting faster” as the first “pain” creating the need for teambased communication technologies, consistent with Opper and Fersco-Weiss (1992), who cite the increased “velocity of time” as the primary impetus for the increasing use of such technologies. Indeed, by the analysis in §2, we expect the use of electronic communication technologies both within and across organizational boundaries to be positively associated with clockspeed.

Electronic communication technologies can be classified into synchronous (or real-time) and asynchronous (or time-shifted) technologies (cf. Khoshafian and Buckiewicz 1995). Synchronous, or real-time, communication technologies are, as the term implies, technologies that enable the communication of information among two or more parties in real-time. This includes everything from the simple phone or pager to videoconferencing and sophisticated multimedia telecommunication units, enabling information to be transferred to (or among) decision-makers in time to take immediate action. Asynchronous or time-shifted communication technologies, on the other hand, involve the processing and storage of data (or information) to be considered and acted on at a later time. Paper-based mail is a form of time-shifted communication—information is stored (in a letter) to be retrieved at a later date (when the letter is received) and then acted on. Electronic mail and voice mail speed up the process, but do not give participants the ability to secure instantaneous attention and feedback for time-critical assignments as their real-time counterparts do (cf. Khoshafian and Buckiewicz 1995, Ch. 1). These media also lack the extra “bandwidth” that is associated with interaction in real time. This implies that compared to time-shifted communication technologies, the use of real-time communication technologies should be more sensitive to clockspeed (cf. Khoshafian and Buckiewicz 1995, Ch. 1; Opper and Fersko-Weiss 1992, Ch. 2). We thus expect that the relationship between clockspeed and usage will be stronger for these technologies.

This is not to say that time-shifted technologies are not useful in dynamic environments (and, in fact, we do find a widespread use of such technologies in our sample). Rather, while time-shifted technologies are useful irrespective of the level of dynamics, we expect the implementation of the more expensive real-time technologies like video-conferencing to be more sensitive to clockspeed.

In our survey, we collected data on the use of technology for better information exchange, both within the organization and between the business unit and external entities. We view the use of Electronic Data Interchange to link the business unit with its suppliers as a way of extending communications beyond the firm’s boundaries. In addition, we examine the availability of systems that support the sales force in its interactions with customers on the customer’s site. As discussed above, we expect the use of these technologies to be positively related to clockspeed. Specifically, we test the hypotheses that (1) the use of electronic communication technologies by development teams; (2) the extent of electronic data interchange (EDI) with suppliers; and (3) the use of portable systems that facilitate on-site information exchange with customers, are increasing functions of clockspeed.

## 3.1. Communication in Development Teams

We obtained data on the use of various types of timeshifted and real-time communication technologies (like e-mail, voice mail, mobile phones, pagers, and videoconferencing equipment) by product development teams, both internally and with external entities (primarily the firm’s suppliers). We coded the data using binary variables, with a value of 1 representing the use of each technology for internal and external communications. We then performed Probit regressions with these variables as the dependent variables and clockspeed as the independent variable. The model, represented by the following equation, is interpreted as the probability of the dependent variable x (e-mail, voice mail, mobile phones, pagers, or videoconferencing equipment) having a value equal to 1:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \{x = 1 \} = \Phi (a _ {0} + a _ {1} \cdot \text { Clockspeed }),\tag{1}
$$

where $\varPhi ( \cdot )$ is the cumulative standard normal distribution function.

Since earlier global studies of other industries found significant differences in organizational practices between different regions (cf., Womack et al. 1990, Clark and Fujimoto 1991), we controlled for such differences using dummy variables for the United States and the Asia-Pacific region, with Europe serving as the reference region. To present the quality of the overall fit of our Probit estimations, we used the likelihood ratio procedure (cf. Judge et al. 1985), based on the fact that twice the difference between the sample log-likelihood and the log-likelihood obtained under the null hypothesis of no relationship is asymptotically distributed as $\chi _ { \mathbf { k } - 1 } ^ { 2 } ,$ where k is the number of regressors in the Probit equation. The results are reported in Table 2.

We first estimated Equation (1) for asynchronous (time-shifted) communication technologies—the use of e-mail and voice mail. We found no significant relationship between the use of e-mail and clockspeed, although the coefficient of clockspeed had the expected sign. It appears that in the IT industry, the use of email is insensitive to clockspeed—e-mail is widely used in all industry segments. Also, there are no significant differences between the different world regions in the use of e-mail, although the United States is slightly ahead. With respect to the use of voice mail, clockspeed is weakly significant. The regression with the locational dummy variables reveals that Europe and the Asia-Pacific regions lag behind the United States. This is indeed consistent with our prior anecdotal evidence. Overall, the results show that the use of asynchronous communications technologies is either not explained by clockspeed or the relationship is weak, as hypothesized. ## 3.2. Electronic Data Interchange with Suppliers

A dynamic environment requires flexibility and speed in communicating and transacting with suppliers. Whereas the previous subsection addressed the role of human communications, both within the firm and between development teams and external entities (primarily suppliers), Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) is designed to accomplish this goal for structured transactions, using computer-based communications. ## 4.4. Does IT Use Drive Clockspeed?

While our discussion in §3 suggested a causal relationship between clockspeed and the use of IT for information exchange, our regression results by themselves cannot distinguish between cause and effect. These results are consistent both with the hypothesis that increased clockspeed leads firms to make more use of IT, and with the hypothesis that faster clockspeed is in fact caused by the use of IT. In general, it is difficult to establish causality, and researchers are content with noting that both directions are in fact consistent with the data. In this case, however, we can make additional inferences.

Our hypothesis is based on contingency theory, which posits a relationship between the attributes of the organizational unit’s external environment and IT. Thus, we measured clockspeed at the industry segment level and studied how it affects IT use. However, if differences in clockspeed are caused by the use of IT by the firm itself, then a business unit with a higher level of IT usage relative to its own industry segment should exhibit a higher level of individual clockspeed compared to its industry segment. Then, we should observe a significant positive relationship between clockspeed (the dependent variable) and IT use (the independent variable) at the business unit level, after controlling for the effects of the industry segments.

We thus estimated a regression of the individual business unit clockspeeds (the dependent variable) against the two aggregate IT variables, with dummy variables representing the various industry segments.<sup>18</sup> he resulting regression equation was

$$
\text { Business   Unit   Clockspeed } = - 0. 6 0 4 + \underset {(t = 1. 4)} {0. 0 8 9} \cdot \mathrm{IT1}
$$

$$
+ \underset {(t = 0. 7)} {0. 0 4 4} \cdot \text { IT2 } + (\text { Segment   Controls }).
$$

The results show that the coefficients of the two IT use variables are small and statistically insignificant.<sup>19</sup> Hence, they do not support the hypothesis that IT use leads to differences in clockspeed across business units. And, even if such an effect exists, it is small compared to that of the business unit’s external environment.<sup>20</sup>

It should be pointed out that the above methodology is in fact biased in favor of finding a positive relationship between business units’ clockspeed and IT use. This is because even if the causality is from clockspeed to IT use, but our industry segmentation were too coarse, the regression equation would still have positive coefficients on IT use, reflecting the disaggregation of the industry segments into subsegments that have not been controlled for. Thus, our results also support the adequacy of our segmentation.

In what follows, we put this hypothesis to test, examining the relationship between clockspeed and the adoption of informational-focus strategies in two key areas of interaction between the firm and its external stakeholders: suppliers and customers. Specifically, we test the following hypotheses:

The number of suppliers that the firm contracts with is a decreasing function of clockspeed.

The number of different parts that the firm purchases and uses is a decreasing function of clockspeed.

Increased clockspeed is associated with the use of key account managers, who focus on information relating to specific customers.

## 5.1. Informational-Focus in Purchasing and Materials Management

Informational focus in the firm’s interaction with its suppliers can be achieved in two different ways.<sup>21</sup> First, the firm can reduce the number of suppliers that it works with. Second, it can reduce the complexity of both its supplier relationships and its internal operations by reducing the variety of parts it purchases. An increase in the number of suppliers increases complexity of choice and requires the creation of new relationships and transactions with additional parties, aggravating the problem of information overload. On the other hand, by reducing the number of suppliers the firm may forego profitable opportunities to find better products, lower prices, or favorable delivery terms. We thus expect that firms in more dynamic business environments, where information overload is a more severe problem, will have a greater incentive to reduce the number of suppliers that they deal with.

The popular management literature recognizes the reduction in the number of suppliers and parts as a complexity-reduction strategy (cf. Childs et al 1991, Sheridan 1998). The turnaround in IBM’s personal computer division (Narisetti 1998) provides direct testimony to the relationship between clockspeed and complexity reduction. As IBM’s David Thomas pointed out following the turnaround, “This is about speed, and we [now] have it.” This was accomplished by hiring away Dell’s procurement manager “to help reduce complexity in buying and using parts,” and by reducing the number of components from 400 in 1994 to 200 in 1997, and the variety of parts in inventory from 56,000 to 15,000 over the same period (Narisetti 1998).

We hypothesize a negative relationship between clockspeed and the number of suppliers that the business unit contracts with. Since the number of suppliers is also a function of the volume of purchases, we had to first estimate the relationship between the firm’s purchasing volume and number of suppliers. We expect this relationship to exhibit substantial scale economies. Indeed, when we regressed the logarithm of the number of suppliers on the logarithm of purchasing volume, we obtained a coefficient of 0.257 (Table 9, Model I). Thus, a sixteenfold increase in purchasing volume is associated, on average, with roughly a doubling of the number of suppliers.

To make this analysis more complete, we also consider another driver of the number of suppliers— namely, the number of different parts purchased from suppliers. We hypothesized that the number of parts will also be cut down to combat complexity and information overload as business dynamics increases. Then, omitting this variable would leave us with a reducedform equation for the effect of clockspeed. Yet, it is of interest to examine how the number of parts will affect the results. To estimate the elasticities of the number of suppliers with respect to both the number of parts and the purchasing volume, we estimated the logarithmic regressions reported in Table 9, Model II. The results suggest that under the null hypothesis that clockspeed has no effect, the number of different parts is a key driver of the number of suppliers: Doubling the number of parts increases the number of suppliers by 50% on average.

In both cases, clockspeed has a strong and significant effect.<sup>22</sup> These results support our hypothesis that business dynamics compels firms to contract with fewer suppliers in response to information overload, and more complex estimations yield similar results.

As we noted earlier, the number of different parts being purchased is another indicator of informationalfocus, as it reduces complexity and mitigates information overload not only in purchasing but also in design and manufacturing. We first estimated the relationship between (the logarithm of) the number of parts and the business unit’s purchasing volume. As expected, the relationship is positive and nonlinear: Doubling the purchasing volume adds about 22% on average to the number of different parts (see Table 9, Model V). Next, we estimated the relationship between the (logarithm of the) number of parts and clockspeed, controlling for purchasing volume. The results, reported in Table 9, Model VI, are fully consistent with our hypothesis: The coefficient of clockspeed is significantly negative, indicating that, for a comparable level of purchasing volume, business units in more dynamic environments purchase and use a smaller variety of items.

These results may also reflect phenomena other than informational-focus strategies. The reduction in the number of suppliers may be driven by the need for closer supplier relationships in more dynamic business environments,<sup>23</sup> and the negative association between clockspeed and number of parts could be the result of faster clockspeed industry segments having more components packed into a single part (e.g., chip). Interestingly, both of these explanations can be interpreted from the perspective of the information processing view of the firm. A tighter supplier relationship goes hand in hand with more information exchange and a higher communication bandwidth. Thus, when close relationships with suppliers are called for, a given capacity for human and organizational communication must be divided among a smaller number of suppliers. Also, packing a larger number of components on a single part may be a viewed as a complexity-reduction technique for the downstream customer. Indeed, the suppliers of highly integrated components explicitly cite the reduction of complexity as an important advantage of their products (cf. Bursky 1994, Electronic Design 1997, Derman 1998).

## 5.2. Management of Key Customer Accounts

When a customer communicates frequently with a firm, it is advantageous to appoint a manager who will focus only on that customer account. As a result, the complexity of communications and information processing is reduced, and the account manager can manage the information pertinent to the customer relationship more easily and more effectively. Just as focussing on a small number of suppliers reduces complexity on the supply side, assigning the full relationship with a major customer to a single individual who is focussed on that customer relationship reduces information overload: The key account manager becomes the focal point of information flows between the customer and the firm.<sup>24</sup> And, since customer relationships are more complex and the required communication bandwidth is greater in more dynamic business environments, we expect a positive association between the incidence of key account managers and clockspeed.

We used a binary variable, KAM, to measure the response to our survey question of whether or not the business unit’s sales/marketing function used key account managers to focus on major accounts.<sup>25</sup> A value of 1 represented the presence of such managers, and 0 the absence. We performed a Probit regression with KAM as the dependent variable and clockspeed as the independent variable:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \{\mathrm{KAM} = 1 \} = \Phi (a _ {0} + a _ {1} \cdot \text {Clockspeed}).
$$

The results in Table 10 strongly support our hypothesis, with a positive clockspeed coefficient, significant at the 95% level. The probability that a firm that was one standard deviation above average in clockspeed would be utilizing key account managers was 99%. The corresponding probability for a firm that was one standard deviation below the mean was 54%.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

In this paper we studied the responses of firms to varying levels of clockspeed, viewing the firm as an information processing system. As a higher frequency of change leads to larger volumes of information that decision-makers must contend with, we hypothesized that firms in more dynamic business environments are more likely to be “interactive” and have a higher “bandwidth.” Consistent with the information processing view of the firm, we found strong relationships

Bounded rationality places limits on the amounts of information that decision-makers can process in a limited amount of time, leading to the problem of information overload. Thus, we proposed that firms that operate in more dynamic business environments would tend to adopt informational-focus strategies to a greater extent—i.e., they would take actions to reduce the number of information-states that their decisionmakers have to contend with. We found strong empirical support for this proposition. Business units that operate in more dynamic environments are, ceteris paribus, more likely to engage a smaller number of suppliers and use less parts. On the marketing side, business units that operate in faster-clockspeed environments have sales managers whose responsibilities are focused on key customer accounts.

Business units that operate in fast-clockspeed environments make increasing use of information technology to improve communication and interaction rather than provide solely traditional data processing services. The emphasis is shifting from “automating” to “informating” (Zuboff 1988), with IT becoming the means to properly route available information among decision-makers and assist them to act on it quickly and effectively. We expect this result to be invariant to the specific technology available at any given time. Accordingly, decision-makers will continue to seek the technologies most appropriate to achieve this goal. We believe this is one reason for the increased business use of World Wide Web-based technologies (such as intranets and extranets) in the later 1990s. It will be interesting to study whether the use of those technologies is related to clockspeed. Casual observation suggests the answer is affirmative, and we hope researchers will test this hypothesis. More broadly, our results suggest that clockspeed is an important driver of the demand for IT, and it will be interesting to examine how both the scale of IT investments and the types of applications used depend on clockspeed.

Another important avenue for further research is to study the relationship between firms’ information management practices and their business performance. In this paper, we have shown that effective information processing is a strategic necessity for survival in fast-clockspeed business environments. The next step (Mendelson 1998) is to examine the degree to which firms’ information management practices actually relate to their market and financial performance.<sup>26</sup>
