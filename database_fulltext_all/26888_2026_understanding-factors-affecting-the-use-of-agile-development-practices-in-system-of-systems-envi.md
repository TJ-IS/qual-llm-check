# Understanding Factors Affecting the Use of Agile Development Practices in System of Systems Environments

Jong Seok Lee,<sup>1</sup> Likoebe M. Maruping,<sup>2</sup> Mark Keil <sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Haslam College of Business, The University of Tennessee, Knoxville, USA, jlee236@utk.edu <sup>2</sup>J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, USA, LMaruping@gmail.com <sup>3</sup>J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, USA mkeil@gsu.edu

## Abstract

Interest in using agile development practices in large corporations continues to grow. However, obstacles exist when large organizations attempt to introduce agile development practices that were designed to be used in small organizational settings. Drawing from the perspective of a system of systems (SoS), this research sheds new light on what makes development teams less likely to use agile development practices in large organizations. Specifically, we suggest that many of the modern information systems in large organizations constitute an SoS—a system of systems, i.e., a large technical system that is comprised of multiple subsystems (e.g., software applications). Building such a system requires different teams working in a coordinated way to integrate the individual applications comprising the system. To understand obstacles to introducing agile development practices in SoS environments, we adopted an engaged research approach and conducted a field study with a Fortune 500 company. This company had traditionally relied on the plan-driven development method (i.e., waterfall development) but was implementing a voluntary transition to agile development practices. Senior leadership wanted to know why teams were unable or unwilling to use agile development practices. Our research at the company showed that a high level of development platform variety across applications within a project is negatively associated with a development team’s use of agile development practices. In addition, we found that the effect resulting from the choice of centralized vs. decentralized decision rights on a development team’s use of agile development practices is contingent on the levels of inter-application dependency and platform variety.

Keywords: Agile Transformation, Agile Development, System of Systems, Inter-Application Dependency, Software Development Platform Variety, Decision Rights Allocation

Andrew Hardin was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on August 3, 2023, and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Recent industry reports show that agile development has become popular among software developers. For example, in a survey of 788 software development professionals, a majority of the survey respondents (71%) reported using agile development (Martin, 2024). However, industry reports also suggest that agile development is more successful in small, nimble organizations and less so in larger organizations. Among larger organizations, only 43% believe that agile development works well in their environment (Martin, 2024). Further, research suggests that among companies that have embarked on agile initiatives, only about half have successfully embraced agile development (Grebe et al., 2024). These reports raise the following question: Why do some companies that embark on a path toward agile struggle to achieve its widespread application? This research addresses this question.

At its core, agile development emphasizes delivering functioning software early and being responsive to business needs (Conboy, 2009). While there are varying definitions of agile development in the literature, there is broad agreement on the set of agile development practices underlying these objectives. The most commonly used agile development practices originate from extreme programming (XP) and Scrum, two popular agile software development methodologies that have been widely adopted for years. Therefore, in this research, we conceptualize agile development as a set of agile development practices that a given organization chooses to embrace.

In this research, we adopted an engaged-research approach (Van de Ven, 2007) with a Fortune 500 company to examine why application development teams may be unable or unwilling to use agile development practices. The company chosen for this research has been ranked in the top 50 on the Fortune 500 Companies list and in the top 20 on the Fortune World’s Most Admired Companies list for many years. More importantly, while this company had traditionally relied on the plan-driven development method (i.e., waterfall development), it was implementing a voluntary transition to agile development practices associated with XP and Scrum. The executive team preferred a gradual and voluntary transition to agile development practices over a sudden, mandated transition because the company had many internally developed proprietary systems coupled with very complex and unique business processes, and it felt that a more gradual transition would be less risky. Nonetheless, despite the encouragement and support for the use of agile development practices by the executive team, there was reluctance to use agile development practices among the development teams. This prompted the company to engage with our research team to better understand why application development teams resist using agile development practices. In addition, as we engaged with the IT leadership team at the company, our observation was that the agile development practices being embraced in the company were those commonly associated with XP and Scrum, as these were the two agile approaches that were being promoted within the company. This led us to focus on the extent to which development teams use specific agile development practices associated with XP and Scrum. This ensured that there was no ambiguity about how development teams understand agile development practices.

Theoretically, we draw upon the system of systems (SoS) perspective (Ackoff, 1971) and suggest that in an SoS environment, teams face a tension between the desire to use agile development practices in their application development efforts and constraints that result from working on an application that is part of a broader assemblage of applications that comprise a system. We focus on two factors that are representative of an SoS environment, namely inter-application dependency and platform variety. Inter-application dependency can be understood as the extent to which an application is dependent on other applications comprising a larger system (e.g., the number of other applications on which an application is dependent for data outputs or for executing function calls). Platform variety can be understood as the extent to which development platforms on which applications comprising a larger system vary. We examine how inter-application dependency and platform variety affect application development teams’ likelihood of using agile development practices.

In SoS environments, there is a need for inter-team coordination. Agile development practices tend to emphasize and promote decentralization in terms of intrateam collaboration and decision-making. In contrast, when it comes to inter-team coordination, there is a lack of theoretical guidance or empirical evidence as to whether a centralized or decentralized approach is more beneficial for promoting the use of agile development practices. Specifically, in SoS environments, decision rights can be centralized (e.g., the project manager overseeing the development of a large system composed of multiple applications or subsystems) or decentralized (e.g., team leads managing each individual software application, which is part of a larger system). Centralized decision rights may enable greater decision efficiency for managing coordination across multiple software applications that are part of a larger system. However, such centralization can constrain development teams’ ability to be responsive to their localized needs. Conversely, decentralized decision rights may give development teams greater flexibility in their decisionmaking in terms of when and how to respond to interteam coordination needs. However, it creates challenges in coordinating activities across multiple development teams whose applications are part of a larger system. Therefore, allocating decision rights in an SoS environment represents a challenge for which current theory offers little guidance. In this research, we suggest that the effect resulting from the choice of centralized versus decentralized decision rights on a development team’s use of agile development practices is contingent on the levels of inter-application dependency and platform variety.

This research makes two notable contributions to the literature. First, inter-application dependency and platform variety are unique features that arise in SoS environments.<sup>1</sup> While inter-application dependency and platform variety may already be known as challenges in large-scale software development (Sekitoleko et al., 2014; Tiwana, 2015), it is relatively unknown if they might affect development teams’ choice of development approach (i.e., make development teams less likely to use agile development practices). Therefore, our research contributes to the literature by drawing on an SoS perspective to suggest that inter-application dependency and platform variety may lead to the underutilization of agile development practices in an SoS environment. This offers a potential explanation for why some companies struggle to switch from traditional plan-driven development methods to a widespread application of agile development practices.

Second, in the literature on agile development, decentralized decision-making is generally viewed as a hallmark feature underlying agile development practices (Šmite et al., 2023). Contrary to this view, we suggest that neither centralization nor decentralization of decision rights concerning inter-team coordination is always a superior choice for promoting agile development practices in an SoS environment. Specifically, in this research, we suggest and show that the choice of centralized vs. decentralized decision rights has no direct effect on an application development team’s use of agile development practices in an SoS environment. Instead, its effect is contingent on levels of inter-application dependency and platform variety. This represents an important advancement of the existing knowledge on decentralization and the use of agile development practices.

In sum, using an engaged research approach, we advance the understanding of why some companies struggle to achieve widespread use of agile development practices. We suggest that SoS environments present unique obstacles (e.g., interapplication dependency and platform variety) that make it less likely for development teams to use agile development practices. Based on this, we present the following research question:

RQ1: To what extent is an application development team’s use of agile development practices influenced by inter-application dependency and platform variety?

In addition, in this research, we offer theoretical insights and managerial implications that inform when to centralize vs. decentralize decision rights concerning inter-team coordination to promote greater use of agile development practices by development teams. Based on this, we present the following research question:

RQ2: To what extent is the effect of decision rights allocation on a development team’s use of agile development practices contingent on interapplication dependency or platform variety?

In the remainder of this article, we discuss research on SoS and relevant research on agile development in large organizations. Next, we discuss our hypotheses and present the research model. We then describe the field study conducted and discuss the results. Lastly, we discuss the implications of our study for research and practice as well as limitations and directions for future research.

## 2 A System of Systems (SoS) Environment

The development of a software application requires not only coding effort to build individual software modules but also team-level coordination effort to bring together and assemble individual modules as a coherent application (Xia et al., 2016). To date, much research has been conducted to better understand effective coordination among team members in a software development team (i.e., intra-team coordination) (Faraj & Sproull, 2000; Kudaravalli et al., 2017). In addition, in recent years, there has been a growing recognition of the fact that in modern organizations (particularly in large companies), software applications rarely operate in isolation.

Traditionally, systems used in organizations tended to be “monolithic,” in that they were built as one standalone unit (Harris, 2022). However, monolithic systems have become less common in modern organizations. Instead, applications are often comprised of smaller applications that are cobbled together to make a large system. For example, a 2020 industry survey of 1,502 software engineers and other systems and technical engineers conducted by O’Reilly Media found that 29% of organizations are adopting and implementing systems comprised of microservices (O’Reilly, 2020). Microservices are individual components that handle discrete functionalities of a software system, and when combined, they provide and support the operations of the whole system (Tozzi, 2021). Individual components comprising a larger system tend to be owned by small, self-contained teams (AWS, 2023). Therefore, many of the modern information systems in large organizations can be understood as a system of systems (SoS)—i.e., a large technical system that is comprised of multiple subsystems (e.g., software applications). Building such a system requires different teams working in a coordinated way to integrate their individual applications (De Weck et al., 2011). This perspective gives rise to the need to examine difficulties associated with introducing agile development practices in settings in which applications are not isolated and a project involves coordination across multiple teams (i.e., interteam coordination). Specifically, while the benefits of using agile development practices are well-documented, there exists a lack of theoretical understanding and empirical insights concerning the obstacles that make application development teams less likely to embrace agile development practices in SoS environments. Recent research on agile development sheds light on the coordination challenges that arise in large-scale agile development in which multiple teams work together to build a large system (Sablis et al., 2020). This research sheds light on what makes application development teams less likely to use agile development practices in SoS environments, and how to allocate decision rights concerning inter-team coordination to promote application development teams’ use of agile development.

## 3 Agile Development in Large Organizations

Software applications in large organizations tend not to operate in isolation. As a result, when agile development practices are used in a large organization where a project involves building a system composed of interconnected software applications, a prominent challenge arises regarding the need to align and coordinate activities across multiple teams responsible for building applications. This requires managing dependencies that may exist at the inter-team level (Edison et al., 2022).

Recent research has begun to address how an organization can deal with inter-team coordination challenges in agile development projects. For example, Berntzen et al. (2023a) conducted a case study of a large-scale software firm and identified 27 inter-team coordination tactics that can be broadly classified into three categories: coordination meetings, coordination roles, and coordination tools and artifacts. In addition, in recent years, several commercial large-scale agile frameworks have been developed, providing practical guidance on how to manage prioritization of requirements and coordination across teams in a largescale agile project (Block, 2023). Examples of such frameworks include the scaled agile framework (SAFe), large-scale Scrum (LeSS), and Scrum of Scrums. Nonetheless, research suggests that there are several challenges associated with applying these large-scale agile frameworks, such as inter-team collaboration and architectural issues (Edison et al., 2022). For example, as the number of teams to coordinate with grows, interteam meetings tend to take longer and become ineffective. Further, software components developed by one team may be incompatible with software developed by other teams. Such incompatibility can often result from a lack of shared understanding regarding the system architecture. Moreover, over-prescribing the practices of commercial large-scale agile frameworks can lead an organization to lose sight of foundational agile principles (Edison et al., 2022).

Nevertheless, the relationship between the characteristics of an SoS environment and how they affect development teams’ likelihood of using agile development practices remains unknown. Ultimately, development teams’ reluctance to use agile development practices can make it difficult for an organization to effectively transition to agile development practices from a traditional plan-driven development method. For example, in SoS environments, teams must coordinate with each other to determine and synchronize the release timing of applications that comprise a larger system because, due to inter-application dependency, the release timing of an application may depend on or be constrained by the release timing of another application. In anticipation of such inter-team coordination needs, development teams may be unwilling to use agile development practices in an SoS environment. For example, a development team using sprints (short, time-boxed periods during which a development team works to complete a set amount of work)—a well-known practice in Scrum—focuses on completing one sprint at a time, but a product typically needs multiple sprints to be ready for release. This means that the release timing of a product is often not set in stone and is, instead, contingent on what is accomplished in each sprint. This emergent nature of release timing in Scrum may present challenges to development teams in terms of coordinating with other application teams regarding release timing in an SoS environment.

In sum, while important progress has been made in the understanding of inter-team coordination issues in largescale agile projects, what remains unknown is why application development teams may choose not to use agile development practices, and how the characteristics of an SoS environment contribute to such a decision. This is an important knowledge gap because many companies struggle to achieve a company-wide transition to agile development practices from traditional plan-driven development methods due to the reluctance of development teams to embrace agile.

In practice, large companies seeking a widespread application of agile development practices often choose either the mandated adoption or voluntary adoption approach. The mandated approach involves a company forcing all teams to switch to agile development practices all at once, and is known as a “big-bang” rollout (Fry & Greene, 2007). Although the mandated approach can be effective, researchers warn of the risks associated with it. The mandated approach can help companies facilitate a quick transition (Fry & Greene, 2007); however, it requires a culture friendly toward agile development practices and many talented and experienced agile practitioners (Rigby et al., 2018). The voluntary approach, in contrast, represents an alternative in which development teams are encouraged to experiment with agile development practices. This approach may be more suitable in scenarios where development teams have traditionally relied on plandriven development, and there are many internally developed proprietary systems coupled with very complex and unique business processes/operations, which was the case for the company that we worked with for this research. Specifically, this company was focused on the voluntary adoption of agile development and wanted to understand the obstacles that might account for their development teams’ reluctance to embrace agile development practices. Therefore, our research has the potential to inform IT leaders at large companies who are trying to successfully transition to agile development practices without mandating it.

Finally, the IT leadership team at the company we worked with noted that an application development team had the latitude to decide which of the practices associated with XP and Scrum to use and to what extent. This is in keeping with the literature on agile method tailoring, which recognizes that every organization or team differs in the agile development practices that are implemented (for examples, see Fitzgerald et al., 2006). Therefore, we focused on understanding the use of six practices associated with XP (unit testing, continuous integration, automated build, coding standards, refactoring, and pair programming) and four practices associated with Scrum (burndown chart, sprint cycle, standup meeting, and retrospective meeting).

## 4 Development of Hypotheses

Coordination involves identifying and managing dependencies that exist between organizational tasks (Malone & Crowston, 1994). Successful coordination requires a team to come up with appropriate organizational arrangements, such as meetings and communication schemes to manage dependencies among tasks and to achieve the team’s collective goals (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). In software development, a dependency exists when the progress of a task is contingent on the input from another task, such as a piece of software code or data (Strode et al., 2012). In largescale development projects, such dependencies tend to exist not only within teams but across teams, hence requiring coordination not only within but across team boundaries (Sablis et al., 2020). Coordinating dependencies that exist across teams tends to be more complex in large organizations (Berntzen et al., 2021).

Dependencies that require inter-team coordination may exist among tasks or resources (Biesialska et al., 2021), but these different types of dependencies often arise from or are rooted in dependencies that exist among software applications that comprise a larger system. For example, in a package delivery business, there may be a system that deals with the management of physical packages. Such a system might be comprised of multiple software applications needed to support different services that are part of the system: a service for retrieving package status, a service for rescheduling deliveries, a service for requesting status change notifications, and so on. These applications must interact with each other (e.g., the application dealing with rescheduling deliveries must be able to send data to the application dealing with status change notifications) to support overall package management. These so-called inter-application dependencies represent the extent to which a software application is connected with other software applications (i.e., the number of ties that an application has with other applications in a system). In other words, we conceptualize inter-application dependency as an application-level factor representing the extent to which an application is dependent on other applications comprising a larger system.

A dependency that an application has with another application may vary along a spectrum with high integration on one end and high modularity on the other (Baldwin & Clark, 2000; Vickery et al., 2016). At the high integration end of the spectrum, the boundary that delineates the two applications is blurred, such that the two applications are essentially viewed as one system. In contrast, at the high modularity end of the spectrum, the two applications are designed to be self-contained but also have predefined interfaces through which to interact with each other (Baldwin & Clark, 2000). The design of contemporary information systems has generally moved toward modular design, as it enhances maintainability (Du et al., 2019) and facilitates greater flexibility in managing new functions via extensions (Benbya & McKelvey, 2006). Even with this modular structure, however, applications with related functionalities tend to require some degree of interaction, as they rely on similar data sources and may rely on each other for data processing.

Modern solutions such as containerization (Zhou et al., 2022) have the potential to help application development teams become more independent. However, the benefits associated with such approaches come at the cost of developing and maintaining interfaces connecting software applications that are part of a larger system. In other words, interfaces must be carefully designed to enable communication and interaction between applications comprising a larger system. Often, multiple interfaces for a single application need to be designed to enable integration with multiple different applications. Thus, an application development team must be aware of other applications that are interconnected with its own application and knowledgeable about the interfaces needed to support interactions with other applications. A case study by Bick et al. (2017) identified a variety of coordination breakdowns between interdependent agile development teams—including misaligned planning activities for specification, prioritization, estimation, and allocation—that occurred due to a lack of dependency awareness. In sum, modern solutions do not completely eliminate the need for inter-team coordination when an application is connected to other applications in an SoS environment.

Interdependencies that an application has with other applications comprising a larger system may present an obstacle to a development team that is contemplating using agile development practices. Specifically, an application’s dependencies with other applications in an SoS environment inevitably require the application development team to work with other teams to align the team’s needs with the needs of other teams (Berntzen et al., 2023b). This is because no single team possesses all the knowledge regarding all applications comprising a larger system, and the team needs to coordinate with other teams to achieve a common, system-level goal (Moe et al., 2019). In addition, a development team may need to carry a greater burden of inter-team coordination when its application relates to a greater number of applications. Therefore, when an application has a high inter-application dependency, this may interfere with the team’s ability to define a measurable and achievable objective for a development iteration. Specifically, an application development team may have less discretion in prioritizing its tasks for a sprint if other development teams need and demand specific outputs in order to advance their own work (Claps et al., 2015). The need to coordinate with other application development teams can add complexity to a focal team’s efforts to prioritize features for each sprint and achieve resolution to key issues during standup meetings. An application development team’s deliverables may need to be provided in a timely manner for another application development to move forward, and this would affect the development team’s ability to remain autonomous in terms of sprint and release planning. Similarly, prioritizing the elements that will be the focus of a team’s development cycle may require considering the availability of output from another team’s application. This can complicate efforts to perform unit tests and continuous integration as the number of other application development teams with which to coordinate increases. A systematic literature review by Shahin et al. (2017) identified dependencies with hardware and other applications, cross-team dependencies, merging conflicts with third-party components, and dependencies in design and code as key challenges that hamper the adoption of continuous integration as a practice. Therefore, when an application has a high number of connections with other applications in an SoS environment, it may create an impediment to the development team’s effort to adopt continuous integration as an agile development practice.

In addition, in the agile development environment, autonomy affords members of a development team the discretion to choose what tasks to work on, and when and how to accomplish them (Hoda & Murugesan, 2016; Hoda et al., 2012)—a key practice both in XP and Scrum. However, managing demands associated with an increasing number of inter-application dependencies may make it difficult for development team members to maintain their autonomy, and this may interfere with an application development team’s desire to use agile development practices. In other words, from the perspective of the application development team, the extent to which its application is interdependent with other applications may influence the team’s ability to use agile development practices. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: An application development team is less likely to use agile development practices when its application has a high inter-application dependency with other applications comprising a larger system.

In SoS environments, systems rarely operate on a single infrastructure. Instead, clusters of subsystems typically operate on different hardware or software platforms. For instance, the web-based client-facing applications of a system may be constructed on a Java platform, while the financial processing applications for managing payments may operate on hardware and software compliant with the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS). Consequently, interactions between the subsystems of an SoS can occur on a singular infrastructure or across a variety of platforms. In the context of systems development, a software development platform can be understood as the development environment in which a software application is developed. A software development platform is said to include a collection of hardware and software tools for software developers to use to build software applications (Taudes et al., 2000). For example, a Java platform typically includes an execution engine, a compiler, and a set of libraries. Each platform tends to have its own unique technological standards, and applications developed within a particular platform must adhere to such technological standards (Tiwana, 2015). Software applications built on a particular platform are generally not interoperable or compatible with software applications built on a different platform. This interoperability issue often leads developers to stick with one particular platform due to the difficulty of having to adapt their application to meet platform-specific standards for multiple platforms (Bröring et al., 2017). Moreover, research shows that when developers find it too difficult to meet a certain platform’s technological standards, they tend to abandon the platform and switch to a different platform (Tiwana, 2015).

In SoS environments, software applications that comprise a system may be tied to different platforms. For example, integrating legacy applications with new in-house-developed software applications and packaged software applications can be particularly challenging because these applications are built on different platforms (Byrd & Turner, 2000). When multiple applications that are part of a larger system are being built on different platforms, development teams must work with one another to enable seamless integration and ensure consistent technological standards across applications. Such a variety of development platforms may present a greater challenge for using agile development practices, such as continuous integration and coding standards (an entire team adhering to a consistent style of coding conventions). In addition, teams choosing to use agile development practices, such as coding standards and refactoring, may fail to reconcile the differences in development platforms existing in an SoS environment because they are focusing on optimizing the internal coding efficiency. Moreover, integrating software applications built on different platforms may require significant integration development efforts to accomplish data mapping and transformation, achieve interoperability, and ensure high-quality security (Ebert et al., 2017; Neifer et al., 2021), making it difficult for development teams to practice automated build or continuous integration. These additional technical considerations arising from platform variety in an SoS environment may present conflicts with agile development practices. As a result, such additional technical considerations may make development teams reluctant to use agile development practices. In sum, we suggest that the challenges arising from a variety of platforms across applications comprising a larger system may make a development team less likely to use agile development practices. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H2: An application development team is less likely to use agile development practices when there is high development platform variety among applications comprising a larger system.

Research on IT project governance focuses on understanding the effective control that can be employed by organizations to guide successful project management (Mähring, 2002). A central element in project governance involves allocating responsibilities for making decisions about project activities (i.e., decision rights) (Tiwana, 2009). In the domain of general IT management, decision rights can be shared to varying degrees between IT and client departments (Weill & Ross, 2004; Winkler & Brown, 2013). In SoS environments, decision rights concerning inter-team coordination can be shared to varying degrees between the project manager overseeing the development of a system (centralized) and individuals responsible for software applications comprising the system (decentralized). In addition, this choice of decision rights allocation can be made for individual applications; some application development teams may be given rights to make decisions for their application, while others may not be given such rights.

The effectiveness of decision rights control can be understood as how well a chosen governance style of decision rights supports project management activities (Tiwana, 2009). In discussing the relationship between decision rights governance styles and decision rights effectiveness, Tiwana (2009) suggests that the alignment between a decision rights governance style and interdepartmental (client and IT) knowledge has a positive effect on decision rights effectiveness. For example, when the client department possesses high technical knowledge, the decentralization of decision rights can positively contribute to the effectiveness of decision rights. Similarly, Kudaravalli et al. (2017) suggest that neither a centralized nor a decentralized approach is always superior in coordinating expertise in software application development teams. Moreover, Xue et al. (2013) found that allocating decision rights to the IT unit can help reduce the negative impact of system modularity on perceived risk associated with a digital system implementation. Taken together, research indicates that a particular governance style (centralized or decentralized) may be effective under certain conditions but not others.

Furthermore, for autonomous agile development teams to be successful, research suggests that team members must be allowed to identify important decisions that need to be made collectively and participate in shared decision-making (Moe et al., 2012). When decisions are made by project managers without involving developers, it can cause developers to not understand what other developers are doing and lead to a lack of trust (Moe et al., 2009; Stray et al., 2011). In SoS environments, project managers must decide whether to make decisions on their own to manage dependencies among software applications (centralized) or allow each development team to make their own decisions (decentralized). In other words, the extent to which inter-application coordination decisions are decentralized at the application level vs. centralized at the system level is an important decision to be made in an SoS environment. Such coordination decisions include establishing the timing of releases of applications comprising a larger system, synchronizing application release cycles with other applications comprising a larger system, and managing the integration of platforms with other applications comprising a larger system.

In SoS environments, allocating decision rights to each application development team can help development teams retain flexibility and team autonomy in their decision-making by eliminating the need to seek approvals from a higher level of authority. Moreover, while enabling development teams to make their own decisions may create additional work for the teams, it facilitates a localized approach to managing demands and enhances the development team’s flexibility in shifting between handling interdependency demands and pursuing their own objectives. For example, decentralized decision rights provide application development teams with the latitude to prioritize which inter-application dependencies to address in a given iteration and release. In addition, under the condition of high inter-application dependency, decentralized decision rights may allow an application development team to respond more effectively to changes in requirements involving multiple applications and integration requests from other applications. Therefore, for applications with high inter-application dependency, enacting decentralized decision rights can be more critical for helping development teams to achieve the autonomy and flexibility needed for agile development practices. In contrast, for applications with low inter-application dependency, the need for inter-team alignment and coordinated decision-making may be low, and decisions can be made at the project level without placing undue burdens on development teams’ ability to make decisions or negatively impacting their autonomy. Therefore, for applications with low inter-application dependency, centralizing decision rights at the project level can be more effective in enabling the application development team to use agile development practices. Building on these lines of argument, we propose the following moderation hypothesis:

H3: An application’s level of inter-application dependency moderates the relationship between decision rights allocation (centralized vs. decentralized) and the application development team’s use of agile development practices.

In SoS environments, there are increasing demands on each development team to collaboratively develop and manage integration processes that connect diverse applications and data stores that are part of a larger system. Specifically, a high degree of software development platform variety requires software application development teams to understand not only the technical requirements and standards unique to the development platform on which its application is built, but also those unique to development platforms on which other applications are built. Resolving interoperability issues of software applications across multiple platforms involves complex design decisions (Bröring et al., 2017). Prior research suggests that when development teams face ill-structured design problems that involve high uncertainty, the decentralization of design collaboration is more likely to lead to effective solutions (Kudaravalli et al., 2017). In contrast, a centralized approach may not be as effective because management may lack technical knowledge specific to each application. Further, modern scaled agile frameworks recommend that technical conflicts and dependencies in a large agile project be managed and addressed through participation and a cohesive effort from multiple teams working on a larger system (Berntzen et al., 2023a), rather than using a centralized approach dictated by the higher-level management.

Moreover, research suggests that in a team setting, it is important for team members to influence managerial decisions to achieve effective alignment and coordinated decision-making (Tata & Prasad, 2004). In a similar vein, we expect that in SoS environments, it may be important for each development team to influence decisions regarding inter-application platform alignment and integration. Research also suggests that in a large-scale agile project setting involving multiple teams, goals set solely by management regarding deliverables and deadlines tend not to be well accepted or understood by teams. As a result, teams may have difficulties in developing a shared understanding of common goals (Moe et al., 2019). Therefore, in SoS environments, there are many important issues to consider regarding the synchronization of different development and release cycles across applications being built on different platforms. Allowing each development team to have decision rights regarding these issues facilitates effective communication across different application teams. In other words, decentralizing decision rights at the application level creates a development environment where development teams can communicate more freely and make appropriate decisions with other application development teams to resolve the technical challenges arising from platform variety.

In addition, in software development, delayed feedback can have a detrimental effect on the outcomes of a software development application, particularly when the application must be integrated into a larger system (Kasauli et al., 2021). Centralizing decision rights at the system level can potentially delay receiving feedback and may be less ideal for a setting where platform variety is high. Specifically, high platform variety requires development teams to create a unified workflow across various stages of the development process to deal with data exchange issues across applications and achieve integrated functionalities. However, centralizing decision rights means that application development teams need to wait for the review and approval of management for platform integration. In addition, centralizing decision rights may prevent application development teams from directly talking with each other, hence presenting a roadblock to creating a unified workflow across application teams. In essence, while centralizing decision rights can have the benefit of managing coordination at the system level, it compromises the autonomy necessary for adaptability and responsiveness associated with agile development practices (Lee & Xia, 2010), rendering it a less enticing approach. Therefore, centralizing decision rights at the project level may make development teams reluctant to use agile development practices when technical challenges exist due to high platform variety across applications within a system.

In sum, while decentralizing design rights can impose additional burdens on a development team to make its own decisions and pay attention to technical interoperability issues with other applications due to platform variety, it also affords them the flexibility to develop a localized approach to solving problems. In other words, we argue that when there is a high degree of platform variety, decentralizing decision rights at the application level may encourage development teams to use agile development practices. In contrast, when platforms on which applications are built are invariant, centralizing decision rights can help alleviate burdens on a development team to make its own decisions without causing communication breakdowns or inefficiency regarding system integration and the synchronization of development cycles. Therefore, when platform variety is low, centralizing decision rights can help application development teams focus on intra-application issues and stay autonomous, hence creating an atmosphere conducive to agile development practices. Based on these lines of thinking, we propose the following moderation hypothesis:

H4: Platform variety across applications within a system moderates the relationship between decision rights allocation (centralized vs. decentralized) and an application development team’s use of agile development practices.

## 5 Research Method

## 5.1 Research Setting

In this research, we engaged with a Fortune 500 company that started an initiative to transition to agile development practices from a traditional plan-driven development approach. Specifically, for many years, the company had relied on plan-driven software development methods, most notably the waterfall method, for its software application development. However, the IT leadership at the company began to recognize several compelling benefits associated with agile development, including improved productivity, enhanced adaptability, and increased user/customer satisfaction.

The company is a global company focused on transportation, e-commerce, and business services. As of 2023, the company had more than 500,000 employees and revenues of over \$90 billion. The IT organization was located on its own 118-acre campus encompassing 20 divisions and 3,000 IT employees. This company provided an ideal setting in which to test our research model.

As a large global company, it was well-suited for examining questions arising from the characteristics of an SoS environment. This is because large companies tend to have software applications that are interconnected and have large systems comprised of and supported by multiple applications. In our conversations with senior IT managers in the company, it became clear that software applications tend to be clustered around specific systems and that each application tends to have varying degrees of interdependencies with other applications within a system. Further, systems tend to involve different platforms upon which software applications are built and deployed. These characteristics, which surfaced in our discussions with senior IT managers, convinced us that this company would be an ideal research context to examine why development teams may be reluctant to use agile development practices in an SoS environment.

## 5.2 Sample and Procedure

First, with the help of the IT organization, we identified 97 information systems development projects that had been completed during the past year.<sup>2</sup> In this study, a project corresponds to developing or modifying an information system, a project manager is assigned to oversee the development of a system, and there can be multiple software applications supporting a system. For example, there may be a project initiated to develop a new online ordering system, and there may be multiple software applications (such as a payment processing application, shipping application, customer management application, etc.) involved in supporting the system. While a team may be dedicated to developing an application, all applications are part of a single system. In identifying projects for the study, we consulted with IT directors and a vice president at the company. They helped us identify 97 projects that involved complex corporate systems. We decided to focus on these systems as each was likely to represent an SoS.

The project manager for each project we identified was invited to complete an online survey about their respective project. Project managers were provided with a URL to the online survey by email and given 2 weeks to complete the survey. At the end of the 2-week period, we received a total of 36 completed responses (out of 97 projects), yielding a response rate of 37%.<sup>3</sup> In the survey, project managers were asked to report the software applications involved in their respective systems, along with the application lead for each application. Using the information provided by the project managers, we invited each application lead to complete an online survey about their respective application in the context of the system to which their application belonged. Application leads were given 2 weeks to complete the survey. Next, we identified the projects where both the project manager and application lead completed their respective surveys and assembled a dataset containing a total of 59 application lead responses nested across 24 different projects. This yielded a response rate of 25% at the project level.<sup>4</sup> This response rate was comparable to prior IT project management research using the nested-design field survey approach (Venkatesh et al., 2018).

## 5.3 Measurement

Independent variables: Our research model involves three independent variables: inter-application dependency and decision rights control are applicationlevel factors, and software development platform variety is a project-level factor (see Figure 1).

First, inter-application dependency was conceptualized as the number of connections that an application has with other applications in a project. Within any given project, there is variation in terms of inter-application dependency across different applications, as some applications will have connections with a greater number of applications than others. In other words, not all applications in a project will have the same level of inter-application dependency when the construct is assessed at the application level. In addition, given that our focus was on understanding the effect of an application’s interapplication dependency on the use of agile development practices by the application development team, it was appropriate to measure inter-application dependency at the application level. Given that our research focuses on application development teams’ use of agile development practices, the model was developed from the perspective of the application development team. Operationalizing inter-application dependency at the application level enabled us to accurately measure the dependencies that each application development team experienced.

To measure inter-application dependency, we asked application leads to name “other applications that must produce deliverables in order for their application development to move forward.” We counted the number of applications named by the application lead and used it as a measure for inter-application dependency. This approach is quite consistent with how dependency is measured in network research. Specifically, in network research, core concepts such as degree centrality are measured at the node level and based on a count of connections that a node has with other nodes (i.e., edges) (Freeman, 1979). For example, an individual may have a lot of connections (friends) in a social network, and the number of connections would then be used to measure how well-connected the individual is in the network.

Second, the choice of centralized vs. decentralized decision rights involves who holds decision rights over inter-team coordination issues across applications in an SoS environment. In this research, we are interested in decision rights allocation between the larger system and smaller applications that are part of it. There are multiple applications that are simultaneously developed, and each application is managed by a different application lead. In our initial discussions with IT managers at the company where this study was conducted, it became clear that different decision rights allocations could be employed across different applications that comprise a larger system. In other words, it is possible for decision rights allocation to vary across applications within a project. Therefore, we believe it is appropriate to measure decision rights allocation as an application-level factor. For decision rights control, we used three measurement items adapted from Tiwana (2009).

Third, based on discussions with IT managers at the company where this study was conducted, it was clear that each application is typically built on a single development platform. Each application can be regarded as a software component that is part of a larger information system (project). Therefore, if we were to measure platform variety within an application, it would not be very meaningful, and there would be a lack of variance. In contrast, since a project consists of one or more applications that are potentially built on different platforms, variance does exist at the project level. Moreover, the challenges being examined in this study pertain to situations in which applications that comprise a larger information system must be integrated later in the development cycle. Therefore, the consequence of having applications that are built on different platforms within a project (platform variety at the project level) and how it affects an application development team’s use of agile development practices is a more relevant question from a theoretical point of view. For software development platform variety, we adapted two measurement items from Xia and Lee (2005). All measurement items and their sources for independent variables are shown in Appendix A.

Dependent variable: Our dependent variable, use of agile development practices, was measured as a secondorder construct consisting of 10 practices associated with two popular and widely used agile development methods: extreme programming (XP) and Scrum. Specifically, we measured the following XP practices: unit testing, continuous integration, automated build, coding standards, refactoring, and pair programming. In addition, we measured the following Scrum practices: burndown chart, sprint cycle, standup meeting, and retrospective meeting. In measuring these practices, we adopted the existing scales from Tripp et al. (2016). All measurement items and their sources for the dependent variable are shown in Appendix A.

Control variables: In this study, we focused on projects and teams within a single company. This feature of our study allowed us to eliminate potential intercompany differences that might have otherwise confounded the results. Therefore, our choice of controls was focused on factors that may vary across projects or development teams. Specifically, we included two control variables at the application level (new technology risk and requirements volatility risk) and two control variables at the project level (project size and agile project management). We included new technology risk and requirements volatility risk, as research suggests that agile development is well-suited when a project involves new technologies (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001) or when requirements tend to be emergent, as opposed to pre-specified (Ramasubbu et al., 2015; Ramesh et al., 2010). This suggests that when new technology risk or requirements volatility risk is high in an application, the application development team may be more likely to use agile development practices; thus, we wanted to control for these two factors. For new technology risk and requirements volatility risk, we adapted two measurement items for each construct from Wallace et al. (2004). In our study, projects involving both new and old systems were included.

In addition, we included project size as a control variable because agile development practices are known to be well-suited for small projects (Reifer et al., 2003). In other words, in our research context, an application development team may be more likely to use agile development practices when it is part of a smaller project. We collected data on the aggregate number of hours spent on analysis/design and requirement specification activities for each project. Based on this, each project was classified as small (1), medium (2), or large (3). Finally, a project environment that has more agile-like characteristics may facilitate the use of agile development practices at the application level. Therefore, we also controlled for this variable. Specifically, this allowed us to disentangle agile development practices (e.g., pair programming, acceptance testing, refactoring) from agile project management approaches. The rationale for doing this is that prior literature has pointed to method tailoring as a common practice whereby some projects only employ agile project management approaches as a framework to guide their development effort, others only employ agile development practices for code construction, and yet others employ both management approaches and development practices (e.g., Tripp et al., 2016). To ensure the robustness of our model, we accounted for the potential influence of agile project management as a strong driver of our dependent variable—use of agile development practices. All measurement items and their sources for control variables are shown in Appendix A.

## 6 Data Analyses and Results

First, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis of latent variables in the model, including control variables. We found that all but two items had high loadings with their associated latent variables (> 0.600). One item for continuous integration had a low loading (0.528), and one item for automated build had a low loading (0.458); thus, we removed these items from further analysis (see Appendix A). In addition, the average variance extracted (AVE) for each of the variables was above the recommended threshold of 0.50 (Hair et al., 2010). Lastly, Cronbach’s alpha for each of the variables was greater than 0.70, indicating adequate measurement reliability.

Given the multilevel nature of our research model and hypotheses, as well as the nested structure of the data, we used hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) to test our research model and hypotheses. Researchers use HLM to test relationships that exist across multiple levels of analysis and partition the variance in the dependent variable at each level (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). We conducted our analysis using the nonlinear and linear mixed-effects (nlme) package in the open source statistical software R (Bliese, 2016). Prior to conducting the analysis, we group mean-centered application-level variables involved in the interaction effects (inter-application dependency and design rights control). Group mean-centering facilitates better interpretability of interaction effects and minimizes the inflation of standard errors that can occur when within and between effects are conflated (Snijders & Bosker, 1999). We also mean-centered development platform variety, which was a project-level factor.

First, we examined the correlation matrix of study variables (Table 1) and did not detect any correlations that were high enough to pose multicollinearity concerns. It is noteworthy that applications in our sample had dependencies with about three other applications, on average (mean = 2.68, SD = 2.92). Next, we examined the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of the dependent variable for our model—i.e., use of agile development practices. ICC measures the proportion of variation in the outcome variable that occurs between groups (in our case, between projects) versus the total variation present (Finch et al., 2019). Our analysis revealed an ICC of 0.296, indicating that 29.6% of the variance in development teams’ use of agile development practices was attributable to between-project differences and that multilevel analysis was appropriate. In addition, in multilevel analysis, some of the variance in the dependent variable of interest can be attributed to between-project differences, whereas some of the variance is attributable to within-project differences. Specifically, we found that the estimate of variation in intercepts across projects was 0.468, while the within-project variation was 0.722. This indicates that the use of agile development practices varied across projects as well as within projects. In addition, we examined descriptive statistics of specific agile practices and techniques across 24 different projects. The descriptive statistics show that there was enough variation in the use of agile development practices across projects to allow for a meaningful analysis. The details are provided in Appendix B.

The results of the model estimation predicting the use of agile development practices are presented in Table 2. In the multilevel analysis, $\hat { R ^ { 2 } }$ values can be calculated for each level of the model; $R ^ { 2 }$ represents the amount of the variance explained in the dependent variable above and beyond what is accounted for in the null model that has no predictors (Snijders & Bosker, 1999). For our model, we obtained an $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.188 for the application level and an $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.163 for the project level. Like standard linear regression modeling, hierarchical linear modeling requires an assumption that the residuals of the model are normally distributed. To test this assumption, we examined both a histogram and a $q { - } q$ plot of the residuals of our model and observed a pattern of normal distribution (see Appendix D). Additionally, we calculated skewness for the residuals of our model, and obtained a skewness score of -0.006 (closer to 0 means closer to a normal distribution). We also conducted a Shapiro-Wilk test for the residuals of our model and obtained a score of 0.989 $( p = 0 . 8 6 4 )$ ). These results support the normal distribution of the residuals. Finally, we conducted two tests to add robustness to our study findings: (1) the same HLM analysis but with a stepwise approach and (2) multiple linear regression using orthogonalization. Results from these robustness checks are consistent with the results from the main analysis. The details can be found in Appendix C.

The coefficient on inter-application dependency is in the expected direction but is not statistically significantly different from zero $( \beta = . 0 . 0 6 7 , p = 0 . 1 4 5 )$ . Consequently, we failed to find support for H1, which posited a negative association between increasing inter-application dependency and the use of agile development practices. In contrast, the negative coefficient on project-level platform variety is statistically significant $( \beta = - 0 . 2 6 3 , p =$ 0.013), providing support for H2. Specifically, this result suggests that development teams are less likely to use agile development practices with projects involving an increasing variety of platforms. This finding corroborates our theorizing about development teams’ reluctance to use agile development practices in an SoS environment, where multiple applications are developed on different development platforms.

Next, we examined the results pertaining to the two moderation effects (H3 & H4). With respect to the interaction between inter-application dependency and the choice of decision rights allocation concerning inter-team coordination (H3), the results show a significant interaction effect between the two variables $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 4 , p$ = 0.045). Further, a plot of the interaction in Figure 2 and the results of the simple slopes test in Table 3 show that decentralization of decision rights was more effective in promoting a development team’s use of agile development practices $( \beta = 0 . 3 4 8 , p = 0 . 0 0 7 )$ when there was high inter-application dependency (mean +1 standard deviation). In contrast, centralization of decision rights was more effective in promoting a development team’s use of agile development practices (β = -0.241, p = 0.046) when there was low inter-application dependency (mean -1 standard deviation). Decision rights allocation had no effect on the use of agile development practices at the mean level of inter-application dependency. These results provide support for H3.

## 7 General Discussion

## 7.1 Implications for Research

Agile development has been proven to be an effective development approach that can help teams develop software applications capable of meeting changing or unpredictable user needs and requirements. However, large organizations hoping to introduce agile development are still having difficulties in making the desired transition. For example, a recent survey of 112 companies reported that 90% of the companies struggle with rolling out organization-wide agile transformation despite prior success with agile development in small projects (Cross et al., 2021).

Introducing agile development in an organization is a process that requires hard work, focus, and continuous learning (Schatz & Abdelshafi, 2005). Research suggests that a successful transition to agile development takes years of preparation and plenty of trial and error (Qumer & Henderson-Sellers, 2008). Further, agile development is more likely to survive and thrive in certain development environments, such as those involving co-located teams, small organizational settings, and software applications that are non-critical, greenfield, and in-house (Hobbs & Petit, 2017; Kruchten, 2013). Efforts to use agile development in other development environments have not been as successful (Dikert et al., 2016). Researchers have thus pointed out the need to examine how organizations can introduce agile development in settings where there may be particular challenges to doing so (Abrahamsson et al., 2009). Consistent with this call, we focus on why large organizations struggle with achieving the widespread application of agile development practices in SoS environments. A major obstacle to introducing agile development practices in large organizations is that development teams are not working on small stand-alone software applications and must synchronize their activities with other development teams that are working on interconnected software applications (Dikert et al., 2016), some of which may be built on different platforms. Drawing on the SoS perspective as a theoretical lens, this research enhances our understanding of obstacles that make development teams less likely to use agile development practices.

The notion of SoS originated from Simon’s (1991) seminal work on the architecture of complexity. In his work, Simon describes how a large system involves a collection of subsystems and how subsystems interact with one another to support the operations of the large system. This conceptualization has been extended to understand the inner workings of different types of complex systems, such as software systems, logistics systems, and telecommunication systems (Colfer & Baldwin, 2016). This conceptualization has been instrumental in advancing the understanding of IT systems as well as systems development. For example, research suggests that modularity can be of value when a system is complex, containing numerous elements (Ethiraj & Levinthal, 2004). However, inherent conflicts among modules exist in a system, and such conflicts can cause delays and errors during implementation, testing and integration phases (Ethiraj & Levinthal, 2004). For example, developing software applications in an SoS environment can be affected by interoperational issues among applications (e.g., variety in software development platform). Thus, one major contribution of this study is the new insights it offers by extending the concept of the SoS to research on the choice of development methods by development teams.

In addition, the shift from monolithic systems to microservices can be understood as transitioning from a single-system environment to an SoS environment. In a single-system environment, a single team is responsible for building the system, and the focus is on intra-team coordination. In contrast, in SoS environments, multiple teams are responsible for building subsystems or applications, and they must coordinate to support a larger system. This characteristic of the SoS environment makes it challenging for development teams to adopt agile development practices. For example, a coherent code style within an application, which is a key element of XP, may be difficult to achieve in SoS environments, where teams are required to deal with the technical variations (e.g., coding standards and conventions) existing in different development platforms across applications comprising a larger system.

Agile development practices tend to emphasize and promote decentralized work in terms of intra-team collaboration and decision-making. What we focus on in this research is decision rights allocation concerning inter-team coordination in SoS environments. In terms of inter-team coordination, there is a lack of theoretical guidance or empirical evidence on whether a centralized or decentralized approach is more beneficial for promoting the use of agile development practices. Specifically, additional coordination and management effort are required when a system consists of multiple applications and teams working together toward a common goal (Petersen & Wohlin, 2010). In such settings, prior research has found that coordination across teams can be achieved top-down by management or bottom-up by individual teams or through communications between individual teams and management (Moe et al., 2021). Furthermore, research suggests that centralizing coordination decisions to a single individual (or a group of a few individuals), such as a chief architect, may be beneficial. Doing so can help teams more effectively resolve the issues of system architecture dependency across multiple applications (Hohpe et al., 2016). Therefore, it is not entirely clear whether centralization or decentralization of decision rights is more beneficial in terms of inter-team coordination. We contribute to the literature by providing a valuable contingency perspective on enacting the centralization or decentralization of decision rights in SoS environments. Specifically, while we did not find a significant main effect of (de)centralized decision rights on the use of agile development practices, we found that its impact is contingent on the levels of inter-application dependency and platform variety. These findings are counterintuitive in that prior literature has emphasized that decentralization is an important principle underlying many agile development practices.

## 7.2 Implications for Practice

How to take agile development, which was intended for small organizations, and introduce it to large organizations has been an important operational topic in recent years in the software industry (Rigby et al., 2018). Our results show that in their efforts to transition to agile development practices in large organizations, managers should be sensitive to the fact that information systems in large organizations may be fundamentally different from those in small organizations. Specifically, agile development practices are often used with an implicit assumption of stand-alone software applications. Managers in large organizations, however, know all too well that software applications in their organizations must be integrated to support the operations of a larger system. What they may not realize, though, is the implication that this may have on efforts to use agile development practices in large organizations. Our study highlights a key factor that is specific to the SoS environment and can undermine the effort to use agile development practices—the degree of platform variety on which software applications are being developed. Managers need to recognize that this condition represents a potential barrier preventing development teams from choosing to use agile development practices.

Informed by our findings, managers wishing to introduce agile development practices in large organizations may attempt to reduce inter-application dependency or software development platform variety, but this may not prove feasible. Therefore, when dependencies between software applications or platform variety within a system are high, managers may need to empower their development teams by decentralizing decision rights concerning inter-team coordination. Doing so can allow the teams to use agile development practices not only for their application development but also to attend to more global demands at the system level. In contrast, decisions that are escalated to higher levels of authority may cause delays in responding to inter-team needs and can even decrease decision quality, as higher-level managers may lack sufficient understanding of application-specific knowledge. However, it is important to note that decentralizing decision rights is not always desirable for allowing development teams to choose to use agile development practices. When dependencies between software applications or platform variety within a system are low, managers may need to centralize inter-team coordination decisions.

## 7.3 Limitations and Directions for Future Research

As with any research, this study is not without limitations. One such limitation is that our sample size was relatively small. Given the nested nature of our design (applications within systems), it was difficult to achieve a large sample size. Nonetheless, prior research has demonstrated that a small sample size (i.e., a sample of ≤ 50) at the first level can lead to biased estimates of the second-level standard errors. Samples larger than this threshold are suitable for unbiased estimates of the regression coefficients, the variance components, and the standard errors (Maas & Hox, 2005). Our study involved 59 applications (first level) and exceeded this threshold. In addition, our sample size at the project manager level compares favorably with that of other research employing a multilevel design (e.g., Rai et al., 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2018). With a small sample size, we had to focus on a relatively parsimonious model. While we achieved a reasonably good R<sup>2</sup> (approximately 19% for the application level and 16% for the project level), it would be fruitful for future research to identify other factors specific to a system of systems environment. Moreover, the effect of interapplication dependency on the use of agile development practices was not found to be significant in our study (p = 0.145). Nevertheless, the effect of inter-application dependency on the use of agile development practices was in the expected direction (i.e., negative effect) and had a small effect size of -0.093. Non-significance could be due to a small sample size. To examine this, we conducted a simulation to estimate power at a range of sample sizes for the effect of inter-application dependency using an R package called simr (power analysis of generalized linear mixed models by simulation) (Green & MacLeod, 2016). Through the simulation, we found that to achieve adequate statistical power (i.e., 0.80), we would need approximately 168 applications. In contrast, our power to detect an effect was only about 0.55. Therefore, we suggest that further research is needed to empirically examine the effect of inter-application dependency with a larger sample.

In this study, we focused on a single company, which involved trade-offs. Focusing on one company allowed us to achieve deep access and establish a multilevel design. While this potentially limits the generalizability of the results, we felt that the benefits were worthwhile. Given the amount of time and energy invested in establishing the level of depth and understanding of one organization to execute this research design, such granularity would likely have been sacrificed had we examined multiple organizations. Nonetheless, we note that our research findings may not be readily generalizable in other company settings. For example, the company where this study was conducted chose a voluntary transition to agile development practices, which means that each development team was able to decide whether to use agile development practices and to what extent. However, other companies might choose mandated adoption of agile development practices by development teams. One direction for future research is to examine obstacles that may arise or resistance from development teams when the mandated approach is used.

Other study limitations include not controlling for the effect of application size in our research model. While we controlled for the project size, an application development team’s use of agile development practices may also be influenced by its application size. Further research is warranted to tease out the effect of application size in testing the hypotheses proposed in this study.

In addition, research suggests that there are several dependencies in settings that involve inter-team coordination, such as human dependencies in large-scale projects (Sablis et al., 2020). In this research, we focused only on two factors: inter-application dependency and platform variety. Further research is needed to examine if other types of dependencies have similar effects on development teams’ use of agile development practices in SoS environments. Moreover, we conceptualized and measured inter-application dependency as the number of ties an application has with other applications comprising a larger system. However, while our inter-application dependency measure captures an essential aspect of the construct (i.e., the number of connections), we acknowledge that there may be other relevant concepts that might allow for a more nuanced measure of interapplication dependency, such as the strength (how critical the dependency is), type (data dependency or functional dependency), and directionality. Furthermore, in this research, we conceptualized and measured the choice between centralized and decentralized decision rights at the application level. However, we recognize that other higher-level project governance structures (project or program level) may influence the decisions and behavior of development teams. This falls beyond our research scope, and further research is warranted to examine the effects of other project governance structures.

Another fruitful direction for future research would be to draw from the mirroring hypothesis to understand how inter-application dependency in a system of systems environment can be managed. There is some similarity between the system of systems perspective and the mirroring hypothesis, as the mirroring hypothesis is built upon the assumption that a technical system consists of subsystems and there exist certain dependencies between subsystems. In addition, the mirroring hypothesis focuses on understanding how ties between organizational units mirror ties between technical components. Thus, the mirroring hypothesis perspective could provide a novel theoretical lens for studying information systems development, particularly with respect to understanding how to effectively manage inter-team dependencies or when it might be needed to break such dependencies, i.e., “break the mirror” (Colfer & Baldwin, 2016). Another similar concept is Conway’s law, which contends that communication structures of organizations that build systems tend to constrain or influence the design of the systems (Herbsleb & Grinter, 1999). Therefore, another direction for future research would be to examine how communication structures between application development teams in a project influence interconnectivity between applications, and how such links influence development teams’ choice of development methods.

Also, modern solutions such as containerization (Zhou et al., 2022) have the potential to mitigate issues arising from inter-application dependency and platform variety by containing each application comprising a larger system. Therefore, another direction for future research would be to examine how modern solutions such as containerization may moderate the relationships examined in our research (i.e., the effects of interapplication dependency and platform variety on the use of agile development practices).

Finally, there are other unexplored factors that may influence development teams’ use of agile development practices in SoS environments, such as the extent of resistance to change, the specific agile tools used, and the composition of development teams. Further research is warranted to examine the links between these factors and development teams’ use of agile development practices in SoS environments.

## 7.4 Conclusion

In recent years, interest in using agile development in large corporations has grown (Rigby et al., 2016). However, many challenges arise when large organizations attempt to introduce agile development practices that were designed for use in small organizational settings (Ebert & Paasivaara, 2017; Rigby et al., 2018). Drawing on the SoS perspective, this study sheds new light on the challenges of using agile development practices in large organizations and offers an explanation as to why such transitions are difficult to achieve. In addition, this study clarifies which decision rights allocation concerning inter-team coordination (centralized or decentralized) is more effective in promoting the use of agile development practices, depending on levels of inter-application dependency and platform variety

## Appendix C: Robustness Checks

We conducted two checks to add robustness to our study findings. First, we conducted the same HLM analysis but with the stepwise approach by adding predictors in separate steps. We first included control variables along with decision rights control in Step 1. In Step 2, we included inter-application dependency (H1) and platform variety (H2). Finally, we included interaction terms in Step 3. The results obtained using the stepwise approach were consistent with the results obtained using the standard approach (see Table C1)

Second, we conducted a multiple linear regression using orthogonalization to check the robustness of our study findings. Orthogonalization helps deal with the problems raised by multicollinearity in regression-based studies (Farrar & Glauber, 1967). With orthogonalization, predictors are replaced by “orthogonalized” variables. Essentially, each predictor is used as the dependent variable against the other predictors in the model. Then, the residuals from each of these regressions are used as predictors (orthogonalized variables) in the main analysis. In our study, we created an orthogonalized variable for each main effect variable: inter-application dependency (H1), platform variety (H2), and decision rights control. We included (de)centralized control, because while its main effect was not hypothesized, it was involved in interaction effects with inter-application dependency and platform variety. Then, we created interaction terms using orthogonalized variables for each of the four hypothesized interaction effects (H3-H4). We then used these orthogonalized variables and the interaction terms to conduct a multiple regression analysis (DV: use of agile development practices). The results from this analysis were consistent with the results of our main analysis (support was found for H2-H4). Specifically, we found the significant main effect of platform variety (H2): β = -0.186, p = 0.045, In addition, we found both interaction effects to be statistically significant (H3-H4): Inter-application dependency × Decision rights control (β = 0.098, p = 0.020, H3) and Platform variety × Decision rights control (β = 0.300, p = 0.004, H4). Taken together, these results add robustness to our study findings.
