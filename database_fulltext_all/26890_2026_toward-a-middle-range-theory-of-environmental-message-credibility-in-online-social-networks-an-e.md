# Toward a Middle-Range Theory of Environmental Message Credibility in Online Social Networks: An Extended Reasoning Model

A. J. Burns,<sup>1</sup> M. Eric Johnson<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University, USA, ajburns@lsu.edu <sup>2</sup>Owen Graduate School of Management, Vanderbilt University, USA, eric.johnson@vanderbilt.edu

## Abstract

We propose and empirically examine an extended reasoning model of science-based environmental message credibility on online social networks (OSNs). Building on traditional claim-evidence reasoning, the model incorporates message source, evidence vividness, and group-based identity as key antecedents of perceived credibility. Results from our survey-embedded experiment show that the extended model explains 38% of the variance in the perceived credibility of science-based environmental OSN posts. Both message reasoning style and individuals’ group identities play a significant role in credibility assessments. We found that vivid evidence increased the credibility of the science-based environmental claim to a level comparable to that of claims supported by graphical evidence presented with scientific backing. Additionally, we uncovered an interaction between groupbased identity and the message source, which provides a novel explanation of the complex relationship between group-based identity and perceptions of environment-related message credibility. Post hoc analyses further show that, among younger individuals, higher education is associated with greater credibility perceptions of science-based environmental claims, regardless of group identity. We discuss the implications of our findings and present significant opportunities for future research.

Keywords: Environmental Messaging, Online Credibility, Middle-Range Theory, Online Social Networks (OSNs), Reasoning Style, Social Identity Theory

## 1 Introduction

There is a growing body of literature that suggests the aggregated choices of individuals play an important role in preserving the environment (Gholami et al., 2016; Melville, 2010; vom Brocke et al., 2013). Within the information systems discipline, researchers often approach the problem by studying the efficacy of communication across online social networks (OSNs) in increasing awareness and changing behavior (Qureshi et al., 2020). This focus on OSN platforms for environmental communication reflects a dramatic shift in how people receive and evaluate media and information. For example, in 2021, about half of Americans reported reading news on social media at least sometimes, and more than half of Twitter users reported regularly getting news on the site (Walker & Matsa, 2021).

One of the key drivers behind environmental-related initiatives is evidence of global warming, which carries broad environmental implications (NASA, 2020). According to the World Meteorological Organization, the 7 years from 2015 to 2021 were the seven warmest years on record (World Meteorological Organization, 2021), and evidence of warming has been accumulating for some time (NASA, 2020). However, many individuals do not believe warming is a severe issue (Kennedy, 2020). In the U.S., environmental beliefs have become a polarizing topic, with 88% of Democrats and 31% of Republicans believing that climate change is a significant threat (Kennedy, 2020).

Group-based views of environmental beliefs pervade OSNs. For example, in 2019, a former U.S. president posted on Twitter: “One challenge will define the future for today’s young generation more dramatically than any other: Climate change” (Obama, 2019). That same year, the sitting U.S. president retweeted a contradictory statement from a former Greenpeace member claiming, “The whole climate crisis is not only Fake News, it’s Fake Science” (BBC, 2019).

To better understand perceptions of science-based environmental OSN posts in this environment of polarization, we developed and empirically examined a middle-range theory of environment-related OSN post credibility. Our novel theory integrates Toulmin’s reasoning model (1958/2003), social identity theory (e.g., Festinger, 1950; Tajfel, 1972; Tajfel & Turner, 1986), and message vividness (e.g., Nisbett & Ross, 1980). Toulmin’s base reasoning model posits several elements, including (1) claim, (2) evidence, (3) warrant, and (4) backing. A claim is simply an assertion, and evidence is the information provided to support a claim (Kim & Benbasat, 2006). Often implied, a warrant is a proposition that links the evidence with the claim, and backing provides corroboration for the warrant and evidence (e.g., why they should be accepted) (Kim & Benbasat, 2006). Figure 1 illustrates the base reasoning model.

Extending the reasoning model for OSNs, we propose a more contextualized model incorporating vivid evidence, scientific backing, message source, and the individual’s group-based social identity. Following prior research (e.g., Nisbett & Ross, 1980), we define vivid evidence as emotion- and imagery-provoking content. This more contextualized reasoning model is needed because social media posts often include visual imagery or evidence rather than textual claims. For example, with the rise in social media adoption, memes (e.g., captioned pictures or videos) have spread virally through OSNs (Weng et al., 2012). Meanwhile, social identity theory (e.g., Tajfel, 1972; Tajfel & Turner, 1986) explains how individuals rely on their perceptions of others to evaluate their own beliefs and opinions.

Our results show that the extended reasoning model substantially contributes to our understanding of OSN credibility, explaining 38% of the variance in credibility perceptions of science-based environmental claims. However, contrary to traditional Toulmin logic, we found that claims supported with vivid evidence were as credible as those including graphical evidence with scientific backing. Our findings have important implications for practice and research, especially given the polarizing nature of many environmental claims on OSNs. Therefore, understanding how people form credibility judgments about environmental claims on social media requires a more contextualized reasoning model.

## 2 Proposing a Middle-Range Theory of OSNs

Although online credibility is not a new topic for IS researchers (Cheung et al., 2012; Kim & Benbasat, 2006; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006; Watts & Zhang, 2008), OSNs are unique from previously existing online communities in their scale and reach (Halevy et al., 2022). For example, from 2005 to 2019, social media use in the U.S. exploded from 5% to 72% of adults connecting through at least one OSN platform (Pew Research Center, 2019). While OSNs offer new opportunities for interaction (Kane et al., 2014), they also create a tremendous amount of information for users to process, comprehend, and evaluate. For example, in 2018, an estimated half a million posts were made on Twitter every minute, resulting in hundreds of billions of posts annually (Marr, 2018). This massive volume of information generated on OSN platforms challenges users to evaluate content for credibility (Moravec et al., 2019). Indeed, with so much information available online, much of which is contradictory, many users have grown distrustful of the information they encounter in the media and online, including that with seemingly reliable scientific evidence (Davies, 2019).

The rise in OSNs has paralleled another consequential trend, the polarization of science-based topics. For example, from the 1980s to the 2010s, conservatives trust in the broader scientific community declined steadily (Gauchat, 2012), a trend that has continued (Kennedy & Tyson, 2023). Thus, we propose and empirically examine an extended reasoning model to better understand how people assess the credibility of science-based messages in OSNs. Given the distinct nature of environmental-related claims in an era of scientific polarization, we contend that a middle-range theory is warranted (i.e., Merton, 1968). As in recent middle-range theories (e.g., Burns et al., 2023), we focus on a single context—environment-related messages on OSNs—and one outcome, post-credibility. Such highly contextualized information systems theories are especially valuable when practical implications are likely (Chen et al., 2024; Hassan & Lowry, 2015; Hassan et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2014).

We adopt the theoretical perspective of Gregor (2006, p. 613), who describes theory as “statements of relationships among constructs that can be tested.” Specifically, we ground our middle-range theory in recent works on reasoning style (Kim & Benbasat, 2006) and group-based social identity (e.g., Roth et al., 2020) to create a highly contextualized model of credibility that accounts for individual and message characteristics. Figure 2 displays our proposed extended reasoning model for OSN credibility. In the following sections, we provide a brief background of these theoretical frameworks and propose related hypotheses.

## 2.1 Reasoning Style

Regardless of the medium, message persuasiveness influences its credibility. Toulmin (1958/2003) formalized this into a reasoning model. As he explained:

A man who makes an assertion puts forward a claim—a claim on our attention and to our belief … Whatever the nature of the particular assertion may be… in each case we can challenge the assertion, and demand to have our attention drawn to the grounds (backing, data, facts, evidence, considerations, features) on which the merits of the assertion are to depend. (Toulmin, 1958/2003, p. 11-12).

Thus, Toulmin’s reasoning philosophy centers on individuals’ evaluations of support (e.g., evidence and backing) for assertions or claims. Prior research has shown that reasoning style influences perceived credibility. For example, Kim and Benbasat (2006) showed that reasoning style affects how people judge message credibility in e-commerce. In particular, they found that claims accompanied by evidence with backing were more credible (Kim & Benbasat, 2006). The overarching premise of Toulmin’s reasoning model is that well-supported claims are more credible and, thus, more convincing (Gregor & Benbasat, 1999).

Though developed outside the context of technology, Toulmin’s model has recently gained traction in IS research across various domains. For example, Hong and Pavlou (2014) examined the model of reasoning style as a mechanism for developing product expectations in online environments. Similarly, Gregor and Benbasat (1999) used reasoning style as a framework to understand explanations in the context of expert systems. Klein and Hirschheim (2001) used reasoning style to explain IS design ideals. Kim and Benbasat (2009) used reasoning style to explain customers’ trust in business-to-consumer e-commerce websites. Other applications of reasoning style include studies of gender differences in online trust (Riedl et al., 2010), group decision-making in large corporations (Ramesh & Whinston, 1994), and an examination of the IS discipline as a whole (Hassan, 2011).

Despite the prior use of Toulmin’s model in IS research, relatively few studies have employed it to understand claims on OSN platforms. A notable exception is Cummings and Dennis (2018), who found that reasoning style helps explain impression formation on professional social networking sites (e.g., LinkedIn). They showed that employees form more favorable impressions of coworkers whose social media profiles include relevant claims supported by evidence and backing (Cummings & Dennis, 2018). This suggests that reasoning style likely influences how users judge credibility on OSNs. However, relatively little research has examined reasoning style in the context of OSN posts. This research gap is significant because much of the content on OSNs comes from unknown or unfamiliar sources. Therefore, users may rely on credibility cues when evaluating claims on OSNs. Thus, reasoning style offers a useful lens for understanding how users evaluate message credibility online.

## 2.1.1 Vividness and Reasoning Style

Complementary to the role of reasoning style in social media (e.g., Cummings & Dennis, 2018), we contend that message features can help explain credibility and efficacy on OSNs. Specifically, the vividness of a message may influence how it is perceived (Nisbett & Ross, 1980). “Information may be described as vivid, that is, as likely to attract and hold our attention and to excite the imagination, to the extent that it is: (a) emotionally interesting, (b) concrete and imagery-provoking, and (c) proximate in a sensory, temporal or spatial way” (Nisbett & Ross, 1980, p. 45). Vividness is a common tactic for enhancing persuasive communication (e.g., advertising); generally, vivid information is more persuasive than less vivid alternatives (Taylor & Thompson, 1982).

The impact of vividness can be explained in terms of resources (Keller & Block, 1997). Prior research shows that vivid information consumes fewer cognitive resources than less vivid content (Keller & Block, 1997). Put another way, vividness increases persuasion under “informational competition” (Taylor & Thompson, 1982, p. 155). Thus, when individuals devote more cognitive resources to less vivid scientific information (e.g., charts), these communications may become more persuasive. Still, in the typical case where personal resources are strained, vivid messages likely outperform (Keller & Block, 1997). We contend that vividness predominates in OSNs because messages are embedded in the “informational competition found in everyday life” (Taylor & Thompson, 1982, p. 155).

In IS research, vividness increases perceptions of social presence (Hess et al., 2009). Additionally, vivid visualizations reduce product fit uncertainty in e-commerce (Hong & Pavlou, 2014). Communication vividness also enhances online advertising effectiveness (Fortin & Dholakia, 2005). In the context of online misinformation, vividness increases the credibility and engagement intentions related to fake news (Lee & Shin, 2022).

Thus, we contend that users of OSNs are more likely to encounter claims in conjunction with vivid evidence (Wang et al., 2022). This phenomenon has been widely observed in the viral spread of memes, some of which even contain false or misleading information (Geeng et al., 2020). We contend that these memes often rely on vividness to boost engagement. To account for the vivid nature of OSNs, the extended reasoning model incorporates the influence of vivid evidence (e.g., Nisbett & Ross, 1980). In line with prior work on vividness (e.g., Keller & Block, 1997; Lee & Shin, 2022), we expect vivid evidence to increase perceived credibility. Thus, our model elevates the credibility of claims supported by vivid evidence above that of graphical evidence, and we hypothesize the following:

H1: Reasoning style will influence credibility perceptions of science-based environmental messages (i.e., claims supported by vivid evidence, and those supported by graphical evidence with backing will be perceived as more credible than claims supported by graphical evidence alone).

## 2.2 Group-Based Social Identity

At the core of our extended reasoning model is the recognition that individuals rely on group-based social factors to make credibility assessments beyond the features of the message itself (e.g., its vividness or backing). For example, in the seminal works on social identity, Festinger (1950, 1954) observed that individuals often rely on social comparisons to evaluate their opinions. As he notes, “opinions, attitudes, and beliefs which people hold must have some basis upon which they rest for their validity” (Festinger, 1950, p. 272). At the same time, there is endemic pressure within social groups toward conformity and agreement (Festinger, 1950). As individuals become more entrenched in the group’s views, they evaluate information through their social identity rather than critical reasoning (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019).

In IS research, social identity has been examined in contexts such as contributions to virtual communities (Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014), electronic health record assimilation (Mishra et al., 2012), and ERP success (Mueller et al., 2019), among others. A principal tenet of social identity theory is that individuals tend to organize together into social groups based, at least in part, on a set of shared values (Hogg & Terry, 2000). Group membership becomes a basis for intergroup comparisons and perceived distinctiveness (Hogg & Terry, 2000). Once formed, one’s social identity “describes and prescribes one’s attributes as a member of that group— that is, what one should think and feel, and how one should behave” (Hogg et al., 1995, pp. 259-260). As explained by Tajfel and Turner (1986, p. 8):

At one extreme (which most probably is found in its pure form only rarely in real life) is the interaction between two or more individuals that is fully determined by their interpersonal relationships and individual characteristics, and not at all affected by various social groups or categories to which they respectively belong. The other extreme consists of interactions between two or more individuals (or groups of individuals) that are fully determined by their respective memberships in various social groups or categories, and not at all affected by the interindividual personal relationships between the people involved.

On OSNs, the clustering of viewpoints creates opportunities to reinforce views and ideologies that might otherwise be challenged by negative feedback. Research indicates that this type of ideological feedback loop is particularly pronounced for polarizing issues (Barberá et al., 2015; Williams et al., 2015). For example, Barberá et al. (2015) found polarized OSN activity surrounding political issues, such as marriage equality, a US government shutdown, and a US presidential election. Similarly, Williams et al. (2015) assessed OSN posts related to environmental issues and discovered attitude-based “segregation into polarized ‘skeptic’ and ‘activist’ groups” (p. 126). One complication arising from this self-organization has been the perpetuation of misleading or distorted content based, at least in part, on reinforcing narratives (Moravec et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2019).

OSN platforms create unique opportunities for social categorization, enabling members to organize into groups based on value signals (e.g., profiles) without establishing offline interpersonal relationships. Many OSN interactions differ from offline relationships in that disparate groups often interact without forming personal ties. In their classic work, Tajfel and Turner (1986) discuss this extreme scenario as being found only on a battlefield or during an intense negotiation between two parties. However, OSN platforms seem to present these opportunities more readily, and the resultant group-based polarization on OSN platforms has been widely observed (Barberá et al., 2015; Del Vicario et al., 2016; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2019).

Studies indicate that individuals identifying as conservative or liberal have differing views on the environment. Most notably, individuals with a liberalleaning perspective are more likely to view environmental threats as more serious (Kennedy, 2020). Social identity theory explains that as conflicts between these identity groups escalate, in-group members tend to act out against members of the out-group (Tajfel & Turner, 1986). Much discourse on OSNs reflects the assertions of social identity theory. For example, researchers have found that certain communities on Twitter posted and reposted large volumes of environment-related content that denied, deflected, or otherwise downplayed the seriousness of environmental issues (Al-Rawi et al., 2021). Given this phenomenon, we assert that group-based social identity will influence perceptions of science-based environmental messages on OSNs and hypothesize the following:

H2: Group-based social identity influences credibility perceptions of science-based environmental messages (i.e., liberal leaning > moderate > conservative leaning).

## 2.2.1 Homophily and Similarity Attraction: Message Source Signaling

Prior research has shown that individuals find messages more convincing when explained in value-consistent terms (Goldberg et al., 2019). This reality reflects what is known as the similarity-attraction paradigm, in which people tend to view similar individuals or organizations more favorably than dissimilar ones (Roth et al., 2020). For example, group-based identities can influence news headlines’ credibility on OSN platforms (Kim & Dennis, 2019). Or as another example, individuals on a professional networking site were found to prefer job candidates with the same political affiliation over those with different affiliations (Roth et al., 2020). Specifically, people perceived as similar were rated as more “hirable” (Roth et al., 2020). As explained by social identity theory, this occurs because expressions of group-based identity tend to signal a shared set of beliefs and values (Roth et al., 2020), and people often prefer associating with others who share similar views, a phenomenon also described as homophily (Goldenberg et al., 2023).

Generalized preferences for individuals similar to oneself are a commonly observed phenomenon rooted in implicit theories (Kramer, 2009). Implicit theories are based on assumptions and beliefs attained without a formalized logical basis (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977). While not always harmful, implicit theories have been linked to a range of organizational risks. In the IS discipline, for example, designers’ implicit theories can lead to flawed designs and even IS failures (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977). Implicit theories about age and similarity preferences may also increase workplace conflict (Standifer et al., 2013).

Individuals and organizations regularly express their ideas and opinions on OSNs by posting and reposting information from third parties (Halevy et al., 2022). Indeed, individuals and organizations that post or repost messages often signal their group-based perspectives. Hence, they frequently use their social media profiles to signal their shared beliefs and establish credibility within their group (Babar et al., 2023; Grewal et al., 2019). Such identity signals are particularly relevant for

OSN research because they likely create an interaction effect between an individual’s group-based identity and the espoused identity of the message source. For example, a recent study finds that using conservative spokespeople for environmental messaging can help persuade ideologically conservative voters to increase their concerns about environmental issues (Goldberg et al., 2021).

On the other hand, social identity theory predicts that as in-group preference increases, antagonism toward outgroups often grows. Accordingly, messages from outgroups often have lower credibility. A match between the group-based social identity of the source and recipient leads to higher credibility, whereas a mismatch leads to lower credibility. Thus, we contend that science-based environmental messages from in-group sources will be deemed more credible than those from out-group sources and hypothesize the following:

H3: Science-based environmental messages from ingroup sources have higher credibility than outgroup sources (i.e., message source’s group-based identity × rater’s group-based identity).

## 3 Study

To conduct our study, we developed a survey-based online experiment. As in several prior studies (e.g., Barberá et al., 2015; Go et al., 2016; Johnson & Kaye, 2015), the OSN context for our study was Twitter.<sup>1</sup> Prior research indicates that Twitter was a particularly relevant OSN platform for news-related posts during our study (Kwak et al., 2010). At that time, Twitter was viewed as complementary to other media sources and provided a platform for sharing political and newsrelated content (Johnson & Kaye, 2015). As of 2024, Twitter (now X) still hosted the largest share of news influencers, with 85% maintaining a presence on the platform (Stocking et al., 2024). In our experiment, each respondent rated the credibility of one randomly assigned post, using a between-subjects design. Before collecting our final sample, we piloted the survey and all treatments with a sample of undergraduate and graduate students at a doctorate-granting university in the US. We discuss our research design in greater detail below. Figure 3 depicts our formal research model and hypotheses.

## 3.1 Sample

To examine our hypotheses, we contracted with a marketing research firm (i.e., Qualtrics) to collect data from a large sample of US adults. Such external panels are well-established in the literature (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2014; Nyhan & Reifler, 2019) and are especially useful for encouraging candid responses to sensitive questions (Kays et al., 2012). Thus, engaging a trusted third party can provide an enhanced sense of anonymity for respondents, which, in turn, encourages more truthful and less biased survey results (Podsakoff et al., 2003). To help ensure data quality, we removed speeders (individuals or bots) who completed the survey too quickly, and included multiple attention check items (e.g., “please respond with …”) (Burns et al., 2023). We also included a recall-based attention check (e.g., “Which of the following best describes something you did earlier in this survey?”) (Goldberg et al., 2019). After removing individuals who failed these attention checks, our final sample consists of 1,774 respondents. The average age was 55-64 (based on age bands), and 49.9% of the respondents were women. Our sample was geographically diverse and roughly matched the proportional state populations (see Appendix A).

## 3.2 Survey-Based Online Experiment

Leveraging online experimentation, we asked our participants to respond to survey items and evaluate three mocked-up Twitter profiles and one of six posts. We first asked respondents to assess three profiles in randomized order to eliminate any order bias: (1) a profile signaling a progressive group-based identity, (2) a profile signaling a conservative group-based identity, and (3) a profile for the Star Wars franchise. <sup>2</sup> We specifically developed the conservative profile (@conbugle) and the progressive profile (@progressreported) for this study. At the time of our research, these profiles did not exist on Twitter. Following Goldberg et al. (2019), we included the Star Wars profile (@starwars) as a control. Figure 4 shows the survey process, and Table 1 summarizes the profiles.

After rating each profile, respondents evaluated one post from either the conservative or progressive source. This portion of the experiment employed a 2×3 betweensubjects experimental design wherein each participant viewed a randomly selected post (i.e., one of six treatments). Like Nyhan and Reifler (2019), we developed science-based environmental posts based on information and graphics from NASA’s climate website at the time of our study. <sup>3</sup> All six posts included the headline “Scientists Agree About Global Warming.” The vivid evidence treatment included a picture of melting ice caps. The graphical evidence treatment included a time series graph of rising temperatures from 1880 to 2020 without any data source attribution. Finally, the graphical evidence with backing treatment was the same as the graphical evidence treatment, except that the backing logos were present (i.e., NASA). Toulmin’s reasoning model, contextualized to the environmental context, is illustrated in Figure 5. Table 2 summarizes the posts, and Figures 6a-6c exhibit vivid, graphical, and graphical evidence with backing conditions.

## 3.3 Measures in the Study

As recommended (Straub et al., 2004), we used previously validated scales in our main study wherever possible. The complete items and construct statistics are in Appendix B. Each of our constructs met standard validity and reliability metrics.

We used a single-item scale from Goldberg et al. (2019) to measure our respondents’ group-based identity. The item asked, “In general, which of the following most closely represents the way you think of yourself?” Respondents selected one of five options: (1) very liberal, (2) somewhat liberal, (3) moderate, middle of the road, (4) somewhat conservative, and (5) very conservative. We then grouped our respondents into three categories: liberal leaning, conservative leaning, and moderate. Table 3 summarizes the ideologies of our sample.

Like Roth et al. (2020), we measured profile similarity with four items adapted from Tepper et al. (2011), each capturing how similar the respondent was to people likely to follow the profile. An example item is: “We are similar in terms of our outlook, perspectives, and values.” Each participant was asked to view one of the six mocked-up posts and respond to four items from Wasike (2017) assessing message credibility. An example credibility item is: “The tweet is reliable.”

We also included several critical controls in our analysis to help isolate the impact of our focal constructs. First, we included two key demographic controls: age and education. Because our study involved newer technologies (i.e., OSNs), we included participants’ age in the model. Additionally, given that our subject matter was scientific, we controlled for respondents’ level of education. Second, we included a control for individual environmental concerns, measured prior to exposure to the experimental conditions. Environmental concern was assessed using 7- point bipolar Likert-type items adapted from Goldberg et al. (2019), anchored by opposing statements. An example item is (1) “In the long run, economic growth is always more important than environmental protection” and (7) “In the long run, environmental protection is always more important than economic growth.”

Because we designed our OSN messages to mimic Twitter posts, we controlled for social media exposure.

Drawing on Bányai et al. (2017), we asked about our respondents’ frequency of Twitter usage. Further, we asked how often they viewed news or fact-based information on social media. We also asked how many individuals respondents interact with on social media each month. Finally, we included a theoretically unrelated marker variable construct (their color preference) to help assess method variance (Miller & Simmering, 2023).

## 4 Results

To examine our empirical model, we constructed a 2×3 between-subjects experimental design with two message sources (@ConBugle and @ProgressReported) and three reasoning styles: vivid evidence, graphical evidence, or graphical evidence with backing. To formally test our hypotheses, we estimated the ANCOVA using linear regression in Stata/SE 18.5. As discussed previously, the model included the main effect of reasoning style (H1), the main effect of group-based social identity (H2), the interaction between message source and group-based identity (H3), and a set of covariates. Tables 1 and 2, along with Figures 6a-6c, provide additional profiles and message details. Appendix C includes cell means and sample sizes.

## 4.1 Model Fit, Assumptions, and Robustness Checks

Before interpreting our results, we examined key assumptions of univariate ANCOVA: approximate normality, equal variance, and independence of errors (Seltman, 2018). Consistent with standard practice, we conducted the analysis while simultaneously assessing model assumptions. This approach is appropriate because the robustness of ANCOVA assumptions often depends on the details of the analyses. Our initial evaluation of the model exhibited a slight deviation from the baseline model assumptions. To address this, we employed a weighted least squares (WLS) approach (Penn State, n.d.) with robust standard errors (e.g., White, 1980). The WLS with robust standard errors provided nearly identical results to the original OLS model. Appendix D includes model details along with additional robustness checks, while Appendix E reports the results of additional experimental manipulation checks.

## 4.2 WLS ANCOVA With Robust Standard Errors

We tested our hypotheses alongside the controls described above using a WLS ANCOVA model with robust standard errors. The model assessed mean credibility as a function of reasoning style, group-based social identity, and message source. We included eight covariates in the model: message source (main effect), age, education, environmental concerns, Twitter use, social media for facts/news, social media interactions, and color scale. (See Appendix B for scales.)

Table 4 shows that reasoning style, group-based social identity, and the interaction between the message source and group-based social identity were significantly related to credibility perceptions. Among the demographic controls, age was significantly associated with message credibility, whereas education was not. Prior environmental concerns were also significantly related to credibility perceptions of the environmental posts. Twitter use and the frequency of consuming news or fact-based information on social media were also significantly related to message credibility. However, the frequency of interpersonal social media interaction was not significantly associated with credibility. Finally, the theoretically unrelated color scale exhibited no relationship with message credibility (t-value 0.17; p = 0.865). The model explained a substantial portion of the variance in credibility perceptions (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.380).

## 4.3 Post Hoc Analyses on Age, Education, and Group-Based Identity

Our primary analysis (see Table 4) revealed a significant positive association between age and post credibility (β = 0.061; p = 0.005). This finding contrasts with recent polling that suggests younger Americans across the ideological spectrum are increasingly concerned about environmental issues (Brenan, 2022; Singleton et al., 2021). Additionally, given the scientific nature of our topic, it is also notable that education did not emerge as a significant predictor of credibility perceptions. These findings, paired with the fact that our primary analysis indicated that group-based social identity significantly moderates beliefs, motivated our decision to explore the interaction among group-based identity, age, and education as a promising direction for future research.

As shown in Table 7, this post hoc analysis revealed a significant three-way interaction among group-based identity, age, and education.

## 5 Discussion

Extending prior work on online credibility, we proposed and empirically examined an extended reasoning model. This extension is important because recent IS research on credibility has often emphasized issues like misinformation and fake news (e.g., George et al., 2021; Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019; Pennycook et al., 2020), while we focus instead on polarization. Moreover, relatively few of these studies have examined environmental-related messages. To formally test our model, we employed a survey-embedded online experiment. We examined how reasoning style and group-based social identity influence the perceived credibility of environmental messages on OSNs. The experiment employed a 2×3 between-subjects design. Each participant rated the credibility of one of six mocked-up environmental posts . Each post was attributed to one of two profiles, and our manipulation check confirmed that respondents recognized whether the source aligned with their group-based identity.

Overall, the results support all three of our model’s hypotheses. First, consistent with H1, we found that vivid evidence and evidence with backing were rated as more credible than graphical evidence alone. Second, consistent with H2, we found that political social identity was significantly related to perceptions of credibility in science-based environmental posts. Third, consistent with H3, we found a significant interaction between message source and group-based identity. Finally, we conducted post hoc analyses to explore how group-based political identity, age, and education interact to influence message credibility. This post hoc analysis revealed a three-way interaction, wherein younger individuals perceived science-based messages as increasingly credible with higher levels of education.

## 5.1 Implications and Contributions

Our findings offer several important implications for research and practice. A key contribution is the insight our study offers into how reasoning style relates to message credibility in polarized OSN contexts. Toulmin’s original reasoning model explains that claims are more credible when supported by evidence and backing (Cummings & Dennis, 2018). Our research extends this framework to account for the role of vividness in OSN messaging. We found that messages featuring vivid evidence were rated as significantly more credible than those supported by graphical evidence alone; however, credibility ratings for vivid evidence did not differ significantly from those for graphical evidence accompanied by backing. Indeed, respondents rated the graph evidence without backing as the least credible. These results extend Toulmin’s original model by shedding light on which types of evidence are more credible in the vivid OSN environment. The finding that graphical evidence without backing received the lowest credibility ratings is particularly informative in the current climate, where increasing skepticism about scientific evidence is widespread, especially when it conflicts with one’s group-based identity. Graphical evidence that lacks adequate backing appears less credible than vivid imagery when supporting science-based environmental claims. However, when graphical evidence is backed by a credible source (e.g., NASA), its perceived credibility improves. Yet, this improvement did not surpass the credibility attributed to vivid imagery.

Drawing on prior work in group-based social identity, the extended reasoning model explains how identity shapes message credibility on OSNs (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Miller et al., 2022). Specifically, conservativeleaning individuals viewed environmental messages as less credible than moderate and liberal-leaning respondents. Likewise, liberal-leaning individuals viewed the same message as more credible than moderate and conservative-leaning people. This finding is consistent with prior research showing that individuals tend to believe messages that reflect their opinions (Kim & Dennis, 2019). However, because we control for individuals’ prior environmental concerns, mere confirmation bias does not fully explain this result. Instead, we contend that perceived credibility of the science-based post depends not only on its content but also on whether it affirms the rater’s group-based identity. This general trend matches homophily theory (e.g., Goldenberg et al., 2023) and the similarityattraction paradigm (e.g., Roth et al., 2020).

We also found a significant interaction between groupbased social identity and message source. Pairwise comparisons support our hypothesis: liberal-leaning respondents rated science-based environmental posts from a progressive profile as more credible, while conservative-leaning respondents rated posts from a conservative source as more credible. Although conservative-leaning individuals generally viewed science-based environmental posts as less credible than liberal-leaning individuals, they rated the same post as more credible when it came from an in-group source. Interestingly, the effect did not occur in one direction only. While liberal-leaning individuals viewed the environmental posts as most credible overall, they rated those from an out-group source (i.e., the conservative profile) as less credible than those from the in-group source (i.e., the progressive profile). These findings have important implications for research and practice.

For example, the significant interaction between an individual’s group-based identity and the message source highlights the complexity of message credibility in the context of polarizing issues. While recent research shows that targeted advertisements using conservative spokespeople may persuade conservative-leaning individuals to consider progressive ideas (Goldberg et al., 2021), relatively little research has examined how liberal-leaning individuals perceive such messages. Thus, we are among the first IS researchers to show that liberal-leaning individuals view environmental messages as less credible when they are posted by a conservative-leaning profile. This result raises important questions for environmental messaging.

For instance, when traditionally conservative organizations make claims of environmental sustainability, our findings suggest that liberal-leaning individuals may not view these messages as highly credible. On the other hand, if conservative-leaning individuals perceive such messages as inconsistent with their group-based identity, these organizations may lose some credibility among their core supporters. These situations might create a lose-lose scenario that alienates in-group supporters while failing to persuade out-group skeptics.

Another implication of our study relates to the increasingly complex role of third-party backing. Toulmin’s model suggests that claims are more credible when supported by both evidence and backing (Cummings & Dennis, 2018). Prior IS literature has adopted this logical framework (Kim & Benbasat, 2006; Ramesh & Whinston, 1994). However, as explained by social identity and political affiliation theories, individuals tend to interpret information through their group-based identities. This reality complicates the role of evidence and backing because interpretation is susceptible to bias. For instance, NASA is widely recognized as a leader in US scientific advancement (Motel, 2015), and its backing enhanced the credibility of graphical evidence. Yet the claim supported with graphical evidence and NASA’s backing was not rated as more credible than an image of melting ice caps (i.e., vivid evidence). This result extends Toulmin’s original reasoning model by showing that backing may not always boost the credibility of evidence beyond vivid evidence alone.

This outcome has important implications because, over time, ideologically neutral third parties may struggle to reach audiences whose group-based identities conflict with their findings. This tendency matches the broader trend of the polarization and politicization of science (Bolsen & Druckman, 2015). For example, a growing political divide is affecting agencies that would ideally be non-partisan, such as the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Pew Research Center, 2015). Accordingly, our findings might help explain the limitations of other formalized OSN credibility cues (e.g., Moravec et al., 2019). In short, third-party evaluations, such as factchecks, may be less influential when these assessments do not align with individuals’ group-based beliefs (Ceci & Williams, 2020).

Finally, our post hoc explorations revealed a significant interaction between age, education, and group-based identity. We found that younger respondents rated science-based environmental posts as more credible as their education increased, regardless of their groupbased identity. This result suggests a potential direction for future research, as science-based polarization may diminish over time with rising education levels (US Census Bureau, 2020).

## 5.2 Limitations and Future Research

There are inherent trade-offs in survey-based experiments. However, such online experiments are an accepted IS research practice and are especially common in OSN research (e.g., Kim & Dennis, 2019; Moravec et al., 2019). In any designed experiment, external validity and generalizability are sacrificed for increased internal validity and control (Seltman, 2018). Thus, external validity and generalization are limitations of our study. We minimized these limitations by editing real profiles and posts using HTML, cascading style sheets (CSS), and professional photo-editing software. We also created our treatments (e.g., vivid evidence, graph evidence, and graph evidence with backing) using actual images, graphs, and logos (i.e., NASA, 2020). We further controlled for differences between the profiles and treatments by making them nearly identical, aside from the manipulations. For example, the two vivid evidence posts were similar in terms of the date and time of the post, the number of reposts and likes, and the visible avatars of profiles that liked the posts. The four graphical posts (with and without backing) were identical in all other respects.

While self-reported data comes with known limitations, we designed our study to help mitigate them. For example, we engaged a third-party panel data provider to collect anonymized responses from our participants. Anonymity encourages candid responses and reduces social desirability bias (Kays et al., 2012; Podsakoff et al., 2003). We collected a theoretically unrelated marker variable and included it as a covariate in our model to help assess potential method bias. If the marker variable significantly explains variance in the dependent variable, it may indicate method variance (i.e., if the color scale significantly correlates with the dependent variable, it is likely because they share the same method and does not necessarily indicate a theoretical linkage). The color preference scale was not significant in our model (p = 0.865).

Although our sample was geographically representative, we also acknowledge that it still constituted a convenient sample of individuals participating in Qualtrics panels. This limitation is offset by the fact that we were interested in individuals who engage in social media. Presumably, individuals who participate in Qualtrics panels will also be active on social media. In support of this assertion, 76.7% of our sample reported using social media at least once in the week before the survey, and 48.6% reported daily use. Our sample also skewed older, with a mean of 55-64 (based on age bands). Although we controlled for age, we acknowledge this potential limitation.

Our dependent variable captured participants’ perceptions of the credibility of the OSN message (i.e., post). We intentionally avoided relying on prospective behavior because of our experimental design. Specifically, we focused on perceptions of the post’s perceived credibility rather than asking whether respondents would be likely to “repost” or “like” the posts. This design aligned with our study’s goals and avoided relying on hypothetical behaviors. This distinction is especially important given the potential social cost of engaging with OSN messages. For instance, asking whether someone would “repost” or “like” a message that conflicts with their group-based social identity may not be a reliable gauge of its credibility because it can introduce social desirability bias. Furthermore, users often do not engage with posts, even if they find them credible. Despite this, future research should consider the factors leading to engagement, including the potential role of credibility in OSN engagement. Future work should also examine whether the extended reasoning model can help explain behavioral changes (e.g., environmentally relevant decisions; see Orlove et al., 2020).

Our results suggest that the extended reasoning model is well-calibrated to the OSN context. Specifically, extending Toulmin’s original work, we found that scientific backing enhanced the credibility of the graph evidence, but not beyond that of vivid evidence. This result presents meaningful opportunities for future research. For example, prior research indicates that vividness is especially effective at breaking through the noise when individuals are processing large amounts of information, as with OSNs (Taylor & Thompson, 1982). Vividness is effective because it is more emotional, concrete, and relatable (Nisbett & Ross, 1980). However, the effectiveness of vivid evidence contrasts with other findings showing that appearing scientific increases persuasiveness (Tal & Wansink, 2016). Thus, future research should further examine the trade-off between vivid and scientific messaging, especially for science-based information. Future research should also explore how vivid evidence combined with scientific backing affects credibility. Additionally, the relationship between group-based social identity and perceptions of vivid evidence warrants further investigation. For instance, our supplemental analyses (Appendix E) suggest that perceptions of vividness can vary by group-based identity. Therefore, future research should clarify what shapes perceptions of vividness in environmental messaging.

Finally, our post hoc analyses suggest new research opportunities, as education was associated with increased credibility perceptions of science-based environmental posts among younger respondents across the ideological spectrum. Future research should continue to examine this trend over time. For instance, a large body of research has investigated increasing polarization online (e.g., Barberá et al., 2015; Del Vicario et al., 2016), but relatively few have explored how shifting attitudes among younger people may present new challenges and opportunities, especially as education levels continue to increase in the US (US Census Bureau, 2020). It remains an open question how younger, more-educated individuals will navigate conflicts with the science-based beliefs held by their older ideological counterparts. Future research should also examine how well the extended reasoning model explains other science-based claims on OSNs. Such work will help clarify whether environmental messaging is distinct and whether the extended reasoning model generalizes to other science-based phenomena.

## 5.3 Conclusion

As people consume more news and fact-based information over social media, understanding perceptions of OSN profiles and message credibility is a significant issue for researchers, policymakers, and the public. To help explain the credibility perceptions of science-based environmental claims, we proposed and empirically examined an extended reasoning model as a middle-range theory. Leveraging a survey-embedded experiment, we asked respondents to evaluate mockedup posts containing science-based environmental claims. Our results show that while backing increases the credibility of graph evidence, it does not outperform vivid evidence. This result is notable because sciencebased claims are precisely the type of claims we might expect to benefit from scientific backing (i.e., they have scientific support).

The compelling nature of vivid evidence complicates online credibility because the provenance of imagery is much more difficult to verify than the backing of an established scientific community, which can be factchecked directly (e.g., visiting nasa.gov). In today’s OSNs, deceptively altered and AI-generated videos and images exacerbate this difficulty (Lee & Shin, 2022).

Specifically, the graph evidence in our experiment was viewed as the least credible. We suggest that the proliferation of false and biased posts may lead to all data being viewed with suspicion on OSNs.

We also found that posts from in-group sources were deemed to be more credible than those from out-group sources. While this aligns with extant theories (e.g., homophily and similarity-attraction theories), it introduces new complexity to Toulmin’s traditional reasoning model. For example, prior research has found that vivid communications from in-group sources can enhance credibility among conservative-leaning audiences (e.g., Goldberg et al., 2021). However, our results suggest that environmental claims promoted by conservative-leaning sources have less credibility with liberal-leaning individuals. Further, it is conceivable that conservative-leaning groups promoting environmental initiatives could even alienate their ingroup constituencies. Thus, the extended reasoning model does not provide a playbook for developing group-based environmental messages but rather a model for understanding the mechanisms by which environmental message credibility emerges on OSNs.

Finally, our post hoc analyses provide fertile ground for future research. Specifically, we found that education is a key factor in enhancing the credibility of sciencebased environmental posts for younger individuals, regardless of their group-based social identity. Perhaps younger, more educated individuals do not view science-based claims as inherently political, a significant trend if opinions about scientific topics are beginning to decouple from group-based social identities. However, this effect did not hold for older individuals, underscoring the complexity of credibility perceptions for environmental claims on OSNs.

Equality of variance: The second assumption relates to equality of variances. In our preliminary analysis in IBM SPSS v.28, Levene’s test of equality of variances reported a significant p-value, indicating inequality of variances. However, Levene’s test is highly powered with large sample sizes and can detect even slight differences in variances (Warner, 2013). Indeed, when groups are relatively equally sized with greater than 30 participants per group, ANOVA/ANCOVA is robust to violations of equal variances assumption (Warner, 2013).

Independent and homoscedastic errors: Finally, the observations are independent by design (between subjects). OLS also assumes homoskedasticity of error variance (i.e., error variance is constant across levels of post credibility). In our analysis, White’s test for heteroskedasticity reports a significant p-value, indicating heteroskedasticity in our errors. It is important to note that heteroskedasticity does not bias parameter estimates but rather the reliability of significance tests (Seltman, 2018; Williams, 2020).

WLS and robustness: To assess the robustness of our results, we constructed WLS and robust standard error (SE) models.<sup>4</sup> The WLS approach essentially weights observations inversely to estimated error variance. In our case, we observed a pattern in the standardized residuals across levels of post credibility that appeared to be caused by the prevalence of strong agreement and disagreement with the DV in the model. This pattern indicates that at lower values of post credibility, the predicted values were higher than actual, and at higher values of post credibility, the predicted values were lower than actual.

The only change across the models is that age is nonsignificant in the HC3 model. All models also explain a similar portion of the variance in post credibility (0.380 to 0.393). ## Appendix E: Manipulation Checks

## Group-Based Identity and Message Source

Because our analyses rely on perceived similarity between an individual’s group-based identity and the signaled identity of the message source, we measured how similar respondents found each profile. Each respondent viewed and rated the three profiles in Table 1 before seeing the environmental messages. The similarity ratings served two purposes: (1) to expose respondents to the profiles used in the subsequent posts, and (2) to provide a manipulation check to confirm that they perceived the ideological orientation of the profiles and responded accordingly. Showing the profiles first is supported by prior research, which finds that a known source prompts individuals to evaluate the message in relation to that source (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Tormala et al., 2006, 2007).

We examined the influence of group-based identity on profile similarity using a repeated-measures ANCOVA in IBM SPSS v.28. This allowed us to estimate the mean perceived similarity of each profile (@ConBugle, @ProgressReported, and @StarWars) for respondents classified as liberal leaning, moderate, or conservative leaning. After controlling for environmental concern, age, education, Twitter usage, social media use for news, interaction frequency, and color scale, we found that perceptions of profile similarity differed in the expected direction: Liberalleaning respondents rated the progressive profile as significantly more similar to themselves than the conservative profile, and conservative-leaning respondents rated the conservative profile as significantly more similar to themselves than the progressive profile. Both liberal- and conservative-leaning respondents rated the Star Wars profile as more similar to themselves than the profile representing the other political orientation, but less similar than the one aligned with their own views. Meanwhile, politically moderate individuals perceived no differences in similarity across the progressive, conservative, and Star Wars profiles. These results confirm that respondents recognized the ideological distinctions among the profiles and responded accordingly. ## Summary of Manipulation Check

First, conservative-leaning individuals rated the vivid evidence higher than the textual claim on all four vividness dimensions. The largest difference between vivid evidence and the text claim appeared on the item “It creates a clear mental image for me.” This difference is statistically significant (p = 0.039). Although not statistically significant, the results suggest that, on average, conservative-leaning respondents rated the vivid evidence higher than the graphical evidence on “It is memorable” and “It creates a clear mental image for me.”

Second, liberal-leaning individuals rated the vivid evidence higher than the textual claim on “It creates a clear mental image for me” and “It makes the topic feel real to me.” They also rated vivid evidence higher than graphical evidence on “It is memorable.” Although these differences were not statistically significant, the results support the claim that our representation of vivid evidence can be at least as vivid as the graphical evidence.

Surprisingly, moderate individuals rated the textual claim highest on all vividness dimensions. This unexpected result may suggest that moderate respondents were not emotionally drawn to any of the infographics. Even so, moderates rated the vivid evidence higher than the graphical evidence on “It is memorable” and “It makes the topic feel real to me.”

To summarize, a central assumption of our design is that the image of melting ice caps in the OSN messages provides vivid evidence of the effects of environmental warming. This additional manipulation check suggests that the melting ice caps image is, on average, at least as vivid to respondents as graphical evidence.
