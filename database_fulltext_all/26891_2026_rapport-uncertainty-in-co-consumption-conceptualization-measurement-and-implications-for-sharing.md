# Rapport Uncertainty in Co-Consumption: Conceptualization, Measurement, and Implications for Sharing Economy Platform Design

Manuel Trenz,<sup>1</sup> Friedrich Chasin,<sup>2</sup> Alexander Frey,<sup>3</sup> Chee-Wee Tan,<sup>4</sup> Daniel J. Veit<sup>5</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Goettingen, Germany, trenz@uni-goettingen.de <sup>2</sup>Offenburg University of Applied Sciences, Germany, friedrich.chasin@hs-offenburg.de <sup>3</sup>University of Augsburg, Germany, alexander.frey@uni-a.de <sup>4</sup>Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong, chee-wee.tan@polyu.edu.hk <sup>5</sup>University of Augsburg, Germany, daniel.veit@uni-a.de

## Abstract

Co-consumption sharing economy platforms digitally connect strangers for mutually beneficial coconsumption of services, such as ride sharing or home sharing. In addition to efficiency gains, this is particularly relevant for the sustainable use of resources. While digital platforms have become efficient at building trust in third-party providers, co-consumption service experiences require rapport between individuals—an intersubjective fit between a client and a provider that cannot be ensured in advance by guarantees or ratings. Little is known about the role of uncertainty concerning rapport when brokering co-consumption services and how platform design can cater to users’ need for rapport. This study therefore introduces rapport uncertainty as a two-dimensional construct consisting of interaction uncertainty and connection uncertainty and draws on the service literature to derive a research model that captures the role of design features in mitigating rapport uncertainty. Our experimental study reveals that rapport uncertainty is a key determinant of platform use beyond technical and environmental uncertainties and demonstrates how platform design can reduce rapport uncertainty and facilitate transactions in co-consumption environments.

Keywords: Sharing Economy, Sustainability, Co-Consumption, Uncertainty, Rapport, Platform Design

## 1 Introduction

Sharing economy platforms have transformed the way consumers access goods by enabling the temporary use of privately owned resources such as cars, homes, and food (Constantiou et al., 2016; Trenz et al., 2018). Beyond providing efficiency gains, these platforms have the potential to substantially contribute to a more sustainable use of resources (Veit & Thatcher, 2023). A significant subset of these services relies on co-consumption, where consumers and providers share resources through inperson interactions (Frey et al., 2019; Kamal & Chen, 2016), as seen on platforms like Couchsurfing for accommodation-sharing, BlaBlaCar for long-distance carpooling (BlaBlaCar, 2018), and EatWith for meal sharing (EatWith, 2018). These digital platforms within the sharing economy offer co-consumption services<sup>1</sup> and mediate enjoyable interactions and personal connections,<sup>2</sup> making interpersonal rapport—a form of “intersubjective fit”—a cornerstone of successful transactions (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000, p. 92).

Despite the value proposition co-consumption platforms offer, consumers often face a critical challenge: uncertainty about their ability to establish rapport with future providers. This challenge, which we define as rapport uncertainty, captures a consumer’s inability to assess whether interactions with a provider will be enjoyable (interaction uncertainty) or whether a personal connection can be formed (connection uncertainty). For instance, in coconsumptive accommodation sharing, potential guests may struggle to predict their compatibility with a potential host when searching for information on the platform. They can read reviews to learn about the rapport between the host and previous guests, but reviews primarily reflect the personal experiences of past guests, whose social preferences and interaction styles may differ from their own. As a result, even highly rated hosts may not necessarily be a good interpersonal match for every guest. This leads to uncertainty about the rapport a prospective consumer might have with a host. This uncertainty is particularly problematic because consumers must decide—before any interaction occurs—whether they are willing to spend significant time with a stranger during a coconsumption service encounter.

When rapport uncertainty goes unaddressed, it has significant consequences for consumer behavior and platform success. Since uncertainty is a known inhibitor of online transactions (Pavlou et al., 2007), consumers may forgo bookings or hesitate to engage on co-consumption platforms, limiting the platform’s ability to scale and thrive. While platforms have implemented mechanisms such as ratings, guarantees, and photos to reduce other uncertainties, these are insufficient to address the deeply personal and subjective nature of rapport uncertainty. As a result, consumers often lack the confidence to make informed decisions about co-consumption services, posing a critical challenge for platform operators.

Existing research on uncertainty has largely overlooked the role of rapport, focusing instead on uncertainties related to sellers (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou et al., 2007; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), resources (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Kim & Krishnan, 2015; Matt & Hess, 2016; Overby & Jap, 2009), and service quality (e.g., Benlian et al., 2011; Kettinger & Lee, 2005). At the same time, these studies focus on functional service attributes (Brady & Cronin, 2001), such as technical skills or environmental quality, and fail to capture the nuances of nonfunctional interpersonal interactions in co-consumption scenarios, where rapport is a nonfunctional yet pivotal determinant of the service experience.

This gap underscores the need to better understand rapport uncertainty and its impact on consumer decisions. Addressing this issue is not only important for advancing theoretical understanding but also for designing effective co-consumption platforms that can mitigate rapport uncertainty that consumers face during the provider selection process.

Against this backdrop, we build upon uncertainty theory and rapport theory to examine how rapport uncertainty affects consumers’ intentions to use coconsumption platforms and how platform design can mitigate this uncertainty. Specifically, we aim to answer the following research questions:

RQ1: How does rapport uncertainty affect the intention to use a co-consumption platform to book a service?

RQ2: How can rapport uncertainty be mitigated through co-consumption platform design?

To address these questions, we pursued a theory-driven empirical approach that combines conceptual modeling with experimental testing. We first developed a model grounded in uncertainty and rapport theory, specifying how rapport uncertainty— comprising interaction and connection uncertainty— influences consumers’ platform use intentions. Based on established rapport-building behaviors, we then identified specific design features that co-consumption platforms can implement to reduce these uncertainties. Finally, we evaluated our model through a controlled experiment in the long-distance carpooling context, systematically varying platform design elements to assess their impact on the two rapport uncertainty dimensions and subsequent booking intentions.

This study makes five key contributions. First, we introduce rapport uncertainty as a novel category of nonfunctional uncertainty, extending prior research that has focused primarily on technical and environmental uncertainties. Second, we conceptualize rapport uncertainty as a two-dimensional construct— interaction and connection uncertainty—offering a new lens to understand consumer hesitation in coconsumption settings. Third, we theorize how platform design features that reflect established rapportbuilding behaviors can reduce rapport uncertainty prior to a service encounter. Fourth, we show that these design features interact in complex ways, suggesting the need for a configurational rather than additive approach to platform design. Finally, we demonstrate that nonfunctional, interpersonal components play a more decisive role than functional aspects in shaping consumers’ intention to book a service on a coconsumption platform, enriching the discourse on service quality and uncertainty in the sharing economy. Our findings translate directly into practice by offering platform operators empirically grounded strategies to overcome the core challenge of brokering transactions between strangers, thereby enhancing platform success and value.

## 2 Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Co-Consumption Services

The term sharing economy (SE) (Chasin et al., 2017; Heinrichs, 2013; Schlagwein et al., 2020; Trenz et al., 2018) broadly refers to a range of services, from private individuals giving away clothes (Schor & Fitzmaurice, 2015) to businesses renting cars (Cohen & Kietzmann, 2014). A distinctive subset of SE services, co-consumption services, entails the collaborative use of resources by individuals who engage in direct, physical interaction (Belk, 2014). In these settings, consumers and service providers coaccess resources such as food, housing, or vehicles, which requires close in-person interactions between service providers and consumers (Frey et al., 2019; Kamal & Chen, 2016). This immediate physical proximity adds a strong social dimension to the service, creating opportunities for social bonds but also interpersonal friction (Wittel, 2011). Accordingly, coconsumption emphasizes nonfunctional (experiential) components of service delivery that extend beyond standard quality metrics (Brodie et al., 2011). Table 1 illustrates the manifestation of the nonfunctional component in co-consumption services.

From the service systems perspective (Pinho et al., 2014; Vargo & Lusch, 2008), co-consumption services can be characterized by: (1) technology-facilitated inperson encounters between private individuals, and (2) three specific characteristics of the service encounter: co-consumption, physical proximity of the parties, and experience focus.

While other services (e.g., guided tours) also feature physical proximity and an experience focus, coconsumption represents a unique service aspect where both providers and consumers share the same value proposition (Belk, 2014). A tour guide, for example, enables the experience but does not consume it. In contrast, co-consumptive sharing implies that both parties have similar consumption goals. For example, both parties in a co-consumptive accommodation sharing transaction desire a clean and comfortable living space. Likewise, co-consumptive ride sharers want to reach their destination swiftly and safely, and those sharing co-consumptive meal experience want to enjoy a fresh, flavorful meal.

This shared value proposition aligns functional expectations and emphasizes the interpersonal dimension of the service experience. As a result, rapport, the subjective perception of mutual fit between participants, becomes critical during the service selection phase, often before any in-person contact has occurred. Yet, unlike functional attributes, such as price or cleanliness, interpersonal rapport is difficult to assess ex ante.

Digital platforms are central to co-consumption because they bring together consumers and providers during the transaction brokering phase before the physical service occurs. At the same time, they cannot control the actual service encounter. Consequently, during the brokering phase, platform design must facilitate uncertainty reduction for consumers searching for providers with whom to share coconsumption experiences in close proximity. In what follows, we introduce rapport uncertainty as an overlooked form of nonfunctional uncertainty and explore its relevance and design implications in coconsumption contexts.

## 2.2 Rapport Uncertainty

Research on uncertainty in service selection has traditionally focused on functional aspects of service quality,<sup>3</sup> such as technical competence or environmental conditions (Brady & Cronin, 2001; Dagger et al., 2007). These uncertainties arise when consumers struggle to evaluate a service provider’s ability to meet their functional needs (Pavlou et al., 2007), such as ensuring a safe and punctual ride or providing clean and comfortable accommodations. Digital platforms have responded by implementing mechanisms, such as ratings and guarantees, to mitigate these uncertainties (Dimoka et al., 2012).

However, co-consumption services introduce a nonfunctional dimension of uncertainty relating to the interpersonal experience itself. This includes the anticipated rapport between consumers and providers: Will the interaction be enjoyable? Will there be a personal connection? While trust mechanisms can help ensure technical conduct, they do not capture these subjective, affect-laden dimensions of co-consumption services.

Rapport is broadly understood as a harmonious, affective connection between individuals (Bernieri et al., 1996; Gremler & Gwinner, 2000). Gfeller et al. (1987) and Carey et al. (1988) view rapport as a relationship characterized by satisfactory communication and mutual understanding. It encompasses enjoyable interactions, mutual attentiveness, and a sense of being “in sync” (Tickle-Degnen & Rosenthal, 1990). In service settings, rapport has been studied in contexts ranging from education to psychotherapy to customer service (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000).

Studies in IS have employed the term rapport when investigating service quality (Benlian et al., 2011; Kettinger & Lee, 2005; Rabaa’i, 2010), development processes and projects (Parolia et al., 2017; Tan, 1994; Troll et al., 2017), or virtual teams (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005), characterizing it as the courtesy and individualized attention conveyed by a service representative (Benlian et al., 2011; Kettinger & Lee, 2005) or as a measure of harmony and affinity (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Tan, 1994). More recently, rapport has been explored in online or AI-mediated environments, such as online contests or virtual advising (Al-Natour et al., 2007, 2011; Hutter et al., 2015).

Despite this rich foundation, existing studies have largely addressed rapport itself—not the uncertainty about future rapport. This is especially relevant for digital platforms where service transactions and the contracted experience occur at separate times and places. In co-consumption contexts, consumers must assess interpersonal fit without an in-person interaction, limiting the consumer’s ability to gauge future rapport with a provider they have not met. To address this gap and elucidate the role of rapport uncertainty in co-consumption services, we draw on the theorization and evaluation of rapport by Gremler and Gwinner (2000), and conceptualize rapport uncertainty as a two-dimensional construct consisting of two components: interaction uncertainty and connection uncertainty.

In the co-consumption context, interaction uncertainty corresponds to the consumer’s uncertainty about whether they will be able to have an enjoyable exchange with a prospective service provider. This includes concerns about conversational compatibility, shared humor, and the overall atmosphere of interacting.

Connection uncertainty refers to the consumer’s uncertainty about whether a deeper sense of personal connection, affinity, or bonding with a provider will develop. This involves shared interests, values, or mutual understanding. Consumers may also be interested in affiliation with a service provider based on intimate ties such as shared hobbies or interests (Al-Natour et al., 2007; Gremler & Gwinner, 2000). The closeness and strength of the relationship developed between a service provider and a customer influence the perceived service quality (Dagger et al., 2007).

These dimensions of rapport uncertainty are particularly salient in co-consumption platforms, where interpersonal experiences are critical to service satisfaction and consumer decision-making. Brokering on the digital co-consumption platform precedes the in-person service encounter, where rapport between the provider and the consumer will shape the service experience. Thus, co-consumption platforms should aim to mitigate rapport uncertainty, which enhances consumer confidence in the rapport they will experience during the in-person interaction and facilitates the consumer’s booking transaction.

Figure 1 illustrates our pre-understanding of the role of rapport uncertainty for co-consumption services, showing that the design of digital platforms can facilitate transactions by reducing uncertainty about the in-person co-consumption service encounter. This refers to both functional and nonfunctional components, which are known to be important for service quality (Bauer et al., 2006; Dagger et al., 2007). Although a co-consumption platform cannot directly influence rapport between service participants, its design can incorporate mechanisms to reduce consumer uncertainties (Gibbs et al., 2006). By focusing on reducing rapport uncertainty, platforms can increase the chances of successful matches between participants by helping them understand whether they will enjoy interacting with each other and develop a bond during the subsequent in-person service encounter, thereby increasing transactions on the platform and improving service quality perceptions.

## 2.3 Design to Mitigate Rapport Uncertainty

Research on rapport has explored the drivers of rapport by investigating how rapport is built and identifying which behaviors contribute to the generation of rapport. In the context of globally distributed teams, Kotlarsky and Oshri (2005) suggested that in order to build rapport, socialization activities should be facilitated prior to formal, face-to-face meetings—for instance, through informal and short visits to co-workers’ offices or virtual interactions via phone, email, video, chat, or other groupware tools (Oshri et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2007). Informal communication and the possibility of engaging in small talk that goes beyond work is also important to build rapport among team members (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005; Zhang et al., 2007).

Gremler and Gwinner (2008) distinguish five approaches to rapport-building: attentive behavior, common grounding behavior, information sharing behavior, courteous behavior, and connecting behavior. We use these approaches as starting points for design aimed at mitigating rapport uncertainty. In other words, we outline how platforms can implement features that leverage these approaches, allowing providers to reduce consumers’ rapport uncertainty regarding the likelihood of both enjoyable interactions and personal connection.

Attentive behavior occurs in situations where the service provider performs actions that are out of the ordinary (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008). This could involve atypical actions that go above and beyond (for example, providing little extras; see, e.g., Bitner, 1990; Price et al., 1995), personal recognition (for example, greeting the customer by name or recalling specific information about them; e.g., Campbell et al., 2006), or demonstrating intense personal interest (for example, making sure the customer feels that they are more important than the sale; e.g., Argyle, 1990). In the IS literature, we also found evidence that attentive behavior (Tan, 1994), can contribute to the generation of rapport. Co-consumption platforms can support perceptions of attentive behavior by offering functionalities that allow service providers to indicate their interest in the coconsumption experience, which can signal their attentive behaviors, prior to the transaction, which can help to build rapport.

Common grounding behavior (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008) is reflected in situations where the service provider tries to discover something that they have in common with the customer since rapport often arises from shared preferences, tastes, and lifestyles (Bristor, 1990). Typically, this happens by identifying mutual interests such as hobbies (Argyle, 1990) or other similarities, such as having the same age or hometown (Clark et al., 2003). Information systems research and practice offer a rich portfolio of knowledge and exemplary solutions for how digital platform participants can capture their personal background, interests, and values using digital platform functionalities (e.g., on dating platforms, Gibbs et al., 2006). Co-consumption platforms can offer functionalities that allow for detailed personal profiles for service providers, which can allow customers to evaluate potential rapport before initiating service transactions.

Gremler and Gwinner (2008) refer to information sharing behavior as a third category of rapport-building behavior in which the customer and service provider share information so that the customer’s needs can be met. Information sharing can take the form of the service provider offering advice or suggestions (Menon & Dubé, 2000) based on their own expertise and knowledge about a subject (Bendapudi & Berry, 1997). Information sharing in terms of rapport-building behavior typically takes in the form of question-andanswer sessions between the customer and the service provider (McKechnie et al., 2007) through various communication channels such as chat or private messaging. By signaling interest in the co-consumption experience, providers can encourage customers to engage in information sharing with them, which can also facilitate the demonstration of attentive behavior.

Contrary to the first three behaviors, which can precede actual service encounters, the remaining two types of rapport building behaviors (i.e., courteous behavior and connecting behavior) occur during the physical service encounter. As such, they are not influenced by the technology design during the brokering phase before the actual service encounter. Courteous behavior refers to situations where service providers demonstrate genuine courteousness during a service encounter, including behavior that might not be in the company’s best interest—for instance, suggesting products from competitors for follow-up transactions (Wood, 2006). Another example of courteous behavior is empathy, where a customer’s problems is treated as one’s own (Beatty et al., 1996). Connecting behavior refers to the service provider’s attempt to connect with a customer through their interactions during a service encounter (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008). This encompasses, for instance, asking a customer how they are doing (Mittal & Lassar, 1996), or engaging with the customer in ways that go beyond what is typical for a service provider to provide a more enjoyable experience for the customer (Schau et al., 2007).

Therefore, we focus on the first three behaviors because courteous behaviors and connecting behaviors refer to the in-person encounter and—in contrast to the other three approaches to rapport building—rely on emotional nuance and nonverbal cues, both of which are more difficult to effectively replicate on a digital platform. Even if some forms of courteous and connecting behaviors could, in theory, be conveyed prior to the actual co-consumption experience—for example, through video chats enabling media-rich personal interactions—such approaches would fundamentally contradict the core function of digital platforms as streamlined transaction facilitators: Such platforms are designed primarily for matchmaking, serving as intermediaries that effectively connect users rather than embedding extensive interpersonal exchanges into the pre-transaction phase. Promoting such interactions could introduce inefficiencies, increase user friction, and create barriers to the completion of transactions, undermining the platform’s core objectives.

Consumers tend to prefer certain over uncertain outcomes (Fox & Tversky, 1995) and will adjust their willingness to book a service or pay for goods and services depending on their evaluations of uncertainty (e.g., Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Kim & Benbasat, 2009; Lee & Gosain, 2002; Matt & Hess, 2016). Similarly, in the finance literature, a relationship between uncertainty and the valuation of a particular offering has been identified (e.g., Hartley, 1996; Lockwood, 2015; Mackie-Mason, 1990). For instance, Nell (2011) showed that uncertainty can lead to rapid shifts in the value of capital, while Kandel and Pearson (2002) observed that the value may increase or decrease under uncertainty.

The nature of the relationship between the consumer and the service provider affects how uncertainties associated with services are evaluated by consumers (Goodwin & Gremler, 1996). Indeed, in the context of commercial service providers, personal relationships between the consumer and the service provider during a service encounter have been shown to influence the evaluation of goods and services (Bitner et al., 1990; Parasuraman et al., 1985).

We argue that the impact of personal relationships between the service provider and the consumer on the evaluation of the service and the consumer’s intention to engage in a service transaction is also relevant before the service encounter in co-consumption services like carpooling. When people do not have sufficient information about an transaction partner, they tend to use process information as a substitute in the assessment of the trustworthiness of the partner, hence affecting the desirability of the transaction outcome (Hui et al., 2004). In the co-consumption context, this translates to and manifests in the level of rapport uncertainty and its influence on the intention to engage with a platform.

Therefore, we hypothesize that the lower the rapport uncertainty (e.g., rapport uncertainty between a driver and a passenger in the carpooling scenario), the higher the likelihood of the consumer engaging (e.g., booking a service on a carpooling co-consumption platform). Since the ability to enjoyably interact with a service provider and a personal connection between the two parties are constituents of rapport uncertainty (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000), we hypothesize:

H1: Rapport uncertainty in terms of (a) interaction and (b) connection is negatively associated with the intention to book a service on a co-consumption platform.

Digital platforms serving as intermediaries that facilitate transactions (Bakos, 1997) can provide mechanisms to reduce uncertainty among the two parties involved in a transaction (Agrawal et al., 2005). For instance, Pavlou et al. (2007) found that different platform features can reduce consumers’ uncertainties regarding online exchange relationships, leading to more purchase transactions in the long run.

The sources of consumer uncertainty are manifold and may affect the consumers’ willingness to pay price premiums (Dimoka et al., 2012). For example, they may relate to the product (e.g., product condition and future performance) or to the opportunistic behavior of the provider (e.g., Pavlou et al., 2007). In the former case, platform features such as visual or textual product descriptions, as well as third-party product assurances, have been shown to help mitigate product-related uncertainties.

As rapport can also be induced by leveraging digital technologies (Oshri et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2007), we argue that digital technology can also mitigate rapport uncertainty from an ex ante perspective by providing additional information that actors can leverage to evaluate prospective interaction partners.

According to Pavlou et al. (2007), perceived uncertainty is determined by agency problems of hidden information and hidden action and can be mitigated from an agency theory perspective through signals (e.g., ratings and reviews) and incentives (e.g., penalties or sanctions). The effects on booking behavior of uncertainty regarding service quality have also been shown in the context of sharing economy platforms (Dewan et al., 2023). However, the perceived rapport between two actors is highly intersubjective. Therefore in the context of the coconsumptive sharing economy, objective signals, such as ratings or reviews from other customers (Kanat et al., 2018; Kokkodis & Ipeirotis, 2016; Lin et al., 2018; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Yoganarasimhan, 2013) and penalties for providers who fail to facilitate enjoyable interactions, are difficult to apply to reduce rapport uncertainty.

We therefore build upon rapport theory and exploit three rapport building behaviors—namely, common grounding behavior, attentive behavior, and information sharing behavior—as well as the platform features that can signal those behaviors. Note that while these behaviors affect both the connection and interaction components of rapport during the service encounter, the nature of rapport uncertainty requires a more nuanced consideration of these behaviors, as they may target one dimension or the other, but not necessarily both.

Common grounding behavior involves the identification of mutual interests or commonalities (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008). Accordingly, we predict that platform features that help establish common ground between the provider and the consumer (e.g., displaying the driver’s interests on the sharing platform) can allow the consumer to identify commonalities with the provider, which will make the consumer more confident in the likelihood of an enjoyable interaction and personal connection during a co-consumption experience and reduce their rapport uncertainty. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H2: The availability of common grounding features reduces (a) the connection dimension and (b) the interaction dimension of rapport uncertainty.

Attentive behavior in the form of signaling personal interest in the consumer contributes to rapport (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008). Therefore, features of a digital co-consumption platform that can be used to signal attentive behavior can support users in their evaluation of the prospect of a personal connection with the provider. These features can allow providers to signal interest in the consumer and in the dyadic relationship during co-consumption. For instance, attentive features such as manual booking confirmation (e.g., Karlsson et al., 2017) can signal an intentional choice rather than an automated transaction. This confirmation mechanism enables service providers to evaluate their potential customers in advance, before they confirm or reject a booking request. This selectivity fosters consumers’ confidence that the provider values personal compatibility, thereby reducing their connection uncertainty. In other words, attentive features can signal whether the providers care about connecting with the consumer and demonstrate to the consumer that the provider is taking the time and effort to carefully select co-consumption partners.

However, attentive features are less informative in terms of whether the in-person interaction with a provider will be enjoyable. Even if a provider signals attentive behavior, this does not tell the consumer whether they will have an enjoyable interaction with the provider. Attentive features signal selectivity and intentional engagement, reassuring consumers that the provider is selective. However, this does not inherently indicate whether the in-person interaction will be enjoyable (for example, practical or safety considerations may be at the root of the driver’s selectivity)—leaving consumers uncertain about the enjoyability of future interactions with the provider. Indeed, overly attentive individuals are not always enjoyable to interact with. We therefore hypothesize that attentive features primarily reduce connection uncertainty but not interaction uncertainty:

## H3: The availability of attentive features reduces the connection dimension of rapport uncertainty.

Gremler and Gwinner (2008) discuss how the information sharing behavior of giving advice and asking questions to understand customer needs also generates rapport. Information sharing features support users in their evaluation of potential in-person interactions with the provider and provide signals of the provider’s interest in information exchange. In the co-consumption service context, providers can indicate their availability for information exchange on the digital platform. However, such information sharing features signal the possibility of information sharing rather than on actually sharing information. While such features can help a provider signal approachability and a willingness to engage, reducing concerns about whether future interactions will be enjoyable, they do not convey shared values or interpersonal compatibility and therefore do not reduce connection uncertainty. As a result, we hypothesize that information sharing features influence only the interaction dimension of rapport uncertainty:

## H4: The availability of information sharing features reduces the interaction dimension of rapport uncertainty.

## 4 Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we designed an experimental study that we situated in a long-distance (usually lasting 3-5 hours) carpooling context as a prime example of a service where consumers and providers are coconsuming a service. Long-distance carpooling was selected as the context of the empirical study of rapport uncertainty for four primary reasons: First, there is high physical proximity between drivers and passengers during a car ride. Second, this type of co-consumption experience typically lasts several hours, making rapport an important aspect of the service experience. Third, carpooling is particularly well-suited for an empirical investigation due to the clear distinction between the functional (arriving safely and on time) and nonfunctional (personal connection and interaction) components of the service. Lastly, carpooling is a comparatively widely distributed and tangible example of a co-consumption service, making it easy for participants to relate to the study’s context.

In our experimental study, participants interacted with a digital platform for booking long-distance carpooling services developed by the research team, allowing a realistic experience of interactions and decision-making. The platform design varied across eight experimental groups (n = 357; between-subjects) for testing the theoretical model. This section starts with a description of the selected manipulations and the experimental procedure and is followed by the introduction of the measurement instrument. The section concludes by depicting the data collection process.

## 4.1 Experimental Manipulations

In order to identify and verify tangible instantiations that represent the three rapport building behaviors in the context of our empirical study, we followed a two-step procedure (identification and confirmation) using three data sources (practical screening, interviews, quantitative confirmation study), as depicted in Figure 3.

Step 1: In order to identify contextualized representations for attentive behavior, common grounding behavior, and information sharing behavior, as described in Table 2, we first screened current sharing economy platforms and their approaches to providing information and signals about transaction partners. We then conducted nine interviews with users of the carpooling platform BlaBlaCar (BlaBlaCar, 2018) to gain a better understanding of the booking process on carpooling platforms and, in particular, how specific platform features can contribute to mitigating the perceived uncertainties for users. Five interviewees reported using the platform exclusively as passengers, one interviewee exclusively as a driver, and three as both a driver and a passenger. All interviews were recorded and transcribed for the analysis. An overview of the interviewed BlaBlaCar users is given in Appendix D. The screening and the interviews revealed three specific platform features in this context that relate to the three rapport building signals we adapted from Gremler and Gwinner (2008): transaction confirmation type (attentive features), the driver’s display of personal interests on the platform (common grounding features), and the opportunity to ask the driver questions before booking the ride (information sharing features).

Regarding attentive behavior, data from our interviews with carpooling users suggested that passengers perceive drivers choosing manual confirmation as showing more personal interest in their passengers, whereas they perceive that instant booking signals less personal interest:

I would choose the driver with manual confirmation. … He checks which people he actually will pick up. He takes care of himself, the car, and the people. I have the feeling that drivers with automatic confirmation only want to have the car fully occupied and mainly want to get the money. (Daniel B., passenger)

When the driver manually confirms, I believe that he has more interest and not only thinks of people who pay. (Benjamin G., passenger)

If the driver confirms manually, I know that the driver doesn’t take everyone along and will feel comfortable with me riding along. (Anna T., passenger)

When it all happens so anonymously and automatically then I just don’t like it. I just think the flair has been lost there, too. (Tamara F., passenger)

Manual confirmation reflects attentive behavior through (1) atypical actions (requiring effort beyond automated norms), (2) personal recognition (passengers feel individually acknowledged), and (3) interest in the consumer (the driver’s engagement signals a focus on the passenger’s needs). Selectivity plays a key role here because it is perceived not as arbitrariness but as a manifestation of thoughtful engagement and a focus on service quality. Accordingly, we identified transaction confirmation type as a potential attentive behavior feature.

Regarding common grounding behavior, sharing platforms can provide additional information about the respective service provider that is not related to the evaluation of the shared service itself, such as selfdescriptions and photos. The following quotes from our interviews indicate that displaying personal interests on the platform may contribute to identifying common interests or other similarities between the driver and passenger:

It is certainly an advantage if you have the same interests and can then also evaluate a bit about [how well you get along with the passengers]. (Daniela H., driver and passenger)

Things like hobbies or something like that would be cool to know, so you might be able to go for common ground. If you drive together for 6-7 hours, you could take similarities in advance as an exclusion criterion, which you would otherwise only experience through the conversation in the car. (Joanna K., driver and passenger)

Drivers displaying their personal interests on the platform reflects common grounding behavior through signaling (1) mutual interests, where shared hobbies and values provide an immediate connection between driver and passenger; (2) interpersonal relatability, allowing passengers to assess compatibility with the driver based on lifestyle and preferences; and (3) conversation facilitation, as recognizing commonalities fosters natural and engaging dialogue during co-consumption. Accordingly, we identified the driver’s display of personal interests on the platform as a potential common grounding behavior feature.

Regarding information sharing behavior, platforms often provide the opportunity to contact the service provider before engaging in a transaction, typically through the use of a contact form. Our interview data suggests that the opportunity to ask the driver questions before booking the ride is also valued, as highlighted by the following quotes:

If you can clarify a few basic data in advance, I always think it’s quite good. Then I’ll ask the driver a question. I find that more pleasant as a passenger. (Simon F., driver and passenger)

I always ask the drivers before I book. I find it strange when I ride along without ever having written a sentence first. It is just a stranger and I can already check by 2-3 messages whether that is sympathetic to me or not. (Miriam Z., passenger)

The opportunity to ask the driver questions beforehand (1) provides passengers with the opportunity to seek clarification, (2) serves as a signal that additional knowledge can be accessed, and (3) allows passengers to communicate their needs. Accordingly, we identified the opportunity to ask the driver questions before booking a ride as a potential information sharing behavior feature.

Step 2: In order to validate and confirm that the three design features (transaction confirmation type, driver’s display of personal interests on the platform, and the opportunity to ask questions before booking) represent contextualized instantiations of features that facilitate information sharing, common grounding, or attentive behaviors for rapport building, we conducted a dedicated empirical study in which participants were exposed to one selected feature in a between-subjects experiment. The participants were asked to what degree the feature they were presented with allowed them to assess attentive behavior, common grounding behavior, and information sharing behavior. Participants (n = 148) were randomly assigned to one of the features. Questions were derived from Hyun and Kim (2014) and Gremler and Gwinner (2008). The results suggest that the transaction confirmation type triggered significantly higher perceptions of attentive behavior than the other design features. Similarly, the driver’s display of personal interests on the platform was more strongly linked with common grounding behaviors than the other features. We found the same result for the opportunity to ask the driver questions beforehand, which was the strongest determinant of information sharing behavior. A more detailed account of this study and the full statistical results of this study can be found in Appendix E.

Given the consistent qualitative and quantitative evidence, we concluded that the selected features represent valid contextualized instantiations of features representing the three rapport building behaviors in question.

## 4.2 Experimental Procedure

The experimental procedure comprised seven parts. At the beginning of the experiment, the participants were welcomed and briefly told that the study dealt with longdistance carpooling (usually lasting 3-5 hours). This introduction included an estimate of the questionnaire’s duration (ca. 15 minutes), a rough description of the structure of the survey, and a statement assuring participants that all answers would be kept strictly confidential in order to reduce social desirability in the answers (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

Subsequently, the participants were shown a brief definition of long-distance carpooling and a description of the purpose of a carpooling platform. In this step, the participants also received a detailed task description for the next steps of the survey. This page also included an attention check to ensure that the participants read the instructions and fully understood the tasks they would be performing (see Section 4.4 for further details).

In a third step, the participants were redirected to a prototypical carpooling platform that we developed ourselves. There, the participants were randomly assigned to one of eight experimental groups and were asked to examine various ride offers in detail and select one of them. We employed a 2 (transaction confirmation type: instant or manual) × 2 (driver’s display of interests: yes or no) × 2 (opportunity to ask the driver questions: yes or no) between-subjects experimental design to test our hypotheses. By employing a between-subject design where every participant faced only one of the eight different scenarios, we were able to prevent carryover effects, which within-subjects designs are vulnerable to (Field & Hole, 2002). This setting also allowed us to include several covariates and enabled us to design and control the independent variables of the potential platform mechanisms to reduce rapport uncertainty. We were also able to use the typical setting of a carpooling transaction on an online platform as the study setting. Figure 4 visualizes the eight different experimental groups and illustrates the implementation in the prototypical carpooling platform. Additional screenshots of the questionnaire and the prototypical carpooling platform can be found in Appendix C.

In the fourth step, the participants were guided back to the actual survey, where they were asked to rate their intention to use the platform to book a ride. Afterwards, participants were asked to rate their perceptions of rapport uncertainty (i.e., connection and interaction dimensions of rapport uncertainty), technical uncertainty, and environmental uncertainty in terms of booking a ride on the platform. Finally, participants were asked control questions and demographic questions. At the end of the questionnaire, the participants were debriefed and thanked for their participation.

For the three uncertainty dimensions developed above, we adapted constructs from service quality measurement scales used in prior studies and adjusted them to match an ex ante perspective. We therefore followed the steps suggested by MacKenzie et al. (2011) to revalidate the scales used in this study. First, we made the conceptual definition of each construct explicit by referring to the definition of the original sources. In cases where no definition was available in the literature, we developed a precise conceptual definition). This step was necessary for precision in “what the construct does and does not refer to” (MacKenzie et al., 2011, p. 295). Table 3 provides an overview of the conceptual definitions of the uncertainty constructs.

According to MacKenzie et al. (2011), the developed items should be evaluated for their content validity. Therefore, in a first step, we generated item candidates inspired by Gremler and Gwinner (2000) and Dagger et al. (2007) and adjusted them to the context of a priori evaluations of rapport, technological, and environmental uncertainty in co-consumption. Four coders were then asked to sort the individual items into classes and to define the respective classes (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). In a second step, following the procedure outlined by Hinkin and Tracey (1999), we constructed a matrix in which definitions of the constructs were listed at the top of the columns and the items in the rows. Then, raters were asked to rate the extent to which each item captured the construct domain on a 5-point Likert scale. To eliminate doubts about the unambiguity of each item, the qualitative feedback of the coders and raters was used to perform minor adjustments in the wording of some items. In this way, content validity was ensured.

After assessing the content validity, we conducted a quantitative pre-test with 50 participants to check the internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, indicator reliability, and discriminant validity of the measurement model. In a scale purification process, items that were not necessary for the conceptual domain or did not meet the threshold of one of the checks were eliminated.

To measure the intention to use a platform to book, we used items from Sia et al. (2009). As control variables, we measured the disposition to trust with items from Gefen (2000), trust in the community (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), trust in the intermediary (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004), and risk propensity (Glaser et al., 2016; Gomez-Mejia & Balkin, 1989; Zhao et al., 2005). The final measurement model consisted of nine first-order reflective latent variables, with 39 items in total. The items and the original sources used for measuring the dependent variables are listed in Appendix F.

## 4.4 Sampling and Data Collection

We aimed to recruit a heterogeneous group of participants and recruited participants for the experiment via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) (Compeau et al., 2012). We had several reasons for selecting MTurk to estimate our research model and test our hypotheses: First, running the experiment on the internet automatically limits the sample to internet users. This sample was well-suited to our needs since the type of carpooling we examined is typically mediated by an online platform. Second, MTurk workers have been shown to be a good data source for theory testing (Steelman et al., 2014), and MTurk is a wellestablished platform for behavioral research (Behrend et al., 2011) and experimental studies (e.g., Hibbeln et al., 2017; Kaufmann et al., 2013). Third, the quality and high reliability of the data and results obtained from MTurk workers have been demonstrated by a variety of studies (e.g., Buhrmester et al., 2011; Steelman et al., 2014). Fourth, MTurk is frequently used for random sample populations (Berinsky et al., 2012), and the behavior of subjects on MTurk is comparable to the behavior of laboratory subjects (e.g., Horton et al., 2011; Mason & Suri, 2012; Paolacci et al., 2010). Fifth, using MTurk allowed us to restrict participants to a specific country of origin. We limited the sampling to participants from the USA.

To prevent participant satisficing behavior, we implemented several features: First, we highlighted the importance of each individual answer for our study to increase the involvement of the participants. Second, by developing our own appealing online platform with typical features, we designed the experiment to be as interesting and as realistic as possible for the participants. Third, in order to increase the statistical power of our experiment, we implemented the instructional manipulation check (IMC) by Oppenheimer et al. (2009). The IMC comprises a specific task hidden within the instructions of the study that participants have to perform—for instance, clicking somewhere other than the next button to proceed with the survey. Accordingly, participants who do not read the instructions can be identified since they will fail to perform the task. According to Oppenheimer et al. (2009), people’s satisficing behavior in the progress of a study is reduced if they receive immediate feedback that they need to read the instructions carefully and get another chance to do so. Therefore, participants who failed the IMC at the beginning of the study were shown a message that it is important to read and understand the instructions to ensure the validity of the study results. Furthermore, we included a second IMC within the survey and asked the participants to click on a certain text in order to proceed with the survey. By doing so, we prevented participants from completing the survey without carefully reading the questions and descriptions.

Besides features to prevent satisficing, we also implemented several measures to detect and eliminate satisficers that passed the checks mentioned above. In order to remove only extreme satisficers from our sample (Chen, 2011), we applied the following measures: First, we excluded participants who failed one of the IMCs more than three times. If a participant was reminded more than three times to read the instructions carefully and failed to do so several times, we assumed that the participant was an extreme satisficer. Second, we deleted answers with a very low response time and excluded respondents who failed to answer two control questions as an attention check (Krosnick, 1991; Malhotra, 2008). Third, we included two duplicate questions and excluded the responses if they deviated more than two points on the Likert scale. After data cleaning, 357 remaining responses were utilized for this study.

As illustrated in Table 4, the gender distribution was nearly balanced in our sample. Furthermore, our sample contained participants from all parts of society, as indicated by the distribution of education and income.

## 5 Analytical Results

This section presents the results of the data analysis. In line with research that combines structural models with experimental data (Trenz et al., 2020), we used PLS-SEM to assess H1 and included the control variables in this analysis. In contrast to regression or path modeling, this approach allowed us to assess the measurement model and the structural model at the same time<sup>4</sup> (Gefen et al., 2011). We then used the factor scores in a multivariate ANCOVA to evaluate the influences postulated in H2-H4.

## 5.1 Analysis of Measurement Model

We evaluated the reliability and validity of the reflective measurement models based on their internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2009). We assessed internal consistency reliability to ensure that the items measuring the same latent variable were all strongly correlated with this latent variable (Henseler et al., 2009). The composite reliability of all measurement models exceeded the suggested threshold of 0.7 (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). We then assessed convergent validity to ensure that each set of indicators measured one and the same underlying construct and thus exhibited unidimensionality (Henseler et al., 2009). All items used in our model had outer loadings of over 0.708, demonstrating that the items shared more than 50% of their variance with the latent variable (Hair et al., 2016). Table 5 provides an overview of these statistics. We then investigated discriminant validity to ensure that each construct was unique and not represented by other constructs (Hair et al., 2016). We assessed discriminant validity in three ways. First, we tested the fulfillment of the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which suggests that the square root of the AVE for each variable should be greater than its correlation with any other construct in the model (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). This criterion was fulfilled by all latent variables, as highlighted in Appendix F. Second, we checked whether the loading of each indicator on the intended latent variable was greater than all of its cross-loadings (Chin, 1998), suggesting that the indicator shared more variance with its latent variable than with any other variable. This criterion was also fulfilled. Details on the cross-loadings are shown in Appendix H. Third, we assessed the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) value.. All HTMT values were well below 1.0, indicating that the constructs are unlikely to be perfectly correlated and therefore can be considered distinct (Henseler et al., 2015). The HTMT values are presented in Appendix I.

Since the data for each participant was obtained using a single measurement method, we applied procedural and statistical remedies to minimize and control for common method bias (CMB), as proposed by Podsakoff et al. (2003). From a procedural perspective, we first assured respondents that their answers would be confidential and explicitly stated that there were no right or wrong answers. These procedures were intended to minimize the social desirability bias. Second, we ensured psychological separation in the form of a cover story suggesting that the predictor variables were not related to the dependent variables. Third, due to the between-subject experimental design, the independent variables were primed by experimental manipulations that were hidden from the participants. Fourth, the questionnaire and the corresponding scales were carefully developed to reduce the satisficing behavior of the participants.

Besides procedural remedies, we also applied two statistical remedies to control for CMB: Harman’s singlefactor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003) and the measured latent marker variable (MLMV) approach for PLS (Chin et al., 2013). First, we conducted Harman’s single-factor analysis (Podsakoff et al., 2003) by using an exploratory factor analysis for the Likert-scaled variables in SPSS. The unrotated principal component analysis revealed eight factors with eigenvalues above 1 and explained 80.23% of the variance. The largest component accounted for 32.03% of the variance. As no single factor emerged and no general factor accounted for the majority of the variance, Harman’s single-factor test indicated that our results were not influenced by CMB (Malhotra et al., 2006). Second, we applied the MLMV approach for construct-level correction (CLC) for PLS suggested by Chin et al. (2013). In this approach, multiple unrelated measures with no nomological relationship with other study questions were collected at the same time as the data collection for the research model. These unrelated measures were modeled as control constructs to impact each model’s construct. After introducing the control constructs, the path coefficients were again estimated. These path coefficients of the constructs represent the model without a CMB impact (Tehseen et al., 2017).

We observed that the differences between the original estimated path coefficients and the path coefficients estimated by CLC were very small. Likewise, there were nonsignificant changes in other parameters, such as the pvalues and R²; the significances of the model remained unchanged. Appendix J summarizes the differences between the original model and the CLC model, and shows the items used for the control construct. Based on the procedural remedies and the statistical tests, we concluded that CMB did not bias the results of our study.

## 5.2 Analysis of Structural Model

We now evaluate the structural model. Since the PLS algorithm assumes that there is no collinearity between the independent and dependent variables (Hair et al., 2016), we needed to make sure that this condition also applied to our variables. For this purpose, we estimated the latent variable scores for each variable and used these scores in SPSS to run a linear regression on each of the endogenous variables. We estimated the variance inflation factor (VIF) based on these regressions (Mooi & Sarstedt, 2011). The VIF quantifies the degree of multicollinearity by showing how much the variance of an estimated regression coefficient is inflated due to collinearity. The VIF for all latent variables was far below the recommended threshold of 5 (even below 3), suggesting that multicollinearity did not lead to bias in the path estimates.

Subsequently, we used SmartPLS 3 to estimate the path coefficients. The significance of each path between the constructs was analyzed using a bootstrapping technique (5,000 subsamples) with 357 cases each. All constructs were modeled as reflective.

The aim of this analysis was to identify the impact of the two-dimensional rapport uncertainty construct while controlling for established uncertainty dimensions. In line with our expectations, the interaction dimension of rapport uncertainty exerted a negative effect on the intention to use the platform (H1a; $\beta = - 0 . 2 1 3 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ The interaction dimension was revealed to be the most important driver of our outcome variable, followed by technical uncertainty, while environmental uncertainty seemed less important in this context. The connection dimension of rapport uncertainty, on the other hand, did not seem to be an important driver of the intention to use the platform to book a ride (H1b; $\beta = . 0 . 0 7 2 ; p > 0 . 0 5 )$

As mentioned above, we also included a variety of control variables to reduce the unexplained variance and control for alternative explanations. Besides trust in the community $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 9 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , none of the other control variables (disposition to trust, trust in intermediary, risk propensity, age, gender, income, or education) showed a significant impact on the intention to use the platform to book a ride.

The model explains a considerable amount of the variance of the intention to use a co-consumption platform for booking $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 0 8 )$ . Table 6 summarizes the results of the structural model.

To evaluate H2-H4, we used a multiple analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) with the two dimensions of rapport uncertainty as dependent variables, the experimental manipulations as factors, and disposition to trust as covariates. Risk propensity, trust in an intermediary, and trust in the community were all insignificant and were thus removed from the analysis.

The requirements of the MANCOVA were fulfilled. The insignificant Shapiro-Wilk tests $( p > 0 . 0 5$ for withingroup tests and overall-sample test) revealed that the rapport uncertainty variables had a normal distribution across the experimental groups. Also, Levene’s test $( p >$ 0.05) for the homogeneity of variances was insignificant, indicating that the variances were equal across the experimental groups. The results of the MANCOVA (see Table 7) showed a significant effect for common grounding features on rapport uncertainty in terms of interaction (H2a; $F _ { ( 1 , 3 4 8 ) } = 1 1 . 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ as well as connection (H2b; $F _ { ( 1 , 3 4 8 ) } = 4 . 7 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

Although we did not find a significant direct effect of attentive features on the connection dimension of rapport uncertainty $( F _ { ( 1 , 3 4 8 ) } = 0 . 0 0 1 , ~ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , our analysis revealed an interaction effect indicating that the importance of attentive features was conditional on the existence of common grounding features $( F _ { ( 1 , 3 4 8 ) } = 4 . 9 8 .$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This suggests that attentive features effectively mitigate rapport uncertainty with regard to connection if common grounding features also exist, providing partial support for H3. Similarly, we also could not identify a direct effect of information sharing features on the interaction dimension of rapport uncertainty $( F _ { ( 1 , 3 4 8 ) } =$ $0 . 3 1 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . The importance of information sharing features appears to be conditional on the existence of common grounding features. ## 6 Discussion

The objectives of this study were to assess the role of rapport uncertainty in determining consumers’ intention to book a service on a co-consumption platform and to understand how platform design can facilitate transactions in co-consumption environments by reducing consumers’ rapport uncertainty. To achieve the objectives, we investigated sharing economy services where consumers and the service providers co-consume a resource in close physical proximity, thus sharing the service experience. Long-distance carpooling, as a prime example of such services, served as the setting for our empirical investigation. In the following, we reflect on our findings, highlight the contributions, and discuss limitations as well as emerging opportunities for further research.

## 6.1 Key Findings

Our results reveal that rapport uncertainty, i.e., a consumer’s uncertainty as to whether there will be an enjoyable interaction with a service provider (adapted from Gremler & Gwinner, 2000), is a significant factor in consumers’ decisions to engage with a co-consumption platform and book a service. In fact, we show that rapport uncertainty accounts for the largest proportion of the variance in the intention to use a platform for booking a service in the context of our empirical study. By distinguishing between different types of uncertainty, we show that rapport uncertainty complements technical and environmental uncertainties, which, until now, have only partially captured the uncertainties that consumers face before booking co-consumption services. This is in line with our theoretical considerations on nonfunctional uncertainties that may play a special role in coconsumption services. In the assessed uncertainty mix, technical uncertainty (i.e., uncertainty regarding consumers’ functional goals, like reliably traveling from A to B in a carpooling scenario) is the second strongest determinant of the customers’ intention to use the platform to book a service. Surprisingly, we could not detect a significant influence of environmental uncertainty on the consumers’ intention to use a carpooling platform to book a service. A plausible explanation could be that ambient environmental factors (e.g., temperature or smell) are not typically noticed by consumers except in extreme cases (Koernig, 2003). Potentially, consumers may have a minimum standard for the environment and thus be relatively unconcerned about the ambient conditions of the physical resource.

Our differentiated view on rapport uncertainty (distinguishing between interaction and connection dimensions of uncertainty) revealed that the interaction component of rapport uncertainty appears to be the most significant determinant of consumer booking behavior. At the same time, the connection dimension of rapport uncertainty was less important in our context. The explanation for this finding may be rooted in the shortterm, periodic, and experiential nature of co-consumption services, where pleasant interactions may be more important than developing strong ties of mutual caring. Consequently, consumers using such services do not seem to require mutual understanding or a shared value system with the service provider. This is in line with current studies highlighting individuals’ growing focus on shortterm enjoyment on platforms and the difficulty of building relationships on platforms (e.g., manifested in the high levels of isolation despite social media platform use; see Primack et al., 2017). However, as our study is the first to empirically disentangle the interaction and connection components of rapport, it may also be that this difference has simply been overlooked when considering rapport as an aggregate construct.

Furthermore, we found evidence that the perceived rapport uncertainty on the consumer side can be mitigated by the co-consumption platform design. We analyzed and tested three mechanisms for rapport building that mediating platforms can implement. Empirical results corroborated our hypothesis that common grounding features that aid in evaluating similarity between drivers and passengers can reduce the rapport uncertainty that consumers experience before engaging with a co-consumption service. Interestingly, we did not find evidence for a direct effect on rapport uncertainty of features showcasing attentive behavior and information sharing behavior. Our findings instead suggest that rapport-building mechanisms do not function independently prior to a transaction but instead interact with one another. While literature on rapportbuilding during service consumption has typically examined real-time interactions where multiple rapport cues operate simultaneously (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000), our results highlight a different dynamic in the pre-transaction phase. In this phase, consumers do not engage in direct interactions but must instead infer potential rapport based on the digital features provided on the platform. As a result, their effectiveness seems to depend on how they are combined. For example, we found that attentive features only reduced connection uncertainty when common grounding features were also present, suggesting that attentiveness alone may not be sufficient to establish an expected connection unless shared interests signal compatibility. Similarly, information sharing features only reduced interaction uncertainty when common grounding features were absent, implying that users may seek additional information only if they have not already formed a rapport expectation through shared interests. These findings suggest that, in digital platform contexts, rapport-building mechanisms operate not as independent influences but as interdependent factors that shape users’ expectations of future interactions.

## 6.2 Implications for Theory and Practice

This study contributes to theory in multiple ways. First, by reconceptualizing uncertainty to include nonfunctional, interpersonal components (i.e., rapport uncertainty), our theoretical framework extends traditional models that have predominantly focused on functional uncertainties related to sellers (i.e., technical uncertainty, Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Dellarocas, 2003; Pavlou et al., 2007; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004) or resources (i.e., environmental uncertainty, Dimoka et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Kim & Krishnan, 2015; Matt & Hess, 2016; Overby & Jap, 2009). The introduction of the nonfunctional component not only represents an advance in the understanding of the role of uncertainty in consumer decisions, it also challenges prior and future applications of traditionally functionally oriented uncertainty frameworks in settings with a high degree of personal interaction between consumers and providers— e.g., the domain of co-consumption. This dual perspective on functional and nonfunctional components of uncertainty in consumer decision-making on digital platforms also reveals that the prospect of anticipated interpersonal interactions plays a decisive role in pretransaction decision-making.

This insight invites a revisiting of prior studies that have focused on the evaluation of consumers’ functional uncertainties during or after service provision, thus excluding a potentially significant portion of consumers whose evaluation of nonfunctional service components led them to abstain from engaging with the service in the pre-transaction phase. In fact, our results suggest that rapport uncertainty is the primary uncertainty dimension in explaining the intention to book a service that involves service participants’ co-consumption in close physical proximity to one another. This offers support to past studies emphasizing the importance of rapport in the evaluation of services (Bitner et al., 1990) and can help future studies position the uncertainty evaluation along the different stages of the service encounter. Although research on co-consumption experiences is scarce, our findings suggest that focusing on functional aspects in areas where interpersonal interactions matter may lead to an incomplete understanding, as individuals may hesitate to transact even when functional criteria appear to be favorable. In fact, prior research on ride sharing has pointed to unexpected gaps in booking intentions that may be attributed to social or emotional factors (Arteaga-Sánchez et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2017).

Our study contributes to this research direction by providing evidence and an explanation for why functional attributes alone fail to account for variations in consumer engagement with co-consumption platforms. Our study addresses an emerging need, as services with blurred boundaries between consumers and providers, where consumer-provider interactions are linked to consumer satisfaction, are on the rise (Ding et al., 2023). Recent research has highlighted the importance of preserving human social bonds (Kirk et al., 2025), and people are increasingly seeking genuine human interaction in a digital world that increasingly simulates human interaction through various digital agents. Thus, the importance of a refined understanding of uncertainties regarding nonfunctional components of the service encounter—such as that provided by our study—is likely to grow.

Second, we introduce the concept of rapport uncertainty to the repertoire of IS research and present the related construct as a means of capturing the nonfunctional interpersonal relationships between consumers and service providers. We thereby contribute to the call for contextualization in information systems research (Avgerou, 2019) by developing an understanding of consumers’ uncertainties regarding rapport, which is an important issue, particularly in the context of coconsumption platforms. In our theorizing about rapport uncertainty, we build upon the extant literature on service quality and rapport and conceptualize rapport uncertainty as a two-dimensional construct. Its dimensions, interaction, and connections are well established in terms of service evaluations that follow the service encounter (rather than a pre-consumption evaluation). In the case of post-consumption evaluation, it is well understood that consumers evaluate both rapport uncertainty dimensions jointly (Gremler & Gwinner, 2000).

However, our study reconceptualizes rapport by taking a pre-transaction perspective that does not relate to the evaluation of the service experience but to its prospect (i.e., rapport uncertainty). This allowed us to disentangle rapport’s two original dimensions (connection and interaction), which are typically empirically merged in post-service evaluations. Our research model and empirical findings suggest that neither the outcomes nor the determinants of the two rapport uncertainty dimensions are identical. This provides additional support for the reconsideration of rapport in our context as consisting of two independent aspects that constitute rapport uncertainty. The concept of rapport uncertainty and its two dimensions are instrumental for researchers seeking to understand how consumer decisions depend on the degree of uncertainty regarding the likelihood of an enjoyable interaction and a personal connection with the prospective service provider. The theoretical and practical value of this disentanglement becomes clear when considering what is missed by viewing rapport uncertainty as a single, undifferentiated construct. Without a two-dimensional lens, researchers may misinterpret why certain interventions succeed or fail, while practitioners may risk implementing features that only solve half of the problem. For instance, our findings suggest that a platform could successfully implement features that reduce a user’s connection uncertainty (e.g., by highlighting shared values) yet still fail to secure a transaction if the user’s interaction uncertainty (e.g., concerns about conversational chemistry) remains high. This two-dimensional conceptualization, therefore, offers a more precise diagnostic tool. It enables the targeted application of design features to the specific aspect of rapport uncertainty they are suited to address, providing a clear picture of the interpersonal barriers to coconsumption.

Third, we develop a theoretical understanding of how IS design can cater to the reduction of interpersonal uncertainties in the context of digitally mediated physical transactions. While prior research has focused on mitigating technical uncertainty (e.g., whether a service provider can reliably deliver the expected functional outcome) and environmental uncertainty (e.g., the quality of the shared physical resource), our study highlights that rapport uncertainty requires distinct reduction mechanisms. Traditional uncertainty reduction mechanisms, such as ratings, reviews, and guarantees, primarily address functional concerns by providing aggregated consumer feedback about service reliability or product quality. However, rapport uncertainty is inherently intersubjective, meaning that others’ experiences do not reliably predict an individual consumer’s future interaction quality or personal connection with a provider. To address this, we demonstrate that platform design can indeed mitigate rapport uncertainty but that the effects of different approaches to rapport building differ. For instance, we conceptualize platform design features that reduce rapport uncertainty through common grounding behaviors of the service participants on the digital platform before the in-person service encounter (e.g., displaying interests of the involved participants). In so doing, we extend the scarce knowledge on rapport in the IS domain by proposing and testing technical ways in which a mediating platform can mitigate rapport uncertainty. As digital platforms become the starting point for many interpersonal interactions (e.g., caregiving work, dating, virtual work, etc.), the theoretical and empirical insights on how platform design can cater to the reduction of rapport uncertainty should be instrumental for future research in these areas.

Fourth, our findings point toward more complex relationships between rapport building behaviors than originally suggested in the service literature. While Gremler and Gwinner (2008) suggest different ways to develop rapport, our findings suggest both complementary and conflicting relationships between different rapport building strategies implemented in platform features. Such interactions among rapport building behaviors, which have not investigated in prior studies on rapport evaluation after the service encounter, become particularly important when features aimed at reducing rapport uncertainty are evaluated concurrently on the platform. However, the finding also more generally challenges the common assumption that all rapport building behaviors contribute to the overall goal of fostering an enjoyable interaction and a personal connection. Departing from here, one may consider a configurational perspective on rapport building behaviors (Maier et al., 2021) with the potential of disentangling those complex interrelationships to enable more effective service design.

Finally, the insights from our study have significant implications for the research context of the sharing economy, particularly in co-consumption scenarios. While early literature emphasized the challenges of assessing services within the sharing economy and understanding consumer engagement, much of this work has focused on trust as a pivotal element, highlighting its importance in enabling sociotechnological innovation (Botsman, 2017). Subsequent information systems research has explored trust in relation to the service’s functional aspects, either through provider capabilities or attributes such as benevolence and integrity (Cheng et al., 2023; Mittendorf et al., 2019; Tripp et al., 2023). This focus on functional aspects extends to addressing consumer uncertainty, emphasizing components like the quality and cleanliness of shared spaces (Dewan et al., 2023). Our study contributes to a more nuanced discussion by demonstrating that in co-consumption scenarios, nonfunctional components are more influential in determining service quality assessments and addressing uncertainties. Specifically, we show that functional components are either irrelevant (as in the case of environmental uncertainty) or less significant (as in technical uncertainty). Thus, our research provides a contextualized contribution to the discourse on the importance of functional versus nonfunctional service quality aspects, highlighting the paramount importance of nonfunctional components in the sharing economy.

Our findings carry significant practical implications for platform operators, providers, and policymakers in the context of co-consumption platforms. For platform operators, our results indicate that conventional uncertainty reduction mechanisms—such as feedback ratings (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Dellarocas, 2003), customer reviews (Hong & Pavlou, 2014; Zhu et al., 2010), and third-party assurances (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004, 2005)— are insufficient for addressing rapport uncertainty, which significantly impacts booking behavior. Thus, platform design should incorporate features that facilitate pretransaction interpersonal assessment, such as common grounding features (e.g., shared interest displays), attentive features (e.g., manual booking confirmation), and information sharing features (e.g., direct messaging or structured Q&A). These features, however, should not be implemented in isolation but orchestrated, as our findings show that their effectiveness is interdependent. Platforms should furthermore facilitate users’ engagement with these features as their availability increases their likelihood of booking. The ability of platforms to successfully reduce rapport uncertainty can lead to a competitive edge.

Our findings also point towards an opportunity for providers on co-consumption platforms, who may be able to increase their booking likelihood by actively signaling interpersonal compatibility through personalized profiles, proactive engagement in pre-transaction communication, and selective booking confirmation processes that demonstrate attentiveness to consumer preferences. From a practical standpoint, platforms can directly address the importance of nonfunctional uncertainties by implementing specific design features. As our study demonstrates, this includes common grounding features (e.g., displaying shared interests), information sharing features (e.g., enabling pre-booking questions), and attentive features (e.g., manual booking confirmations) that allow users to better infer their potential rapport with one another before committing to a transaction.

From a policy and regulatory perspective, our findings suggest that consumer protection efforts in digital platforms should extend beyond functional service quality to include interpersonal compatibility considerations. While transparency in functional attributes (e.g., safety, service reliability) is wellregulated, the interpersonal assessment may give rise to biased decisions that may require new considerations on anti-discrimination safeguards, ensuring that rapportbased filtering does not lead to exclusionary effects.

## 6.3 Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Our study is subject to limitations and creates avenues for further research. First, we focused on analyzing consumers’ intentions to book offers on co-consumption platforms in an experimental setting. To mitigate this concern, we relied on a platform prototype on which participants could browse and engage with the platform. Nevertheless, the resulting limitation of the study in the form of relying on the theoretical link between behavioral intentions and actual behavior (Ajzen, 1991) could be bridged by translating the study to a field experiment in the future. Our experimental setting was furthermore limited to the long-distance co-consumptive carpooling scenario only. Our research results can be strengthened by replication studies in the context of other co-consumption service scenarios. Analyzing on-demand ride-for-hire services like Uber or Lyft could, for example, lead to interesting findings because these services differ in terms of the duration of the resource-sharing experience and other aspects. Also, the replication of the study in the context of accommodation co-consumptive offerings on Airbnb or wimdu could be promising, as the change in the physical resource shared (i.e., the home) can influence the uncertainty landscape consumers face and shed light on nuances that have not been captured in our research. Furthermore, the replication of our study with a sample from a different cultural value system could uncover differences that may be important for the co-consumption platform design.

Second, this study did not seek to evaluate the effectiveness of different platform features to reduce rapport uncertainty. Instead, it aimed to test whether rapport uncertainty can be mitigated by the mediating platform at all. Therefore, future studies could extend our findings on the importance of rapport uncertainty by investigating other potential mitigation mechanisms for this novel uncertainty category. We were able to show that rapport uncertainty can be mitigated with a common grounding behavior, namely displaying the interests of the service provider on the platform profile. However, we could not provide evidence for attentive behavior and information sharing behavior directly influencing rapport uncertainty, although we carefully designed our manipulations by triangulating three different pieces of information and validated that they were perceived by the participants. Future research could build on these findings and investigate other platform mechanisms that can potentially enable attentive behavior and information sharing behavior for rapport-building.

It must be noted that we designed our study in accordance with the status quo mode of operation for most co-consumption services, where there is typically a clear separation between the matchmaking phase in the digital realm and the in-person service encounter with interactions in the physical space. However, in scenarios where the service encounter shifts to the digital space through the introduction of more elaborate communication options—a potential outcome of addressing rapport uncertainty—behaviors like courteous and connecting behaviors, which were excluded from our research scope, could become more important, representing a further avenue for research. However, we caution that although digital tools may enhance elements of rapport building, the deeply interpersonal and spontaneous nature of courteous and connecting behaviors will remain difficult to replicate in a pre-transactional digital environment without disrupting the platform’s core value proposition of efficient matchmaking.

The third limitation—and a clear target for future work—is the tension between signaling rapport on digital platforms and protecting privacy. Our results suggest that platforms should make it easier for users to reveal personal, nonfunctional cues that reduce rapport uncertainty. But sharing interests, communication style, and values also creates privacy risks, from unwanted contact to discrimination or security breaches. Future studies should map this trade-off by questioning which personal cues ease rapport uncertainty at the lowest privacy cost, and how platforms can offer fine-grained controls or use privacy-preserving features that build trust without compromising safety.

Beyond further research directions related to the limitations of the study, our conceptualization of rapport uncertainty offers a fruitful avenue for structuring and delimiting future research on consumer behavior in services that bridge the digital and physical worlds and focus on the joint experience of service participants in physical proximity (Johnson & Neuhofer, 2017). We analyzed and argued for the importance of rapport uncertainty in the context of coconsumption services only. However, service provision in many contexts is shifting to digital platforms that are responsible for matching consumers and service providers who will later will meet in the physical world. Therefore, we expect that rapport uncertainty will become an increasingly important topic for both academic research and platform design beyond the context of co-consumption-based services in the sharing economy.

## Appendix A: Service Selection Against Uncertainty in the Service Quality

Consumers in co-consumption services, like in any service context, look for service quality when selecting a service, with the latter being subject to uncertainty. The view in IS that uncertainty reduces consumers’ confidence in the expected service quality (SQ) (Dimoka et al., 2012) builds upon research in service marketing where service quality (cf., Jia et al., 2008) is primarily studied from the consumer-centric view as the consumer’s overall impression of the service’s relative inferiority or superiority (Zeithaml, 1988).

Service quality acknowledges different stages of the service process (Surprenant & Solomon, 1987): before, during, and after the service encounter (Posselt & Gerstner, 2005), each representing potential points for consumers’ SQ evaluations. Accordingly, SQ comes into focus for consumers as early as the service selection stage and continues to be pertinent during and after the service encounter. Furthermore, service quality includes both functional and nonfunctional components. The functional aspects of the service reflect the service provider’s technical competence—for instance, knowledge, qualification, and skill (Dagger et al., 2007)—in adhering to high standards of service provision. Functional aspects also include environmental aspects that reflect the quality of the physical environment in which the service takes place (Bitner, 1990; Brady & Cronin, 2001). Perceived physical environment quality encompasses atmospheric aspects (e.g., temperature, smell, and sound) and tangible aspects (design, the function, or the layout of the environment) of the service provision (Brady & Cronin, 2001; Dagger et al., 2007). Beyond functional components, service quality considers enjoyment, entertainment, and fun in the consumer’s service experience (Bauer et al., 2006) as nonfunctional components.

The uncertainty in service quality becomes a relevant consideration when looking at the relationship between service quality and consumers’ behavioral intentions. SQ has been found to affect long-term aggregated behaviors like customer retention, as well as individual consumers’ intentions to remain loyal, switch services, and pay more (Cronin et al., 2000; Dabholkar et al., 2000; Zeithaml et al., 1996). In the digital realm, SQ has been identified as a precursor to behavioral intentions toward digital services, manifesting in forms like willingness to pay (Lowry & Wilson, 2016), customer loyalty (Xu et al., 2011), website usage (Cenfetelli et al., 2008; Nishant et al., 2019), job satisfaction, and continuous use of ride-hailing services (Kang et al., 2020).

In the relationship between SQ and behavioral intentions, uncertainties represent incomplete or partial knowledge (Garner, 1962; Knight, 1921). Consumers face uncertainties in the valuation of service quality (Taylor, 2018) across all service stages (Viglia et al., 2019), leading to changes in behavioral intentions.<sup>5</sup> For instance, service uncertainties may hinder consumers from committing to a service transaction (Pavlou et al., 2007) and can lead to more critical evaluations of services (Liu et al., 2001). Ultimately, service uncertainty can alter consumer demand structures and subsequently affect optimal pricing strategies, especially in on-demand service platforms (Taylor, 2018). Particularly during the provider selection, uncertainties lower consumers’ confidence in the expected service quality (Dimoka et al., 2012).

From the perspective of uncertainty in service quality (SQ), IS literature inherits the separation of service stages in its analysis of uncertainty in service quality, distinguishing between uncertainty during and after service selection (Pavlou et al., 2007). It considers both functional (technical and environmental aspects) and nonfunctional components of the service (Benlian et al., 2011). Reducing functional uncertainties, both technical and environmental aspects (Mudambi & Schuff, 2010), for instance, through ratings and reviews (e.g., Kanat et al., 2018; Kokkodis & Ipeirotis, 2016; Lin et al., 2018; Moreno & Terwiesch, 2014; Yoganarasimhan, 2013) or through mechanisms like paying after the service encounter (Viglia et al., 2019), are important, as functional acceptability of a service is critical for consumers who expect the service to lead to a desired outcome (Grönroos, 1982)

We draw attention to the literature’s implicit emphasis on addressing functional aspects of service uncertainty, such as focusing on changes in services’ functional utility to consumers (Bordley, 2001). This emphasis leaves uncertainties regarding nonfunctional aspects of the service during the selection process, particularly in the context of digitally enabled coconsumption services, where online selection must address consumers’ uncertainties regarding an offline service, as a research lacuna. Appendix B corroborates this gap by providing an overview of IS literature on service uncertainty, showing no single work on nonfunctional service uncertainties and no explicit account for the unique nature of co-consumption services, where online selection of a service is followed by offline interactions with the provider during co-consumption.

The lack of nonfunctional service uncertainty considerations is important because, while functional aspects of uncertainty (technical and environmental uncertainties) are present in co-consumption services (e.g., uncertainty regarding a carpooling driver’s ability to safely reach a destination or uncertainty regarding the perception of the smell, temperature, and ambience inside a car), they do not cover uncertainties regarding the nonfunctional interaction quality between consumers and providers. We refer to the latter as rapport uncertainty, highlighting its relation to the evaluation of the service’s quality and consumers’ resulting intentions to book co-consumption services. This knowledge gap is further manifested in the lack of guidance for coconsumption platforms on designing their platform in a way that reduces rapport uncertainty for consumers booking a service.

## Appendix B: IS Literature on Service Uncertainties

To capture the IS literature on service uncertainties, we queried the Scopus and AISel databases for the combination of “service” and “uncertain\*” in the title, abstract, and keywords. We identified 47 papers, of which 10 were relevant as they considered uncertainties faced by consumers. The remaining papers either did not explicitly consider service uncertainty as a construct or focused on organizational uncertainty (e.g., uncertainty in the context of service outsourcing; Benaroch et al., 2010). We only considered online service selection in combination with online or offline encounters because focusing on service selection within IS research implies that the selection process occurs in the digital space. Additionally, offline selection implies that consumers interact with the potential provider before selection, which serves as an uncertainty reduction instrument when considering nonfunctional aspects of the service. In sum, the literature not only lacks considerations of nonfunctional uncertainty aspects in service quality, but it also focuses predominantly on uncertainties in the context of online services, with only one study considering uncertainties regarding offline services where potential co-consumption can take place (Airbnb) (Dewan et al., 2023). However, even the latter study does not differentiate between rental and sharing settings and explicitly considers only the functional component, examining the effect of functional certification of platform providers by Airbnb.

## Appendix E. Confirmatory Study on Design Features and Rapport Building Behaviors

Three types of rapport building behaviors may be applicable to the context of collaborative consumption: information sharing behavior, attentive behavior, and common grounding behavior (Gremler & Gwinner, 2008). However, as collaborative consumption services are presented and selected online, features must serve as cues instead. We identified three potential features that may serve this purpose from our practical screening and interviews. The study fulfilled two major purposes:

1. Confirm that the design features are indicative of the rapport building behaviors they instantiate (i.e., that the feature is strongly associated with the specified type of rapport building behavior).

2. Confirm that the availability of these design features does not influence other rapport building behaviors (i.e., that the features are not associated with other rapport building behaviors).

This confirmatory study therefore presents evidence that the three selected design features can serve as empirical instantiations of the theoretically derived rapport building behaviors in the context of collaborative consumption platforms.

The study employed a between-subjects design with three groups in which each participant was exposed to a single design feature.

The procedure was the following: First, we introduced participants to the concept of long-distance carpooling (as in the main study). Second, we presented them with a carpooling platform and asked them to evaluate one particular feature of the platform. The instructions highlighted that the participants should not evaluate any actual content being displayed (which was constant across all three groups) but should look at the feature in detail. This is in line with our research focus, which is not about whether one specific ride is selected but about the use of a platform to book a ride. Third, after inspection of the feature, participants were asked to evaluate the platform feature concerning its potential to serve as attentive feature (e.g., “This platform feature allows me to see if the driver has an interest in me as a person”), information sharing feature (e.g., “This platform feature allows me to see if the driver is willing to share knowledge relevant to my trip before we start”), and common grounding feature (e.g., “This platform feature allows me to identify things we have in common”) with two items each. The items were adapted from Gremler and Gwinner (2008) and Hyun and Kim (2014). Lastly, participants were questioned about their experiences with carpooling and had to fill out a manipulation check and a realism check. Then, they were debriefed.

We recruited 165 participants based in the UK using the platform Prolific and its representative sampling service to mitigate a potential bias arising from a restricted sampling frame. Participants were paid according to the suggested average payment of the platform (12£ / hour). We removed 17 participants due to extreme rushing and failure to pass the manipulation check, ending up with an effective sample of 148 participants for the pre-study. Participants were, on average, 50 years old and 50.3% female. There were no differences with regard to carpooling experience or demographics across the groups, suggesting that random assignment to the three features was successful.

## About the Authors

Manuel Trenz is a professor of interorganizational information systems at the University of Goettingen, Germany. He holds a PhD from the University of Mannheim, Germany. His research focuses on data-driven innovation and digital platforms, with particular emphasis on human interaction in data-rich contexts and the responsible use of data for innovation and algorithmic decision-making. His work has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and other journals. He currently serves as a senior editor for Information Systems Journal.

Friedrich Chasin is a professor of information systems at Offenburg University of Applied Sciences. He was previously Deputy Chair of Information Systems & Systems Engineering at the University of Cologne. He received his PhD in information systems from the University of Münster, where his dissertation earned the Karl-Vossloh Prize for excellence in mobility research. His work examines how digital technologies and automation drive the circular economy, sustainable mobility, and sustainable credit provision in the financial sector. His work has been published in outlets such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, MIS Quarterly Executive, and Transportation Research.

Alexander Frey served as a research assistant in the Chair of Information Systems and Management at the University of Augsburg. His research centers on the sharing economy, with a particular emphasis on how organizations within this sector can create value for their users and the pivotal role that information technology plays in this process. After earning his doctorate in 2018, he transitioned to the private sector and currently holds a senior management position at a major insurance company.

Chee-Wee Tan is a professor in the Department of Management and Marketing at the Faculty of Business, Hong Kong Polytechnic University. He received his PhD in management information systems from the University of British Columbia. His research interests focus on design and innovation issues related to digital platforms. His work has been published in leading peer-reviewed journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Operations Management, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, and Decision Support Systems, among others. Apart from serving as a senior editor for MIS Quarterly, he has served or is currently serving on multiple editorial boards of leading journals. Finally, Chee-Wee was the vice president of Publications for the Association for Information Systems.

Daniel J. Veit holds the Chair of Information Systems and Management in the Department of Business Administration of the Faculty of Business and Economics of the University of Augsburg, Germany. He received degrees from the Justus Liebig University Giessen and the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. In his research he focuses on digital responsibility regarding an economically, societally and ecologically sustainable future. His work appears in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, and Journal of Service Research. He currently serves as a senior editor on the editorial board of Journal of the Association for Information Systems as well as a senior editor on the editorial board of Information Systems Journal.
