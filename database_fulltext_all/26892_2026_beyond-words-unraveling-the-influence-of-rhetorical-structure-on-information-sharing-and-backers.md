# Beyond Words: Unraveling the Influence of Rhetorical Structure on Information Sharing and Backers’ Support in Crowdfunding

Ehsan Ghasemi,<sup>1</sup> Alireza Sheikh-Zadeh,<sup>2</sup> Jaeki Song <sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Robert C. Vackar College of Business & Entrepreneurship, University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, USA, ehsan.ghasemi@utrgv.edu <sup>2</sup>Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, alireza.zadeh@ttu.edu <sup>3</sup>Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, jaeki.song@ttu.edu

## Abstract

Effective communication is critical in crowdfunding, where information asymmetry poses a significant challenge. While prior research has emphasized the persuasive power of rhetorica content, less is known about how the structure of rhetoric—beyond its content—shapes backers’ cognitive and behavioral responses. This study examines the role of hierarchical rhetoric in crowdfunding and its impact on actual funding outcomes, backers’ elaboration, perceived information sharing quality, and their willingness to fund. Drawing on computational linguistics and dual-process theories of information processing, we conducted a multiphase investigation that combines observational data from the Kickstarter platform with controlled experiments. Our findings show that the use of hierarchically structured rhetoric is positively associated with improved funding outcomes, longer information processing duration, higher perceived information sharing quality, and greater willingness to fund. We also found that these effects differ across project categories, suggesting that the effectiveness of rhetorical structure is context dependent. This study contributes to the literature on platform-mediated communication and crowdfunding by highlighting the cognitive and perceptual significance of rhetorical structure in shaping backer funding behavior and offering practical guidance for entrepreneurs seeking to optimize communication strategies.

Keywords: Hierarchical Rhetoric, Rhetorical Structure, Crowdfunding, Information Processing, Perceived Information Sharing Quality, Funding Outcomes, Willingness to Fund

## 1 Introduction

Crowdfunding, a form of platform-based online crowdsourcing, has become a popular and constantly growing capital-sourcing method for entrepreneurs worldwide. In the US, for instance, there were 1,398 crowdfunding organizations in 2023; the global crowdfunding market size was \$1.41 billion in the same year, and is projected to grow from \$1.60 billion in 2024 to \$4.50 billion by 2032.<sup>1</sup> Crowdfunding relies on digital platforms, data management, and communication tools. Despite the increased significance of crowdfunding, most projects still fail to secure funding. For instance, the success rate on Kickstarter, a globally known crowdfunding platform, hovers around 40% (Frimpong et al., 2025), with many other platforms performing even worse. <sup>2</sup> These failures limit entrepreneurs’ access to capital and constrain broader economic benefits, such as job creation and innovation (Du et al., 2022). This persistent challenge underscores the need to understand what drives crowdfunding outcomes.

One significant reason for the high failure rate on crowdfunding platforms lies in how project information is communicated. Unlike traditional fundraising environments, where formal due diligence mechanisms such as financial audits or business plan evaluations are available, crowdfunding projects rely heavily on persuasive project narratives to convey legitimacy and feasibility (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018; Taeuscher et al., 2021). In these contexts, project creators must effectively craft their narratives to reduce information asymmetry and establish credibility with potential backers. As a result, rhetorical strategies have become central to shaping backers’ perceptions and funding decisions (Anglin et al., 2018b; Raab, 2023).

Prior research has shown that rhetorical features of crowdfunding projects—such as persuasive storytelling, linguistic style, and emotional appeals—play a significant role in shaping funding outcomes (Frimpong et al., 2025; Lin & Boh, 2021; Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021a). These studies found that well-crafted rhetoric improves transparency, builds trust, and increases the likelihood of gaining support (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021b). While prior research has focused heavily on rhetorical content, relatively little attention has been given to the structure of information presentation. Consider two samples of project rhetoric, distinct in structure, adopted from two crowdfunding projects on Kickstarter—one raising funds for a documentary and the other for a music album:

Rhetoric 1: The Sweet Science will be a passion-driven project that tries to shine a deserving light once again on the sport of boxing. It will follow the professional and private life of a currently 100 super middleweight boxer from Colorado, represented by Ryan Roach (yes, THAT Roach). He’s a father of a newborn, a husband, and a future champion. We will look at the work he puts in at the gym and at home that will take him one step closer to his championship dreams.

Rhetoric 2: If you had told me a few years back that I would have an album out & now taking a chance on myself and seeing what Say Less can do nationally, I would’ve laughed. But I truly do believe God put me on this earth to help heal the land through my music. Music that makes you dance, music that makes you think, music that changes your mood when you’re down. I funded my first album by myself and that is/was a great accomplishment for me.

These two samples of rhetoric illustrate different structures shaped by using distinct groups of function words (also known as structure words). In Rhetoric 1, ideas are organized hierarchically, moving from the general theme of boxing to specific details about Ryan Roach, his professional and personal life, and the work that brings him closer to his championship dreams. This logical structure, shaped by the frequent use of articles and prepositions (words such as a/an, the, of, from, etc.), builds a clear mental model of the project and its goals (Boyd et al., 2020). In contrast, Rhetoric 2, with fewer articles and prepositions, is narrative and personal. It relies on the greater use of pronouns, adverbs, auxiliary verbs, and conjunctions (words such as myself, me, would, but, etc.) to emphasize the creator’s journey and experiences and to create an engaging story (Jordan et al., 2019). Instead of logically following a hierarchical structure and layering concepts, it presents concepts informally, making the rhetoric more personal.<sup>3</sup>

Existing literature on the role of rhetoric in crowdfunding project funding outcomes focuses mainly on the content itself rather than on how that content is structured. In particular, prior studies have mostly focused on content words—such as nouns, main verbs, and adjectives—that convey direct meaning and are commonly used to signal project merits (e.g., project characteristics, creator attributes, emotional tone) (Raab, 2023). In contrast, function words—such as articles, prepositions, and conjunctions—have received comparatively little attention despite their central role in shaping the structure of rhetoric and thus facilitating information sharing (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017). While function words carry little semantic weight on their own, they play an important role in organizing rhetorical content and shaping how audiences process and perceive information (Boyd et al., 2020; Jordan et al., 2019; Pennebaker et al., 2001). This represents a significant gap in the literature, as investigating rhetorical structure could offer insights into how potential backers process information and make funding decisions (Costello & Lee, 2022; Markowitz, 2023; Zhou et al., 2018). Moreover, the effectiveness of rhetorical structure likely varies depending on the project category (e.g., profit-driven vs. mission-driven), as this distinction provides a cognitive framework that shapes how backers interpret and respond to rhetorical cues (Taeuscher et al., 2022). Exploring this moderating role can offer actionable implications for aligning rhetorical structure with audience preferences across crowdfunding contexts.

The current study, situated within the framework of computational linguistics and informed by insights from dual-process theories of information processing, aims to address these gaps in the IS literature. More specifically, we examined the impact of rhetorical structure on information sharing and funding outcomes on crowdfunding platforms. Through a multiphase empirical design, we first conducted an observational study to examine whether hierarchical rhetoric influences actual funding outcomes and whether this effect is moderated by project category, establishing the external validity of hierarchical rhetoric’s significance in shaping actual backer behavior. Next, in two mechanism-focused studies, we investigated the influence of hierarchical rhetoric on backers’ information processing and perceptions, finding evidence of its positive influence on both. Finally, drawing on the findings from these studies, we conducted a confirmatory study to test how hierarchical rhetoric affects communication effectiveness across different project categories through its influence on backers’ information processing and perceptions, with a focus on backers’ willingness to fund—a theoretically grounded predictor of actual funding behavior (Ajzen & Kruglanski, 2019; Ciuchta et al., 2016).

This study contributes to the IS literature on platformmediated communication by highlighting the distinct role of rhetorical structure, as opposed to content, in shaping communication effectiveness on crowdfunding platforms. First, we offer a novel perspective on how rhetorical structure, particularly as shaped by the strategic use of function words, affects information sharing and, ultimately, funding outcomes. In particular, we found that hierarchical rhetoric not only leads backers to spend more time engaging with the project description, indicating higher elaboration, but also improves their perceptions of information sharing quality by improving the relevance of the content, supporting the formation of a clearer mental model of the project, and increasing the perceived informational richness. While prior research has primarily used rhetoric length as a proxy for information sharing in platform-mediated contexts (Isaak et al., 2021), we provide evidence that rhetoric structure—how ideas are organized and connected—also plays a significant role in user cognition, even when controlling for length.

Second, we find that the effect of hierarchical rhetoric on backers’ information processing and perceptions is more pronounced in profit-driven projects than in mission-driven ones, indicating that the type of project can shape how backers evaluate and interpret the information presented. These findings improve the understanding of cognitive-based dynamics of information sharing via rhetoric in these contexts. Third, our findings advance the ongoing scholarly debate about the value of rhetoric in securing support on crowdfunding platforms—a debate that has largely focused on rhetorical content—by demonstrating how rhetorical structure can also contribute to gaining support from backers (Anglin et al., 2018a; Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018). Finally, our study offers practical guidance for platform users and designers on tailoring rhetorical structure according to project category to improve communication effectiveness and funding outcomes. These findings have broader implications for other platform-based domains, such as e-commerce, real estate, and freelance marketplaces, where rhetorical strategies play a central persuasive role (Mavlanova et al., 2012).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by reviewing the existing literature, providing a theoretical background, and developing our hypotheses. Next, we describe the multiphase empirical design and present results from the observational, mechanismfocused, and confirmatory studies. We conclude with a discussion of the study’s contributions and implications.

## 2 Review of Existing Literature

Existing research on crowdfunding has investigated different types of information that project creators share with potential project backers to improve the chances of funding success (Mollick, 2014; Wang et al., 2021a). This includes information about project creators (e.g., experience, social capital, interaction with backers) as well as project characteristics (e.g., project category, project duration, funding goal) (Taeuscher et al., 2021). Rhetoric serves as a powerful means for effectively sharing information about project characteristics (Kim et al., 2016; Rossolini et al., 2021; Tajvarpour & Pujari, 2022b; Zhu, 2022) and creator characteristics (Anglin et al., 2018a).

Existing studies on rhetoric can be divided into three categories: those focusing on the affective content, those examining the rhetorical cues about project creators, and those analyzing rhetorical communication strategies (Raab, 2023). The first group explores rhetoric that appeals to backer affect by measuring the number of emotionally charged words (Raab, 2023), often finding a positive relationship between positive emotional cues and project performance (Koch & Siering, 2019), though some report inverted U-shaped effects or null results (Costello & Lee, 2022; Tafesse, 2021; Zhou et al., 2018). Evidence on the impact of negative emotional cues remains inconclusive (Raab, 2023). The second group of studies on rhetoric includes investigations of rhetorical cues related to the characteristics of project creators, such as hope, optimism, and resilience, etc. (Anglin et al., 2018a). Like the previous group, they emphasize rhetorical content over structure.

Studies in the third group (i.e., those focusing on rhetorical communication strategies) are more diverse than those in the previous two groups and have investigated factors such as the linguistic style (Lin & Boh, 2021), rhetorical concreteness and readability across project categories (Costello & Lee, 2022; Parhankangas & Renko, 2017; Zhou et al., 2018), cognitive and emotional styles; image-based rhetoric (Moradi & Badrinarayanan, 2021; Patel et al., 2021), and the effect of punctuation and rhetoric lengths on project outcomes (Moradi & Badrinarayanan, 2021), to name a few. Compared to the studies in the first two groups, which focus on the content of rhetoric, the third group includes a few studies that have investigated the structure and organization of rhetoric (Kim et al., 2016; Lin & Boh, 2021; Parhankangas & Renko, 2017; Zhu, 2022) (Appendix A summarizes these studies and the rhetorical strategies they investigated).

The literature on the relationship between rhetoric and funding outcomes on crowdfunding platforms reveals gaps that, if addressed, would increase our understanding of the role of rhetoric in information sharing and the funding outcomes of crowdfunding projects. First, most existing studies focus on words that convey meaning in rhetoric (e.g., verbs, nouns, adjectives), with fewer studies exploring words with low semantic weight that primarily shape the structure of rhetoric (e.g., prepositions, articles, conjunctions) (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017). While the content of rhetoric carries the overall substance of the communication, the structure of rhetoric plays an essential role in the organization of ideas and arguments (Chung & Pennebaker, 2007), and investigating it could provide insights into the cognitive processes of project backers (Markowitz, 2023).

Second, despite our knowledge of the importance of information sharing in platform-mediated communication contexts such as crowdfunding (Costello & Lee, 2022; Zhou et al., 2018), most existing research primarily examines funding success as the key outcome variable (Rossolini et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022); there is a lack of studies exploring the mechanism of how the rhetoric structure could lead to changes in backers’ cognitive processes, their perceptions of information sharing quality, and their funding behaviors. This gap exists despite our understanding of how the structure and organization of concepts and ideas in rhetoric can contribute to engagement, ease of understanding, and persuasion (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017; Zhou et al., 2018). Finally, while rhetoric has been found to influence the outcomes of crowdfunding projects, existing research has not sufficiently examined the role of crowdfunding project categories—such as profit-driven versus mission-driven—in the relationship between rhetorical structure and backers’ intentions and behaviors. Different project categories provide distinct cognitive frames that could influence how backers evaluate and respond to rhetorical styles (Taeuscher et al., 2022).

## 3 Theoretical Foundation and Hypothesis Development

This section discusses the psychological significance of rhetorical structure in crowdfunding and its influence on cognitive processing. Specifically, we discuss how rhetoric that is more hierarchically structured through function words improves information organization, improves cognitive engagement, and fosters deeper elaboration. We also discuss how this structured approach to rhetoric could affect backers’ perceptions of the quality of information sharing and, ultimately, their willingness to fund projects. Additionally, we consider how these effects may vary across different crowdfunding project categories.

## 3.1 Rhetoric, Language, and Persuasion

Rhetoric is the art, technique, and method of using symbols, primarily language, to persuade or influence others to change their attitudes, beliefs, values, or actions (Cheney et al., 2004). It involves the use of ethical (ethos), logical (logos), and emotional (pathos) appeals to shape the legitimacy of practices and to mobilize and sustain support from an audience. Rhetoric has a humanistic tradition and is fundamentally rooted in the study of persuasion. The formal study of rhetoric was developed by the ancient Greeks and Romans, with contributions from philosophers such as Aristotle, Plato, and Cicero (Cheney & McMillan, 1990). While the status of rhetoric declined during the Renaissance, by the mid-20th century, its application had broadened to fields such as sociology, economics, and physics, highlighting the persuasive power of symbols (Cheney & McMillan, 1990).

Language and rhetoric are closely intertwined, with language serving as the primary medium through which rhetorical strategies are implemented. Language operates as a system of symbols, where each element represents and communicates specific ideas or concepts. The use of language is inherently rhetorical: It is through language that persuasive messages are crafted, presented, and received. At the broader community level, language and rhetoric can reflect and shape cultural norms and play a crucial role in public discourse and societal evolution (Kirby, 1992).

Persuasion through rhetoric is a complex phenomenon that involves three main components: source, message, and audience (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). These correspond to the classical rhetorical appeals—ethos, logos, and pathos (Cheney et al., 2004). Ethos refers to the credibility and trustworthiness of the writer or speaker. Logos refers to the logical appeal in rhetorical arguments, including both content and the way information is structured and presented. Pathos appeals to the audience’s emotions to influence their attitudes or behaviors. Together, these rhetorical appeals represent how language can influence an audience and legitimize the target action (Erkama & Vaara, 2010).

Rhetoric is, by nature, dynamic and involves the interplay among text (the content), context (the environment in which communication occurs), and rhetorical strategies (Kirby, 1992). Rhetoric differs from mere text in its strategic use of language designed explicitly to persuade within particular contexts. While text consists of specific words and phrases, context and rhetorical strategies significantly affect the effectiveness of persuasion. In the following, we discuss hierarchical rhetoric—a strategic approach to information sharing— especially in crowdfunding platforms, where conveying detailed project information clearly and concisely within limited space is essential (Bi et al., 2017).

## 3.2 Hierarchical Rhetoric

Hierarchical rhetoric refers to a structured approach to organizing information in a logical, layered progression, facilitating information sharing by ensuring clarity and coherence. Hierarchically structured rhetoric enables the message sender to break down complex or novel ideas into simpler, interconnected components, thereby improving the effectiveness and clarity of communication. This structure can also help the recipient develop mental models of interconnected concepts that facilitate comprehension (Markowitz, 2023; Jordan et al., 2019). In contrast to the loosely connected or narrative nature of nonhierarchical rhetoric, hierarchical rhetoric imposes a structured flow that aids clarity and comprehension. Predominantly used in formal contexts, hierarchical rhetoric conveys a formal tone through its reliance on linguistic markers that signal structured organization (Jordan et al., 2019; Markowitz, 2023; Pennebaker et al., 2001).

A key feature of hierarchical rhetoric is its frequent use of two groups of function words, including articles and prepositions (Jordan et al., 2019). These word groups help shape the structure and organization of concepts within hierarchical rhetoric (Chung & Pennebaker, 2007).

Including articles reinforces the concepts’ hierarchy by distinguishing between general and more specific ideas. Articles define nouns as specific or general, distinguishing between overarching categories and individual elements within those categories. The use of the definite article the signals specificity and continuity (i.e., previously mentioned or unique concepts), whereas the indefinite articles a and an introduce new concepts within the rhetorical structure. This distinction enables a layered, hierarchical presentation of ideas, where each new point builds upon the previous one. For instance, in rhetoric, adding the before a noun can signal an intended concept and isolate it from others.<sup>5</sup> Moreover, using articles can present concepts in a logical sequence (e.g., the first … , the second … , etc.), where each element builds upon the previous one. This feature also facilitates the transition from one idea to the next or from a new concept (preceded by a) to the details of this concept (preceded by the). Articles help maintain logical flow and guide the audience to follow the progression from general to specific concepts. For instance, shifting from a to the can lead readers from the introduction of a new concept to a more detailed discussion of that same concept.

Prepositions, the second group of function words whose frequency is positively associated with hierarchical rhetoric, help clarify the relationship between different concepts. A preposition (e.g., to, at, on, of, etc.) is defined as a word used before a noun or a pronoun to show its connection with another lexical item in the same sentence (Yates, 1999). <sup>4</sup> Prepositions situate concepts within specific contexts (e.g., in, on, under), show the boundaries of concepts (e.g., between, among, through), specify how details are related to each other as well as to the overall topic in the rhetoric (due to, because of, beyond), and show the dependency of concepts (e.g., under new management).

Concepts form the building blocks of our mental representations of entities, attributes, or phenomena, helping us categorize and organize information in meaningful ways (Goguen, 1969). Concept maps (also known as conceptual maps) are graphical tools that depict the relationships and hierarchies between concepts, facilitating understanding and analysis of the structure of knowledge. These maps typically consist of nodes representing concepts and links representing relationships between them, forming networks that illustrate how various concepts are interconnected.

## 3.3 Hierarchical Rhetoric and Information Processing

The structure of rhetoric is psychologically significant and can impact the audience’s internal processing, including their thoughts and feelings (Boyd & Schwartz, 2021). For a long time, dual-process models have been used to explain how people think and process information (Markowitz, 2023; Petty & Briñol, 2011). Based on the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), a dual-process theory of information processing, when people engage in some form of communication, they can process this communication at varying levels of elaboration (i.e., thought), ranging from low to high (Petty & Briñol, 2011). People who engage with high levels of elaboration are more likely to process information via a central route. The central route requires greater cognitive effort and involves deeply processing the content of a message, focusing on its logic and the quality of its arguments (Lin & Boh, 2021). In contrast, the peripheral route requires less cognitive effort and involves evaluating messages based on surface-level cues rather than the quality of the arguments (Petty & Briñol, 2011).

One way to facilitate deliberate and systematic thinking in communication is through the use of hierarchical structure in rhetoric, which can be linked to verbal analytical thinking (Markowitz, 2023). <sup>5</sup> This structure aligns closely with the concept of the high degree of thought and the central route of information processing (Petty et al., 1986), as it involves a deliberate, structured approach to organizing information (Evans, 2003).

Hierarchically structured rhetoric enables project creators to present information in a clear, logical progression, improving backers’ cognitive engagement and encouraging them to invest more time in reading and processing the conveyed information (Mun et al., 2013; Pancer et al., 2019). In hierarchical rhetoric, the writer— i.e., the project creator in crowdfunding—first introduces key concepts, followed by a structured hierarchy of details that moves from general ideas to specific elements (Pennebaker et al., 2001) (Figure 1). Function words, such as articles (a, an, the), help define and structure these concepts, while prepositions (e.g., on, in, to) establish relationships between them, mirroring the way people process and articulate complex problems (Jordan et al., 2019; Pennebaker et al., 2001).

Also, hierarchical rhetoric, through its structured presentation of concepts using articles and prepositions, improves backers’ elaboration by appealing to their reasoning (i.e., logos) (Markowitz, 2023). Compared to nonhierarchical rhetoric, hierarchical rhetoric incorporates a higher frequency of articles—function words that precede nouns and other content words, shaping the meaning and structure of rhetoric. The increased use of articles fosters deeper cognitive elaboration, requiring backers to engage more actively with rhetoric to interpret the concepts that follow, such as nouns (e.g., a pledge of \$50 or more), adjectives (e.g., an advanced solar panel system), gerunds (e.g., the designing of our product), and ordinal numbers (e.g., the first batch) (Markowitz, 2023; Markowitz & Griffin, 2020). Similarly, the frequent use of prepositions—function words that connect nouns, pronouns, or phrases to other words within a sentence—plays a significant role in the wellstructured organization of concepts within hierarchical rhetoric. This conceptually rich structure increases the informational density of the message, which, in turn, requires more time and cognitive effort to process and integrate, leading to longer comprehension durations (Markowitz, 2023; Markowitz & Griffin, 2020).

Backers’ information processing duration is a primary characteristic of information sharing in project rhetoric, indicating the cognitive resources that backers are allocating to understanding the project (Dennis et al., 2008; Downs & Mohr, 1976; Petty et al., 2009). Backers’ engagement with hierarchical rhetoric can be further understood through the lens of cognitive load theory, which complements the ELM by explaining how the structure of information affects the distribution of cognitive effort during processing (Paas & Sweller, 2012; Sweller et al., 1998). When reading the project rhetoric, backers must integrate multiple interrelated concepts into a coherent mental model of the project. Hierarchical rhetoric can facilitate this by increasing germane and intrinsic cognitive loads, both of which require deliberate effort, while minimizing extraneous load associated with poor organization or ambiguity (Sweller, 2010). As a result, we expect hierarchical rhetoric to lead backers to spend more time reading and processing project descriptions. This extended processing duration reflects purposeful engagement with well-structured, information-rich content (Klepsch et al., 2017; Paas et al., 2003).

H1: Hierarchical rhetoric is positively associated with backers’ information processing duration.

## 3.4 Hierarchical Rhetoric and Backers’ Perception of Information Sharing Quality

We argued in Hypothesis 1 that hierarchical rhetoric can lead backers to engage in longer information processing, primarily due to the cognitive load required to construct a mental model of the subject matter. This extended engagement results from the information-rich and conceptually dense structure of hierarchical rhetoric. It signals cognitive involvement with richly structured content, which can also improve backers’ perceptions of information sharing quality. Perceived information sharing quality represents the secondary characteristic of information sharing in crowdfunding projects’ rhetoric (Downs & Mohr, 1976; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). <sup>6</sup> We define perceived information sharing quality as backers’ subjective assessments about how effectively crowdfunding projects’ rhetoric communicates key information, facilitates the construction and refinement of a mental representation of a project, and demands cognitive effort to process and comprehend it (Dennis et al., 2008).

Hierarchical rhetoric can positively impact backer perception by improving the relevance of information conveyed (Le et al., 2020). When rhetoric is organized in a way that builds upon prior information, where each section provides necessary context for understanding the next, backers can effectively identify and absorb key details about the project (see Appendix B). Moreover, hierarchical rhetoric minimizes unnecessary repetition and irrelevant details, such as trivial personal anecdotes or tag questions. Given that information relevance is fundamental to information sharing, crowdfunding projects employing hierarchical rhetoric are likely to be perceived as higher quality by backers (Boyd et al., 2020; Dennis & Kinney, 1998).

Beyond enhancing informational relevance, hierarchical rhetoric can also improve how project information is presented, specifically by making it clearer, more logically structured, and more transparent (Pancer et al., 2019). By structuring information logically and illustrating relationships between concepts, hierarchical rhetoric in projects can reduce backers’ wasteful (extraneous) effort and redirect cognitive resources toward meaningful (germane) processing (Mun et al., 2013). This structure makes the project’s objectives more transparent, fostering a greater sense of trust (Miranda & Saunders, 2003; Pancer et al., 2019). Unlike simple or loosely structured narratives that may encourage shallow processing, hierarchically structured rhetoric presents project information in a way that necessitates a more analytical approach (Miranda & Saunders, 2003). This cognitive effort, while requiring more time and mental resources, could lead to backers’ more positive judgments about the depth and quality of a project’s information sharing.

Hierarchical rhetoric can positively impact backers’ perceptions by facilitating the construction of a mental model of the subject matter. Backers need to develop accurate mental representations of a project’s characteristics (e.g., purpose, features, feasibility, and impact) before making funding decisions. Hierarchical rhetoric can significantly facilitate this cognitive process by systematically breaking down communicated information into smaller, interrelated components. This would allow backers to process information logically and sequentially (Lin & Boh, 2021). Prior research suggests that individuals construct mental models by integrating new information with their existing knowledge structures, which enables them to develop a coherent understanding of a given topic (Mathieu et al., 2005). When a project description is organized hierarchically, each part contributes to a growing understanding of the overall project and the ways different elements fit together. This structured approach contrasts with the loosely connected presentation of concepts that hinders backers’ abilities to form clear mental pictures (Dennis et al., 1998). A welldeveloped mental model improves backers’ perceptions of the project’s information sharing quality.

Together, these structural and cognitive features shape backers’ perceived information sharing quality. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2: Hierarchical rhetoric is positively associated with backers’ perceived information sharing quality.

## 3.5 Rhetoric Structure and Backer Willingness to Fund

Rhetoric plays a significant role in helping project creators gain support in crowdfunding environments, where traditional legitimacy signals are often lacking (Taeuscher et al., 2021). <sup>7</sup> For rhetoric to effectively influence backers’ decisions, it must be understandable (Spence, 1978; Wang et al., 2022). Hierarchical rhetoric supports this by structuring information through concrete and detailed descriptions and a more informative linguistic style (Chung & Pennebaker, 2007; Parhankangas & Renko, 2017). It can simplify complex ideas into more coherent and understandable concepts (Jordan et al., 2019) and make them more understandable to the audience (Wang et al., 2022).

Moreover, hierarchical rhetoric signals professionalism and logical thinking on the part of the creator, which can positively affect perceptions of competence and trustworthiness (Wang et al., 2022). Because crowdfunding decisions often involve weighing risks and rewards, a clear, logically structured project description enables backers to engage more easily in cost-benefit analyses (Khan et al., 2017). This alignment between rhetorical structure and rational decisionmaking preferences makes hierarchical rhetoric effective in increasing the likelihood that backers will support a project. Therefore, we expect a positive relationship between the use of hierarchical rhetoric and backers’ willingness to fund.

Based on our arguments for H1 and H2, we also anticipate that information processing duration and perceived information sharing quality partially mediate this relationship. These constructs represent distinct cognitive and perceptual mechanisms through which hierarchical rhetoric influences backers’ willingness to fund. Hierarchically structured rhetoric increases the cognitive elaboration required to process project information, leading to deeper engagement and more time spent reading. According to dual-process theories such as the ELM (Petty & Briñol, 2011), such systematic processing fosters attitude certainty, or confidence that one’s evaluation is well-founded. This decision confidence makes backers more likely to act on their judgments. While attitude certainty can reinforce either positive or negative evaluations, we expect that deep engagement with well-structured rhetoric is more likely to strengthen favorable assessments, as it promotes clarity, reduces uncertainty, and aligns with backers’ preferences for rational decision-making.

Hierarchical rhetoric also shapes perceptions of information sharing quality by presenting content in a clear, logically structured, and relevant manner. This makes backers more likely to view the project as credible, which reduces uncertainty and increases support intentions. We therefore anticipate that both information processing duration and perceived information sharing quality serve as mediators explaining how hierarchical rhetoric can influence willingness to fund.

H3: Hierarchically structured rhetoric has a positive relationship (both directly and indirectly through information processing duration and perceived information sharing quality) with backers’ willingness to fund crowdfunding projects.

People tend to associate effort with value (e.g., IKEA effect, effort justification, sunk cost fallacy) (Harmon‐Jones et al., 2024; Norton et al., 2012). In the context of crowdfunding, the increased processing time induced by hierarchical rhetoric is likely to be interpreted by backers as a signal of substantive, high-quality information sharing. Rather than viewing this extended engagement as a cognitive burden, backers may infer that their own efforts reflect the value of the content. This perception is further reinforced by the belief that the project creator has likewise invested considerable effort in organizing and communicating the information. Through this effort-justification process, longer information processing duration improves backers’ perceived quality of the information, which in turn, strengthens their willingness to fund the project (Norton et al., 2012).

Prolonged cognitive engagement with hierarchical rhetoric can foster a sense of intellectual satisfaction and reinforce trust in the information presented. Its structured format presents a logical sequence of concepts, enabling backers to process project information more deliberately and thoroughly. Compared to informally structured narratives, hierarchical rhetoric demands deeper analysis (Markowitz, 2023). This form of structured cognitive processing supports the construction of a comprehensive mental model of the crowdfunding project, making backers more likely to form favorable evaluations and act on them. When backers spend more time processing hierarchically structured content, they are more likely to interpret the rhetoric as conveying relevant information, supporting comprehension, and inviting deliberate cognitive effort, key dimensions of perceived information sharing quality. In this way, increased processing duration can contribute to backers willingness to fund by improving the perceived informativeness of the rhetoric and the credibility of the project creator (Petty & Briñol, 2011).

Accordingly, we anticipate that perceived information sharing quality will mediate the relationship between information processing duration and willingness to fund, as backers rely on their perceptions of information sharing quality to justify their funding decisions.

H4: Information processing duration resulting from hierarchical rhetoric positively influences backers willingness to fund through perceived information sharing quality.

## 3.6 Rhetoric Structure and Project Category

Project categories shape backers’ perceptions and evaluations in crowdfunding by providing a cognitive framework for understanding and assessing projects (Taeuscher et al., 2022). Categories serve as “a cognitive infrastructure that allows audiences to make sense of and evaluate otherwise unknown offerings” (Taeuscher et al., 2022, pp. 2102-2103). They can impact backer perceptions and evaluations through mechanisms such as filtering and anchoring, and for best results, project creators should conform to the conventions of their product/service category (Zhao et al., 2017). Broadly, crowdfunding literature distinguishes between commercial, profit-driven projects and noncommercial, mission-driven ones. Commercial projects focus on creating products or services to generate revenue, while noncommercial projects aim to make a social impact without a profit motive (Mollick, 2014; Scheaf et al., 2018). Despite these motivational differences, acquiring financial support is considered a measure of success for both categories.

The level of institutionalization in crowdfunding project categories influences the rhetorical strategies that project creators must adopt to communicate with backers. The commercial category (i.e., technology projects) is wellestablished and has become highly institutionalized (Mollick, 2014). <sup>8</sup> Since projects in this category are typically based on scientific innovation and primarily focus on profitability and functionality, their rhetoric often includes more jargon, technical specifics, and complex information. These projects also target informed backers who are particularly interested in the technical aspects of the offered product or service. In such institutionalized contexts, structured rhetoric, such as hierarchical rhetoric, is likely to be more persuasive because it aligns with backers’ expectations for detail and professionalism, facilitates cost-benefit analysis, and supports calculated decision-making (Bi et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2017). Conversely, in less institutionalized categories (e.g., art projects), hierarchical rhetoric may be less effective for audiences that expect more inclusive or emotionally resonant communication. Because of these institutional differences, the project category is likely to moderate the effectiveness of hierarchical rhetoric.

These motivational orientations can also influence the depth and style of information processing. Individuals who are motivated, due to factors such as personal relevance, interest, or anticipated accountability, tend to engage in more effortful and systematic evaluation of available information (Petty & Briñol, 2011). In crowdfunding, commercial backers often perceive funding decisions as financially significant, which increases the relevance of project details and encourages deeper engagement with project rhetoric. Hierarchical rhetoric supports this by presenting information in a logically structured way that facilitates analytical evaluation. In contrast, noncommercial backers, typically driven by emotional motives, are more likely to rely on peripheral cues. For these backers, the cognitive demands of hierarchical rhetoric may not align with their motivational orientation, making it less persuasive in noncommercial contexts. As a result, hierarchical rhetoric is likely to be more persuasive in commercial projects than in noncommercial ones, where the mismatch between rhetorical structure and motivational orientation may reduce its effectiveness.

Consistent with this distinction, emotional appeal plays a key role in the success of noncommercial projects, which often depend on backers’ willingness to support initiatives that may not offer a tangible return (Gleasure & Feller, 2016). Rhetoric in this category should evoke relatability among potential backers (Bi et al., 2017). Less hierarchically structured rhetoric naturally improves emotional connection, as it incorporates more pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, negations, and auxiliary verbs—function words that make the rhetoric more personal, more relational, and less analytical (Jordan et al., 2019; Markowitz, 2023). Thus, we expect a stronger positive relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and backers’ willingness to fund commercial vs. noncommercial projects.

H5: Hierarchical rhetoric has a stronger positive relationship with the willingness to fund commercial projects vs noncommercial projects.

We recognize that the effect of hierarchical rhetoric on the mediators (i.e., information processing duration and perceived information sharing quality) may vary across different project types. Specifically, commercial projects may rely more on hierarchical rhetoric to improve backers’ perceptions of legitimacy, leading to longer information processing duration and a higher perceived quality of information sharing. In contrast, noncommercial projects, which tend to be more affect driven, may not benefit as much from hierarchically structured rhetoric, reducing its influence on information processing duration and perceived quality of information sharing. Given these theoretical considerations, we examined whether the project category moderated these effects in our analysis. However, since our primary focus was on the influence of hierarchical rhetoric on willingness to fund, we treated this as an exploratory analysis rather than formulating separate hypotheses. Figure 2 summarizes our research model.

In summary, the proposed model suggests that hierarchical rhetoric influences the willingness to fund through multiple, complementary pathways associated with systematic processing. These include the depth of engagement with the message (via information processing duration) and the evaluation of informational value (via perceived information sharing quality). The direct path from hierarchical rhetoric to willingness to fund is interpreted as reflecting additional deliberative effects that are not fully captured by the mediators.

## 4 Methodology

Our study was conducted in multiple phases, beginning with an observational study, followed by two mechanism-focused studies, and concluding with a confirmatory study (Figure 3). In the initial phase, we conducted an observational study to establish an empirical relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and funding outcomes using data from a crowdfunding platform. However, while observational studies identify correlations, they do not establish causality.

Therefore, in the intermediate phase, following the observational study, we conducted two mechanismbased studies to explore the cognitive and perceptual processes influencing backers’ decision-making. In these studies, we examined how hierarchical rhetoric affects backers’ information processing duration (a primary characteristic of information sharing via rhetoric) and their perceptions of the quality of information sharing (a secondary characteristic of information sharing via rhetoric) (Gardhouse & Anderson, 2013; Moore & Benbasat, 1991) (see Appendix C for the definitions of core constructs in our research model). These studies provided valuable insights into how and why hierarchical rhetoric may influence backers’ funding behaviors, but they did not fully map the entire pathway of its effects.

## 4.1 Observational Study: Hierarchical Rhetoric and Funding Outcomes in Crowdfunding

In this initial phase, we examined whether hierarchical rhetoric is associated with actual funding outcomes and whether project categories influence this relationship. Using secondary data from a known crowdfunding platform, we provided empirical evidence that hierarchical rhetoric correlates with better funding outcomes and project categories modify this relationship, suggesting that rhetorical structure significantly predicts backers’ funding behavior.<sup>9</sup> Investigating this effect on a real crowdfunding platform helped to validate our assumptions and set the stage for our subsequent mechanism studies.

## 4.1.1 Data

We collected project rhetoric directly from the Kickstarter website in a two-stage process. First, using a scraper robot, we extracted monthly data for US-based projects launched in 2020 to ensure consistency with English language use. To avoid duplicates and live listings, we curated unique project URLs. Then, a secondary program gathered detailed data from these URLs, focusing on projects with “successful” or “failed” statuses. This resulted in data on 10,527 completed projects. <sup>10</sup>

## 4.1.2 Dependent Variable

We used the dollar amount raised (i.e., “funds pledged”) to represent crowdfunding project funding outcomes (Scheaf et al., 2018). Using the continuous measure of funds pledged allowed us to capture the full range of project outcomes and examine the extent of support. In the crowdfunding context, a small number of projects collect disproportionately large sums of money, leading to a highly right-skewed distribution of funds raised (Short & Anglin, 2019). To address this right-skewness in our data, we applied a logarithmic transformation to our dependent variables. This transformation helped linearize relationships and stabilize variance in the residuals, thereby improving the efficiency of our linear regression models.

## 4.1.3 Independent and Control Variables

Hierarchical rhetoric, project category, and their interactions served as our independent variables in this study. We measured hierarchical structure by computing the proportion of function words reflecting this structure using the LIWC software package (Appendix B). LIWC is a text analysis tool that provides insights into the psychological states of the language user based on the frequency of words in different linguistic categories (Pennebaker et al., 2001).<sup>11</sup>

To account for differences in project types, we categorized the 15 project types in our sample into commercial and noncommercial projects.<sup>12</sup> In line with existing literature, we considered the technology, games, and design categories representing commercial projects, with the rest classified as noncommercial projects (Scheaf et al., 2018). These three categories have the characteristics of traditional fundraising with commercial purposes: They usually deliver concrete products as rewards, they are profit-driven, and the platform requires them to provide a manufacturing plan and delivery date for rewards (Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014). We used a binary variable to differentiate them and created an interaction term to analyze how project categories relate to hierarchical rhetoric.

We included a set of rhetoric-related control variables to represent rhetorical legitimation dynamics associated with ethos (the source of rhetoric) and pathos (the audience) (Cheney et al., 2004). This set included PsyCap (positive psychological capital), a second-order construct that captured the shared variance of four first-order constructs: the project creator’s hope, optimism, resilience, and confidence (Anglin et al., 2018a). Additionally, we included scores for positive and negative emotions. These variables represented rhetorical legitimation strategies linked to ethos (appealing to the credibility of the project owner) and pathos (appealing to the audience’s emotional dynamic) (Pennebaker et al., 2001).

We also included another set of control variables that have been identified in previous studies as influential in funding outcomes (Frimpong et al., 2025). These variables included rhetoric length; funding goal, representing the funding goal of the project; project backed, indicating the number of projects a creator has backed; project created, signifying the number of projects a creator has created before and representing the project creator’s experience; project duration, measuring how long the project is available to be funded; comments count, accounting for the number of comments from backers and creators; number of rewards; and average reward price, representing the average price of all rewards.

## 4.2 Mechanism Study 1: Hierarchical Rhetoric and Information Processing

In the first part of this intermediate phase, we examined whether more hierarchically structured rhetoric leads to a longer information processing duration for backers (testing H1). The time backers invest in processing information conveyed through project rhetoric, which served as an objective variable, is a key characteristic of the information sharing process (Downs & Mohr, 1976; Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Paas & Sweller, 2012). Backers’ time investment may also influence their judgment of the quality of information conveyed through rhetoric as well as their subsequent propensity to fund (Harmon‐Jones et al., 2024; Norton et al., 2012). Here, through a main experiment and a robustness check experiment, we rigorously investigated the nature and significance of this relationship.

## 4.2.1 Sample

Our sample in this phase included 500 project rhetoric samples, randomly sampled from the projects launched on Kickstarter in 2020. We limited our sampling of project rhetoric samples to data points between Q1 (first q artile) − 1.5 Q (interq artile range), and Q3 (third quartile) + 1.5 IQR to exclude too-long or too-short samples of rhetoric and to avoid participant fatigue. We checked for reading difficulty using the Flesch-Kincaid (F-K) readability test. This test is based on a formula that considers word and sentence lengths (236.835 − 1.015 $\begin{array} { r } { \left( \frac { T o t a l W o r d s } { T o t a l S e n t e n c e s } \right) - 8 4 . 6 ( \frac { T o t a l S y l l a \mathbf { \bar { b } } e l s } { T o t a l W o r d s } ) ) } \end{array}$ . A lower score on this test represents lower readability. The standard readability score is 70.0-60.0, considered plain English. The average readability of all rhetoric in our project pool was 49.93. The median was 53.65, falling within the fairly difficult (60-50) and difficult (50-30) ranges. To ensure that our experimental rhetoric samples reflected this central tendency, we limited our sample of 500 rhetoric samples to those with F-K scores between 40 and 59. This range was selected based on visual inspection of the readability distribution and the descriptive statistics of the full dataset (N = 10,527) to exclude extreme outliers while covering both the fairly difficult and difficult categories.

## 4.2.2 Dependent Variable

We measured information processing duration using standardized silent reading time, which reflects the time taken to read and comprehend a text silently. Processing persuasive messages, such as rhetoric, involves cognitive effort, which inherently requires more time (Cooper-Martin, 1994; Petty et al., 1986). In dual-process models such as the ELM, central route processing involves careful evaluation and elaboration of message content, making reading time a relevant indicator of processing depth. Empirical research supports this link, showing that longer reading durations correspond to increased cognitive effort and deeper elaboration (Strayer et al., 2019). Theories of communication in IS literature also highlight that individuals engaging in communication need time to analyze information, interpret it, and form mental models of situations communicated (Dennis et al.,

2008; Robert et al., 2018). This aligns with cognitive mechanisms like attention, engagement, deliberation, and comprehension—all critical for deep information processing (Rayner et al., 2016; Vieira, 2016). This interpretation is further supported by evidence showing that reading time systematically increases with conceptual richness and that slower, self-regulated pacing during reading reflects greater cognitive engagement and processing depth (Coll-Florit & Gennari, 2011; Dyson & Haselgrove, 2001). Accordingly, the time people spend comprehending rhetoric is a relevant objective measure of their information processing.

## 4.2.3 Independent Variables

Like the first study, hierarchical structure in rhetoric was the independent variable in this study. We measured it using the LIWC software package (Pennebaker et al., 2001).

## 4.2.4 Procedures

We hired two students from the College of Media and Communication at a large public university in Texas to read 250 randomly assigned project rhetoric samples each and record their silent reading comprehension times in seconds. The participants received training, including an introduction to the study, the definition of silent reading, and an example of how to record the silent reading time for each project rhetoric. The project rhetoric samples were placed in a spreadsheet along with a column to record the silent reading time. In addition, we paid our participants on an hourly basis to dissuade them from reading faster than their normal reading speeds. To account for the different reading speeds of our participants, after measuring reading times in seconds, we conducted within-reader standardization of silent reading times.

## 4.2.5 Results

The average word count in our sample was 271.19 (SD = 109.735). On average, adults read 238 words per minute during silent reading; therefore, we were confident that the sampled project descriptions would not fatigue our readers.<sup>14</sup> Table 2 represents descriptive statistics of the variables of interest.

We calculated bivariate correlations between the hierarchical structure score and silent reading time. The correlation between these two variables was moderate to large (r = 0.465) and statistically significant (p < 0.001), and the hierarchical structure explained about 22% of the variance in the standardized silent reading time. These results show that hierarchical rhetoric is associated with longer information processing duration, providing initial support for H1.

## 4.2.6 Robustness Check

In the previous section, we opted for a relatively large, randomly selected sample of rhetorical texts and adopted a naturalistic approach in our experiment by asking participants to record their own silent reading times, primarily to improve the internal validity and ecological validity of our findings. However, the involvement of only two participants raises questions about the accuracy of silent reading time recordings and whether participant characteristics influenced the results. In addition, although we controlled for the complexity of the rhetoric samples used in the main experiment, we assumed—based on theoretical justifications—that spending more time reading hierarchical rhetoric reflects better elaboration rather than poor presentation of concepts in the rhetoric (Leppink et al., 2013; Paas & Sweller, 2012). To further ensure the robustness of our findings against alternative explanations, we conducted two additional controlled experiments using groups of participants on the Prolific platform. We recorded silent reading times in the background and analyzed variance in silent reading times both between and within groups; we also included the potential impact of the ineffective presentation of concepts in the rhetoric as our control variables (see Appendix D for details of these experiments). The results of these experiments further supported the positive relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and information processing duration and revealed that the longer information processing duration of high-hierarchical rhetoric was not the result of poor presentation of concepts.

Overall, these findings support H1 and substantiate the argument that longer information processing duration reflects backers’ deeper elaboration of the hierarchical rhetoric used in crowdfunding projects. We designed the main analysis and the robustness check to serve complementary purposes. The main analysis prioritized a diverse set of project rhetoric samples to better reflect real-world variation, using a naturalistic procedure with two trained readers. In contrast, the robustness check introduced tighter experimental control through background-tracked reading times and additional controls. Together, these approaches strengthen the validity and generalizability of our findings.

## 4.3 Mechanism Study 2: Hierarchical Rhetoric and Perceived Information Sharing Quality

In the second part of the intermediate phase, we examined whether hierarchical rhetoric improves backers’ perceived quality of information-sharing by project creators (testing H2). As a subjective variable, perceived information-sharing quality represents a secondary characteristic of the information-sharing process (Downs & Mohr, 1976; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). This variable could play a significant role in shaping backers’ perceptions and decision-making as they evaluate whether the informational rhetoric is clear, reliable, and persuasive (Nicholas & Brookshire, 1993). In this study, we first rigorously developed a scale to measure perceived information sharing quality and then investigated this relationship via a controlled experiment.

## 4.3.1 Sample

We performed an a priori power analysis using G\*Power 3 to determine the sample size required to have a medium effect size (d = 0.5), 0.95 power, and ?? error probability of 0.001 for a matched-pair t-test. It suggested a total sample size of 103. We recruited 125 participants from the Prolific platform to conduct this study.

## 4.3.2 Dependent Variable

The outcome variable in this study is perceived information sharing quality. To our knowledge, no existing scale measures how backers perceive the quality of information sharing via rhetoric in crowdfunding. In IS literature, information quality is primarily studied under the construct of perceived information quality (PIQ) (Nicolaou & McKnight, 2006). PIQ is a broad construct that assesses the information provided by information systems, focusing on standards crucial for trust building and decisions related to system adoption. However, for our study in the crowdfunding context, we required a more specific measure that effectively evaluates how information communicated through rhetoric is perceived. Additionally, PIQ primarily gauges the quality of information from the perspective of IS users. In contrast, our research necessitates a scale that measures the adequacy and impact of information sharing via rhetoric from the backers’ perspective. Unlike PIQ, which examines the inherent quality of information, perceived information sharing quality should emphasize the effectiveness of both information transmission and reception in communication. Therefore, we needed to develop a new scale to measure the perceived information sharing quality that is specifically suited to the crowdfunding context.

We used prior literature on information sharing as a conceptual guide during the item development process. This body of work helped us define the construct’s scope and informed the generation of scale items across relevant dimensions.

We followed a comprehensive three-step process (i.e., item creation, scale development, and scale testing) recommended in IS literature to develop this scale (Lewis et al., 2005; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The developed scale had 16 items belonging to three content areas, including perceptions of transmission of relevant information, perceptions of transmission of information that help form a mental model of the situation, and perceptions of cognitive demand, all of which were measured on a 7-point Likert scale (Dennis et al., 2008; Miranda & Saunders, 2003; Robert et al., 2008, 2018). Appendix E includes detailed information on the conceptualization and operationalization of this construct and the scale development processes.

## 4.3.3 Independent and Control Variables

Like in the previous studies, hierarchical rhetoric served as our independent variable. To maintain ecological validity, we used two samples of rhetoric adapted from a real crowdfunding project for our experiment: one with a high-hierarchical score (97.78) and another with a low-hierarchical score (34.14), both having the same length (151 words) . We kept the core information the same across the two samples of rhetoric but changed the contents associated with the hierarchical structure (See Appendix F).<sup>15</sup> In a separate experiment, we compared the two samples of rhetoric in terms of their hierarchical structure; the results confirmed a significant difference between the two (t (177) = 2.16, p < 0.05).<sup>16</sup> Control variables included attention to the project descriptions, familiarity with crowdfunding, interest in the project pitched, confidence in understanding crowdfunding, investment experience, number of projects viewed in the past, and number of projects supported in the past (Allison et al., 2017; Ciuchta et al., 2016).

## 4.3.4 Procedure

We conducted a within-subject design experiment on the Prolific platform. Participants underwent a screening process and provided informed consent before voluntarily participating in our study. The screening ensured that participants were familiar with crowdfunding and were native English speakers. Participants were presented with two samples of rhetoric—one high-hierarchical and one lowhierarchical—in a counterbalanced order to control for potential order effects. <sup>17</sup> They rated their agreement with the items of the perceived information sharing quality scale on a 7-point Likert scale. After that, they answered two comprehension questions, followed by questions related to control variables and demographics.

## 4.3.5 Results

Of 125 participants, three were excluded from our data because they answered both comprehension questions incorrectly. The remaining 122 participants (male = 77, female = 44, other = 1) demonstrated attention during the experiment (M = 6.61); their familiarity with crowdfunding (M = 5.28), confidence in understanding crowdfunding (M = 5.56), experience in crowdfunding (campaigns viewed M = 16.30; campaigns funded M = 2.65; general investment experience M = 3.48), and interest in the projects (M = 4.33) were all at reasonable levels (all out of 7).

79% of participants were between 25 and 44 years old, and the rest were older. The perceived information sharing quality scale revealed acceptable psychometric properties (see Appendix F). We conducted a paired samples t-test to compare the participants’ perceived information sharing quality in the high- vs. lowhierarchical rhetoric samples. The results revealed a statistically significant difference between the two rhetoric conditions (i.e., low- and high-hierarchical rhetoric), providing support for the argument that hierarchical rhetoric is positively associated with perceived information sharing quality, M = 74.03, SD = 8.22 versus M = 71.91, SD = 9.11, t (121) = 2.46, p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.22 (see Figure 5).

When project creators use highly hierarchical rhetoric, backers are more likely to view the communication as clear, informative, and cognitively engaging. ## 4.4 Confirmatory Study: Hierarchical Rhetoric Effect on Backers’ Willingness to Fund

The two mechanism studies above allowed us to establish longer information processing duration and higher perceived information sharing quality as independent and empirically validated outcomes of reading hierarchical rhetoric that can influence backers’ willingness to fund. Isolating these mechanisms in controlled settings was necessary to confirm the influence of hierarchical rhetoric on how backers process and evaluate information, insights that might have been obscured in the complete research model, where multiple factors interact, making it difficult to disentangle causal pathways.

In the final phase, we conducted a confirmatory betweensubject experiment and investigated the entire research model. This study aimed to test whether information processing duration and perceived information sharing quality mediate the effect of hierarchical rhetoric on backers’ willingness to fund. It also explored the role of project category in these relationships.

## 4.4.1 Sample

We recruited our participants from the Prolific platform. To qualify for participation, participants had to be native speakers of English and be familiar with crowdfunding. They also needed to give consent for their participation. We aimed to recruit 950 participants when we made our experiment available on Prolific.

## 4.4.2 Dependent Variable

We used backers’ willingness to fund a project as our dependent variable (Ciuchta et al., 2016). This was measured using a two-item scale assessing participants willingness to fund a project on a 7-point Likert scale (Ciuchta et al., 2016) (see Appendix G). This measure captured the degree of willingness to fund, reflecting the strength of participants’ motivation to support a project. Similarly graded attitudinal measures have been used in crowdfunding research to capture funders’ evaluative responses to campaign content in contexts where actual contribution behavior is not observed (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020).

## 4.4.3 Independent and Control Variables

Hierarchical rhetoric and project category served as the independent variables. The project category (commercial vs. noncommercial) was manipulated based on the project’s objective (profit-driven vs. mission-driven). We used four samples of rhetoric— two profit-driven and two mission-driven—adapted from real crowdfunding projects, as in Mechanism Study 2, for our experiment. Each category included one sample with a high-hierarchical rhetoric score and another with a low-hierarchical rhetoric score, both of equal length (Chan et al., 2021).<sup>18</sup> As in Mechanism Study 2, in addition to maintaining equal length, we ensured that the information related to the projects’ objectives, product names, and ideas remained consistent across the two rhetorical variations within each project category. The differences were limited to the content associated with function words contributing to the hierarchical structure. The control variables remained the same as those in Mechanism Study 2 (Ciuchta et al., 2016).

## 4.4.4 Procedures

After answering screening questions and consenting to participate, participants were randomly assigned to one of the four possible conditions (2 [commercial/ noncommercial] × 2 [high-hierarchical/low-hierarchical] design). They were instructed to read a project description and then respond to several measures: the perceived information sharing quality scale, the willingness to fund scale, comprehension questions, and bio and control variable questions. The time they spent reading their assigned rhetoric sample was recorded in seconds; the timer started the moment they landed on the rhetoric page and stopped when they moved to the next page. To ensure the relevance of the projects, the goals of the participants were explicitly manipulated after they read the assigned rhetoric and before they answered the willingness to fund questions (Bargh et al., 2001). The participants assigned to the commercial project rhetoric samples were told to imagine they needed the product pitched (a game app) and had the means to fund it if they chose to. The participants assigned to the noncommercial rhetoric samples were told to imagine they had the means to fund prosocial projects like the one pitched in the rhetoric (music recording), and they were generally interested in the product.

## 5 Discussion

In this multiphase study, we first conducted an observational study in a real-world crowdfunding context. We provided evidence for the positive impact of hierarchical rhetoric on funding outcomes and the moderating role of project categories. Next, through two mechanism-based studies, we investigated whether hierarchical rhetoric influences backers’ information processing duration and their perceptions of information sharing quality. The results indicated a positive influence of hierarchical rhetoric on both outcomes. Finally, we tested our research model in a confirmatory study. Our findings revealed a positive relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and information processing duration, perceived information sharing quality, and willingness to fund. Additionally, we found that perceived information sharing quality mediates the relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and willingness to fund, whereas information processing duration does not. These relationships varied across project categories. In the following section, we discuss the implications of these findings.

## 5.1 Theoretical Implications

Influence of rhetorical structure on backers’ information processing and perceptions: Our study shifts the focus from content words to function words in crowdfunding projects, demonstrating that the rhetorical structure shaped by these words can significantly impact backers’ information processing duration and their perceptions of information sharing quality on crowdfunding platforms. The definition and quantification of information is a long-standing philosophical issue (Floridi, 2002). Traditionally, the amount of information conveyed in rhetoric has been measured by word count (Isaak et al., 2021). However, relying solely on word count overlooks the influence of different language structures beyond words (e.g., phrases and sentences) and can conceal the role of the project creator’s communication style (e.g., formal vs. informal language, verbosity vs. succinctness, relevance, etc.) (Parhankangas & Renko, 2017). Hierarchical rhetoric accounts for the organization and layering of concepts, providing a deeper understanding of how much information is conveyed in rhetoric. Unlike word count, assuming that more words equate to more information, hierarchical rhetoric considers how ideas are structured and connected, providing a more meaningful indicator of the richness of information conveyed (Dennis & Valacich, 1999; Jordan et al., 2019).

Our finding that hierarchical rhetoric is associated with increased silent reading time, representing increased information processing duration, could contribute to our understanding of dual-process models such as the ELM in explaining backer behaviors in crowdfunding. The ELM explains how people process persuasive stimuli differently depending on the route of information processing they take and how these processes impact attitudes and behavior (Allison et al., 2017; Bi et al., 2017). In this study, we found that variations in hierarchical rhetorical structure can influence the time backers spend engaging with rhetoric, which could reflect their levels of elaboration. An implication of this finding is that rhetorical structure may shape the cognitive route backers take when processing information. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to explore and provide preliminary evidence that the structure of rhetoric, particularly in terms of how it is shaped by function words, could be associated with differences in information processing levels. While additional research is needed to further validate this implication, our findings have broader relevance beyond the crowdfunding context, particularly in situations characterized by asymmetric information distribution.

The differential impact of rhetoric structure across project categories: Despite the implications of some recent findings on how backers respond differently to design features of different types of crowdfunding projects, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to investigate how the structure of rhetoric impacts backers’ behaviors and propensity to fund projects differently across different categories. We contrasted commercial, profit-driven crowdfunding projects— resembling traditional fundraising—with those driven by noncommercial, mission-oriented goals. Our findings suggest that hierarchical rhetoric has a more positive impact on the former. This insight enhances the understanding of how the effectiveness of information sharing through a specific rhetorical structure (i.e., hierarchical rhetoric) can vary across different types of entrepreneurial endeavors. It also paves the way for further research on the role of rhetorical structures in platform-based communication in other sectors and domains.

The stronger relationship between hierarchically structured rhetoric and the willingness to fund commercial projects implies that backers of these projects are more willing and motivated to engage with elevated levels of elaboration and scrutiny. This suggests that backers of commercial projects are likely to prioritize logic and detail in their decision-making, making them more responsive to hierarchical rhetoric. Along the same lines, our findings show that for noncommercial projects, backers may pay less attention to the quality of information sharing in rhetoric (organization, logicalness, cohesiveness, etc.), implying the role of other variables in their decisionmaking (Allison et al., 2017; Bi et al., 2017).

Influence of rhetorical structure alongside content in gaining support in crowdfunding: Finally, our study contributes to the ongoing debate in management research regarding the credibility of rhetorical signals in crowdfunding by offering new evidence of their strategic value. In management literature, there has been a traditional skepticism toward the value and reliability of rhetoric in reducing information asymmetry (Anglin et al., 2018a). This skepticism stems from the assumption that rhetorical signals— such as positive tone and persuasive language—are costless to produce and equally accessible to both high- and low-quality senders. As a result, they are often considered unreliable indicators of project quality, as they do not prevent low-quality projects from mimicking high-quality ones. However, recent studies in the crowdfunding context have begun to challenge this view by demonstrating the important role of rhetorical linguistic cues in improving project performance and encouraging backers’ contributions (Steigenberger & Wilhelm, 2018).

While these studies—and related work in crowdfunding—have primarily focused on the content of rhetoric, often relying on content words to capture constructs such as tone, emotion, or positive psychological capital, our study extends this stream by examining the structure of rhetoric. We argue that how rhetorical content is organized—through variations in function word use—can meaningfully shape backers’ perceptions and funding decisions. This shift from what is said to how it is said introduces a new dimension to understanding rhetorical effectiveness on crowdfunding platforms. By conceptualizing and operationalizing the structural elements of rhetoric—specifically hierarchical rhetoric—our study opens new avenues for exploring how the structure of rhetoric can be strategically crafted to improve crowdfunding performance, offering a valuable complement to existing content-focused approaches. This approach lays the groundwork for future research on other structure-oriented rhetorical constructs.

## 5.2 Practical Implications

Our study provides actionable insights on how to structure rhetoric based on the type of crowdfunding project for better persuasion and funding outcomes. These insights are particularly valuable given the importance of crowdfunding as a modern, digital capitalsourcing venue and its contribution to creating jobs worldwide. Our insights on effective communication strategies can help project creators improve the overall success rate of crowdfunding efforts. Our findings suggest that project creators should consider a strategic approach to structuring crowdfunding messages by organizing them hierarchically, thereby improving backers’ comprehension and engagement with campaign content. Our findings also provide insight into how to strike a balance between conciseness and relevance by hierarchically structuring the rhetoric for better communication outcomes. Hierarchical rhetoric, characterized by an organized presentation of concepts, facilitates more effective information sharing, even when controlling for the length of the rhetoric. This structure helps maintain relevance by presenting essential information related to the pitched product or service while also supporting conciseness by logically organizing the content.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Work

Like any study, the current study has some limitations that future work could address. While participant motivations were controlled for during the experimental phases, the observational phase was conducted on a reward-based crowdfunding platform, where project creators may pursue both profit-driven and missiondriven goals. In this section of the paper, we used the project labels provided by Kickstarter as the basis for our classifications. We acknowledge that this distinction may not always be clear-cut. Future work could assess the external validity of our findings by comparing the significance of hierarchical rhetoric on platforms mainly designed for profit-driven purposes (e.g., equity-based crowdfunding) with those designed for mission-driven purposes (e.g., charity-based crowdfunding platforms). Also, to further test whether more hierarchically structured rhetoric is associated with increased deliberate information processing and deeper engagement, future work could triangulate behavioral data, such as reading time, with physiological or neurocognitive indicators, such as eye tracking or fMRI, to validate and strengthen interpretations of cognitive involvement (Minas et al., 2014).

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we examined whether and how hierarchical rhetoric, shaped by function words, influences backers’ levels of information processing, their perceptions of information sharing quality conveyed in rhetoric, and their willingness to fund projects across different project categories on crowdfunding platforms. Our study provides a foundation for further research on how the linguistic structure of rhetoric, beyond its content, could be adjusted to transmit richer information, enhance audience engagement, and persuade them to take the desired action. In particular, researchers and practitioners may go beyond traditional measures of information sharing in rhetoric and investigate how the linguistic structure of rhetoric could be adjusted to achieve these goals (Zhou et al., 2018).

## Appendix B: Hierarchical Rhetoric and Information Sharing Quality (Examples)

In the following, we exemplify how rhetoric characterized by hierarchical structure tends to exhibit a more logical nature, conveys more specific concepts, builds a better mental model of a project, and potentially leads to higher perceptions of information sharing quality and longer processing duration. We define the mental model as “cognitive structures that capture shared understanding” (Gonzales et al., 2010, p. 6). We present two samples of crowdfunding rhetoric below, which were also mentioned in the introduction section, characterized by different levels of hierarchica structure, measured by verbal analytical thinking (Boyd et al., 2020; Jordan et al., 2019; Pennebaker et al., 2001). In these examples, function words positively associated with hierarchical structure are in blue font (including articles and prepositions), while those negatively associated with hierarchical structure are in red (including pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, negations, and auxiliary verbs).

Example 1 (high-hierarchical rhetoric): The rhetoric of a project to make a documentary about the life and work of a professional boxer.

The Sweet Science will be a passion-driven project that tries to shine a deserving light once again on the sport of boxing. It will follow the professional and private life of a currently 100 super middleweight boxer from Colorado, represented by Ryan Roach (yes, THAT Roach). He’s a father of a newborn, a husband, and a future champion. We will look at the work he puts in at the gym and at home that will take him one step closer to his championship dreams.

To illustrate how this rhetoric builds a mental model of the project for potential backers, we visualized a concept map, as shown in Figure B1. In this concept map, rounded-corner rectangles represent the concepts introduced by articles (i.e., a and the), and arrows represent how these concepts are connected using prepositions (e.g., to, on). The concepts that belong to each sentence are color-coded.

![](/api/attachments/SCNH9KHC/fulltext/images/5388078a8281eb9cf8fd0be383fcea9ec249207077517a6a09ded25c3ab14140.jpg)  
Figure B1. Concept Map of Example 1

In this example, hierarchical rhetoric helps organize the ideas from the broad project theme down to specific details about the subject’s professional and personal life. This rhetoric begins with mentioning “The Sweet Science”—a passion-driven project highlighting the sport of boxing—as a general concept. Then, the focus of the project rhetoric becomes more specific by mentioning that the project will explore both the professional and private life of a notable super middleweight boxer named Ryan Roach in Colorado. Then, some background, including his family role and career ambition, is provided. After that, the rhetoric becomes even more specific by mentioning the activities covered in the project, including professional and personal activities and the goal pursued.

The hierarchical structure of rhetoric, which results from a high frequency of articles preceding nouns and prepositions, enables backers to more easily connect concepts and construct a mental model of the information being conveyed. Making a similar mental representation for rhetoric that is low in terms of hierarchical structure is relatively more difficult. For instance, the following is a crowdfunding rhetoric almost the same length as the first example from another real crowdfunding project.

Example 2 (low-hierarchical rhetoric): The rhetoric of a crowdfunding project for making a music album.

If you had told me a few years back that I would have an album out & now taking a chance on myself and seeing what Say less can do nationally, I would’ve laughed. But I truly do believe God put me on this earth to help heal the land through my music. Music that makes you dance, music that makes you think, music that changes your mood when you’re down. I funded my first album by myself and that is /was a great accomplishment for me

The rhetoric presented in this example is more narrative and personal as it focuses on the individual’s journey and experiences instead of providing an outline of a project aimed at a specific goal. The high conveyance of persona information is evident in the proportion of words in red, words that are negatively associated with hierarchical structure. Unlike the previous example, which had a clear hierarchy from the project’s general concept down to specifics, with easy-to-identify connections between ideas, this rhetoric lacks a structured flow into distinct layers of detail. The rhetoric begins with a thought on the nature of the project creator’s career (“ f yo had told me a few years back …”). Then, it mentions the philosophical purpose of the project creator (“God put me on this earth to help heal the land through my music”). After that, the effects of music in general are described (“Music that makes you dance, music that makes you think, music that changes your mood when you’re down”). Finally, the rhetoric concludes with a personal achievement (“I funded my first album by myself”). This rhetoric is structured as a series of loosely connected concepts; it jumps from personal details to beliefs and achievements without analytical depth and layering concepts in a structured hierarchy.

In general, rhetoric that is low in hierarchical structure tends to be informal, personal, and intuitive (Pennebaker et al., 2001). These characteristics make it challenging to create logical connections between the concepts mentioned in rhetoric and create a coherent and cohesive mental model of the project, as illustrated in the first example above. The lack of hierarchical structure in this type of rhetoric could be attributed to the proportion of function words that are associated with hierarchical rhetoric. In rhetoric that is low in hierarchical structure, there are relatively fewer concepts preceded by articles compared to rhetoric that is high in hierarchical structure. ## Appendix D: Mechanism Study 1 Robustness Check (Details)

We conducted a between-subject design experiment to assess between-group and within-group variances in silent reading times of rhetoric samples with different levels of hierarchical structure. We did an a priori power analysis using G\*Power 3 to determine the sample size required for an α error probability of 0.05, 0.80 power, and a medium effect size (f = 0.25) in a fixed-effects omnibus ANOVA. It suggested a total sample size of 159. We recruited 160 participants to conduct this experiment.

We randomly assigned participants to three groups, each reading three rhetoric samples with either high, mid, or low levels of hierarchical structure—high (M = 90.02, individual scores: 83.25, 93.93, 92.87), mid $( M = 4 6 . 9 9$ , individual scores: 53.94, 54.00, 33.04), and low (M = 18.36, individual scores: 5.86, 24.12, 25.10). The rhetoric samples were selected from Kickstarter projects and were minimally edited for their length and readability.<sup>20</sup> All samples of rhetoric across the three groups were 180 words in length. The Flesch Reading Ease scores demonstrated comparability in terms of readability across three groups of rhetoric samples, ranging from fairly easy to fairly difficult to read: the high group averaged 58.26 (scores: 58.1, 48.6, 68.1), the mid group averaged 70.16 (scores: 80.2, 73.2, 57.1), and the low group averaged 74.23 (scores: 67.8, 84.7, 58.4).

The readings happened on Qualtrics pages, where each rhetoric sample was placed on a separate page, and the silent reading time for each rhetoric sample was recorded behind the scenes from the moment the participants landed on the page until they clicked on the arrow that took them to the next page. Participants were provided with instructions on what silent reading is and what to expect when they clicked on the arrow that would take them to the next page. They were informed that they would be answering two multiple-choice comprehension questions after each rhetoric sample. Before engaging with the main rhetoric samples, participants were first presented with a practice rhetoric sample followed by two comprehension questions. This was designed to acquaint them with the page layout and the task at hand. They read four rhetoric samples in total, but only three were included in the analysis. The participants then answered questions on a set of control variables, including attention, familiarity with crowdfunding, number of campaigns viewed and funded in the past, and interest in crowdfunding.

Seven participants were deleted from our sample because they gave wrong answers to both comprehension questions on the practice rhetoric sample. The remaining 153 participants (Gender: male = 99, female =54, Age: 24-34 = 39; 35- 44 = 64, 45-54 = 29, 55-64 = 21) answered both or at least one of the practice rhetoric questions and the three main rhetoric questions correctly. No significant differences were observed between the three groups of participants in control variables, including attention $( F = 0 . 8 0 , d f = ( 2 , 1 5 0 ) , p = 0 . 2 2 )$ , familiarity $( F = 1 . 5 7 , d e = ( 2 , 1 5 0 ) , p = 0 . 1 3 )$ campaigns viewed in the past $( F = 1 . 6 3 , d f = ( 2 , 1 5 0 ) , p = 0 . 2 0 )$ , campaigns supported in the past $( F = 1 . 0 1 , d f = ( 2$ 150), p = 0.33), and interest (F = 2.20, df = (2, 150), p = 0.11). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference between the three groups in terms of their correct answers to the multiple-choice comprehension questions (F = 2.45, df = (2, 150), p = 0.78). The analysis of variance revealed a significant effect of hierarchical structure on the time participants spent on the rhetoric samples (F = 3.55, df = (2, 150), p < 0.05). Post hoc comparisons showed that participants in the high group spent more time than those in the medium (M = 170.50 vs. 150.6, p < 0.05, d = 0.37) and low groups (M = 170.50 vs. 131.72, p < 0.001, d = 0.56), with no significant difference between the low and medium groups (p = 0.40).

To further control for the potential confounding effect of reading difficulty on our results, we selected one rhetoric sample from each of the three groups that demonstrated nearly identical Flesch Reading Ease scores (reading ease scores of 58.1, 57.1, and 58.4 for the rhetoric samples selected from high-, mid-, and low-hierarchical rhetoric samples). We replicated our analysis of variance along with post hoc pairwise comparisons. The analysis confirmed a statistically significant influence of hierarchical structure on reading times $( F = 3 . 3 3 5 , p = 0 . 0 3 8 )$ . Post hoc comparisons revealed a significant difference in the reading times between the participants assigned to the low and high groups $( p = 0 . 0 1 2 )$ and a marginally significant difference in the reading times between the participants assigned to the mid and high groups $( p = 0 . 0 9 )$ . However, no significant differences were detected between the participants assigned to the low and mid groups $( p = 0 . 4 0 )$ . These findings further supported the hypothesized positive relationship between hierarchical rhetoric and information processing duration.

In another replication of this experiment, we further checked whether the significant differences in time spent on each rhetoric sample was due to better elaboration on the content or from poor presentation of concepts (154 participants, male = 75, female =77, other =2, Age: 18-24 = 22, 25-34 = 49; 35-44 = 34, 45-54 = 26, 55-64 = 15, 65-74 = 4, 75-84 = 2, 85 or older = 2 ). We followed the same procedures, but this time, we added two additional questions—one measuring participant perception of cognitive load from the content itself (associated with intrinsic cognitive load), and one measuring participant perception of cognitive load from poor presentation of concepts (associated with extraneous cognitive load).<sup>21</sup>

Consistent with the first experiment, the results indicated a positive association between hierarchical structure and time spent on reading rhetoric $( F = 3 . 3 8 , d f = ( 2 , 1 5 1 ) , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . We analyzed variance to examine the difference in participants’ perceptions of cognitive load across the three conditions (low, mid, and high). The results revealed a significant difference in perceived cognitive load from content itself $( F = 8 . 7 9 , d f = ( 2 , 1 5 1 ) , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and no significant difference in perceived cognitive load due to poor presentation of concepts $( F = 2 . 2 0 , d f = ( 2 , 1 5 1 ) , p = 0 . 1 1 )$ . The post hoc pairwise comparisons of the three groups in terms of perceived cognitive load from content revealed a significant difference between the low and mid groups $( M _ { \mathrm { \ l o w } } = 2 . 6 5 , M _ { \mathrm { \ m i d } } = 3 . 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and low and high groups $( M _ { \mathrm { l o w } } = 3 . 2 6 ,$ $M _ { \mathrm { \mathit { h i g h } } } = 3 . 5 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but no significant difference between mid and high groups (p = 0.25).

These findings suggest that the increased time spent on higher hierarchical rhetoric is not due to a poor presentation of concepts, which would introduce extraneous cognitive load. Instead, it results from greater elaboration related to the content itself. As discussed in Appendix B, higher hierarchical rhetoric integrates more concepts and establishes stronger connections between them, while controlling for word count. This structure facilitates the simultaneous processing of multiple interrelated elements (Sweller, 1988), leading to deeper elaboration.

## Appendix E: Perceived Information Sharing Quality Scale Development

We followed three steps—item creation, scale development, and scale testing—to develop a perceived information sharing scale. In the first step, item creation, we determined the scope and generality of the information sharing construct, drawing from the established framework of communication in IS literature and the definition of information sharing (Dennis et al., 2008; Dennis & Kinney, 1998; Robert et al., 2018). Information sharing (i.e., the conveyance of information) is defined as “the transmission of a diversity of new information—as much new, relevant information as needed—to enable the receiver to create and revise a mental model of the situation” (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 580). Individuals who participate in information sharing “engage in substantial information processing activities so that a potentially large, diverse set of information can be exchanged in a variety of information formats”; information sharing “will often require time to perform information processing—the cognitive processes necessary to analyze the information, make sense of it, and build their mental models.” (Dennis et al., 2008, emphasis added).

Based on the definition of information sharing, in the context of using rhetoric for persuasive purposes on crowdfunding platforms, we define “perceived information sharing quality” as the extent to which individuals believe that the rhetoric of crowdfunding projects conveys relevant information, aids in the creation and revision of a mental model of the project, and necessitates cognitive processing. The following three content areas of the construct were identified from the definition of information sharing after consulting two senior IS faculty experts: transmission of relevant information, transmission of information that creates a mental model of the situation, and cognitive demand. These three content areas align with the central route of information processing as well (i.e., transmission and reception of information, systematic information processing, high degree of cognition, and extensive information processing, respectively).
