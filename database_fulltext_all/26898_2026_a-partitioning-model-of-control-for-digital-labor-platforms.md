# A Partitioning Model of Control for Digital Labor Platforms

Hui Hao,<sup>1</sup> Monideepa Tarafdar,<sup>2</sup> Traci J. Hess<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, USA, hhao@memphis.edu <sup>2</sup>Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst, USA, mtarafdar@umass.edu <sup>3</sup>Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst, USA, thess@isenberg.umass.edu

## Abstract

Digital labor platforms (DLPs) digitally connect human workers with consumers and arrange for payment in a type of work known as digital platform work. DLPs face challenges due to the complexity of control in digital platform work: controlling a large number of workers, high flexibility of work, and aggravated worker resistance. Accordingly, they execute algorithmic control, which, however, presents problematic scenarios, such as a lack of autonomy and fairness and low worker well-being. Problematizing control executed solely by DLPs and using only algorithms, we address the research question: How are workers in digital platform work controlled by (multiple) algorithmic and human controllers? We conducted a study of digital platform work in the context of one of the world’s largest app-based food delivery DLPs. We investigated different control mechanisms and how they were executed. Based on 28 interviews with workers and managers, our findings revealed three themes—controllers, control functions, and control means. Analyzing the themes, we developed a “partitioning model” for control in digital platform work. We theorize the distribution of control in digital platform work between multiple controllers (i.e., the DLP, third-party intermediaries, and customers) and between algorithmic and non-algorithmic control mechanisms. Control partitioning provides a novel theoretical perspective that brings the human back into the loop of control, in contrast to existing studies, which suggest that DLPs are the only controllers and algorithmic control is the exclusive control means. Our study furthers the IS literature on DLPs and the control literature by highlighting and addressing the complexity of control in digital platform work and the problems associated with its algorithmic control.

Keywords: Digital Platform Work, Algorithmic Control, Non-Algorithmic Control, Digital Labor Platform, Partitioning Model of Control, Human-in-the-Loop Control

## 1 Introduction

Digital labor platforms (DLPs) digitally connect human workers (i.e., service providers) with service consumers, manage the workers, and mediate payment for services provided (Duggan et al., 2020; Rai et al., 2019; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2018). Examples of DLPs include Uber, DoorDash, Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), and Upwork. Mediated by DLPs, human workers interact with algorithms to accomplish work such as driving taxis, delivering food, and executing human intelligence tasks. This type of electronically<sup>1</sup> mediated work is known as digital platform work (Tarafdar et al., 2023; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2018). Digital platform work is considered a type of alternative work arrangement (AWA) that symbolizes the changing nature of work in terms of how work is (digitally) arranged and configured, its contractual terms, and its primary arbiter (the DLP) (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020).

Digital platform work differs from traditional work in terms of workers’ non-employee status and a technologically mediated task environment (Anicich, 2022). Specifically, workers on DLPs are non-employees, with restricted access to employee benefits, and unpredictable earnings and work schedules (Abraham et al., 2021). The work is highly flexible, as workers can engage in short-term tasks whenever and wherever they wish (Constantinides et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2020). DLPs focus on managing the scale and commoditization of work, rather than the particular needs of each task and worker (Barley & Kunda, 2004). Human workers interact with algorithms to accomplish work, such that workers are highly dependent on the platform to complete the work and are controlled by algorithms (Anicich, 2022; Kellogg et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023).

Control of digital platform work is complex for a number of reasons. First, DLPs face challenges due to their large size; it is difficult for them to address the individual needs of each worker, assess worker quality, and establish interpersonal connections with workers (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Rai et al., 2019). Second, workers have a high degree of flexibility, allowing them to work whenever and from wherever they want (Constantinides et al., 2018) and making it difficult for DLP managers to monitor and align workers’ behaviors with DLP objectives. Third, the lack of interpersonal interaction between workers and managers, combined with workers’ non-contractual job status, can aggravate worker resistance. For example, using social media and aided by social movement organizers, Uber drivers have organized online resistance forums to protest against perceived injustices of digital platform work (Kellogg et al., 2020).

Research has primarily focused on control in digital platform work through algorithmic control (AC), which is defined as the use of algorithms as a means to align worker behaviors with controllers’ objectives (Möhlmann et al., 2021; Wiener et al., 2023). However, in focusing on AC, the literature reveals a number of problematic scenarios. First, human workers often do not fully understand the algorithmically generated outputs and the mechanisms behind them, leading them to experience confusion, role conflict, and role ambiguity (Rahman, 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023). Second, algorithms cannot make fair judgments on ambiguous and complex issues (Binns, 2022; Ma & Kou, 2022; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). For instance, while they automatically penalize workers for negative ratings, a negative rating does not always indicate poor performance by the worker because it is subject to the customer’s viewpoint. The customer may have been in a bad mood or may have made an unreasonable request that the worker could not fulfill. Thus, the information used by algorithms—in this case, a negative customer rating—lacks sufficient situational detail. Third, AC is associated with a loss of autonomy, privacy, and identification with work, which is detrimental to worker well-being (Cram et al., 2022; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023). These observations show that AC has inadequacies in that it does not provide adequate explanation and feedback to the worker, cannot make contextually appropriate decisions in ambiguous situations, and does not take interpersonal and empathetic considerations into account when making judgments.

Research has also primarily considered DLPs as the only controlling entity in digital platform work. However, the history of work shows that when traditional firms first started to incorporate more independent contracting work, similar to what DLPs do, they used staffing agencies as third-party entities for brokering the market to mitigate the high costs of searching for contract workers with the required skills (Barley & Kunda, 2004). In digital platform work, practical examples are beginning to emerge of DLPs using third-party agencies (Zheng & Wu, 2022) to hire and manage workers on their behalf to tackle the complexity of control. It is thus reasonable to suggest that a theoretical understanding of the role of third-party agencies can contribute to the literature on control in digital platform work. Based on the above, we problematize the literature’s focus on AC as the primary control means and on DLPs as the only controllers, and address the following research question: How are workers in digital platform work controlled by algorithmic and human controllers?

Our empirical context of digital platform work is an appbased food delivery platform in China. We investigated control mechanisms deployed by the platform through 28 interviews conducted with 25 different workers and managers. Guided by an open-ended interview protocol, we examined how workers are controlled, how AC and non-AC are used, and the role of different types of controllers. Our findings indicate three primary themes, namely controllers, control functions, and the control means, together with their corresponding subthemes. Interpreting the themes, we conceptualize the notion of “control partitioning,” through which we explain how multiple controllers work together to control workers, using both algorithmic and non-algorithmic means. Control partitioning provides a novel theoretical contribution to the literature by suggesting that control in digital platform work is distributed among multiple controllers, with each controller in charge of specific control mechanisms. Unlike third-party agencies in traditional agency work, whose primary function is to help firms with matching and brokering workers with skills (Barley & Kunda, 2004), we explain how third-party agencies in digital platform work help DLPs in the control of workers. Specifically, they execute control in two ways, namely by extending and reinforcing the DLP’s control. Further, the control mechanisms are distributed among algorithms and humans. Through control partitioning (1) among multiple controllers and (2) between algorithms and humans, the DLP tackles the complexity of control in digital platform work and addresses the problems of AC. We thus go beyond the literature that more simplistically suggests that DLPs are the primary controllers and AC is the exclusive control means. In terms of practical implications, DLPs can benefit from our findings by considering working with other controllers, being cautious about the limitations of AC, and considering how and when to use algorithms for control.

## 2 Theoretical Background

Our study draws on research from two foundational areas: how work is arranged and controlled. In this section, we first introduce relevant concepts regarding different work arrangements and organizational control. We then present a literature review of our phenomenon of interest—control in digital platform work. Following this, we problematize the literature and lay out our research objective.

## 2.1 Alternative Work Arrangements

A traditional job refers to a job that pays a wage or salary, has a predictable work schedule and earnings, is supervised by the firm paying the salary, and has an implicit or explicit contract for a continuing employment relationship with that firm (Abraham et al., 2021). In traditional jobs, firms screen and hire employees, socialize them into the firm’s culture, train them, and evaluate their performance for rewards and promotions (Spreitzer et al., 2017). In addition, the employing firm fulfills legally, socially, and culturally prescribed obligations to employees, such as health insurance and retirement plans. In return, employees are expected to offer their loyalty and work efforts (Barley & Kunda, 2004).

Current times are characterized by a changing labor market in terms of the way people work, for whom they work, how that work is arranged, and how they are paid (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Researchers and policymakers have expressed concerns that the changes herald a number of issues, including the perceived fracturing of relationships between workers and employers, the loss of job security and benefits for workers, and the growing importance of workers’ access to technical training and education as the impacts of new technologies emerge (Barley & Kunda, 2004; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020; Pignot, 2023). These issues are often associated with alternative work arrangements (AWAs), which include work with unpredictable schedules, such as on-call work; independent contracting work, such as freelancing and day labor; agency work, such as work accessed through temporary help agencies; and electronically mediated work, such as crowd work and app work (Abraham et al., 2021; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020; Duggan et al., 2020; Spreitzer et al., 2017). In this section, we describe different types of AWAs. Specifically, we first describe the three initial AWAs, namely (1) work with unpredictable schedules, (2) independent contracting work, and (3) agency work. We then introduce a newer form of AWA—digital platform work, which is the domain of this study.

## 2.1.1 Traditional Alternative Work Arrangements

Table 1 describes the different types of AWAs as compared to traditional jobs, distinguished on the basis of four characteristics. The first is whether the worker is paid a standing wage/salary (as an employee) or paid for temporary projects (as a contract worker) (Abraham et al., 2021). Employers fulfill legally prescribed obligations, such as offering health insurance and retirement plans, to employees only. The second and third characteristics pertain, respectively, to whether the worker has a predictable work schedule and whether the worker’s earnings from this work are predictable. The fourth characteristic concerns control in the work, i.e., whether it is done by the same firm that pays the worker or by a different organizational entity (Abraham et al., 2021). These four characteristics can be used to describe traditional jobs and the three traditional AWAs. Digital platform work is different from the traditional AWAs in terms of a fifth characteristic, that of the digital mediation of work (Anicich, 2022; Rai et al., 2019; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2018).

In work with unpredictable schedules, workers are directly employed by a firm, but their work hours change depending on the needs of the firm, and there may be periods when they do not work at all (Abraham et al., 2021). Examples of such work include on-call work (e.g., substitute teachers) and seasonal employment work (e.g., retail clerks during busy holiday periods) (Spreitzer et al., 2017). On-call workers or seasonal employees are employed by the firm, are paid a salary, and have benefits such as health insurance and retirement plans. Further, they are typically supervised by the firm that employs them (Abraham et al., 2021). What distinguishes them from traditional jobs is the predictability of their work. They do not have predictable hours for work, even though their pay while working may be relatively predictable (Abraham et al., 2021).

In independent contracting work, workers are contracted by a firm to apply their knowledge and skills to a specific, short-term project, for hourly or projectbased pay rather than a salary (Spreitzer et al., 2017). Examples include high-skilled freelancers, such as writers, editors, designers, and programmers, as well as low-skilled contract workers, such as day laborers who negotiate spot contracts for daily jobs (Spreitzer et al., 2017). Independent contracting work is similar to traditional employment in that work outcomes are controlled by the paying firm, and different from it in that workers are non-employees. Independent contract workers face challenges stemming from their exclusion from conventional employment and labor benefits, such as paid sick leave, health insurance, retirement plans, and employer-sponsored training (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Furthermore, their earnings may be unpredictable (Abraham et al., 2021; Spreitzer et al., 2017). Such work is therefore associated with job insecurity (Bidwell & Briscoe, 2009; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Consequently, independent contracting workers may have lower affective organizational commitment (Spreitzer et al., 2017) and lower-quality work outcomes, such as poor customer service (Johnson & Ashforth, 2008).

Agency work represents the third type of traditional AWA. The employment relationship for agency work is between three parties—the worker, a staffing agency employing the worker, and a firm for which the worker works (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Spreitzer et al., 2017). Agency workers are paid standing wages, have relatively predictable hours and earnings, and work at the firm site (Abraham et al., 2021). However, agency workers are supervised by the firm, despite not being directly compensated by it (Abraham et al., 2021). Agency workers face the dissonance of being supervised by the firm but compensated by a staffing agency (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Consequently, they may have lower job satisfaction and affective organizational commitment due to increased feelings of job insecurity and lower perceived employability (Spreitzer et al., 2017).

Staffing agencies emerged in the 1980s, when firms started to hire larger numbers of contract workers (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Bidwell & Fernandez-Mateo, 2010). These third-party agencies became popular because they lowered the firm’s search costs for contract workers through consolidation and scale economies and managed the distribution of salaries and basic benefits for these workers (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Examples of such staffing agencies include temporary help agencies. Staffing agencies are responsible for brokering the market—they gather information on firm job openings and the job needs of contract workers, negotiate matches, and close the contract deals (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Bidwell & Fernandez-Mateo, 2010). Two types of frontline occupations are central to the staffing agency’s practice (Barley & Kunda, 2004). Account managers identify firms, locate their hiring managers, convince them to advertise for job roles, and close the contract deals. Recruiters locate the contract workers whose skills match the jobs. Staffing agencies act as the workers’ employer of record; they provide their members with training and business services and are responsible for regulatory compliance, such as payroll and employment taxes (Barley & Kunda, 1992, 2004; Spreitzer et al., 2017). As the employer of record, staffing agencies protect contractors from the costs of participating in a market as lone entities (Barley & Kunda, 1992).

Large-sized staffing agencies (e.g., “body shops” in the global IT industry) operate at scale, tend to broker standardized jobs, have transactional relationships with firms, and often neglect individual contract workers’ needs. In contrast, small-sized staffing agencies (e.g., “mom and pop” agencies in the global IT industry) are more likely to establish personal relationships with firms and contract workers, but they are often limited in their scale of operations (Barley & Kunda, 2004).

## 2.1.2 Digital Platform Work: A Digitally Mediated AWA

More recently, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2018) defined a new type of AWA based on the characteristic of digital mediation of work. Described as “electronically mediated work,” it consists of short jobs or tasks that workers find through websites or mobile apps that both connect them with customers and arrange payment for the tasks (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2018). The underlying digital infrastructure that designs and hosts the websites and mobile apps is referred to as a digital labor platform (DLP) (Constantinides et al., 2018). DLPs are “online environments where digital services are sourced and delivered in exchange for compensation with constituent tasks for the services determined, executed, and coordinated by human and AI agents” (Rai et al., 2019, p. iv). Examples of DLPs include Uber, DoorDash, Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), and Upwork. Different terms are used to allude to the type of work supported by DLPs, with overlap among the terms. The IS literature primarily refers to it as algorithmic work (Tarafdar et al., 2023) or platform work (Zheng & Wu, 2022), while the management literature tends to call it gig work (e.g., Duggan et al., 2020; Spreitzer et al., 2017) or on-demand work (e.g., Abraham et al., 2021). However, both gig work and ondemand work are broader terms that have been used to describe not only work on DLPs (Duggan et al., 2020;

Spreitzer et al., 2017) but also independent contracting work, such as self-employed writers (Abraham et al., 2021) and musicians (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2018). Similarly, algorithmic work is also used to describe traditional jobs, such as warehousing, that are managed and controlled through algorithms (Kellogg et al., 2020). To foreground both the digital mediation from DLPs and the contractual aspect of the work, we use the term “digital platform work” in this paper.

Digital platform work can be classified into two categories: crowd work and app work. Crowd work is conducted on work-mediating digital labor platforms where workers remotely complete tasks (Duggan et al., 2020), such as customers posting tasks on MTurk that workers select and complete remotely. App work is work that is mediated by digital labor platforms and performed locally (e.g., ride sharing and food delivery), such as Uber (Duggan et al., 2020). Tasks associated with digital platform work can vary by location, variety, and structure. Task location is the place where a task is conducted and can be either local (Uber) or remote (MTurk) (Duggan et al., 2020). Task variety represents the distinct types of jobs (Zaniboni et al., 2013). MTurk tasks range from filling out surveys to labeling data, while ride-sharing tasks are similar in that all Uber drivers drive customers to a destination. Task structure is the extent to which tasks are clearly defined. In a highly structured task, procedures are well-defined, whereas in an unstructured task, there is uncertainty about how to proceed. Uber tasks are highly structured; for example, drivers accept orders, pick up customers, and drive to destinations. By contrast, on MTurk, specific activities that constitute a task are more difficult to predict.

As shown in Table 1, digital platform work shares characteristics with independent contracting work in that workers are hired and controlled by a firm, use their knowledge and skills for short-term projects, are paid by task rather than salary, and have work schedules and earnings that are unpredictable (Abraham et al., 2021). However, it is different from independent contracting work in that the firms are not traditional organizations but DLPs. DLPs are different from traditional organizations in a number of ways. First, through digital infrastructures, DLPs can coordinate and orchestrate interactions between vast numbers of workers and customers that need work completed (Constantinides et al., 2018; Hong et al., 2016; Möhlmann et al., 2021). The large scale of operations provides efficiency in matching and completing tasks while potentially commoditizing tasks and workers. Second, the use of digital infrastructures enables the matching of workers and customers across many geographic locations, which makes digital platform work more flexible—workers can engage in short-term tasks, irrespective of time and space (Constantinides et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2020). Third, DLPs digitally mediate the interactions between consumers and workers through a digital interface for search and payment, recording and ranking, and communications and feedback (Constantinides et al., 2018; Rai et al., 2019). Consequently, human workers interact primarily with algorithms to perform work such as driving taxis, delivering food, and executing human intelligence tasks—they are highly dependent on the platform to complete the work and are exposed to control from algorithms (Kellogg et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023). In this paper, we investigate control in digital platform work, drawing on the literature of organizational control, which we turn to next.

## 2.2 Organizational Control

This literature defines control as “any process whereby managers direct attention, motivate, and encourage organizational members to act in ways desirable to achieving the organizational objectives” (Cardinal et al., 2010, p. 56). The interests of managers and workers often diverge, as managers aim to ensure the productivity of workers, while workers strive to retain their power and resist being treated like a commodity (Edwards, 1979). Thus, the purpose of organizational control is to align workers’ actions with organizational objectives and goals (e.g., Cardinal et al., 2010; Schafheitle et al., 2020). Control is dyadic in the sense that there is a controller (e.g., the employer who decides how to direct the implementation of controls) and a controlee (e.g., the organizational member/employee who is the subject of control) (Sitkin et al., 2020). Early literature mentions two forms of control: entrepreneurial control, in which the business owner or entrepreneur has direct and hands-on involvement in managing operations and decision-making, and hierarchical control, in which they delegate control to senior employees, such as supervisors or managers. As organizations have grown larger and more complex, technical control (involving technologies such as assembly lines) and bureaucratic control (based on rules, procedures, and policies to standardize and regulate employee behaviors) have become increasingly important. (Edwards, 1979). Technical control and bureaucratic control are two forms of rational control, through which managers obtain desired behaviors from workers using clearly articulated methods and systems (Kellogg et al., 2020; Barley & Kunda, 1992). Studies have also examined normative control, used by controllers to influence workers’ behavior based on managerial ideologies for enhancing human and industrial relations, and improving organizational culture, with a focus on “winning their hearts and minds” (Kellogg et al., 2020; Barley & Kunda, 1992).

Control mechanisms refer to the molecular units of control within a system of control, such as standards, processes, and norms (Cardinal et al., 2010). They can be both formal and informal (Cardinal et al., 2010). Formal control mechanisms apply officially sanctioned institutional aspects, such as written rules and procedural directives, whereas informal control mechanisms are based on values, norms, and beliefs that guide workers’ actions and behaviors (Cardinal et al., 2010; Ens et al., 2023; Sitkin, 1995). Control targets refer to the specific elements of organizational processes (i.e., inputs, behaviors, or outputs) to which control mechanisms are applied (Cardinal et al., 2010). Specifically, input control mechanisms focus on inputs to work, such as worker skills; behavioral control mechanisms regulate the specific worker actions taken to complete the work; and output control mechanisms focus on work outcomes, such as sales outcomes, performance quality, and customer satisfaction (Cardinal et al., 2010).

Control functions refer to specific types of control processes that align workers’ (i.e., controlees’) behaviors with employers’ (i.e., controllers’) objectives (Edwards, 1979). They comprise direction, evaluation, and discipline (Edwards, 1979). Direction refers to the specification of what tasks need to be completed, in what order, with what degree of precision or accuracy, and in what period of time (Edwards, 1979; Kellogg et al., 2020). For example, with bureaucratic control, the employer specifies workers’ task requirements through job descriptions, rules, checklists, and scripts for task activities (Kellogg et al., 2020). Evaluation is defined as a procedure in which the employer supervises and evaluates workers to correct mistakes in production, assesses each worker’s performance, and identifies individual workers who are not adequately performing work tasks (Edwards, 1979; Kellogg et al., 2020). An example of an evaluation is an annual performance review for workers. During the performance review, the supervisor assesses the workers’ performance against specific job duties and objectives that are set at the beginning of the year. Discipline refers to the means the employer uses to punish and reward workers to elicit cooperation and enforce compliance with the process (Edwards, 1979; Kellogg et al., 2020). For example, a worker who consistently arrives late to work may receive a written warning as a punishment, and a worker who meets or exceeds their sales targets may receive a bonus as a reward. Control mechanisms can be framed along these three control functions. For example, recommending a route for a driver is a control mechanism for the control function of direction (Kellogg et al., 2020).

## 2.3 Control in Digital Platform Work

DLPs execute digital platform work by controlling a large number of independent workers through algorithms in a technologically mediated task environment (Anicich, 2022). Drawing on the concepts of organizational control, we define control in digital platform work as any process whereby DLPs (i.e., controllers) take action to influence workers doing digital platform work (i.e., controlees) to act in a manner that creates value and is consistent with the controllers’ objectives (Cardinal et al., 2010). The current literature on DLP focuses exclusively on algorithmic control means. ## 2.3.1 Complexity of Control in Digital Platform Work

With the emergence of AWAs, control has held its fundamental role as the means for managers to align worker behaviors with organizational objectives (Cardinal et al., 2010). Control can be particularly complex in digital platform work for a number of reasons. First, DLPs algorithmically facilitate the matching of supply and demand, resulting in a far greater number of workers needing to be controlled. For example, as of 2022, there were 5.4 million Uber drivers, and the drivers were located in 10,000 different cities across 72 countries (Campbell, 2023). Due to the large number of workers, DLPs typically find it challenging to address the individual needs of each worker, assess worker quality, and establish personal connections with workers (Rai et al., 2019). Second, the level of flexibility regarding work location and work schedule tends to be high in digital platform work (Spreitzer et al., 2017). While in traditional factory settings, managers can directly monitor workers’ behaviors during set work hours, monitoring the work behaviors of freelancers working from home is challenging. The work on DLPs is even more flexible because workers on DLPs can start and stop work at any time and location, making it even more difficult to monitor worker behaviors. Third, the lack of interpersonal interaction between workers and managers, as well as workers’non-contractual job status, can aggravate worker resistance. Connected by algorithms, familiarity between workers and managers is, at best, arm’s length. With few interactions with managers, workers often perceive themselves as commoditized (Barley & Kunda, 2004; Möhlmann & Henfridsson, 2019). Workers on DLPs have shown resistance at both individual and collective levels (Kellogg et al., 2020). Emerging literature has documented new forms of individual worker resistance, including ignoring information provided by algorithms, gaming algorithms, and personal negotiation with clients outside the DLP (Cram et al., 2022; Kellogg et al., 2020; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023). Collective worker resistance includes working with social movement organizations and using social media and online forums to mobilize quickly, coordinate actions globally, and draw public attention more effectively to highlight perceived injustices from AC (Kellogg et al., 2020). It is important that DLPs develop strategies to tackle such new resistance tactics.

Different DLPs also focus on different control targets based on their task characteristics. For app work like Uber, tasks are highly structured and are implemented locally with similar content for each task (Duggan et al., 2020). Thus, control mechanisms can target work input (e.g., maintaining human resource quality), behavior (e.g., defining and organizing worker activities), and work output (e.g., customer satisfaction) (Cardinal et al., 2010). By contrast, for crowd work like MTurk, tasks are executed remotely and have high variety and low structure (Duggan et al., 2020). Thus, control mechanisms are mainly focused on work output because it is difficult to influence the quality of human resources or worker activities (Cardinal et al., 2010).

## 2.3.2 Algorithmic Control

DLPs use AC to tackle the complexity of control in digital platform work (Wiener et al., 2023; Benlian et al., 2022). Two forms of AC that have been investigated are gatekeeping AC and guiding AC (Cram et al., 2022; Wiener et al., 2023). Gatekeeping AC focuses on who is allowed to commence or continue working for the DLP. Guiding AC focuses on influencing how workers conduct their daily work (Cram et al., 2022; Wiener et al., 2023). For guiding AC, studies have proposed AC mechanisms associated with the three control functions of direction, evaluation, and discipline (Kellogg et al., 2020). These are, respectively, restricting and recommending, recording and rating, and replacing and rewarding (Kellogg et al., 2020). Compared to previous technological systems, algorithms are more real time (Kellogg et al., 2020), can collect and analyze an enormous quantity and variety of data about workers (Kellogg et al., 2020), and can support interactive interfaces for DLPs, enabling diverse types of users (e.g., workers, customers, and managers) to access the platform (Kellogg et al., 2020).

In digital platform work, workers perceive algorithms as having multiple roles (Tarafdar et al., 2023). AC focuses on the role of algorithms as controllers, controlling digital platform work with the goal of aligning worker behaviors with the DLP’s objectives (Möhlmann et al., 2021; Wiener et al., 2023). Such a role is often associated with a broken feedback loop (Tarafdar et al., 2023) because algorithmic computations can take into account only those worker inputs that can be entered through specific fields in the digital interface. The controller role of algorithms in digital platform work is different from the role of algorithms in traditional organizations, where algorithms are used in operational and managerial tasks through automation (algorithms take over a human task) and augmentation (algorithms augment humans’ capabilities and actions to perform a task). Automation is used for relatively well-structured tasks, such as completing invoices or expense claims, and augmentation is used for more complex and ambiguous tasks, such as diagnosing diseases (Raisch &

Krakowski, 2021). For the latter, algorithms are used to complete certain activities, with domain experts staying “in the loop” to complete other activities and for overall human oversight (Benlian et al., 2022; Binns, 2022; Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020).

## 2.3.3 Problematizing Control in Digital Platform Work

The current literature on control in digital platform work has two assumptions: (1) that AC is the primary means of control and (2) that there is only one controller, the DLP.

Focusing on AC as the primary means of control leads to a number of problematic scenarios. First, human workers may not fully understand the algorithms’ outputs and the mechanisms behind them because of the opacity of algorithms (Rahman, 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023). The opacity is a result of multiple factors, including the inherent opacity of machine learning logics, the technical literacy of the workers, and the intentional secrecy maintained by the DLP (Burrell, 2016). Moreover, the algorithms are often interconnected in real time (Tarafdar et al., 2023). With the outputs of one algorithm serving as inputs into others, it is even more difficult for workers to figure out the logic behind each algorithm and the relationships between them. The primary objective of control is to align workers’ performance with specific requirements, but workers may find it challenging to understand the requirements, adjusting their behaviors accordingly. Workers may perceive their interactions with algorithms as difficult and confusing, and they can be subject to algorithm-driven role conflict and role ambiguity (Page et al., 2017; Tarafdar et al., 2023).

Second, algorithms have limited ability to make fair control-related decisions on ambiguous and complex issues, such as unsatisfactory ratings and replacing workers, since they are not sensitive to situational nuances and do not have human attributes, such as a sense of self or purpose, intuition, common-sense judgment, creativity, and advanced communication (Binns, 2022; Choudhury et al., 2019; Ma & Kou, 2022; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023). Unlike human managers, who can integrate the worker opinions, customer feedback, and their own observations to make better sense of unsatisfactory ratings (Cameron & Rahman, 2022; Tarafdar et al., 2023), when an algorithm assesses a worker’s performance, it typically does so based largely on unsatisfactory ratings provided by the customer through the app without affording the worker the opportunity to provide explanations or considering situational details, such as the customer’s mood. Unfair judgments can lead to workers’ resistance to control (Wiener et al., 2023).

Third, AC can create work environment tensions that are detrimental to worker well-being. Algorithms cannot incorporate interpersonal and empathetic aspects of management or interpret human perceptions and emotional reactions (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023). As a result, workers may experience tensions regarding autonomy, privacy, and identification (Wiener et al., 2023), which can lead to frustration and stress and undermine their well-being (Cram et al., 2022; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023). Emerging findings from practice show that some DLPs have set up help centers to provide manual support to workers through phone calls or emails, answering their questions, such as how to deal with irregular cases and how to address the effects of negative evaluations (Uber, n.d.). Such feedback provided by human staff members is an example of potential control outside the realm of AC.

The second assumption, that DLPs are the only controllers who control workers’ behaviors (Cram et al., 2022; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Wiener et al., 2023), is problematic due to the complexity of control in digital platform work. The literature has shown that when firms encountered challenges in controlling independent contract workers in previous AWA settings, staffing agencies emerged as the third-party agencies between firms and independent contract workers, brokering the labor market and acting as the employer of record for the workers (Barley & Kunda, 2004). Practical examples of third-party agencies are also emerging for digital platform work on DLPs. For example, many workers on Upwork are organized and managed by third-party agencies. The workers’ profiles are branded with the third-party agency’s identity, and workers are paid by the agencies rather than the platform (Upwork, n.d.). Therefore, it is reasonable to suggest that a theoretical understanding of the role of third-party agencies can contribute to the literature on control in digital platform work.

In summary, the current literature on control in digital platform work (problematically) focuses on AC as the primary control means and the DLP as the sole controller. The literature on alternative work arrangements provides insight into the role of agencies in recruiting and managing workers, and the literature on task augmentation in traditional organizations suggests that humans and algorithms can work in a complementary manner. These ideas give us a theoretical foothold to examine how multiple controllers, using both algorithms and humans, can tackle the complexities of control in digital platform work, with the potential for conceptually expanding the more simplistic worker-firm arrangements found in the literature (Table 1).

## 3 Methods

We conducted 28 semi-structured interviews (over 28 hours, in total) with 25 different workers and managers from an app-based food delivery service platform in China. In this section, we introduce the research context, data collection, and data analysis of our study.

## 3.1 Research Context and Empirical Site

With 144 billion food orders delivered in 2021, our empirical site is one of the largest app-based food delivery service platforms in China. According to the platform’s Rider Report, as of 2021, more than 5 million riders were working for this DLP, and the number of daily active riders exceeded 1 million. The workers on this DLP are called “riders,” because they ride scooters for food delivery. Using the app, customers can search restaurants, browse food menus, order dishes, and have the food delivered to their door. There are multiple types of riders with different work modes. One type is platformexclusive riders (Zhuansong Qishou in Chinese) who work only for this DLP (Zheng & Wu, 2022). They work on regular shifts and are recruited and paid monthly by third-party intermediaries (TPIs). The payment is calculated based on the total number of orders delivered within a given month.

We focused on this specific DLP in our study for two reasons. First, food delivery is a typical example of app work in which algorithms are commonly used for control. Workers, customers, and restaurants are connected by the app, and all delivery tasks are conducted and managed through the app. Second, this DLP is one of the largest app-based food delivery platforms in the world (Curry, 2024). Accordingly, the findings of our study can offer practitioners insights into other DLPs.

## 3.2 Data Collection

We conducted 28 semi-structured interviews (over 28 hours in total) from March 2022 to February 2023 with 25 different platform-exclusive riders, TPI site managers, and a DLP employee. We adopted a qualitative analysis method in the interpretive tradition (Walsham, 1995) and used a semi-structured interview protocol to unearth the contextual circumstances (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) of digital platform work on this DLP. By interviewing these managers and workers with an interpretive stance, we attempted to make sense of the interviewees’ experiences, for example, how control is executed and how algorithms are used for control in their work environment. We encouraged respondents’ free expression and followed up with additional questions to clarify or elaborate on any relevant aspects in more detail. In doing so, we facilitated the emergence of new insights and ensured that our understanding of their responses was accurate and comprehensive. Based on the interpretations of interviewees’ experiences, we developed theoretical explanations for control in digital platform work. Before conducting the interviews, we conducted archival research on reports, social news, and relevant research papers about the DLP to familiarize ourselves with the organization and contextualize our questions.

We used a convenience sample with a snowball sampling method to identify riders and TPI site managers. Such a method is considered appropriate for app workers, who are typically difficult to reach (Tarafdar et al., 2023). We first recruited a DLP employee from our personal network. After the interview, she recommended a former TPI manager as a potential candidate. During our conversation with the TPI manager, we discovered that some riders were active on social media platforms. Leveraging this information, we searched on social media and obtained the contact information for a staff member from a third-party recruiting company. The staff member recommended a TPI site manager, who became the first candidate we interviewed from the TPI. To expand our pool of potential candidates, we asked each interviewee to recommend individuals who would be a good fit for our study. This approach enabled us to identify the rest of the interviewees. We recruited site managers and riders from the same TPI. This allowed us to examine a focused and coherent set of control mechanisms in a consistent milieu vis-à-vis delivery locations, organization structures, cultures, etc., as methodologically suggested (Dyer & Wilkins, 1991).

The details of the interviewees, length of work experience in the role, and interview focus are shown in Table 3. We interviewed some of the interviewees twice, when additional information and clarifications were needed. Our rider interviewees were between the ages of 27 and 48, comprising 18 men and 2 women. Their work experience as riders ranged from 1 month to 4 years; four of the riders had less than 6 months of work experience, while the remaining 16 were experienced riders. Within the group of experienced riders, we interviewed three individuals who had been Delivery Kings, a title given to riders accruing the highest number of orders during a single month within a given site. The interviews ranged from 37 minutes to 2 hours, with an average duration of 1 hour and 4 minutes. Each interview was conducted after the riders’ work hours—for example, from 9 pm to 11 pm. All the interviewees were compensated with 100 yuan (15 USD) for their participation. The interviews were conducted online through VooV, the Chinese version of Zoom. Interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed with the respondent’s consent. The interviews were conducted in Chinese, and the transcripts were translated into English by one of the authors, who is fluent in both languages. English transcripts were shared among all the authors, and English interview memos were used for discussions.

The semi-structured interview protocol is shown in Appendix A. We asked the interviewees common questions about control mechanisms, controllers, and control means. For example, to investigate how riders were evaluated, we asked questions, such as “How is riders’ work performance evaluated?” and “What are some important evaluation indices?” In terms of the controllers, for each identified control mechanism, we asked, “Who is using this indicator for evaluation?” To gain a better understanding of the control means, whether algorithmic or non-algorithmic, we asked questions, such as “Is this mechanism executed by the system or by humans? How is it accomplished?” For instance, if a rider described their experience of being evaluated by their ontime rate, we would ask: “Is the on-time rate executed by the system or by humans? How is it accomplished?” These interview questions remained consistent throughout all interviews, which ensured that the interviews remained relevant and meaningful to our research question and enabled the comparison of interviewees’ responses. We also collected specific information from interviewees holding different roles. Through our interview with the DLP employee, we investigated the background information of the DLP and the relationship between the DLP and the workers. Through our interviews with the former TPI manager and site managers, we learned about the TPI’s background and its interaction with the DLP and the riders. Through our interviews with the riders, we investigated the salience of the identified control mechanisms.

We reached the point of research saturation after conducting 15 interviews, at which point we had gathered sufficient data to address our research question and discovered no new information (Saunders et al., 2018). We conducted 28 interviews with 25 different workers and managers to ensure that further data collection was not yielding new insights.

## 3.3 Data Analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis of the data. In moving through the texts, we used either open coding or axial coding. While open coding helped us categorize the emerging text into new themes or subthemes, such as control means and algorithmic rewarding, axial coding merged repeated or similar texts into existing subthemes (Boyatzis, 1998). When new themes/subthemes emerged, we returned to previously analyzed interview data to reexamine them in light of newly identified and revised themes (Anteby & Chan, 2018). To identify themes and subthemes, we iterated back and forth across interviews, and between our data and the concepts in the literature (Braun & Clarke, 2006). One of the researchers coded the interviews. Discussions took place among the authors when new subthemes arose or when there were ambiguities.

As shown in Appendix B, three themes emerged: (1) controllers, (2) control functions, and (3) control means. We identified three controllers, namely the DLP, the TPI, and the customers. For control functions, we identified three subthemes, each corresponding to one of the three control functions: direction, evaluation, and discipline. Under each control function, several second-level subthemes were identified. We found two types of control means: AC and non-AC. The themes, subthemes, and illustrative texts are shown in Appendix B. We describe the three themes and their corresponding subthemes in the next section.

## 4 Findings

We organized our findings as shown in Figure 1. Specifically, our analysis yielded three themes— controllers, control functions, and control means—each with several subthemes. The first theme concerns controllers—that is, entities executing control. We identified three subthemes of controllers: the digital labor platform (D), the third-party intermediary (T), and the customers (C). The second theme focuses on control functions, which refer to specific types of control processes that align workers’ behaviors with the controllers’ objectives (Edwards, 1979). The three firstlevel subthemes of the control function theme are direction, evaluation, and discipline. Control mechanisms, such as dispatching, ratings, and monetary penalties, are the second-level subthemes. The third theme concerns the control means, the subthemes for which are algorithmic control (A) and non-algorithmic control (N). We describe the themes and subthemes, with illustrative interviewee quotes.

## 4.1 Controllers

A controller refers to an individual or entity who is responsible for executing control mechanisms. This theme has three subthemes: the DLP (D), the TPI (T), and customers (C), which are labelled in red in Figure 1. We next describe the three controllers and their general functions.

## 4.1.1 The Digital Labor Platform

The DLP matched and controlled riders through the app. The workers did not directly interact with DLP employees; they completed each order using the app. When a customer made a food delivery order, the app matched customers and riders by dispatching the order automatically to a rider and transmitting the order information. During the delivery process, the location of the rider was tracked by the app, and the rider was asked to report manually on the app in real time when they accepted the order, arrived at the restaurant, got the food, and delivered the order to the customer. Riders could also report unexpected events to the system, such as late delivery because of a restaurant delay. Interviewees described similar work processes in which they worked closely with and were guided by the app, as exemplified by the following excerpt:

When arriving, we need to report on the app by clicking arrive at the restaurant. … If all these things are correct, we will click food picked up and leave. If the food is not ready within 5 minutes after we get there, we will report that the process is delayed by the restaurant on the app. Once the order is delivered to the customer, we click delivered. (R6, experienced rider)

In addition to completing orders online, riders also used other functions on the app. For example, they were required to complete online exams that mainly concerned riders’ safety, they could check their real-time performance data, and they could participate in gamified reward campaigns to earn extra money.

## 4.1.2 The Third-Party Intermediary

The DLP worked with TPIs to manage riders. In Beijing, there were around 20 TPIs working with the DLP at the time of writing. Many TPI companies managed multiple rider sites. Within a site, there could be 50 to 150 riders managed by a TPI site manager. The DLP held regular inperson meetings with managers from the TPIs. Each site was responsible for a specific delivery area. The funds received by each site from the DLP varied. Specifically, the DLP distributed funds to each site based on the delivery difficulty of the area and the star rating of a specific site. SM1, a site manager, described the determinants of delivery difficulty as follows:

First, delivery work is more difficult in urban areas, where there are more office buildings, more vehicles, and more complex road conditions. Office buildings are sometimes lined up for an hour. Second, natural factors can affect delivery difficulty. For example, a river or an overpass can complicate traffic in a certain area, resulting in an increased delivery difficulty level.

SM3, another site manager, discussed the star rating evaluation:

The star rating of a site can be determined by a lot of indicators. Examples include the number of late-delivered orders of the site and the number of unsatisfactory ratings of the site.

The TPIs were responsible for recruiting, compensating, and managing riders. Platform-exclusive riders were recruited by site, and each rider was enrolled in the app as a member of a particular site. All riders were paid monthly, with a step-based pay rate determined by the

TPI. For example, the unit price of the first 500 orders may be 8 yuan, with the unit price of subsequent orders being 8.5 yuan. Third, the TPI site managers were in charge of the rider management. To ensure the profitability of a site, managers sought to balance worker performance with worker well-being. SM2, a site manager, discussed this balance:

We need to care about the needs of riders as well as the performance indicators for sites. It is the site manager's responsibility to coordinate and balance these two needs.

Moreover, SM1 stated that:

Riders need humanized management, and regulations should not always be executed excessively strictly. Given the relatively high turnover rate of riders, as a site manager, I need to play both the bad cop and the good cop. Some riders respond better to the rulebased approach, while others require more emotional support.

Similarly, SM3 said that:

The riders are directly coordinated by the site managers. The site managers are responsible for ensuring riders’ work efficiency and for their psychological well-being. Site managers and riders have personal relationships. We work together. The riders follow me, and I want to make sure they are happy at work.

The TPI site managers tracked riders’ performance, provided necessary training, conducted daily morning meetings, addressed irregular issues, mediated conflicts, provided rewards, and communicated with riders as needed. Their objective was to enhance riders’ work performance while ensuring riders’ well-being.

## 4.1.3 Customers

The DLP involved customers in the control system, aligning with their organizational value of being customer-centric. Through the app, customers were able to contact riders and track riders’ real-time locations. They could also rate riders’ performance, report unsatisfactory driver behavior, cancel orders, and leave tips for excellent service.

## 4.2 Control Functions

Control functions comprise the specific types of control processes that align workers’ behaviors with the controllers’ objectives (Edwards, 1979). Drawing on the categorization of control functions, we found three firstlevel subthemes within the theme of control functions: direction, evaluation, and discipline. Furthermore, we identified specific control mechanisms as second-level subthemes to each first-level subtheme, as shown in Figure 1 and described below.

## 4.2.1 Direction

Through the function of direction, controllers informed the workers on what needed to be done, in what order, with what degree of accuracy, and in what period of time. Under the first-level subtheme of direction, we identified six second-level subthemes: dispatching, messages, route recommendations, morning meetings, and customer instructions. These second-level subthemes were the control mechanisms associated with the control function of direction, as described below.

Dispatching: All orders were dispatched automatically by the DLP to riders. Information about each order, such as the name and address of the restaurant, the food ordered, the estimated delivery time, and the customer’s address and contact information, was provided to the rider taking the order. The automated dispatching mechanism clearly specified what to do and when. Platform-exclusive riders on the DLP were allowed to transfer up to three dispatched orders per day. If no other riders were available to accept the transferred order, the original rider would remind responsible for completing it. Therefore, as one of the site managers, SM1, clarified: “The dispatching of orders is not a recommendation because platform-exclusive riders cannot freely decline the orders.”

Additionally, site managers could dispatch irregular orders. For example, if a rider’s vehicle broke down during a delivery, the site manager had to manually redispatch the order to another rider by contacting them directly.

Dispatching orders through the system is the main way. We dispatch only irregular orders. We can only see the information of an order when it has been dispatched by the app. (SM2, site manager)

Many participants, including site managers and riders, mentioned an irregular situation for dispatching— during severe weather such as thunderstorms and snowstorms. During such conditions, the number of orders surged while the number of available riders decreased, such that each rider had a significantly higher workload, in addition to facing perilous travel conditions. Riders reported experiencing high levels of stress in these situations, with one rider stating that delivering orders felt like risking his life. In such situations, site managers played a crucial role in supporting riders:

When there is severe weather, the situation becomes quite challenging. At this time, the riders on duty can become mentally overwhelmed. In such cases, we try our best to comfort the riders. Additionally, we request [the DLP] to reduce the number of orders. (SM2, site manager)

Messages: The second control mechanism used for direction was messages. The DLP could automatically send messages to riders. For example, when a rider approached the destination, the app sent a message that they were approaching the restaurant/customer. Riders stated that this was useful because it helped them confirm that they were on the right route in real time. A personalized setting for audio notification was also available. Riders could choose to turn this function on or off on the app based on their preferences. R6, an experienced rider, stated: “When I get close to a customer, the system automatically tells me that ‘you have arrived near the customer. Do you want to call the customer?’ The message helps me determine whether I am at the correct location.”

In addition, TPI site managers could use the app to send messages to riders. For example, site managers could send messages to riders if they forgot to take online exams or if they violated important rules.

Moreover, customers could send messages to specify their needs. For example, a customer might send a message to the rider requesting that they don’t knock on the door, requesting that they place the food in a specific location, etc. This source of direction was important. R16, an experienced rider, shared that he received an unsatisfactory rating because he did not pay attention to the customer message and knocked on the door when the customer message asked him not to knock when he arrived.

Route recommendations: Another mechanism used by the DLP to direct riders is route recommendations. On the DLP, the routes to the restaurant and to the customer were recommended by algorithms. Moreover, riders were allowed to work on multiple orders at the same time. The route recommendation algorithm could plan routes for multiple orders simultaneously. Through this mechanism, the DLP could tell riders what needed to be done and in what order.

The system plans out the routes for you, indicating where you should pick the order up and where it should be delivered. When there are multiple orders, the system considers all of them and highlights each order with a different color for easy identification. (R12, new rider)

In addition, some TPI site managers offered new riders route recommendations. These managers tracked new riders’ real-time locations and called them when they were not on the correct route.

We can see the real-time location of each rider in the system. I only pay attention to new riders from the system. … If a rider is riding in the opposite direction, I will contact them immediately and tell them the right way. (SM2, site manager)

Training: Training was another effective mechanism used to direct riders. Through training, riders learn how to perform tasks correctly and efficiently, such as completing deliveries in a timely manner. Two methods of training were implemented to guide riders: training through the app and in-person training conducted by the TPI. Training through the app was mainly in the form of videos, text, and online exams. The videos introduced regular delivery processes and common disruptions. The text-based training explained rules and policies. Online exams focused more on riders’ safety. By contrast, multiple types of in-person trainings were provided at each site. Specifically, at the beginning of a rider’s career, a mentor was assigned by the site manager to provide inperson training. Mentors were experienced riders who provided new riders with guidance, support, and knowledge. They received additional rewards for their training role. The in-person training covered instructions on the use of the app, showed the locations of difficult-tofind restaurants, emphasized important rules, and answered any questions the trainee may have. Moreover, new riders gained tacit knowledge from mentors, such as how to communicate with customers/restaurants, how the algorithms worked, and how to be a good rider. Based on his experience as a mentor, R5 explained how important mentors were:

During the training, I teach new riders not only the rules but also strategies. Some riders are no longer new, but they still contact me when they have questions. One new rider said, “I only listen to my mentor. (R5, experienced rider)

On a daily basis, during non-peak times, site managers provided in-person training to riders who were struggling with performance.

In the less busy afternoon hours, we call the riders who have violated rules back to the site. We then discuss their performance, analyze the performance data, and provide guidance on how to avoid these violations in the future. (SM2, site manager)

Site managers provided explanations to riders who had questions and concerns about work. Some explanations were based on official documents, while some were based on analyses of riders’ performance data.

Some riders have a good understanding of the logic behind dispatching, while others may not be as proficient. However, when we present and analyze the performance data to the riders and explain the logic carefully to them, they start to compare and deepen their understanding. These insights are not explicitly stated by [the DLP]; rather, they arise from our ongoing data analysis efforts. (SM1, site manager)

Morning meetings: Riders were required to attend morning meetings in person every day. The morning meetings were organized by TPI site managers. SM1, a site manager, reflected on why they held morning meetings:

The purposes of the meetings include informing riders of new rules and policies, training and communicating regarding ill performance, sharing the results of monthly rewards, and explaining shared concerns and questions. (SM1, site manager)

Customer instructions: Leaving specific delivery instructions for riders was a common practice adopted by customers for direction. Once a customer finished ordering food on the app, they had the option to leave instructions for the rider. In most cases, the requests were order-related, such as bringing more napkins or prioritizing the order. Sometimes, customer instructions were not order-related, such as buying water or cigarettes from another store or throwing away trash near their front door.

Customers leave their requests in the instructions section of the app. Sometimes, a customer has some additional requests, such as bringing a bottle of beverages or bringing a bag of cigarettes. The customers asked me to make an extra stop during the delivery to get these items because the items were not available from the restaurant where they had ordered food. As a rider, I try to fulfill customers’ requests. (R16, an experienced rider)

## 4.2.2 Evaluation

Evaluation was a procedure in which the controllers supervised and evaluated riders to correct mistakes, assess worker performance, and identify individual workers’ performance. Under the subtheme of evaluation, we found four second-level subthemes: direct recorded data, performance indicators, ratings and order cancellations, and manual evaluations. These correspond to control mechanisms associated with the control function of evaluation, as described below.

Directly recorded data: Directly recorded data referred to data that were automatically recorded by the app and could immediately result in penalties. Examples of the most influential types of directly recorded data included reject an order, click delivered in advance, unsatisfactory ratings from customers, etc. These recorded data could directly lead to a penalty. These data were used by both the DLP and the TPI.

Performance indicators: Performance indicators were algorithmically calculated values used to evaluate worker performance. Examples of performance indicators used by the DLP and the TPI include duration of work, on-time rate, average rating, number of orders, total delivery distance, level, ranking, etc. For instance, the level of a rider was determined by credits—the more credits a rider received, the higher their level would be. Shown on the app, the credits were determined by the length of service, duration of work, performance in bad weather, and unsatisfactory ratings from customers. Another example of a performance indicator was ranking, which referred to a rider’s position in comparison with other riders based on their performance. Some performance indicators were accessible by riders, such as the daily number of orders, level, and ranking. Some indicators were recorded by the system but were not accessible to riders, such as average time of delivery and job tenure. The DLP and the TPI used performance indicators to assess the work of each rider and to provide corresponding rewards or training.

Ratings and order cancellations: Customers could evaluate worker performance for each order, and their assessments provided influential evaluation metrics for the DLP. Ratings and order cancellations were two examples. During our research period, the DLP adopted a sequential rating system. Specifically, for each order, the customer reported their satisfaction with the service (satisfactory or unsatisfactory) and then provided reasons for unsatisfactory service, such as bad service attitude, food spilled, late delivery, etc. Customers could also describe the specific reasons underlying unsatisfactory ratings. SM3 described the current rating system as follows:

After the rider completes an order, the customer can indicate whether their experience is satisfactory or unsatisfactory. After that, the app will provide unsatisfactory rating labels, such as lack of meals, late delivery, and poor service attitude. Customers can also describe their specific reasons for their dissatisfaction.

The specific rating labels were considered differently. While some rating results, such as late delivery, were subject to arguments and possible exclusion from the DLP, others, such as poor service attitudes, were undeniable. Such unnegotiable feedback could make riders feel helpless.

We can’t report poor service attitude ratings. A customer was quarantined during the COVID-19 pandemic, and he left an instruction asking me to take his trash away. Should I? I did not, and I received an unsatisfactory rating, which could not be eliminated. (R2, experienced rider, used to be the Delivery King of the site)

Customers could also cancel an order for a selected reason, such as slow delivery. Once an order was cancelled, the app would determine whose fault it was. If the rider was deemed responsible, a substantial fine would be imposed.

Customers’ feedback was important to both the DLP and the TPI. The customers’ feedback data, that is, ratings and order cancellations, were recorded by the system and acted as directly recorded data and performance indicators. For example, if a rider received an unsatisfactory rating from a customer due to poor service attitude, they would receive a fine immediately. Furthermore, their performance evaluation results would be negatively affected.

Manual evaluations: In addition to the mechanisms listed above, TPI site managers had their own assessments of riders off the app. These evaluations played a complementary role to the online evaluations.

Morning meeting attendance was an example of a manual evaluation conducted by TPI site managers. An experienced rider, R8, stated: “We have morning meetings. Unless there are exceptional circumstances, it is mandatory for us to attend morning meetings every day.”

Level of collaboration was another example of the TPI site managers’ manual evaluations. This indicator referred to the extent to which a rider accepted requests from the site. For example, if a rider accepted manually dispatched orders more frequently, which were normally not popular, their level of collaboration would be perceived as high. According to SM3, a site manager, “I can tell whether a rider is collaborative based on how often they accept manually dispatched orders from me during busy times. After all, nobody wants to work on an additional order when it is a busy time.”

## 4.2.3 Discipline

Discipline was used to punish and reward workers to elicit cooperation and enforce compliance with the process. As described below, under the first-level subtheme of discipline, we identified seven secondlevel subthemes: monetary penalties, non-monetary penalties, monetary rewards, non-monetary rewards, customer tips, gamified rewards, and algorithmic rewarding. These were the specific control mechanisms used for discipline.

Penalties: Riders would receive penalties when their work performance did not comply with DLP or TPI direction. All the control mechanisms for penalties (both monetary and non-monetary) were implemented by the TPI.

Monetary penalties were the most frequently used penalty mechanisms. The major reason why a TPI imposed fines on its riders was that the TPI was charged by the DLP. Specifically, the DLP would penalize the TPI when a rider within the site rejected an order, clicked delivered in advance, spilled food, delivered late, received an unsatisfactory customer rating, received a cancellation request from a customer, etc. The TPI then set rules to financially penalize riders accordingly. The DLP set a maximum threshold for the TPI on how much a rider could be charged to ensure that riders were not charged excessively. Fine details were shown on riders’ monthly pay stubs, which were provided by the TPI. Riders could request an explanation of the charge from site managers.

Riders were sensitive to the fairness of penalties. If they perceived decisions to be unjust, they could ask the site manager to file an appeal to the DLP. This process provided riders with a channel to make their voices heard.

The rider's accountability is determined automatically by the system. We can file appeals for reasons such as the pandemic or malicious users. However, the approval rate of these appeals is low because it is difficult to provide evidence. (SM1, site manager)

Site managers also used non-monetary penalties, such as replacing and handwriting rules. Replacing refers to firing underperforming workers and replacing them with substitute workers (Kellogg, 2020). TPI site managers decided whether a rider should be replaced according to the rules made by the TPI and the DLP. For example, a rider might be fired because of a positive or improperly uploaded COVID test result (DLP rule). If a rider was absent from work for more than 10 consecutive days, they might also subject to termination (TPI rule).

Even if you’re going through a challenging situation that prevents you from working, the site manager will fire you after a 10-day absence from work. The site manager is required to follow specific rules, and he ha to fire a rider under these circumstances. (R8, experienced rider)

Another example of a non-monetary penalty mechanism was handwriting rules. Specifically, site managers could ask riders who violated the rules to repeatedly write down the rules.

If the performance of a rider is poor, the rider may be required to copy some rules at the site. Some riders just don’t want to write. It is tougher for some riders to repeatedly write down the rules than to pay for fines. (SM1, site manager)

Rewards: The TPI offered both monetary and nonmonetary rewards to their riders. Monetary rewards included monthly attendance bonuses for riders with perfect attendance, as well as monthly bonuses for topperforming riders (referred to as Delivery Kings) and well-behaved riders, as selected by site managers. Opportunities for promotions were a type of nonmonetary reward provided by the TPI. Specifically, experienced riders with good performance could be promoted as team leaders or site mentors. Promoted riders could receive extra compensation as well as a higher sense of achievement.

I used to be a rider, and now I have been promoted to a team leader. I am thinking about getting further promoted, for which I need to get more experience. (R5, experienced rider)

Furthermore, riders could receive customer tips through the app for providing good delivery service. However, this was not a major component of rider rewards, considering their relatively low frequency and amounts.

I have received tips from customers with a total amount of around 190 yuan during the past two years. (R1, experienced rider, previously the site Delivery King)

The DLP occasionally provided gamified rewards as shortterm monetary rewards on the app. Riders needed to consistently achieve goals every day to receive a cumulative incentive by the end of the campaign period.

For example, if there is a campaign for a week, in addition to the payment you get from each order as usual, the app will reward you with additional money if you can deliver a certain number of orders within this week. (R11, experienced rider)

Algorithmic rewarding: The dispatching rate, calculated by DLP algorithms, represented a significant algorithmic rewarding. This mechanism immediately rewarded workers who complied with algorithmic assignments with more work, higher pay, and increased flexibility (Kellogg et al., 2020). The dispatching rate represented the priority of getting an order from the app. The higher a rider’s dispatching rate, the more likely it was that the DLP would dispatch orders to them, even if there were few orders. Through this mechanism, orders were dispatched based on riders’ performance, such that higher-performing riders would receive more orders and thus higher payment (as a reward), while lower-performing riders would have fewer opportunities and thus earn less (as a punishment). R1, an experienced rider, explained the dispatching rate:

Whoever has a better dispatching rate can get more orders. Like yesterday, there was a rider at our site who delivered 8 orders a day. I delivered 24 orders. He just has a low dispatching rate. (R1, experienced rider, used to be the Delivery King of the site)

Dispatching rate was a term used among riders and TPI site managers. However, the mechanism behind it had not been explained by the DLP. According to the riders’ guesses, the dispatching rate was determined by their previous work performance. However, riders’ guesses on how performance was assessed differed from rider to rider. For instance, some riders believed that the dispatching rate was influenced by the rider’s level, while others thought that their work hours played a role. Here are some examples of different guesses from the riders we interviewed.

The higher the level of a rider, the better the delivery rate. (R13, new rider)

Many algorithms are based on work hours. The longer the online work hours are, the higher the dispatching rate will be. (R2, experienced rider, used to be the Delivery King of the site)

By data, I mean the number of late-delivered orders. The more late-delivered orders you have, the fewer orders will be dispatched to you; therefore, the lower your dispatching rate is. (R1, experienced rider, used to be the Delivery King of the site)

I pay much attention to customer feedback. The fewer unsatisfactory ratings you have received, the higher your dispatching rate will be. (R7, experienced rider)

The dispatching rate is determined by multiple factors. I personally feel that the speed of order delivery matters the most. (R8, experienced rider)

## 4.3 Control Means: Algorithmic and Non-Algorithmic

The third theme is control means, which considers how control information is decided. This theme consists of two subthemes: AC and non-AC. When algorithms are used to decide control information, the control mechanism is categorized as an AC mechanism. By contrast, control mechanisms that rely on human decision-making for control information are categorized as non-AC mechanisms. In Figure 1, we label these two control means in blue as A (AC) and N (non-AC).

## 4.3.1 Algorithmic Control

In the AC mechanisms, controllers used algorithms to automatically determine control information. Specifically, controllers used algorithms to automatically direct riders through dispatching, route recommendations, messages, and training. They evaluated riders based on directly recorded data and performance indicators. The AC mechanisms adopted for discipline were algorithmic rewarding and gamified rewards. The mechanisms listed above shared similar characteristics. First, they were highly structured— that is, their procedures could be clearly defined. Second, they were repeated, that is, executed many times in the same way. Third, their frequency of occurrence was high.

## 4.3.2 Non-Algorithmic Control

For non-AC mechanisms, human controllers determined control information without using algorithms. For example, TPI managers determined the topics and agenda for the morning meetings by themselves instead of using algorithms. Specific non-AC mechanisms for direction included dispatching, messages, route recommendations, training, morning meetings, and customer instructions.

Ratings and order cancellations represented non-AC mechanisms for evaluation executed by customers. For discipline, non-AC mechanisms for penalties and rewards were executed, such as fines, replacing, monthly bonuses, promotions, and customer tips.

Compared with AC mechanisms, non-AC mechanisms were less structured. Furthermore, they were more subjective, which required the controller to incorporate interpersonal criteria. For instance, TPI managers often relied on interpersonal criteria, such as teamwork and communication skills, when evaluating workers and selecting candidates for promotion. These criteria could not be easily quantified. Moreover, non-AC mechanisms required sensitivity because they impacted workers’ personal lives and could lead to negative emotions. For example, when workers were fired, their income was abruptly terminated, and they were thus likely to experience negative emotions, such as frustration and anger.

## 5 Discussion

Drawing on our findings, we theorize how digital platform work is controlled. We develop a partitioning model of control in digital platform work, as shown in Figure 2. We define partitioning of control in digital platform work as distributing control in digital platform work between (1) multiple controllers (the DLP, TPIs, and customers) and (2) control by algorithms and humans. The goal of the first, which we term controller partitioning, is to address the complexity of control in digital platform work, which stems from factors such as the large number of workers (Rai et al., 2019), the high flexibility of work (Spreitzer et al., 2017), and worker resistance (Kellogg et al., 2020). The goals of the second, which we term control means partitioning, are to address both the above complexity and the problems associated with AC, including algorithmic opacity (Rahman, 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023), unfair decisions without considering contextual information (Wiener et al., 2023), and negative impacts on worker wellbeing (Cram et al., 2022; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Tarafdar et al., 2023; Wiener et al., 2023). In Figure 2, controllers and controlees (the DLP, the TPI, customers, and workers) are marked by blue boxes. Arrows represent control relationships. Solid arrows represent direct control mechanisms (where the rules of control mechanisms are independently made by the controller), and dotted arrows indicate indirect control mechanisms (where the rules of control mechanisms are not independently made by the controller). The three control functions, i.e., direction, evaluation, and discipline, are implicitly present in all control relationships.

## 5.1 Controller Partitioning

As our literature review shows, in traditional AWAs, thirdparty agencies, such as staffing agencies, are responsible for brokering the market, finding contract workers, and serving as the employer of record (Barley & Kunda, 2004). In digital platform work, DLPs themselves can efficiently match supply and demand (Constantinides et al., 2018; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2016). However, and in contrast, they face a different set of challenges due to the complexity of control, as explained in Section 2.3.1. To tackle these challenges, the DLP uses algorithms to execute direct rational control over the riders. Rational control implies that managers should obtain desired behaviors from workers using clearly articulated methods and systems (Barley & Kunda, 1992). The DLP does this in two ways. First, it implements supervision through algorithmic control mechanisms, such as dispatching, route recommendations, directly recorded data on worker behavior, and performance indicators. Second, it uses algorithms to communicate and enforce a formal system of rules, procedures, and roles to guide worker behavior through control mechanisms, such as messages, training, gamified rewards, and algorithmic rewarding. The ostensible goal of the DLP’s control is to standardize and restrict worker behavior through the preset work pace and rules, monitor worker behavior, and conduct consistent control on a large number of workers.

Figure 2. Partitioning Model of Control in Digital Platform Work

However, the DLP’s rational control is inadequate, leaving three key control challenges insufficiently addressed. First, it does not address the individual needs of each worker, such as providing training based on context-specific factors, such as traffic conditions, weather, restaurant density, and individual riders experience, expectations for work, and personality. Second, it cannot comprehensively assess worker quality. While directly recorded worker behavioral data can assess performance metrics, such as on-time rates and negative ratings, they cannot measure other important indicators of worker quality, such as workers communication skills. Third, it does not address the resistance of workers because it does not enable the DLP to directly engage with them. Furthermore, this resistance often occurs offline and goes unseen by the DLP, making it an even more pressing issue. One way in which the DLP addresses the limitations of its direct rational control is through controller partitioning, that is, by assigning control roles to TPIs and customers.

## 5.1.1 The Role of Third-Party Intermediaries

TPIs have two roles. The first is to extend the control of the DLP by introducing new control mechanisms that complement those of the DLP through the direct normative and rational control of the workers. TPIs conduct direct normative control by managing workers in small groups, which allows them to attend to the individual needs of each worker and tackle worker resistance. For example, experienced riders are assigned as mentors to the groups. They offer personalized in-person training to new riders and build personal bonds with them. As another example, site managers talk with riders who are having work performance problems to help them find ways to improve. To address worker resistance, TPIs build interpersonal relationships with riders to make them feel less commoditized, shielding them from isolation and loneliness. They shore up workers’ well-being during stressful periods, such as food delivery during severe weather. We note here that the literature suggests that normative control can address the diminished organizational integration resulting from rational control by enabling managers to engage with workers’ attitudes, beliefs, and emotions (Kellogg et al., 2020; Barley & Kunda, 1992). TPIs conduct direct rational control by developing rules for control mechanisms, such as manual evaluation (e.g., level of collaboration by riders), monetary rewards (e.g., Delivery King designation), non-monetary rewards (e.g., promotion), and non-monetary penalties (e.g., handwriting). These rules are more flexible than the rules used by the DLP in their direct rational control over the riders and allow TPIs to better address the individual needs of each worker and provide a more comprehensive assessment of worker quality.

The second role of TPIs is to reinforce the control of the DLP through indirect rational control, such that the rules of certain control mechanisms are not independently made by TPIs but are determined by the DLP. These control mechanisms include performance indicators, directly recorded data, and monetary penalties. The DLP allocates funding to TPIs based on the automatically recorded and calculated performance data of their riders, such as their on-time rate and average ratings. TPIs evaluate riders’ performance using the same indicators to obtain more favorable evaluations and more funding from the DLP. TPIs impose monetary penalties on riders because they face the same penalties from the DLP. For example, when riders within their sites have late delivery and unsatisfactory ratings, TPIs face penalties from the DLP. Consequently, TPIs impose fines on such riders. Through such indirect rational control, TPIs reinforce the DLP’s control over riders and further enable consistent control among all workers. To facilitate the TPI’s indirect control over the worker, the DLP executes direct rational control over the TPIs. Specifically, the DLP directs TPIs by holding regular inperson meetings, evaluates the TPIs based on their workers’ performance indicators and directly recorded data, and disciplines the TPIs using monetary rewards and penalties. Through these direct rational control mechanisms, the DLP ensures goal alignment between the TPIs’ control over riders and the DLP’s control over riders. We note here that the relationship between the TPI and the DLP is different from that between a staffing agency and a firm in traditional AWA settings, where a staffing agency may work with multiple firms because it functions as a labor broker (Barley & Kunda, 2004). However, in our case, TPIs worked exclusively with one DLP, such that the relationship between the DLP and TPIs is hierarchical, with TPIs functioning as subcontrollers under the DLP.

## 5.1.2 The Role of Customers

Customers extend the control of the DLP through direct rational control of the workers through the app. They introduce new control mechanisms that complement those of the DLP and TPIs. Specifically, they direct riders by leaving personalized and contextualized instructions and messages, and reward riders by offering tips for good service. These control mechanisms address the individual needs and circumstances of each worker. Customers also evaluate riders through ratings and order cancellations. These control mechanisms provide additional evaluation metrics for a more comprehensive assessment of worker quality. The DLP enables and shapes customers’ control over workers through platform design that incorporates control-related features (e.g., instructions, ratings, and tips) and makes them available to customers through the app interface. Customers can provide ratings only in the format specified on the app by the DLP, such as a star rating format or a satisfactory/unsatisfactory format.

## 5.2 Control Means Partitioning

The second aspect of control partitioning is highlighted by the division of control between AC and non-AC. Contrary to the literature, which largely considers algorithms being used for control in digital platform work, we find that not all control mechanisms are executed by algorithms. Through control means partitioning, the DLP tackles both the complexity of control in digital platform work and the problems associated with AC.

With regard to tackling the complexity of control in digital platform work, AC makes it possible to implement efficient control among all workers because algorithms can efficiently and consistently handle a large quantity and variety of worker data. Through AC, the DLP conducts dispatching, messages, directly recorded data, and algorithmic rewarding in a consistent manner for all workers. AC further tackles the challenge of monitoring worker behaviors in a highly flexible work environment by facilitating the real-time accessibility of a variety of worker data, such as worker location, online or offline status, length of work shifts, etc. AC also enables the consistency of control between different controllers (i.e., the DLP and TPIs) through data sharing. However, AC cannot tackle the complexity of control in digital platform work in its entirety, making non-AC necessary. First, the challenge of attending to individual needs is addressed by non-AC. For example, TPIs provide in-person training to address riders’ personal needs, and customers provide order-specific instructions to riders. Second, the challenge of providing comprehensive assessments of workers is addressed by non-AC mechanisms implemented by human managers, such as manual evaluation (e.g., level of collaboration) and nonmonetary rewards (e.g., promotion). Such assessments require decisions using intuition, common sense, judgment, and creativity, where AC is inadequate.

Additionally, AC by itself has inherent shortcomings that require non-AC. To address the issue of the opacity of AC, human controllers are needed to respond to riders’ questions or concerns. Their explanations can be based on official documents from the DLP, analyses of workers’ performance data, and managers’ interpretations. To address the problem of unfair decisions, human controllers can help workers file appeals to the DLP. Making their voice heard when they perceive unfair treatment can help workers feel empowered and supported in seeking fair treatment. Human controllers can also develop personal bonds, alleviate workers’ isolation and loneliness, and support work-related well-being, which research shows is a fallout of AC (Anicich, 2022; Möhlmann & Henfridsson, 2019; Walker et al., 2021; Wood et al., 2019). That being said, incorporating non-AC does not completely solve all problems. For instance, a rider might not understand how AC works even after receiving an explanation from the site manager. Similarly, a decision about a fine might continue to be perceived as unfair even after an appeal to the platform.

Through control means partitioning, we theorize a human-in-the-loop approach to control in digital platform work. The human-in-the-loop approach has been applied in traditional organizations, where algorithms are applied to tasks, automating wellstructured tasks, such as completing invoices, and augmenting complex and ambiguous tasks, such as diagnosing diseases (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Lebovitz et al., 2021; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). Our theorization, in contrast, focuses on control in DLPs, which are non-traditional organizations. While tasks involve a worker and an operation, such as completing invoices or diagnosing diseases, control involves a controller and a controlee, between whom there may be tension—while controllers aim to ensure productivity and efficiency, workers seek to retain their power and resist being treated like a commodity (Edwards, 1979). Therefore, the human-in-the-loop approach we suggest with respect to control has implications for humanistic considerations.

Extending the notion of algorithms as co-workers (Tarafdar et al., 2023), we unpack how the algorithm functions in the controller role through AC mechanisms, such as dispatching, performance indicators, and algorithmic rewarding. Moreover, we also theorize how DLPs can repair the broken feedback loop (Tarafdar et al., 2023) by responding to workers’ affective experiences and by incorporating non-AC mechanisms for control in digital platform work. Through non-AC mechanisms, such as in-person training and morning meetings, humans can provide informational and emotional support when workers are confused about incompatible requirements and can provide more personalized explanations gathered from multiple sources of information.

We also link to the concept of algorithmic bureaucracy (Ma & Kou, 2022) regarding creator moderation on video-sharing platforms, where algorithms manage creators’ income, content visibility, and identities through actions, such as algorithmic recommendations and algorithmic monetization. Algorithmic bureaucracy thus automates creator moderation through the logic embedded in these algorithms. These platforms also implement human support and interpretation to resolve queries and complaints with regard to the automated creator moderation for more privileged content creators. Algorithmic bureaucracy thus describes content creators sharing content on video-sharing platforms as being managed by algorithms and humans. Our theorization of control partitioning, in contrast, explains how and why different controllers and different types of control means are integrated in the control of workers on digital labor platforms.

## 6 Contributions and Implications

We examined digital platform work in the context of an app-based food delivery platform to investigate how workers are controlled by multiple controllers and how humans and algorithms work together for control in digital platform work. Our analysis yielded three themes: controllers (the DLP, TPIs, and customers), control functions (direction, evaluation, and discipline), and control means (AC and non-AC). Through these themes, we investigated how workers are controlled by multiple controllers, and how both AC and non-AC are used. Specifically, we developed the partitioning model of control, with two types of control partitioning: (1) controller partitioning through the DLP, TPIs, and customers, and (2) control means partitioning through AC and non-AC. We next describe the study’s theoretical contributions, the boundary conditions of our theorizing, directions for future research, and practical implications for DLP managers and policymakers.

## 6.1 Theoretical Contributions

We contribute to the IS literature on digital platform work by introducing the novel theoretical perspective of control partitioning to better understand control in such work. To our knowledge, this is the first study that problematizes the literature’s focus on the DLP as the sole controller and AC as the sole means of control in digital platform work. Our study develops a novel theoretical framing that explains the role of different controllers and non-AC means of control to take the literature forward.

The existing literature has examined the DLP as the primary controller. Our study, in contrast, reveals a more complex control ecosystem where the DLP executes control mechanisms together with other controllers. We show that the DLP’s control of the worker through algorithms is limited to rational control, which cannot fully tackle the complexity of control, including the need to control a large number of riders (workers), their high work flexibility, and worker resistance. To overcome this, the DLP works with other controllers. In particular, TPIs act as third-party agencies (Barley & Kunda, 2004; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020) between the DLP and workers, controlling workers on behalf of the DLP. We conceptualize the mechanisms of TPIs’ control as extending (i.e., executing additional direct normative and rational control over the worker that is not executed by the DLP) and reinforcing (i.e., executing indirect rational control over the worker that strengthens the DLP’s own control).

Scholars have called for new forms of human work to support algorithmic control (Gal et al., 2020; Kellogg et al., 2020). Our study responds to this call by theorizing how control by intermediaries can support and expand the control assumed by DLPs in digital platform work. In a general sense, controller partitioning distributes control in digital platform work among different controllers, with the additional controllers extending and/or reinforcing the DLP’s control. Specifically, as the platform owner and app deployer, DLPs can execute rational AC for consistent and structured control mechanisms, such as automatic dispatching and performance recording, thus focusing on workers’ productivity. In contrast, other controllers can manage workers in smaller groups and execute additional normative control through mechanisms such as training, and rational control through mechanisms such as non-monetary rewards (e.g., promotions), thus focusing on both the productivity and well-being of workers. Through this contribution, we advance the control literature, which has largely focused on the context of managers and workers who are employees in the same organization and where control unfolds in the setting of established bureaucratic systems. We introduce a hybrid control configuration that spans multiple organizational entities (the DLP and TPIs) and meshes AC and non-AC for the control of workers on DLPs. We contribute to emerging conversations on control in platform-mediated multi-entity settings (Rahman & Valentine, 2021).

In traditional AWA settings, staffing agencies help firms address market-brokering challenges, such as high search costs for contract workers (Barley & Kunda, 2004). Our study extends this literature by revealing the role of external agencies in control in digital platform work. We do so by explaining how TPIs help DLPs tackle challenges arising from factors such as the large number of workers, difficulty in monitoring workers due to high work flexibility, and worker resistance. In so doing, we highlight that the involvement of third-party agencies is changing the nature of digital platform work, as compared to the simpler worker-firm arrangement depicted in Table 1, and thereby contribute to the literature on AWAs (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020; Abraham et al., 2021). When the DLP serves as the only controller, contract workers are hired, supervised, and paid by the DLP and are controlled solely by algorithms. With the introduction of TPIs as additional controllers, contract workers are hired, supervised, and paid by TPIs. While they continue to complete their tasks through algorithms, they are jointly controlled by both the TPI (the paying firm) and the DLP.

The literature on digital platform work has also focused primarily on control mechanisms used by algorithms (Cram et al., 2022; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Wiener et al., 2023). The notion of control means partitioning addresses the complexity of control on DLPs, including the need to control a large number of workers, the high levels of work flexibility, and potential worker resistance (Rai et al., 2019; Spreitzer et al., 2017; Kellogg et al., 2020; Möhlmann et al., 2021). It also addresses the drawbacks of AC, such as opacity of algorithmic outputs, ambiguous decisions made by algorithms that workers perceive as unfair, and diminished worker well-being (Binns, 2022; Cram et al., 2022; Tarafdar et al., 2023) by integrating algorithmic and human capabilities. We thus theorize a human-in-the-loop approach to control in digital platform work. Furthermore, the literature on digital platform work suggests that workers on DLPs may suffer from algorithm-driven role conflict and role ambiguity when they face algorithms as co-workers (Tarafdar et al., 2023). We conceptualize non-AC as a potential approach to tackling these issues. Overall, we theorize how the DLP’s rational control through AC mechanisms can be counterbalanced by TPIs’ normative control, making for a more humanistic control configuration.

## 6.2 Boundary Conditions and Future Research

Our theorization of control partitioning can be generalized to other digital platform work contexts because the complexity of control on DLPs and the problems of AC are common to different DLPs. However, DLPs vary in terms of factors such as the nature of work, platform design and the capabilities of algorithms, worker autonomy, market competition and stakeholder interests, and regulatory contexts (Constantinides et al., 2018; Duggan et al., 2020; Rahman & Valentine, 2021; Deng & Galliers, 2024). Consequently, future research should investigate potential variations in the implementation of control partitioning across different settings, as explicated below.

First, the differing nature of work in DLPs may lead to different roles for third-party agencies. While workers on app-based platforms perform tasks individually, there is increasing recognition that workers on crowd work platforms such as Upwork can collaborate with each other because their projects often require diverse skills or involve complex tasks that could benefit from collective input (Upwork, 2023). This introduces a new layer of complexity of control, highlighting the need for research to examine other roles of third-party agencies in the context of such DLPs. The nature of work can also influence the extent of control experienced by the worker. Tasks on app-based platforms, such as food delivery and ridesharing, are standardized and welldefined, as compared to those on crowd work platforms, such as MTurk, which are more varied and difficult to predict. As a result, workers may perceive the extent of control on crowd work platforms to be limited compared to app-based platforms. Investigating such a varying extent of control presents an interesting direction for future research.

Second, platform design can drive the involvement of additional controllers, with access to the app provided to different types of users. For example, restaurants play a critical role in app-based food delivery platforms—they sell food to consumers and work with delivery workers to facilitate its transport (Deng & Galliers, 2024). DLPs could assign a controller role to restaurants, allowing them to rate the performance of delivery workers on the app, similar to customers. These ratings would offer an additional perspective on worker performance, extending the directly recorded data from the DLP’s algorithms, manual evaluation from TPIs, and assessments from customers. Investigating the role of such additional controllers could further enrich the concept of controller partitioning. Relatedly, the capability of algorithms used by DLPs influences the implementation of control means partitioning. As more advanced algorithms are developed and used, AC may be able to better tackle the challenges of control complexity of DLPs, so that the distribution between AC and non-AC mechanisms can evolve. Such evolving arrangements could be further investigated.

Third, with regard to worker autonomy, workers affiliated with multiple DLPs (in contrast to our context of study) could compare control practices and the extent of control. They could also develop a better understanding of their work conditions and be less dependent on any single DLP. These factors would enhance worker autonomy, suggesting that high worker resistance could be a more significant challenge in such contexts. Greater emphasis on normative control may be needed, offering a future research opportunity to explore additional normative control mechanisms. More autonomous workers are also less likely to tolerate problems associated with $\mathrm { A C } ,$ such as unfair decisions and detrimental impacts on worker well-being, potentially leading to the need for less use of AC and more involvement of human managers.

Fourth, industry competition can influence relationships among different DLPs in the same industry, with implications for controller partitioning. In our case, the DLP we targeted is the largest food delivery platform in China, giving it significant power. Thus, in our context, the TPIs worked exclusively with one DLP, functioning as subcontrollers beneath it. However, for DLPs facing more competition, TPIs may work with multiple DLPs and DLPs may work with multiple TPIs, meaning that DLPs may have to contend with the interests of these stakeholders. TPI actions, such as platform switching, may influence the DLP’s control over TPIs and TPIs’ control over workers. Settings with industry competition represent an interesting area of future research.

Fifth, and relatedly, industry labor laws may impose stricter regulations on working with third-party agencies. For instance, labor laws in the U.S. include regulations that support workers’ rights when workers are supervised and controlled by multiple entities (U.S. Department of Labor, 2016). In such cases, DLPs may need to adjust their strategies for working with third-party agencies by finding new ways to distribute control mechanisms. The division between AC and non-AC could also be influenced by regulations related to artificial intelligence (AI). For instance, the EU AI Act requires entities that develop AI algorithms (including DLPs) to ensure that users (including workers) possess adequate AI literacy (European Union, 2024). In these contexts, DLPs will need to explore how to facilitate AI literacy, considering the technical knowledge, experience, education, and training of their workers. As a result, DLPs may need to adjust the extent and implementation of AC, which has implications for control means partitioning.

Sixth, it is also important to consider how control partitioning changes over time as an important aspect of the complexity of control in digital platform work. Specifically, future research could build on and contextualize the concept of control balancing (Gregory et al., 2013) to investigate dynamic processes with regard to how the mix of control mechanisms (rational/normative), control means (AC/non-AC), and the roles of different controllers (e.g., the DLP, TPIs, customers) shifts over time to balance DLP workers’ wellbeing and performance.

Future research could also investigate the potential challenges and unexpected consequences of control partitioning. With respect to controller partitioning, such challenges could include the diverging and competing goals of different controllers and how they shape control mechanisms. We did not find evidence of conflicting goals between the DLP and the TPI in our study. However, literature on third-party agencies (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) suggests that it can be problematic when the contracting organization and third-party agencies hold different goals. It would be interesting to examine how control in digital platform work unfolds when DLPs and TPIs have conflicting goals. Future research could also examine problems associated with divided responsibility among different controllers, which could potentially lead to a lack of coordination in control, insufficient understanding of risks to workers, and the failure to take sufficient responsibility for worker health and well-being (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Regarding control means partitioning, we do not claim that critiques of AC can be entirely resolved through augmentation by non-AC, and we acknowledge that the use of non-AC in digital platform work may also generate unexpected consequences. For example, it could be associated with problems of human bias and the abuse of power. Indeed, historically, new forms of control that emerge to address contemporary challenges often create new ones in turn (Edwards, 1979). We suggest that future research should examine potential problematic issues associated with the use of non-AC on DLPs and explore how such issues can be addressed, with particular attention paid to the notions of work alienation and worker well-being. Additionally, issues related to transparency, fairness, and well-being exist both for non-AC (Edwards, 1979) and AC (Barati & Ansari, 2022; Binns, 2022; Ma & Kou, 2022; Pignot, 2023). Future research should thus examine how AC and non-AC means can interact and explore the consequent effects of this interaction on control in digital platform work.

## 6.3 Implications for Practice and Policy

Our research has significant practical implications for DLP managers. First, we suggest that DLPs should consider incorporating additional controllers that can extend the DLP’s rational and algorithmic control and make up for the latter’s shortcomings. Such controllers could be intermediaries, such as TPIs, as well as customers. Second, managers of DLPs should recognize the limitations of AC. Although AC is commonly used on DLPs, it is needed to complement it with non-AC to mitigate problems related to algorithmic opacity, unfair judgments, and the erosion of autonomy and privacy, thus enabling workers to better align their behaviors with DLP objectives. Third, we suggest that the decisions regarding AC and non-AC need to be made based on the fit between the capabilities of algorithms and the nature of control mechanisms. Specifically, algorithms can be used for efficient and consistent rational control applied to a large number of workers, while control measures that address individual needs, require holistic assessments, or focus on workers’ wellbeing should be undertaken by human managers. When there are complex and emotionally impactful control mechanisms at play, humans should be in the loop. Finally, our study highlights that digital platform work draws its lineage from a tradition of the changing nature of work and should thus be guided by accompanying labor market policies that focus on worker welfare. Our research has the potential to benefit policymakers in the labor market domain by helping them recognize and understand the nuances of digital platform work for the development of informed and effective labor policies. Such policies could require DLPs to disclose information about automated AC processes, including how the algorithms work, and to clarify the locus of responsibility for providing worker benefits, such as health insurance and retirement plans.

Monideepa Tarafdar is the Charles J. Dockendorff Endowed Professor at Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst. Her current research interests include algorithmic management, technology and well-being, algorithmic/AI bias, and AI/human-in-the loop configurations. She has received research grants of over \$1.5 million as principal investigator from, among others, Leverhulme Trust (UK) and the Economic and Social Science Research Council (UK), and has collaborated on research with organizations in the US, UK, EU, and India.
