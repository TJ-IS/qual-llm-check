# On Influence in Online Creative Craft Communities and the Relevance of Virtuosity and Community Engagement

Gorkem Turgut Ozer,<sup>1</sup> Anand Gopal,<sup>2</sup> Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Paul College of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire, USA, GT.Ozer@unh.edu <sup>2</sup>Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore, anandasivam.gopal@ntu.edu.sg <sup>3</sup>McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, USA, Sirkka.Jarvenpaa@mccombs.utexas.edu

## Abstract

Creative craft communities have existed for years, allowing craftsmen to meet, interact, and share knowledge as they create products with aesthetic and utilitarian value. In recent years, these communities have moved online. In this paper, we study one such online creative craft community, Ravelry, a community devoted to fiber arts such as knitting and crocheting. Combining prior research on creative craft, online communities, and reputation building, we examine how influence is established by the designers in a creative craft community. Specifically, we argue that designers as artist-craftsmen are likely to achieve influence by presenting highly sought-after craft designs for reuse by others in the community. Using panel data on designers collected from Ravelry, we show that artistic influence, as measured by the extent of design reuse, is positively associated with the extent to which the designer demonstrates virtuosity, in terms of design difficulty and creative variety across product categories. Furthermore, we show that community engagement positively moderates the relationship between virtuosity and design reuse. Theoretically, this study contributes to our understanding of how social norms shape community dynamics as creative craft communities move online and how designers as artist-craftsmen take on the role of influencers.

Keywords: Online Communities, Creative Craft, Artist-Craftsmen, Influence, Virtuosity, Community Engagement, Co-creation, Econometrics

## 1 Introduction

Online creative craft communities exist within the larger context of creative craft and craftsmanship (Kroezen et al., 2021). <sup>1</sup> These online communities reflect the elements that are typically associated with the broader creative craft ethos, which is based on a humanist and holistic approach to product or service design and delivery combined with a clear creative element (Holmes, 2015; Ravasi et al., 2025). Members of such communities focus on enhancing mastery of technique, form, and utility. Creative craft is a uniquely human combination of “knowledge, power, skill” with a “sense of wisdom” and “resourcefulness” in work (Ravasi et al., 2025). Creative craft-based communities can be traditionally found in many different sectors—from songwriting (Smolka & Heugens, 2022) to gourmet cooking (Louisgrand & Islam, 2021), photography (Treadaway, 2007), and glass making (Comunian & England, 2019). Common to all of these sectors is an appreciation for how crafted products are designed, in terms of both how aesthetically pleasing they are and how useful they are. Thus, within the craft context, judgments of quality typically seen in manufactured goods are replaced with an appreciation not only for the finished crafted product but also for the process of crafting and recognition of the craftsman’s expertise and influence on the community.

Building on this reasoning, within creative craft communities, craftsmen retain a great deal of importance since they concurrently influence activities within the community while also providing benchmarks for developing mastery over the craft process itself. Creative craft communities are associated with a set of norms that are shaped by and result from the behavior of craftsmen—skills development leading to mastery, community engagement, intrinsic motivation, informal learning, and a commitment to innovation while remaining within the craft philosophy (Becker, 1978; Kroezen et al., 2021; Sennett, 2008; Thorlindsson et al., 2018). These norms that develop within the community are distinctive since they are rooted in the dynamic identity that individuals adopt as they join the community (Oyserman, 2009). Therefore, regardless of who these individuals are in their private lives, when they join a creative craft community, they identify with the role of a craftsman and take on the motivations and norms inherent to that identity. In this paper, we thus focus our attention on the behavior of craftsmen within creative craft communities—in particular, online creative craft communities.

While physical locations like craft guilds, marketplaces, galleries, and exhibitions served as appropriate forums for such communities in the past (Becker, 1978), several craft communities have moved online in recent years, leading to the establishment of vibrant craft-based online communities. These craft-based online communities retain the norms that characterize creative craft communities in general. Although considerable research exists in understanding online communities more broadly, there is little work at the intersection of online communities and creative craft focused on the specific norms and behaviors that are visible in online creative craft communities. That is, in online craft communities, members not only need to navigate the norms and behaviors of the online community but also the norms and traditions of the creative craft. Hence, our work advances insights at the intersection of the online community literature and creative craft literature. In particular, we study how some individuals within online creative craft communities gain influence within a community through their activities, primarily through the pathway of virtuosity with the craft process. We refer to these individuals as artist-craftsmen, consistent with prior work on craft (Becker, 1978).

Within the creative craft community, artist-craftsmen are highly influential and are viewed as innovators in the community (Becker, 1978). They tend to occupy informal positions of authority since they are considered to be thought leaders within the community, given their craft-based expertise and commitment to the craft philosophy. Their influence within the community is based on their demonstration of expertise and quality, and is thus positively noted by other community members. Their activities within the community are driven by their identity as experts and skilled craftsmen (Oyserman, 2009; Oyserman & Destin, 2010). The online activities of artist-craftsmen are thus an amalgam of their identities as craftsmen, the norms that operate within craft communities in general (Kroezen et al., 2021), and the norms that are broadly associated with online communities (Butler et al., 2008; Johnson et al., 2015; Malinen, 2015; Masson & Parmentier, 2023; Tausczik & Huang, 2020).

While there are similarities in terms of the norms of online communities, there are also differences that can create tension in producing influence. Online communities emphasize peripheral boundaries, such as norms of easy access and low barriers of entry (Faraj & Johnson, 2011). In comparison, craft research emphasizes expertise hierarchy, craft skills, and the continuous honing of those skills in community settings (Dioun et al., 2025; Ravasi et al., 2025). Hence, more needs to be understood regarding how influence studied in non-artisanal, non-craft online community settings might operate differently in craftbased online community settings. Specifically, how do members of the online creative craft community actively influence others within the community? Thus, at the intersection of these diverse literatures, we ask the following research question: How do artistcraftsmen generate and exert influence in an online creative craft community, and can we identify the processes by which influence is gained?

In order to address this research question, we collaborated with Ravelry, the world’s largest online creative craft community devoted to individuals interested in fiber arts. Ravelry offers a large and active online forum on designs and strategies for fiber craft enthusiasts. In recent years, communities of individuals interested in developing their fiber craft skills have moved online, leading to the development of platforms like Ravelry. There are two types of Ravelry users—designers and members. Designers develop and share their original designs with members of the community. Designers typically offer their designs to members of the community for free, but occasionally charge a small price. Members download these designs and reuse them to create something of their own in pursuit of creative expression and their own virtuosity. From an original design (digital) to the eventual end product (physical), members have complete control of artistic and aesthetic expression, supported by the community.

Within this community, a particularly important role is played by designers in their capacity as close approximations to traditional artist-craftsmen (Becker, 1978). Designers, in their community-based identity as artist-craftsmen, seek to influence others in the community through the frequent reuse of their designs by others in the community. However, not all designers achieve this goal—we thus study how some designers achieve influence within the community of craft enthusiasts. In particular, we study decisions made by designers who offer designs to others in terms of how they choose to display their virtuosity and how they choose to engage with the community as pathways to achieving influence within the community. We conceptualize influence through the extent to which the designer’s offerings are reused by other craftsmen in the community, consistent with the dynamics observed in other online communities (e.g., Faraj et al., 2015; Goh & Wasko, 2012; Johnson et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2019). The “products” offered by designers on Ravelry are digitized designs, usually in the form of PDF files, potentially with other accompanying (also digital) resources. Members of the Ravelry community acquire these designs and reuse them, using the detailed instructions provided as a part of the design, into clothing items and accessories such as sweaters, handbags, and hats.

Empirically, we focus on two factors that capture the extent to which the designer’s designs are reused by others in the community, thus representing influence. First, we consider the difficulty level of the design portfolio offered by designers on Ravelry. The sophistication of a designer’s designs is indicative of their virtuosity and should be associated with greater influence within the community (Becker, 1978; Cattani et al., 2017). The online context allows this to be easily visible and available, relative to offline contexts where such indications of virtuosity are less salient and likely to be corrupted by social and individual factors (Hwang et al., 2015). Second, we study the extent to which designers offer designs across multiple design categories, thus displaying a higher level of creative variety (Kroezen et al., 2021). The ability to span categories within a craft is highly valued (Becker, 1978) and is also easily visible through the online context. Beyond these two factors, we also consider the moderating role of community engagement, a key element of both craft-based communities and online communities in general. While offline contexts require the organization of exhibitions and forums where craftsmen can interact with others in the guild (Becker, 1978), the online context allows easier access to community members, allowing designers to engage more fully with others for collaboration and guidance (Ray et al., 2014). We provide formal hypotheses linking these factors to the level of reuse observed for designs offered by designers within the community (Butler et al., 2008; Johnson et al., 2015; Kroezen et al., 2021).

We tested our hypotheses using a longitudinal dataset of designers and their activity on Ravelry. Our results show that the difficulty incorporated in a designer’s portfolio is positively associated with reuse at a decreasing rate. While this finding may seem surprising, a desire for challenge is to be expected in this context. Furthermore, we found that designers who span multiple categories and demonstrate higher levels of creative variety in their designs also see increased reuse of their designs. We also observed that the effects of virtuosity (proxied and measured by difficulty and creative variety) on reuse are positively moderated by community engagement, an important element of the craft ethos, corroborating evidence in prior work (Sennett, 2008). Finally, in a post hoc exploratory analysis, we considered how designers price their designs on Ravelry, choosing between offering all their designs for free versus offering some designs for payment. We examined how such pricing decisions relate to the influence in the community, finding that pricing is used as a signal of mastery.

Our work extends prior work in information systems (IS) on the conceptualization of influence in online communities to the creative craft context. Such online communities are not only important because participants engage in creative work but also because they support lifelong learning and skills enhancement. In particular, we focus on evaluating how influence is established by artist-craftsmen of such creative craft communities and show how the effect of virtuosity and community engagement can help translate into greater influence in the community. This is consistent with the finding that the guiding force of artist-craftsmen is critical for creative craft communities to thrive (Becker, 1978) and is also consistent with the motivations of designers in their identities as artistcraftsmen (Oyserman, 2009). Beyond virtuosity, we also show how other decisions regarding community engagement and pricing influence the status of artistcraftsmen within their communities. Our work contributes to the literature by examining the role of influence at the intersection of online communities and creative craft at a time when many craft communities are moving online. Such work is important because it embraces the unique ethos of online creative craft communities, which combines the norms and behaviors of both online communities and creative craft communities.

## 2 Theoretical Development

To advance the insights derived from integrating multiple literatures, we begin by discussing the concept of influence and then review its treatment in the online community literature. We next introduce creative craft concepts and use the lens of identitybased motivation theory from psychology to deepen our understanding of influence in online creative craft communities.

## 2.1 Influence and Online Communities

In recent years, influence has largely been associated with and studied in the context of social media and can be defined as the act of changing the opinion of a broader set of individuals in intentional and purposeful ways. However, a number of other fields, including psychology and management, offer important preceding discussions of this construct (e.g., Cialdini, 2003; March, 1955; Parsons, 1963). Different types of influence exist, such as shaping and otherwise manipulating another person’s opinions or actions, as discussed in the psychology literature, or achieving desired behavior in the workplace as a leader, as discussed in the management literature. Within this conceptualization of forms of influence, for our context, we adopt the conceptualization of artistic influence, a concept that is multidimensional and difficult to explicate and measure but central to the creative craft setting (Abe et al., 2013; Saleh et al., 2016; Stamkou et al., 2018).

The professional status of an artist comes largely from drawing on an artistic identity and projecting it to others (Bain, 2005). The acknowledgement and appreciation of an artist can take many forms, depending on the type of art, but usually involve an indication of popularity, such as exhibition of the artwork, payment offered for the artwork, or interactions and engagement with the artwork. Such acknowledgment and appreciation of artwork entail and are often circumscribed by the community that the artist belongs to and engages in on a sustained basis. In physical art, for instance, the larger community where the artist’s influence is perceived could include patrons, galleries, museums, collectors, and dealers (Marshall & Forrest, 2011). The artist’s influence may occasionally span outside of this ecosystem but is fostered by interactions primarily within this community. The craft-based community is analogous in terms of how influence is generated within the community of craftsmen. Interestingly, as artist and craft communities move online, the dynamics associated with online communities tend to replace these physical locations (such as art galleries and museums) in allowing the artist or craftsman to generate influence in appropriate ways. The research on online communities thus provides a suitable source for understanding how influence may be garnered in online art and creative craft communities.

At their core, online communities can be defined as open collectives of dispersed individuals who share common interests or goals and interact primarily through the internet (Faraj et al., 2011; Johnson et al., 2015; Sproull & Arriaga, 2007). In the last three decades, the research on online communities has expanded to the point where there are nearly unlimited ways to categorize and classify online communities observable across different domains, as well as the motivations and norms that drive behavior within these communities, depending on their specific contingencies. Indeed, contextualization to the specific domain in which an online community exists is potentially more useful than generalizations to all forms of online communities and can also recognize the commonalities shared by online communities in general. In our specific case, since we focus on online creative craft communities, we contextualize our discussion to the specific aspects of how creative craft communities operate, particularly in their online permutations. In this discussion, we not only invoke the goals of online creative craft communities but also discuss the norms and identities that these communities incorporate and enact on community members. These norms, identities, and goals influence and impact the ways in which individuals participate or interact with others in the community (Chandna & Salimath, 2020; Kairam et al., 2012; Masson & Parmentier, 2023) and are foundational to our research hypotheses.

The literature on online communities shows a multitude of ways in which influence is characterized, largely driven by the specific features of the community and its norms. We present some of the measures that have been used to characterize influence in online communities in Table 1. In certain cases, empirically measuring influence is based on the extent to which a community member’s activity or creations are used or demanded by others in the community. In other cases, where influence is generated by being a central node within an online community, centrality determines influence. In yet other contexts, influence is held by the most active individual in the community. In effect, the definition of influence within the online communities literature is driven by the context and the norms that are prevalent within that community. Hence, we next provide a definition of our context, creative craft.

## 2.2 The Definition of Craft and Creative Craft

Early work by Becker (1978) provided a simple definition of craft: a body of knowledge and skill that can be used to produce useful objects or services, such as food, music, and pottery. This perspective included three important factors: utility (usefulness), an aesthetic goal that is used to evaluate the end product, and the importance of building skill. Within the craft community, the importance of aesthetic standards and virtuosity allows certain individuals with the advanced skills needed to generate innovations within the craft domain to differentiate themselves as artist-craftsmen. Artist-craftsmen distinguish themselves not only by displaying an “extraordinary control of material and techniques” but also by “mastering a wide variety of techniques, being able not only to do things better than most others but also to do more things” (Becker, 1978, p. 865). Recent work provides a more expansive definition of craft. Thorlindsson et al. (2018, p. 117) defined craftsmanship as work characterized by the “intrinsic motivation, engagement in the task at hand, holistic understanding, emphasis on informal learning, and the honing of skills that are needed to accomplish the task at hand.” This definition includes utility and virtuosity, as discussed earlier, but also brings in the community aspect by emphasizing the role of informal learning and intrinsic motivation within a communal setting of peers (Sennett, 2008).

Within this broad definition, Kroezen et al. (2021) identified several different types of craft, starting with traditional and industrial craft. Traditional craft refers to the traditional forms of production associated with European guild systems, which were replaced by industrial firms over time. The introduction of machines into production gave rise to industrial craft, representing a combination of mechanical skills and machines. Within industrial craft, specialized skills that allow humans to interact with machines at a higher level of sophistication than would be possible without automation constitute the third category of technical craft, such as watchmaking (Raffaelli, 2019). Many types of IT work, such as hardware and software design, may be viewed as industrial craft (Barley, 1996). The fourth category, pure craft, eschews all forms of mechanical work while “evangelizing” the purest form of production (Massa et al., 2017). The most relevant category in our context is the fifth type, creative craft (Kroezen et al., 2021). While these categories retain elements of the craft ethos, there are differences in how individual kinds of craft have evolved over time; thus, theorizing about how craft communities operate requires understanding of the specifics of that craft community.<sup>2</sup> We thus retain our focus on creative craft here, as opposed to pure and technical craft, where novelty is not emphasized (Fetzer, 2025).

Creative craft represents work associated with a creative purpose and involves making unique products and services that help individuals express their identity while also developing their skills. Individualized talent and know-how are appreciated in craft communities, and members may compete in a “friendly” setting for honors and awards. Examples of such craft show up in the context of handicraft (Kuhn & Galloway, 2015), maker spaces (Browder et al., 2019), and, in our context, handmade articles. Members of creative craft communities, such as Ravelry, operate under a set of shared norms built on the elements of craft and craftsmanship, with deviations from these norms often resulting in community disapproval. Thus, these norms legitimize the key elements discussed above—emphasis on virtuosity and low-scale production, an attitude of informal and practice-based learning, reputation created through expertise that is both deep and wide, and the importance of community (Kroezen et al., 2021; Sennett, 2008). For artist-craftsmen in particular, influencing the community through one’s creations and skills is often an important goal (Becker, 1978).

Financial motivations tend to be underemphasized, in contrast to building expertise and virtuosity. Market considerations are not absent here—indeed, creative craft has often flourished in offline settings where individual craftsmen were able to make a living using their skills (Becker, 1978). However, the norms that underlie craft philosophy tend to dominate the decisionmaking processes of community members, creating a form of community solidarity and unity. Moreover, remaining consistent with the core elements of the craft perspective—virtuosity, skill building, aesthetic value and utility, community engagement—is likely to be associated with influence within the community (Johnson et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2022). While all craftsmen within the community may aspire to this role, few are typically able to achieve the level of influence needed to be viewed as artist-craftsmen. We now discuss the identity of artist-craftsmen and how the definition of this identity motivates them to seek influence within their communities.

## 2.3 Understanding the Identity and Motivation of Artist-Craftsmen in Creative Craft Communities

To understand who artist-craftsmen are and why they serve the craft community, we argue that it is important to understand their motivations. Recall that creative craft is based on intrinsic motivation to learn craft-based skills and invest in lifelong learning—the roots of this behavior can be understood through the literature on identity-based motivation (Oyserman, 2009). Identitybased motivation argues that individuals’ identities are dynamically constructed through a series of contextual cues, combined with memory and priming. Therefore, which identity is invoked for an individual is a product of situated cognition, i.e., the specific situation invokes a specific identity. Once such an identity is invoked, people regulate themselves in terms of actions based on the motivations that are associated with that identity. Thus, within a creative craft community, a member who joins the community as a craftsman is likely to adopt that identity and take on the social norms, expectations, and behaviors associated with that identity. In this context, individuals will use this dynamically constructed identity to guide their activities and make sense of the environment (Oyserman et al., 2017), even if they follow a different professional path in their “real” lives. Thus, the motivations that follow from the dynamically constructed identity are important in determining suitable action within that context (Oyserman, 2009).

Beyond dynamically constructed identities, the invoked identity has implications for how willing individuals are to accept task-related difficulties. Whether a task is viewed as “difficult and impossible” or “difficult but important” is driven by the identity that is generated and the congruence of the task with that identity (Oyserman, 2009). The motivations for virtuosity and mastery can thus be better understood through identity-based motivation. While the effort and time required to achieve virtuosity in a given creative craft may be viewed as difficult and unnecessary for an individual without the identity of a craftsman, the same individual may find this process difficult but important to achieve if they adopt the identity of a craftsman. Thus, achieving virtuosity in craft is “identity-congruent” for craftsmen; most members of a creative craft community will likely be willing to invest the effort and time needed to achieve this virtuosity (Oyserman, 2015). In this manner, we are able to link the identity of a craftsman with the norms and behaviors that are typically associated with being a member of a creative craft community.

We can extend this reasoning to artist-craftsmen, who identify with the norms and principles of creative craft within the community, thus reflecting both the expectations and the actions associated with that identity. Artist-craftsmen will thus be willing to invest the hard work and effort required to be a leading member of the craft community. These efforts further their own mastery of the craft through deliberate practice and work and enable others to do so as well (Becker, 1978; Kroezen et al., 2021) through their engagement with the community and their influence on other craftsmen. Therefore, their efforts will likely be viewed as identity-congruent and thus “difficult yet necessary.” Hence, we argue that identity in creative craft communities is an important means of motivating community members to perform actions that benefit the community, even if they are time-consuming, difficult, and uncompensated (Oyserman, 2009). Having identified the sources of identity and their behavioral implications, we next discuss how influence is garnered in creative craft-based online communities like Ravelry.

## 2.4 Artist-Craftsmen as Influencers in Craft-Based Online Communities

In theorizing about influence exerted by artist-craftsmen within Ravelry, we draw from the literature on influence in online communities (see Table 1), viewed through the lens of creative craft and identity. The literature on online communities offers multiple ways in which influence is characterized, largely driven by the specific features of the community and the norms operating therein. We present some of the measures that have been used to characterize influence in online communities in Table 1. As discussed above, the empirical measurement of influence is driven by the context and norms of the community and may be based on demand, centrality, or activity. Therefore, we conceptualize influence within the online creative craft community using concepts from the creative craft literature (Becker, 1978; Kroezen et al., 2021). In traditional craft communities, artist-craftsmen build their reputation through an ability to innovate within the boundaries of the craft and craftsmanship (Becker, 1978; Kroezen et al., 2021). When such craft communities move online, influence would thus be built on the ability to innovate and others’ emulation and adoption of these innovations.

Within the Ravelry online community, in particular, designers are artist-craftsmen since they offer their creations to others and generate new ideas through their design portfolios. Designers with influence are likely to be at the center of the community network since their work, ideas, and designs are likely to be emulated, reused, and celebrated (Huffaker, 2010; Johnson et al., 2015). Therefore, building on the work summarized in Table 1, an important measure of influence exhibited by an artistcraftsman in this context is the extent to which the individual’s offerings are reused or emulated by others within the Ravelry community. For ordinary members in a creative craft community, achieving the stature of a reputed artist-craftsman can also be seen as aspirational. We argue that those artist-craftsmen who achieve the level of influence needed to be considered a de facto community influencer will do so by adhering to the norms of the community with respect to virtuosity and craft mastery. Influence is thus not driven by formal roles within the community but rather based on the designer’s craft portfolio and the way they interact with others, consistent with the findings of prior work on online communities (Johnson et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2022).

Why would designers be willing to invest the time and effort to create designs to offer to the community? As argued above, their identity as artist-craftsmen will induce adherence to these community norms. In the Ravelry community, most designers start as crafters, i.e., regular members of the craft community. At some point, they may start to create and offer designs for others to reuse, taking on the contextually driven dynamic identity of a designer. Once this identity is dynamically constructed, it also ensures that the norms, beliefs, and behaviors consistent with creative craft and the identity of a craftsman within an online community are invoked. These norms, derived from a combination of online communities and creative craft communities, generate a dynamically created identity of a “designer.” All efforts thus aimed at being influential within the community are identity congruent, i.e., they are considered acceptable and, indeed, necessary in their assumed identity of an artist-craftsman.

Within the portfolio of designs that are offered by a designer, what features of the portfolio are more likely to lead to greater influence within the community? Here again, we draw upon the creative craft literature. One clear pathway to influence is through the demonstration of virtuosity in the specific creative craft (Kroezen et al., 2021; Becker, 1978). Virtuosity here pertains to two features that are associated with creative craft: utility and aesthetics (Kroezen et al., 2021). Artist-craftsmen distinguish themselves and influence others through displaying their virtuosity by creating crafted products that incorporate both aesthetics and utility and also displaying sophistication and complexity (Becker, 1978), i.e., “extraordinary control of material and techniques.” Thus, designers on Ravelry seeking to grow their influence in the community should generate sophisticated and complex design portfolios. This approach to building influence is consistent with both the creative craft ethos (Kroezen et al., 2021) and the literature on identity-based motivation (Oyserman, 2009) and also builds on the work on influence in the online communities literature (see Table 1). In the next section, we delve deeper into how complexity and difficulty may be conceptualized within the context of Ravelry and designers’ portfolios.

## 2.5 Influence Through Design: Difficulty, Creative Variety, and Community Engagement

As discussed above, designers on Ravelry seek to enhance their influence through their design portfolios. Specifically, these portfolios incorporate and thus indicate the designer’s virtuosity and mastery of the craft. The Ravelry online community features at least two ways to display virtuosity at scale within the online context. The first and most direct measure of virtuosity is the difficulty level of the designs offered by individual designers online (depth of expertise). Recall that designers provide design documents outlining their creations, which members of the online community can adapt as they see fit, either for free or for a nominal price. The difficulty level of each design is determined by feedback provided by other users and typically represents the level of skill needed to implement the design. Members downloading and reusing difficult designs will do so because their identity as craftsmen motivates them to take on the effort required to successfully implement these designs. Thus, the difficulty of the designs included in a designer’s portfolio offers a visible and effective measure of virtuosity within the context of the Ravelry creative craft community.

A second measure of virtuosity is based on the designer’s ability to provide designs across different categories on the Ravelry platform (breadth in expertise). Some designs are often uniquely associated with one category (socks, for instance), while others extend across similar categories (blankets, tablecloths). Designers can thus choose to offer designs across such categories or stick to one category. In the context of creative craft, exhibiting variety in creations suggests a higher level of virtuosity (Kroezen et al., 2021). Indeed, Becker (1978, p. 865) refers to this as “mastering a wide variety of techniques, being able not only to do things better than most others but also to do more things.” Greater creative variety is consistent with the artistcraftsman ideal type, emphasizing novelty and aesthetics, in addition to utility (Caves, 2000). Also, offering a wider variety of designs across categories is not easy for the designer, but represents a task that is “difficult but important” since it is consistent with social norms within the creative craft context.

Designers who exhibit their mastery by offering relatively difficult designs or those who offer designs across categories are likely to enjoy greater levels of influence in the community, demonstrated by the frequent reuse of their designs, for two reasons. First, such designers are displaying mastery of technique and creativity, serving as an inspiration to other members of the community seeking to enhance their own skills, consistent with the norms of creative craft communities. The effort needed to learn, hone, and display this level of mastery of technique and creativity is also closely associated with the identity of the artist-craftsman and is likely to be viewed as such by members of the community. Second, designers who demonstrate mastery of technique serve as benchmarks for other designers seeking to emulate them, thus again leading to greater reuse of their designs. As designers seek to enhance their own skills over time, they are likely to emulate other designers whom they view as more skilled. Thus, we expect designers who offer portfolios that include creative variety or difficult designs to attract more interest, as indicated by greater reuse of their designs by members of the community.

While we expect such designers to enjoy higher levels of reuse (influence), we do not expect this relationship to be monotonous. That is, increasing the difficulty of designs and creative variety will not indefinitely increase the level of reuse because, beyond a certain threshold, designs may become too difficult for community members to implement. In such cases, while such designers would still be demonstrating mastery, their designs may exceed the ability of the user community to suitably adapt and implement them. Accordingly, we expect diminishing returns from increasing levels of difficulty and creative variety in terms of reuse. On the basis of our arguments, we therefore propose the following hypotheses:

H1a: Artist-craftsmen (designers) who offer online design portfolios with higher levels of difficulty enjoy higher levels of design reuse, with limits defined by a monotonically increasing relationship.

H1b: Artist-craftsmen (designers) who offer online design portfolios with higher levels of creative variety enjoy higher levels of design reuse, with limits defined by a monotonically increasing relationship.

Beyond these main effects, we also consider the moderating effect of community engagement by artistcraftsmen, an important dimension of creative craft communities. The role of community engagement has long been associated with craft communities as an integral part of craft philosophy (Becker, 1978). Craft communities emphasize the value of sharing knowledge and helping build expertise and skill in other members of the community, especially by senior and skilled members of the creative craft community (Kroezen et al., 2021; Thorlindsson et al., 2018). Thus, engaging with the community by helping others build expertise or providing guidance and mentoring in the development of skills remains an important component within the creative craft ethos. Senior or highly regarded community members engaging in the community also reflects common practice in online communities, particularly in the form of knowledge sharing and collaboration (Faraj et al., 2011).

While community engagement by itself may not contribute to influence in communities where virtuosity in the underlying craft is necessary, it can help enhance the extent to which an artist-craftsman designer’s virtuosity leads to the increased reuse of their designs and thus can increase the influence of designers on other members. By engaging with the community, designers can generate interest in their designs and highlight the difficulty and variety of their work. Feedback from the community, in turn, can help designers learn about the community’s needs and how these needs can be embedded into better designs, thereby creating a virtuous cycle for the effects of difficulty and variety. Increased engagement with the community also helps increase the designer’s visibility and direct attention to their designs, allowing virtuosity to shine through. <sup>3</sup> Finally, even though community engagement is effortful and timeconsuming, artist-craftsmen will be willing to devote the effort needed due to the norms and behaviors that are consistent with their identity. Therefore, we propose:

H2a: Community engagement positively moderates the relationship between the difficulty of a designer’s portfolio and the level of design reuse.

H2b: Community engagement positively moderates the relationship between the creative variety of a designer’s portfolio and the level of design reuse.

## 3 Setting, Data, and Analysis

## 3.1 Research Setting: Ravelry

Ravelry, known as the Facebook of fiber crafts, such as knitting, crocheting, spinning, and weaving (Battan, 2021), is the world’s largest community of yarn and fiberbased creative craft with nine million members, of which a million are active every month. Ravelry hosts an online community of creative craftsmen who share designs, share ideas and techniques, and provide community support to each other as they work to enhance their skills. Membership falls into two broad categories and is open to all individuals. The first category comprises designers, who develop and offer their designs to other users for adaptation and reuse, which we designate as artistcraftsmen. The second category comprises craft enthusiasts (members), who are interested in crafts but do not contribute designs for other members to reuse. These members represent the majority of the community. Note that the two categories are not mutually exclusive— designers are also members in their own right and can also reuse designs from other designers. Designs offered on Ravelry are also called “design patterns” or “patterns” and are digitized, usually in the form of PDF documents, and may include accompanying documents or other files relevant to the design. Designs can either be free or sold at a price set by the designer.

In 2020, a total of \$28 million worth of designs were sold on Ravelry. Ravelry’s owner collects only 2% of the sales. This low fee is consistent with norms within craft communities and emphasizes crafters’ devotion to the work rather than monetization. Indeed, in its community states, instead of revenue, Ravelry reports the number of projects completed, the number of designs posted, and the amount of yarn used, etc.<sup>4</sup> The design patterns are listed in a directory, shown in Figure 1. A sample design or pattern listing is shown in Figure 2. Listings often include pictures of the physical end product that members can expect if they closely follow the included instructions. The designer chooses the design category and provides a description and supplemental information on how to complete the project. ## 4 Data and Variable Operationalization

At the start of our collaboration with Ravelry in August 2016, more than 42,000 designers were offering about 260,000 designs on Ravelry. Through our collaboration with Ravelry, we received complete information on the reuse of designs offered by 12,258 designers over 103 months (between May 2007 and

January 2016).<sup>7</sup> This data formed the core dataset used in hypothesis testing, as described below.

## 4.1 Dependent Variable

Our primary, time-variant dependent variable is reuse, which measures the number of times a designer’s designs were reused to create a physical object, proxying a channel for the designer’s artistic influence. We used this rich measure because a representation of the process and the ultimate physical object is demonstrated as a “project” of a user on the site, along with the progress of creation.<sup>8</sup> We aggregated reuse counts to the designer-month level, representing the extent to which a designer’s work was reused in a given month. This variable captures the focal designer’s influence on the community since it measures the number of times the designer’s output was reused by other members of the community. Because we focused on the influence observed at the designer level, aggregating reuse to the designer level seemed appropriate. An exposure variable was then used in the regression analysis to practically transform these counts into the rate of user interest.

## 4.2 Independent Variables

Difficulty: As mentioned earlier, difficulty is a crowdsourced, exogenous, time variant measure of the level of skill a user needs to implement a given design and is rated by members on a 0-10 scale, with 10 representing the greatest difficulty. We calculated the average difficulty of the designs in a designer’s portfolio for this purpose.

Creative variety: The measure for creative variety is based on the Shannon-Wiener diversity index. This time-variant variable measures the level of design variety created by a designer across design categories.<sup>9</sup> As a designer exhibits greater breadth by offering designs across multiple categories, the calculated value becomes higher. Variety was calculated using the antilog of the Shannon-Wiener index based on an entropy measure (Shannon, 1948). Building on the notion of entropy as change and disorder, Shannon’s measure is used to measure the diversity of different types in a group in prior work (e.g., Chi et al., 2010). Shannon’s measure is appropriate here to measure product variety (of different product types) for at least three reasons (Straathof, 2007). First, the measure is equal to the number of design types when the design types are symmetric (i.e., same proportions in a mix of designs). Second, the measure can be decomposed without losing its functional form, i.e., creating larger clusters of designs in a mix always leads to a decrease in the measured variety of the design portfolio. Third, the addition (or removal) of a design category with a weight of zero does not cause a discrete change in the measure, thereby preventing deviations when the change of design categories in a mix is negligible. These features offer a good fit for calculating design variety for designers because we needed a measure that would account for both the categories in which a designer offers designs and how evenly the designs are distributed across the categories. Using the Shannon-Wiener index, the variety across categories $( V _ { t } )$ for designer ?? is:

$$
V _ {t, d} = \exp \bigl (- \sum_ {c = 1} ^ {C _ {t}} p _ {c, t} l n p _ {c, t} \bigr),\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { c , t }$ is the proportion of products in the $c ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ category in a total of $C _ { t }$ categories in month ??. This variable used the categories existing on Ravelry and therefore reveals the extent to which designers spanned categories in their craft output.

Community engagement: We measured the extent to which a designer engaged and interacted with the broader community by counting the number of times the designer posted to a forum on Ravelry, “Favorites,” which features designs offered by other designers and provides comments on other designs. These forms of interactions are consistent with community engagement observed in online community settings and represent identity-congruent effort for designers (Faraj et al., 2011).

## 4.3 Control Variables

We included a series of control variables to account for the specific context of the study. First, we controlled for the total activity on Ravelry in the form of all reuse instances in a month and included it in the model as an exposure variable (i.e., the ceiling of the number of reuse instances a designer can hypothetically have in a month). Using an exposure variable to analyze rates makes use of correct probability distributions (Agresti, 2011; Long, 1997; Long & Freese, 2006). Distributional assumptions regarding the dependent variable are not violated by transforming, for example, count outcomes into rates. Thus, using an exposure variable in the model that estimates reuse counts allows the dependent variable to effectively constitute the rate of reuse demand for the designs offered by a designer. In our context, namely a growing creative craft community, traffic in earlier time periods is typically small compared to later periods. Using the exposure variable allowed the estimation to be robust to such changes across time. We also controlled for a second set of control variables that are potentially time-variant, with varying influence on designer performance. These variables are the number of products per designer and designer popularity, which we measured using the number of ratings a designer received. Descriptive statistics for all variables are shown in Table 2.

## 5 Analysis and Results

We used regression analysis to estimate the effect of difficulty and creative variety on reuse. Since our dependent variable reuse took only discrete nonnegative integer values, a Poisson estimation was deemed appropriate (Hausman et al., 1984). To control for any time-invariant effects (i.e., effects that may vary between designers but not within designers over time), we used designer and time (month) fixed effects. In this context, time-invariant factors can include, for example, creative talent and intrinsic motivation that can be assumed to be relatively stable within a designer but may vary across designers. We thus ensured that our estimations were not biased by potential heterogeneity in the inherent characteristics of designers. Similarly, we included month fixed effects to control for varying seasonality effects, since failing to control for such unobserved heterogeneities could result in specification errors and biases (Heckman, 1979). Hence, our regression model takes the following form (Hausman et al., 1984):

$$
\begin{array}{l} E \left(y _ {i, t}\right) = \lambda_ {i, t} = \exp \left(\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i, t}\right) \alpha_ {i} \text {   for   } t = 1, \dots , n _ {i} \text {   and   } i = \\ 1, \dots , n, \end{array} \tag {2}
$$

where $y _ { i , t }$ are count measures for the dependent variable (for designer ?? at time ??) and follow a Poisson distribution, $x _ { i , t }$ are time- and designer-variant regressors, and $\alpha _ { i }$ are designer-specific, time-invariant effects. The regressors are represented as $x _ { i , t }$ . The exposure variable was added to the estimator as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} E (y _ {i, t}) = \exp {(\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i, t} + l n \rho_ {i, t})} \alpha_ {i} \text {   for   } t = 1,..., n _ {i} \text {   and   } i = \\ 1,..., n, \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

where $l n \rho _ { i , t }$ is the natural logarithm of the exposure variable $\rho _ { i , t } ,$ , whose coefficient is constrained to 1, transforming the dependent variable $y _ { i , t }$ into the rate of reuse for designer ??’s designs at time ??.

## 5.1 Main Results

We used a Poisson quasi-maximum likelihood estimator (QMLE) that can resolve the well-known issues with logged count outcome variables (Arvis & Shepherd, 2013). We first addressed the hypothesized direct effects before moving to the moderation results. The results pertaining to the direct effects from Hypotheses 1a and 1b are shown in Table 3a.<sup>10,11</sup>

We first introduce the control variables and then subsequently add the key independent variables. Column 2 shows a positive and statistically significant coefficient for both portfolio-level difficulty and creative variety on the number of times the designer’s designs were reused within the community. Since an exposure variable was included in the model, the coefficient can be viewed as a rate. In the case of difficulty and creative variety, we see a significant increase in the rate at which the designs were reused by other members. In terms of effect size, a 1% increase in difficulty score corresponds to a 0.2% increase in the rate of reuse. A 1-standard-deviation increase in creative variety, on the other hand, corresponds to a 36.3% increase in the reuse rate.<sup>12</sup> These results confirm that indicators of virtuosity are likely to enhance the designers’ influence in terms of reuse, thereby leading to greater influence in the community and contributing to identity formation. Table 3b shows that the key research variables in the model do not pose a multicollinearity risk, given the variance inflation factors.

We further explored the possibility of nonlinear effects by adding second-order terms for difficulty and creative variety, as shown in Columns 3 and 4 of Table 3a. The coefficients show that returns to the increased difficulty and creative variety of designs in terms of reuse instances are diminishing, as anticipated. The relationships between difficulty and creative variety and the number of reuse instances for the designer are concave, showing an inverse U-shape, indicating that there is an optimal point beyond which increases in difficulty and variety do not provide greater influence within the community (limits to returns). The complete model with all independent variables shown in Column 5 produced consistent results across both variables.

While we show the direct effects of difficulty and creative variety, it is possible that these two indicators interact to provide even greater influence for the designer. Therefore, we explored the interaction effects between the two variables as a post hoc analysis. Column 6 of Table 3a shows that this interaction effect is negative and significant.<sup>13</sup> Designers seemed unable to achieve the best of both worlds, as increasing both expressions of virtuosity actually reduced reuse instances significantly. Interestingly, we see that each indicator of virtuosity may be unwittingly crowding out the other indicator, in terms of reuse. One potential reason for this could be that the final product created is a physical object, e.g., an actual handmade item. Thus, the physical limitations inherent in producing such designs could lead to such a crowding-out effect (designs are products of iterations between ideas, drawings, and prototypes of eventual end products). These limitations could come from resource costs—yarn and other supplies needed to test a design can be expensive and, in some cases, not easily available. A fuller examination of these potentially limiting effects is out of the scope of our analysis but remains an interesting avenue for future research.

## 5.2 Moderating Effect of Community Engagement

In Hypotheses 2a and 2b, we considered the moderating role of community engagement, an important social norm in creative craft communities. In order to test these hypotheses, we executed a splitsample analysis based on median values of community engagement. Those who interacted more with the community (i.e., above median) were, interestingly, more prolific than those below the median of community engagement (10.7 designs vs. 2.82 designs, on average) and offered designs with relatively higher levels of difficulty (2.70 versus 2.30, on average). Whether this community engagement resulted in differential levels of reuse of designs remains to be tested. The split-sample results in Table 4 show that designers who engaged more with the community saw a much higher marginal effect of difficulty on reuse, relative to those who did not, providing strong support for Hypothesis 2a. These designers thus consistently acted in accordance with the predictions from existing work on identity-based motivation and creative craft— they offered difficult designs, engaged with the community, and were thus rewarded with greater reuse of their designs and enjoyed greater influence within the community.

In terms of creative variety, we executed a similar split-sample analysis. In contrast to difficulty, we see that both subsamples showed positive contributions of creative variety to reuse, although the marginal effect was higher for designers who engaged less with the community. Since these designers produced fewer designs in general, compared to those with high levels of community engagement, observable increases in creative variety may have been viewed positively by members of the community, relative to designers who were already more prolific and engaged closely with the community. In effect, we did not find empirical support for Hypothesis 2b. However, across all levels of community engagement, the marginal effect of creative variety on reuse remained positive and significant, with a relatively small difference in the effect size. In robustness tests, we also used interaction terms, rather than split-sample analyses, to test these moderation effects. As shown in Columns 3 and 4 of Table 4, the results from the interaction tests are fully consistent with those obtained from the split-sample analyses.

## 5.3 Do Prices Matter?

One of the interesting features of Ravelry is that designers can choose to charge a fee for the reuse of their designs. Such an approach is not typically viewed as consistent with the norms of creative craft communities since seamless knowledge sharing is considered desirable (Kroezen et al., 2021). Furthermore, the designer identity, predicated on the norms and beliefs of creative craft, does not emphasize financial gains within the community. However, Ravelry allows for such transactions. We therefore examined, albeit in an exploratory manner, why designers may choose to charge for design reuse and the ramifications of such decisions across the community. Given that these decisions are made on individual designs rather than at the level of the portfolio, we did not estimate formal regression models but simply considered subsamples for our exploratory analysis.<sup>14</sup> We therefore note that our conclusions from this analysis are not causal; rather, they are intended to deepen the understanding of pricing decisions made by designers.

Since charging for designs is not the norm within craft communities, we first identified those designers who actually charged for their designs. Interestingly, the majority of designers in our sample (over 70%) did not charge for designs and chose to give them away for free. Therefore, as a first step, we divided our sample of designers into two sets: those who did not charge for any of the designs in their portfolio, and those who charged for some of the designs in their portfolio (none charged for all their designs). Our interest was naturally drawn to the second category, whose portfolios revealed a ratio of free to paid designs on offer. We thus calculated this ratio and rank-ordered the designers from the lowest ratio to the highest ratio of free to paid designs. We then compared the characteristics of designers exclusively offering free designs with those offering a mix of free and paid designs, sorted by the ratio of free to paid designs.

Interestingly, when we examined the portfolios of these two sets of designers, we found that they were not homogeneous in terms of their other parameters. Designers offering only free designs had fewer designs, on average (approximately three designs), than those offering a mix (approximately nine designs). It appears that novice designers may be more likely to offer free designs as they seek to enhance their own mastery of the craft. Designers offering a mix of free and paid designs were clearly more experienced in terms of design productivity. Furthermore, when we divided designers offering a mix of paid and free designs into quartiles based on the ratio of free to paid designs within their portfolio, those in the top quartile (highest fraction of paid designs) offered approximately 13 designs, on average; designers in the other three quartiles offered a mean of four designs. Thus, offering paid designs appears to correlate with the extent to which the designer is prolific and experienced in terms of design output. In other words, experienced designers are more likely than inexperienced designers to offer some paid designs.

Additionally, while there was variation in terms of the ratio of free to paid designs, we note that the price of designs did not vary as much across designers. The mean price per paid design was roughly \$4.75 (SD = \$1.88) across designers and remained constant even across varying levels of design difficulty. While designers who charged for some designs offered designs that were marginally more difficult on average than those offered by the designers offering only free designs (2.56 versus 2.40), prices tended to be largely independent of design difficulty. Finally, community engagement across the two groups of designers varied significantly; designers offering only free designs were, on average, much less engaged with the community than those offering a mix of free and paid designs. These descriptive statistics are provided in Table 5.

Our exploratory analysis thus suggests that there were three sets of designers within the community when viewed through the lens of pricing decisions. The first set of designers offered only free designs but were less active, offered designs with lower levels of difficulty and creative variety in their portfolios, engaged less with the community, and were less influential according to their reuse statistics. Column 1 of Table 5 shows the metrics for this group. In contrast, the second set of designers offered some paid designs and were more prolific, displayed higher levels of difficulty and creative variety, engaged with the community more, and were rewarded with higher incidences of reuse. Column 2 of Table 5 summarizes the metrics for this group. The descriptives appear to be consistent with the norms of creative craft in terms of influence. Finally, within this second group of designers, those in the upper quartile represent a more visibly successful set of artist-craftsmen, given their likely influence within the online creative craft community. They were more likely to charge a nominal price for some of their designs (\$4-5). They do not appear to see this as contravening social norms; indeed, charging a nominal price for a design appears to be a legitimate norm within the Ravelry community.

In all other ways, these artist-craftsmen appear to be fully adhering to the social norms described within creative craft communities (Kroezen et al., 2021). Moreover, this exploratory analysis suggests variations in the extent to which identity-based motivation is influential in the decisions made by designers regarding pricing. Experienced designers with potentially stronger associations with this identity appear to be comfortable offering some paid designs, relative to newer designers. This raises the issue of heterogeneity in the extent to which identity-based motivations are instrumental across individuals—prior work suggests that individuals differ in terms of how deeply they invoke dynamically constructed identities, with implications for their subsequent behavior and actions (Oyserman, 2015). We see some support for that argument here. A deeper examination of pricing is unfortunately out of the scope of this paper, given our unit of analysis and the limitations of our dataset.

## 5.4 Robustness Checks

In this section, we describe a series of robustness tests executed to check the validity of our findings. The results we obtained are largely consistent with those discussed above. First, the Poisson estimation requires the mean and variance to be equal, an assumption that often does not hold true (Cameron & Trivedi, 2013). Our dependent variable shows signs of the opposite, leading to an overdispersion problem, which may have underestimated standard errors (Cameron & Trivedi, 1986). The results of a likelihood-ratio test showed that significant overdispersion spuriously inflated the statistical significance levels of the coefficients in our model.<sup>15</sup> To test the robustness of our results against a bias in standard errors due to overdispersion, we replicated our results using a negative binomial model which incorporated an overdispersion parameter that was permitted to vary across groups, here designers (Hausman et al., 1984). However, the fixed-effects estimator of the negative binomial model is not a “true” fixed-effects model (Allison, 2009; Allison & Waterman, 2002), i.e., it does not fully control for the time-invariant effects. Because statistically controlling for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity, such as creative talent and intrinsic motivation, was critical to avoid confounding our results, we retained the negative binomial model only as a robustness test here. We estimated the following negative binomial model (Hausman et al., 1984):

$$
\begin{array}{l} E \left(y _ {i, t}\right) = \lambda_ {i, t} = \exp \left(\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i, t}\right) \alpha_ {i} / \varphi_ {i} \text {   for   } t = 1, \dots , n _ {i} \text {   and   } i = \\ 1, \dots , n, \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

where $y _ { i , t }$ are the count measures for the dependent variable (for designer ?? at time ??) and follow a negative binomial distribution, $x _ { i , t }$ are time- and designer-variant regressors, $\alpha _ { i }$ are the designer-specific, time-invariant effects, and $\varphi _ { i }$ is the negative binomial overdispersion parameter. The results in Table 6 are consistent with those from the Poisson model reported earlier.

Second, our results may have been biased due to the potential endogeneity of difficulty and creative variety. Designers who adopt community-specific norms are likely motivated by a desire to retain influence rather than short-term strategic goals; therefore, it is unlikely that these practices would change in the short term due to variations in reuse instances (Qiu et al., 2017). Further, to the extent that the social norms are deeply embedded in the creative craft community of Ravelry members, designers would be less likely to strategically change their activities in the community in the short term. Nevertheless, we conducted additional analyses to account for the endogeneity of difficulty and creative variety for each designer. Before discussing our endogeneity checks, we note that difficulty is less likely to be endogenous since the metric is crowdsourced over time rather than defined by designers per se. Nevertheless, we used two instrumental variables to mitigate the biases from the potential endogeneity of both difficulty and creative variety.

The instruments we used are the number of resources (e.g., books, magazines, raw materials) designers kept in their (1) “library” and (2) “stash.” The library and stash sections of designer profiles are provided by Ravelry to showcase the resources designers utilize. The library includes mostly books and magazines, while the stash includes information about yarn and other resources that are used or discussed by the designer. We used a measure of the number of resources in the designer’s library and stash sections as instruments. The number of resources showcased by designers in a library or stash may affect designers’ products. Arguably, the greater the number of resources, the more likely it is that a focal designer will create designs that are more difficult and that incorporate variety, since these resources may represent sources of design ideas, techniques, and elements of creative design. However, there is no reason to expect the number of resources to directly influence the number of reuse instances, thereby complying with exclusion restrictions. Any residual influence of the resources on reuse would likely occur through the pathway of the two independent variables, which is the specific mechanism we tested. Within the two-stage instrumental variable approach, we used the number of resources in the first stage as the control.

The results are provided in Table 7 and are consistent with the results obtained from the main model. We tested the relevance criterion using the two F-tests reported in the table, showing that the instruments were relevant to both variables (shown as “Weak instrument test” in the table). In addition, Wu-Hausman test results indicate that the model using the instrumental variables produced more consistent results in this case. We acknowledge that it is not possible to completely rule out endogeneity in this context, short of a randomized experiment where difficulty and creative variety are exogenously manipulated across designers, which is unfortunately not feasible here. We believe our results are robust, even in the presence of some endogeneity.

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion

Creative craft, defined as the “pursuit of creativity in making and fueled by social movements promoting individual freedom and expression” (Kroezen et al., 2021) is important human activity, primarily conducted offline and rooted in community, lifelong learning, and the pursuit of virtuosity; it comprises a uniquely human combination of “knowledge, power, and skill” with a “sense of wisdom” and “resourcefulness” (Ravasi et al., 2025, p. 446). Craft communities are characterized by influence through the path of virtuosity, which is recognized informally within the respective craft community (Kroezen et al., 2021; Thorlindsson et al., 2018). In recent years, many craft communities have moved online. By drawing from the literatures on online communities (Faraj et al., 2015; Johnson et al., 2015; Sproull & Arriaga, 2007) and creative craft (Kroezen et al., 2021), we study one of the central pillars of creative craft—the emergence of influence within the online creative craft community.

How is influence, in the form of design reuse by others, established in an online creative craft community? We started by using identity-based motivation to understand and explain why designers may be willing to invest uncompensated and costly effort into creating designs that are then offered to their colleagues on the creative craft community online. Theoretically, individuals who identify with their role as craftsmen or designers within the creative craft community will find it useful and indeed necessary to invest in actions consistent with these identities. Thus, on the one hand, members of the Ravelry community, by virtue of adopting the craftsman identity, will find it important to enhance their skills and mastery of the craft. On the other hand, designers, in their identity as artist-craftsmen, will find it important to enhance their influence on the community through their innovations, creations, and mastery of the craft. The ability to master difficult designs is thus viewed as achievable and essential, and is also indicated as such by designers seeking to signal growth and help others achieve mastery. By publicly documenting revisions (e.g., “Version 2: In response to requests for additional sizing and yardage, I added a second version…”), designers show their commitment to collaborative growth.<sup>16</sup> Designers also frequently include disclaimers like “An elegant but simple lace crochet pattern, perfect for those with intermediate skills or wanting to expand their basic crochet skills,” positioning difficulty as a desirable and achievable hurdle for committed crafters.<sup>17</sup> These behaviors are consistent with behaviors seen in online communities pertaining to other forms of craft, such as those devoted to technical craft. An example would be in online coding communities where tackling difficult problems and finding effective coding solutions is seen as a badge of honor; experts in such communities will be expected to guide and mentor others in these efforts. Thus, we show that a combination of identity-based motivation and creative craft motivates designers to gain influence within the community through their designs.

A central element of our theorizing pertains to influence, i.e., how designers find ways to achieve influence online. In our context, influence is measured through reuse instances, representing and building on the concepts of artistic influence studied in prior research. Since online communities provide visibility and reach (Faraj et al., 2011) as well as a greater level of access (Fleming & Waguespack, 2007), influence is likely to be driven bottom-up through exhibited virtuosity as well as visible community engagement (Lu et al., 2022). Thus, we show that artist-craftsmen in creative craft online communities exhibit socially oriented influence (Lu et al., 2022). We also show that returns to virtuosity in the form of reuse instances in our specific context have limitations, indicating a concave (inverse Ushaped) relationship, supporting the theoretical expectations. Furthermore, we study how community engagement serves as a significant moderating influence—virtuosity can better establish influence in the presence of elevated levels of community engagement by designers within the community.

Our work is also positioned within a growing set of communities where activities are not fully online or offline but exist in a hybrid form. While the designs are largely digital, since they are encoded in PDF documents and can be downloaded easily by a large number of users, the actual manifestation of the design is a physical product. Fiber artists maintain that it is important in this process to be able to “feel the yarn,” emphasizing the importance of the physical material itself. This is in contrast to many other online communities where the activities are primarily online and dealing with digital goods—e.g., open source software communities (von Krogh et al., 2012) and app developer communities (Qiu et al., 2017). What are the implications of the hybrid nature of the Ravelry community? We argue that there are two direct implications that emerge from this setting, similar to prior work set in other hybrid contexts, such as 3D printing (Kyriakou et al., 2017) and glass making (Ravasi et al., 2025).

First, the online component allows a level of scale and reach that would otherwise not be possible in terms of the craft community as a whole. The ability to digitize certain components of the activity, even if imperfectly, allows what is essentially a physical activity to transition to an online avatar, thereby creating a much larger community that can participate and collaborate successfully. In such settings, the relevance of influence becomes even more critical, since influence is not demarcated by physical limitations (a local guild, school, or studio) but is driven by the size of the online component of the community. This increase in scale is not trivial, and the motivations of individuals that emerge from this scale are similarly significant. For instance, in Ravelry, the designs of leading designers are downloaded several thousand times—this would be completely impossible in a pure offline setting. Thus, the role of influence in such hybrid settings is critical since the online component of these communities generates tremendous value. More importantly, the definition of influence blends elements of offline influence with sources of online influence. Thus, while one source of influence is observing the number of times a specific designer’s designs have been downloaded (online influence), influence could also be garnered by someone reacting online to an actual garment made using a design offered by a highly regarded designer in an offline setting. In both cases, influence is established by combining offline and online channels; likewise, it becomes a part of designers’ identity both offline and online.

Second, as we see in our analysis, there are limits to the extent to which difficulty and creative variety lead to greater influence within the community, i.e., there are diminishing returns to virtuosity. Similarly, in robustness tests, we saw that the interaction of difficulty and creative variety is also diminishing in reuse. Essentially, unlike other settings where influence may be monotonically associated with signals of virtuosity, the physical limitations that are inherent in the fiber craft context indicate that there is a sweet spot in terms of how much difficulty craftsmen can tolerate while remaining consistent with the norms of craft. Inasmuch as creative variety and difficulty signal mastery, we suggest that the physical component of the Ravelry community, i.e., the actual creation of the physical product, leads to diminishing returns. Practically, even if designers were to offer designs that are highly difficult, the effort and commitment required to create these actual products would be so high for members that the effort would likely be seen as no longer worthwhile. In other words, beyond a threshold, the “important to achieve” sentiment would switch to an “important but impossible” setting. Kroezen et al. (2021) argue that this is the point where creative craft may well transition to industrial or technical craft since highly complex designs may require additional machinery. However, these trends may not manifest in online communities verbatim since physical limitations may not be as constraining there. Of course, more research is needed to investigate similar hybrid communities to clearly establish the generalizability of the patterns observed in our setting.

An important question that arises in our context is that of product quality—How is virtuosity different from quality, given the preeminence of quality in product markets? In the specific case of Ravelry, the notion of quality appears to be multifaceted since the actual product is created by the user who acquires the design from the designer. Therefore, the quality of the final product is not entirely in the hands of the designer but is also influenced by the skills of the other members. Moreover, the designer’s virtuosity is better measured through parameters that are directly linked to the actual craft, rather than typical quality measures that are studied in other product markets, such as online reviews or ratings. Indeed, while designer web pages on Ravelry do show ratings—for example, those observed in online reviews—these ratings also include feedback on the design of the webpage, the quality of the instructions in the design documents, the clarity of the language, etc. All of these are reflective of the designer’s webpage but not directly related to the designer’s craft virtuosity. Thus, in our work, we chose to conceptualize the designer’s influence through the displayed virtuosity in the designs rather than simplistically considering the ratings of the designer’s webpage. More to the point, the dimensions of virtuosity we study are more indicative of the “quality” of the craft practiced by the craftsman than of the quality of the product per se.<sup>18</sup>

## 6.1 Managerial Implications

From a managerial perspective, our work provides several takeaways that are pertinent to the management of online communities that specifically host creative craft communities, which also helps to distinguish these from other online communities. First, our results highlight the importance of influence within the community and show how artist-craftsmen signal their virtuosity through their portfolios. In the context of fiber crafts such as knitting and crocheting, we conceptualize mastery through difficulty and creative variety, but in other settings, other sources of virtuosity are likely to be important. For managers of online communities, it is important to be able to provide functionalities that allow such indicators of virtuosity to be suitably visible so that members of the community can observe, appreciate, and reward virtuosity. Absent such functionalities, a critical element of the identity associated with craft and artist-craftsmen will remain unsupported on the platform. Thus, designing for influence and signals of virtuosity should remain important design goals in creative craft communities, given their importance within the craft ethos and the norms that are relevant within these communities.

Second, we show that even though virtuosity is rewarded through increased influence, there are limits to how much the community is able to absorb, or indeed respond to, virtuosity. As we observe in our analysis, there are diminishing returns to increased difficulty and creative variety in terms of reuse. While we suggest that these effects may be driven by the physical nature of the end-product (as argued above), it is worth noting that the craft context does not monotonically reward virtuosity. Whether this is generalizable to other craft settings, such as glass blowing, script writing, or photography, remains to be studied. But we note that while the craft ethos indicates lifelong learning and skills mastery (Kroezen et al., 2021), pragmatically, there appear to be some limits to how much difficulty and complexity members of the community are able to handle, with implications for the levels of influence that are possible.

Finally, we note the important role played by community engagement in terms of enhancing the influence accruing to designers in the community. Community engagement by experts remains an important component of many online communities, and in the context of creative craft, community engagement by designers becomes even more relevant since it dovetails with one of the central norms of the craft ethos. Managers of creative craft communities should note how important community engagement is in this context and pay particular attention to ensuring that channels for community engagement exist and are easily accessible to all craftsmen within the community. Barriers that stand in the way of community engagement, whether technological or organizational, would need to be cleared for creative craft communities to flourish and to allow craftsmen and artist-craftsmen to fully engage with the community. Digital technologies routinely incorporate functionalities for community engagement, but the organizational and social norms within the community are less easily predictable—our work shows that managers should pay keen attention to ensuring that community engagement occurs online by managing both the technological and social aspects.

## 6.2 Limitations

Our work here is subject to several limitations. First, while creative craft communities are rich environments for cooperative and creative work, we only focus on reuse as indicative of influence in this paper. We acknowledge that in a creative context, influence may be established using other factors associated with designers’ output. Unfortunately, we were not able to observe any other such factors at scale. However, we hope that more research considering aspects of the creative process unfolding within such online communities can address these limitations. Second, our work is aggregated to the designer level; thus, we did not focus on individual designs and their specific characteristics. We believe this, too, is a rich area for future research. Third, we recognize that designer decisions on creative variety (and to some extent, difficulty) may be endogenous; while we use instrumental variables to address the problem, we recognize that the identification possible through a field experiment is infeasible in this context. We acknowledge this limitation. Fourth, our work is focused on a particular community, where individuals make choices specific to activities in the domain, fueling concerns about context specificity and generalizability. Although the specific variables we study apply to the Ravelry context, the constructs advanced here, such as virtuosity, may apply to other creative craft settings. Fifth, while we classify the adoption of designs from a focal designer as reuse, it could be argued that this is simply use and does not require the added complexity associated with the term “reuse.” We acknowledge this nuance and prefer reuse since the adapting user does not have to strictly adhere to the design provided by the designer. Thus, this added agency is indicative of reuse and creative co-production rather than simply use. Sixth, our study is subject to the limitations of the availability and type of data. While we alleviated some issues, such as overdispersion, by developing alternative models, we were not able to rule out all potential issues, some of which may have led to biases, for example, due to the available designer subsample.

Finally, this paper opens up an important domain, “craft,” that has received limited attention in the IS research. While “craft” can be perceived as fringe or unimportant, we demonstrate broad relevance on several fronts. The craft context provides insight into influence in norm-based communities with relatively few financial incentives. Also, the study demonstrates how norm-based communities can maintain rather than lose their valuable practices and traditions as they go online. We encourage future research to examine how the norms and lifelong learning in craft may help to renew platform-based logics with humanistic traditions.

## 6.3 Conclusion

In summary, we study how designers in an online creative craft community achieve influence through adhering to norms consistent with creative craft, particularly those involving virtuosity and community engagement. To the extent that influence is an aspirational goal for most craftsmen, our work also shows how artist-craftsmen emerge in such communities through a focus on virtuosity signaled through the difficulty of their designs and the creative variety inherent in their portfolios. Building on prior research on identity, online communities, and creative craft, we theorize that through the specific identity of artist-craftsmen that is generated dynamically and the social norms that are prevalent in creative craft communities, designers work towards achieving influence within the community through the decisions they make in assembling their design portfolios. In craft communities, utility, aesthetics, mastery of technique, and community building take primacy. Thus, these become central to the activities of the designers online and also incentivize them to generate portfolios of designs and activities in the community that reflect these elements. The resulting influence that is garnered in the community shows up in the form of increased instances of design reuse, a measure that is largely identified with success and achievement within Ravelry. Through our work here, we also hope to advance a research agenda that recognizes the specific nature of creative craft-based online communities and focuses attention on the unique elements that define them while also building on the existing work in online communities and influence.

## About the Authors

Gorkem Turgut (G.T.) Ozer is an assistant professor of Decision Sciences at the Paul College of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire. He received his PhD from the McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, in information, risk, and operations management. His research lies at the intersection of business, technology, and society, and focuses primarily on solving computational causal problems in and around digital platforms and peer-to-peer markets using statistical and machine learning methods, and the role of big data analytics and artificial intelligence on creativity and decision making in organizations and society at large.

Anand Gopal is the President's Chair of Information Systems and Innovation at Nanyang Business School. Previously, he served as the Dean's Professor of Information Systems at the Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland. His research interests are broadly in technology platforms, contracts, and entrepreneurship. He has specific projects in technology-based entrepreneurship, secondary markets for tech products, mobile platforms, and healthcare. He received his PhD in information systems from the Tepper School of Business, Carnegie Mellon University.

Sirkka L. Jarvenpaa is a professor of information systems and the Bayless/Rauscher Pierce Refsnes Chair in Business Administration at the McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin. She is a recipient of the Association of Information Systems Fellow and LEO Awards (LEO is awarded for the lifetime achievement of exceptional global contributions in the field of information systems). Her research focuses on interorganizational and interpersonal collaboration and innovation in fast-paced and technologically advanced data and knowledge environments with regulatory and policy implications. Her work has appeared in information systems, management, engineering, accounting, marketing, psychology, and anthropology journals.
