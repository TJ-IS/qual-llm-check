# Breaking Fad: Unpacking the Orchestration of Blockchain Ecosystems

Swanand J. Deodhar,<sup>1</sup> Arvind Sahay,<sup>2</sup> Tara Tiwari<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad, India, swanandd@iima.ac.in <sup>2</sup>Management Development Institute Gurgaon, India, arvind.sahay@mdi.ac.in <sup>3</sup>Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad, India, taratiwari@iima.ac.in

## Abstract

Prior work has advocated blockchain as a promising tool for organizations across sectors. However, given the ecosystem nature of the technology, blockchain’s benefits may not be fully realized unless it encompasses stakeholders in the focal organization’s value chain. As a result, examining how organizations orchestrate blockchain ecosystems is crucial for organizational research on blockchain. Our study addresses this question by drawing on the existing literature on ecosystem orchestration. For primary data, we relied on in-depth interviews with senior decision makers from blockchain technology vendors who were directly involved in these use cases. We inductively analyzed these data and developed visual maps of eight distinct blockchain ecosystems, spanning as many sectors. Our findings can be categorized into two groups. First, our visual maps revealed four distinct archetypes (collation, representation, replacement, and extension) of the intended benefits of blockchain ecosystems. These benefits, we argue, captured the business logic for the stakeholders to engage in a blockchain ecosystem. Our analysis further revealed a hierarchy between these artifacts. Interestingly, we found that organizations seek these intended benefits through multiple pathways. Second, our study elaborates key decisions in orchestrating blockchain ecosystems. These decisions pertain to (1) adding a stakeholder in the ecosystem, (2) designing the blockchain artifact acting as the digital hub of the ecosystem, and (3) formulating the governance rules between the stakeholders. Collectively, these insights advance the current theoretical and practical understanding of how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated.

Keywords: Blockchain, Archetypes, Ecosystem Orchestration, Visual Mapping, Strategic Considerations

## 1 Introduction

Blockchain is widely considered an innovative technology (Andersen & Bogusz, 2019). While its earliest use cases pertained to cryptocurrency (Underwood, 2016), blockchain has evolved as a “radical innovation” (Beck & Müller-Bloch, 2017, p. 5390) with applications in several verticals, such as logistics and supply chain (Nærland et al., 2017), advertising (Ghose, 2018), and intellectual property (IP) management (Engelhardt, 2017). Research also shows that when paired with other emerging technologies, such as the Internet of Things (IoT), the benefits of blockchain’s core features—such as decentralization and immutability—can be substantially enhanced (Chanson et al., 2019). Furthermore, prior work has also identified emerging business models that involve blockchain-based offerings (Chong et al., 2019).

Cognizant of this potential, companies offering blockchain solutions have attracted significant investments <sup>1</sup> and deals (e.g., acquisitions). <sup>2</sup> In summary, prior research and industry trends paint an optimistic picture of blockchain’s innovative potential.

However, for an organization to fully realize the benefits of blockchain, its adoption and use among stakeholders in the value chain is warranted. This necessity stems from blockchain being a technology that operates within an ecosystem (Kohli & Liang, 2021). For example, faced with the problem of counterfeiting—fake wine packaged in authentic bottles and sold to unsuspecting customers—Chateau Lafite, a major French winery, considered a blockchain-based solution that enabled tracing a bottle of wine easily and reliably across the supply chain. However, to achieve this objective, multiple stakeholders in the wine supply chain were required to adopt the same solution (e.g., growers, auction houses, and retail outlets). In summary, our central tenet is that a blockchain-based solution invariably requires a robust ecosystem, typically comprising organizations along the value chain. Here, we consider orchestration as “deliberate, purposeful actions undertaken by the hub firm as it seeks to create value and extract value” (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006, p. 659). We rely conceptually on orchestration, given its common association in the literature on ecosystems (Stonig et al., 2022; Harima et al., 2024).

Regardless, orchestrating an ecosystem remains an enduring challenge, which, if unmet, can have catastrophic implications. For instance, in 2018, Maersk and IBM developed TradeLens, a blockchain-based solution for reliably and efficiently tracking packages worldwide. While the solution was robust and enjoyed considerable adoption, it could not be sustained because major industry players did not adopt it, thereby hampering ecosystem orchestration and leading Maersk to discontinue it in 2023 (Lu et al., 2024). This challenge goes beyond the blockchain context (Smedlund & Faghankhani, 2015), as recent studies have documented challenges in orchestrating ecosystems in various contexts (Harima et al., 2024; Cozzolino & Geiger, 2024).

Despite growing interest in ecosystem orchestration, limited attention has been paid to how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated. Such emphasis on context is warranted, given that orchestration logics may vary significantly across different contexts (Giudici et al., 2018). A few exceptions, such as Sarker et al. (2021), span the ecosystem, but they, too, assume the presence of an ecosystem (i.e., the agricultural supply chain) and examine how blockchain helps tackle corruption. However, as stated earlier, our study steps back to examine how a hub firm orchestrates an ecosystem around a blockchain solution. Naturally, such orchestration would be based on an intended benefit.

To this end, we adopted an inductive approach, given the formative nature of the technological context, which renders precise causal relationships opaque. As a result, the hypothetico-deductive research design is less meaningful (Birks & Mills, 2015). Prior information systems (IS) research has a rich tradition of using such inductive approaches (e.g., Orlikowski, 1993), including in studies of enterprise blockchain. For example, Chong et al. (2019) used multiple case studies to ascertain blockchain-driven business models. Dozier and Montgomery (2020) employed grounded theory to investigate how financial organizations made decisions regarding the use of blockchain.

We collected primary data through in-depth interviews with senior decision makers representing 20 distinct blockchain technology vendor organizations. In some instances, a single interview involved multiple respondents from the same organization, including one case in which we simultaneously interviewed four respondents. Collectively, the respondents were involved in more than 60 blockchain implementations. <sup>3</sup> Each interview lasted between 30 and 70 minutes. For analysis, we draw on Langley’s (1999) recommendations, developing visual maps of the blockchain use covered in the interviews. Our motivation for relying on visual maps is twofold. First, blockchain use varies considerably across organizations (Ziolkowski et al., 2020). Visual maps can help us capture these variations more clearly. On a related note, visual maps capture connections between stakeholders, which are instrumental in understanding the orchestration and function of the ecosystem. Second, visual maps facilitate direct comparisons across ecosystems, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of blockchain ecosystem orchestration (Diriker et al., 2023). Our findings are based on four critical considerations underlying ecosystem orchestration: (1) the intended benefits of blockchain, (2) the sides included in the ecosystem, (3) governance formulation (Schmidt & Foss, 2025), and (4) design features of the blockchain artifact. Given our methodology, we did not impose a theoretical structure ex ante, allowing the data to guide our theorization. However, we did evaluate the aforementioned considerations, derived from a review of prior work (Section 2.2).

Our analysis begins by noting distinct archetypes of the intended benefits of blockchain. In the blockchain solutions we studied, these archetypes were identified by the hub firm and served as the basis for the ecosystems that emerged. Interestingly, archetypes of intended benefits varied from relatively passive ones, such as blockchain used to collate data across stakeholders, to more active ones, such as organizations using blockchain to augment their existing offerings. Moreover, we found a hierarchy among these intended benefits, with some more fundamental than others. The second part of our analysis focused on the remaining three contours of ecosystem orchestration: side inclusion, governance formulation, and design features of the blockchain artifact. For each of these considerations, we identified different dimensions. For example, for side inclusion, we identified three roles (i.e., information source, information recipient, and information custodian) that a side must play to be included in the ecosystem.

Our study offers several important contributions. First, the four distinct archetypes of intended benefits of blockchain go beyond the usual technologically articulated benefits (e.g., immutability and traceability) and examine how a hub firm can identify a potential benefit relevant to the wider ecosystem. This insight is particularly useful, given that prior research has not clarified how businesses can benefit from blockchain. As a result, for organizations, the path to value from blockchain remains unclear (Dozier & Montgomery, 2020, p. 1129). Second, our study provides a more comprehensive understanding of how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated, building on prior related work that focuses on orchestration with a single intended benefit (e.g., Gligor et al., 2022) or categorizes ecosystems within a single industry (e.g., Papadonikolaki et al., 2023).

## 2 Background Literature

## 2.1 Blockchain in Organizational Contexts

A growing body of research presents blockchain as a powerful technological paradigm with applications spanning multiple verticals, including finance (Hendershott et al., 2021), supply chain management (Cui et al., 2023), and the art market (Li et al., 2024). Furthermore, empirical evidence reveals the precise conditions under which organizations can benefit from blockchain (Liang et al., 2021). In short, there is evidence showing that blockchain can offer significant benefits to organizations. The related work has expanded significantly, covering a broad spectrum of research issues (see Appendix A for a summary of prior work). We categorize this literature into four distinct themes.

The first set of studies examines newer business models based on blockchain offerings. Notable studies include Chong et al. (2019), who reported five distinct business models, ranging from providers who work with fundamental blockchain technology to those who create custom applications. Next, Schlecht et al. (2021) predicted the multifaceted impact of blockchain on existing business models of blockchain vendors and user organizations. More precisely, Schlecht et al. developed “projections” of blockchain’s impact. Consistent with these projections, subsequent work reports business model implications of blockchain. For example, Jeong and Lim (2023) discussed the role of blockchain in creating new services and markets, such as real-time transaction systems for international payments and car e-wallets, to simplify the use of vehicle services.

The second stream of work assesses blockchain through its salient affordances and features. For example, Du et al. (2019) showed how blockchain affordances are actualized in a single case study of a diversified Chinese conglomerate. Shin and Hwang (2020) demonstrated that users’ perceptions of blockchain’s affordances influence their acceptance and trust. Gan and Lau (2024) adopted a similar approach, in which affordances determine users’ perceptions and use of blockchain. Lichti and Tumasjan (2023) reported a similar set of affordances, including verification and identity protection, in their study of users of early blockchain manifestations (i.e., Bitcoin). Collectively, this stream of work unpacks blockchain in terms of technological (e.g., decentralized ledger) and functional (e.g., transparency) components.

The penultimate research stream examines blockchain from an adoption perspective, although some studies adopting the affordance perspective can also be categorized under this stream. Additionally, some studies have examined adoption behavior without directly mentioning the affordance concept. For example, Li and Juma’h (2022) argued that the unique needs of auditors, such as the need for a shared and decentralized database and transactional traceability, shape the adoption of blockchain for audits. Liang et al. (2021) showed the importance of fit and viability as driving blockchain adoption in healthcare and financial organizations. Guo et al. (2021) underscored the effect of managers’ risk-taking propensity on the adoption of blockchain. More generally, this stream of work connects and draws from more classical research on technology adoption (Liang et al., 2021).

The final stream of work examines blockchain through the community lens. These studies focus on how blockchain communities emerge, self-govern, and are sustained. Interestingly, such communities may exhibit equally novel organizational forms. For example, Ellinger et al. (2024) studied several decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) and identified their operating patterns. Miranda et al. (2022) emphasized underlying conflicts in such democratized communities and studied how the diversity of ideas and frames integrates into an otherwise coherent blockchain community. Miranda et al. showed that the coherence between community members is predicated on their affinity with blockchain technology.

While each of these streams may appear different (e.g., the theoretical lenses they adopt), their respective empirical contexts allude to the ecosystem nature of blockchain technology. However, in most studies, this idea is implicit and plays no active role in the eventual findings (e.g., Zhang et al., 2021). Given this limitation, greater attention should be paid to examining how such ecosystems emerge and sustain. Our study advances current understanding on this front by assessing how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated.

A notable study that is closely related to ours is Sarker et al. (2021), which demonstrated the use of blockchain to reduce corruption across an agricultural ecosystem spanning multiple continents. However, Sarker et al. (2021) also presumed a functional ecosystem among those involved in the agricultural supply chain and then considered addressing the inherent problems within it (i.e., corruption). In contrast, our study takes a step back to examine how ecosystems are orchestrated in the first place. In other related studies, such as Gligor et al. (2022) & Papadonikolaki et al. (2023), valuable insights were offered for blockchain ecosystem orchestration. However, these studies focused on either a single intended benefit (i.e., traceability) or the categorization of ecosystems within a single industry. Our work complements these insights, providing a more comprehensive view of how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated, highlighting the key considerations the hub firm must navigate. As the next section describes, these considerations are informed by the existing literature on ecosystem orchestration.

## 2.2 Ecosystems Orchestration

Organizational scholars define orchestration as “deliberate, purposeful actions undertaken by the hub firm as it seeks to create value (expand the pie) and extract value (gain a larger slice of the pie) from the network” (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006, p. 659). Thus, for the orchestration to occur, the presence of a hub firm, acting as a conduit for other stakeholders interested in focal economic activity, is warranted. In such a setup, the hub firm formulates activities that promise and deliver mutual benefits for other stakeholders. For example, Harima et al. (2024) proposed a framework of ecosystem orchestration to illustrate the role of anchor (i.e., hub) organizations in managing and coordinating various elements of the ecosystem. The importance of the hub firm and its ability to set up the ecosystem rules is underscored by Shi et al. (2024), who traced the emergence of a handset manufacturer from being a mere complementor in the Android ecosystem to a hub firm of its “derivative ecosystem” (Shi et al., 2024, p. 1084). In short, ecosystem orchestration begins with the hub firm setting the rules and formulating policies for stakeholders. This rather intuitive statement, however, masks a significant complexity: the hub firm must navigate four distinct yet equally critical aspects when orchestrating the ecosystem. These aspects, discussed subsequently, make orchestrating ecosystems a nontrivial endeavor.

The first aspect pertains to identifying the intended benefits of the ecosystem, starting with those for the hub firm. Prior work on ecosystem orchestration has alluded to such intended benefits. For instance, Dhanaraj and Parkhe (2006) articulated the importance of creating and extracting value (p. 659), which we argue would be contingent on the intended benefits as articulated by the hub firm. Further, in empirical works involving ecosystem orchestration, such intended benefits are evident. Giudici et al. (2018) examined ecosystem orchestration among venture networks and incubators. The intended benefit of this ecosystem was to “support dispersed entrepreneurial activity among its members” (Giudici et al., 2018, p. 1369). Similarly, Shi et al. (2024) discussed Xiaomi’s development of its own ecosystem, with the intended benefit for stakeholders being “expansion of business” through efficiency and new product lines (p. 1105). In the context of blockchain, however, articulating such benefits is complicated, given the technology’s formative role. As a result, firms may view blockchain as a “lower priority due to the lack of a clear path to value” (Dozier & Montgomery, 2020, p. 1129). Therefore, in our exploration of blockchain ecosystem orchestration, we pay special attention to the intended benefits.

The second aspect the hub firm must navigate concerns the sides to include in the ecosystem. While the traditional network effect argument suggests that adding multiple sides is more beneficial (given the higher volume), the hub firm needs to be watchful, as each side may entail different risks and priorities. As a result, while more sides may provide higher volume, managing the ecosystem is likely to become more complex. Therefore, for the hub firm orchestrating the ecosystem, the inclusion of a side should be a conscious decision. For instance, SAP’s migration to the ecosystem model, with its NetWeaver solution, warranted the addition of several stakeholders, including business process communities and developer networks. However, for many such stakeholders, inclusion in the SAP ecosystem came with significant risks, such as becoming an acquisition target or the creation of competing products (Huang et al., 2012). Similarly, Sydow et al. (2022) advocated for hub firms to be selective in deciding which sides to add to create reliable, less risky ecosystems. Busch and Barkema (2022) also emphasized the importance of including competent stakeholders to build an efficient, scalable ecosystem.

The penultimate consideration concerns governance formulation. As frequently discussed by prior work, ecosystems often struggle to manage complex governance issues. Autio (2022) highlighted the evolving need for governance as ecosystems mature, identifying four layers of ecosystem orchestration, each with distinct governance considerations. More crucially, however, he reported various governance challenges in orchestrating the ecosystems, such as stakeholders’ varying goals, which render a unified governance approach somewhat infeasible. Similarly, Rietveld et al. (2021) suggested that certification is a crucial governance tool, but its utility depends on the design and motivations of the stakeholders involved in the ecosystem. In summary, the importance of governance in ecosystem orchestration and development cannot be overstated. The manifold interpretations of governance further complicate this problem. For instance, a typical understanding of governance stems from classifying ecosystems along dimensions such as centralized versus decentralized (Ferlito & Faraci, 2024; Hoffmann et al., 2023) or open versus closed (Giudici et al., 2018). However, these classifications offer only a structural understanding of governance, capturing what an ecosystem may look like. This understanding can be significantly augmented by a more process-oriented view in which governance is translated into what the ecosystem stakeholders should do.

To this end, we draw from a recent insightful work on “governance capabilities” of the hub firm in an ecosystem (Schmidt & Foss, 2025). This study identified two distinct types of governance capabilities: capabilities for selecting and controlling participants. The first class of capabilities affords the hub firm to “decide who gets to participate and who is excluded” (Schmidt & Foss, 2025, p. 1494), making them akin to the first aspect of ecosystem orchestration discussed earlier (i.e., side inclusion). Therefore, here we focus on the second class, namely, capabilities for controlling participants. More specifically, these capabilities allow the hub firm to “exercise control over participants’ activities or the nature of the output they produce” (Schmidt & Foss, 2025, p. 1494). As our visual maps reveal, in the context of blockchain, control capabilities involve determining stakeholders’ access to information, where a particular stakeholder is allowed to add information to the blockchain artifact (e.g., a distributed ledger) or to read the current information. Moreover, our work is motivated by the fact that blockchain ecosystems pose a distinctive governance challenge (Beck et al., 2018).

Finally, the hub firm must consider the design and features incorporated into the digital artifact, which, in this study, is a blockchain-based solution. Yet again, in the prior studies on ecosystem orchestration, particularly those developed around digital technology, this aspect has received considerable attention. The underlying logic of this emphasis is that the design elements of the core digital artifact may shape stakeholders’ behavior and, in turn, influence the ecosystem’s success. For example, Danatzis et al. (2024) presented principles for creating “end-user centric design,” comprising multiple layers of data protection and data exchange control (p. 1771). In their study of browser ecosystems, Tian et al. (2022) found that the hub firm of the smaller ecosystem technologically introduced “compatibility” with the bigger ecosystem, hoping to attract developers from the latter (p. 1966). However, this design change led to asymmetric effects concerning how developers “multihomed” on the two browser ecosystems. Lastly, Ye et al. (2014) reported the implications of eBay’s decision to alter its feedback system, which involves both sellers and buyers, on the former’s participation in the popular online auction ecosystem. Thus, prior work has repeatedly emphasized the importance of designing the core digital artifact keeping ecosystem stakeholders in mind.

In summary, to assess how blockchain ecosystems are orchestrated, we focus on four distinct aspects of orchestration (see Figure 1). As the later sections discuss, this template informs the development of visual maps from interview-based primary data obtained from blockchain solution providers.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Data Collection

We collected data through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with representatives from 20 technology firms offering blockchain solutions (hereafter, blockchain vendors). Each interview lasted between 30 and 70 minutes (see Table 1). Further, each interview included 1- 2 respondents from a given blockchain vendor. However, in only one instance did we interview four representatives of a single blockchain vendor. Collectively, our interviews yielded over 800 minutes of rich and experiential content on blockchain implementations and their applications. Table 1 provides a summary of respondents’ backgrounds and involvement in the blockchain space.

Interviews are a prevalent method of data collection in IS research (Polkinghorne, 2005). Data from in-depth interviews offers pivotal insights into adopting, implementing, using, and evaluating various technologies. For example, Tarafdar et al. (2023) conducted a single-case qualitative study with 23 individuals to assess how ICT practices enhance workplace inclusion. Similarly, Mann et al. (2022) conducted 26 interviews as part of their longitudinal case study on orchestrating digital transformation. Lastly, blockchain process studies have primarily used interviews for data collection. For instance, Dozier and Montgomery (2020) reported findings from interviews on how fintech organizations make decisions about blockchain. Similarly, Du et al. (2019) revealed a process model depicting the actualization of blockchain affordances in fintech (p. 62).

In the interviews, respondents primarily conveyed information about one or more use instances of their blockchain solutions (Koh et al., 2011). However, given the largely exploratory nature of the work, we allowed and encouraged respondents to freely share their experiences. This approach enabled us to gain a more detailed understanding of how the user organization adopted and utilized blockchain. Furthermore, for each of the eight selected instances of blockchain implementation, we also searched for secondary sources, including press releases and industry reports. For example, the press release about the blockchain solution for the car manufacturer highlighted it as part of the manufacturer’s broader “car-as-a-platform” strategy.<sup>4</sup>

## 3.2 Data Analysis

For analysis, we developed visual maps inductively, creating visual depictions of each blockchain use (Langley, 1999). In general, inductive methods of data analysis are recommended when researchers are relying on data to discover insights, allowing them to thus bypass the hypothetico-deductive approach that is typically used. Naturally, inductive methods are better suited when the study context is considerably unexplored, such that the underlying causal or associative linkages are unclear (Birks & Mills, 2015). Prior IS research has successfully adopted inductive methods, particularly in contexts of technological changes in emerging domains (Wiesche et al., 2017) and the evolution of novel technologies (Ellinger et al., 2024).

While visual maps stop short of formal theory, they do offer a certain degree of abstraction from the raw process data. With this view, Langley (1999) refers to visual maps as a “mid-range” theorization technique. We selected visual mapping as the primary analysis technique for two reasons. First, the ecosystem view of blockchain use (see Section 2.1) requires depicting the roles of different stakeholders and their interconnections. Such complexities can be readily captured with visual maps. For example, Huber et al. (2017) employed visual maps to illustrate how the interorganizational understanding of the goal influences the interaction between relational and contractual governance in the IT outsourcing context, where both forms coexist. Second, visual maps enable more efficient comparisons across different use cases and the elicitation of more abstract insights into how organizations utilize blockchain. To these ends, other inductive theorization techniques may be less tractable, especially given the number of instances of blockchain use (i.e., eight) (Huber et al., 2017).

We developed our visual maps iteratively. First, we scanned each interview for information on the specific use instance. Although the respondents collectively discussed more than 60 instances of blockchain implementation, each example varied in depth and scope. Given this heterogeneity, we relied on eight instances to develop the visual maps (Table 2). We arrived at this number because assessing more instances did not add meaning to the insights from visual maps (Eisenhardt, 1989).

Another factor that aided in selecting specific use instances was the information the respondent provided for each instance. For example, our first interview referenced four distinct use cases, including a blockchain solution that offers traceability of precious metals for a major stock exchange and a solution to measure sustainable sourcing and production for a large metal manufacturing firm. However, the respondent discussed the stock exchange use instance in greater detail. Hence, we chose the former to develop the visual map, as it provided a significantly richer depiction of its use. Similarly, in another interview, the respondent narrated several examples without venturing into the depths of the implementation.<sup>5</sup>

For developing the visual maps, we emphasized considerations for ecosystem orchestration, as reviewed in Section 2.2. For the eight instances, we began by identifying the quotes from respondents about the specific implementation of the blockchain solution. First, we sought to identify the central entity or the hub firm. As the orchestration literature has emphasized, this actor is crucial in bringing other stakeholders into the ecosystem. Next, we sought to articulate the intended benefit to the hub firm from the ecosystem. This benefit formed the core proposition for the hub firm to consider a blockchain solution and orchestrate the ecosystem around it. To identify these intended benefits, we reviewed interview transcripts, focusing particularly on questions related to the need for blockchain use (see Appendix B for the interview protocol). From each response, we identified sections that explicitly stated the need. Therefore, we combined these first-order categories into more general archetypes. In this way, we abstracted archetypes of intended benefits from the interview quotes (Marx et al., 2025). We followed this process until we arrived at four distinct archetypes that capture the intended benefits of blockchain applications across the eight cases mentioned above (see Table 2). Appendix C provides the quotes and first-order categories for each of the four archetypes.

In the next step, we assessed other ecosystem stakeholders and their roles in delivering the intended benefits outlined above. As discussed in Section 2.2, this aspect of visual map development concerns side inclusion when orchestrating the ecosystem. In identifying these stakeholders, we also ascertained, using quotes, their roles in the ecosystem. Continuing with the stock exchange example, we identified the stakeholders, such as a gold exporter, a refiner, and a lab manager, each performing a distinct function in the gold supply chain. However, we particularly emphasized the role of the stakeholder in terms of information, assessing whether the focal stakeholder generated, consumed, or both, allowing us to capture how they interacted with the core system, specifically the blockchain components.

This approach segued into the next step in visual map development: the design features and functions of the technology, which concerns the next key consideration in ecosystem orchestration—design selection (Section 2.2). For instance, in the stock exchange scenario, we examined the physical and digital artifacts involved in ecosystem creation. For example, we identified Gold Vault and the audit system as physical and digital artifacts in the ecosystem, respectively. The purpose of these artifacts was to facilitate information flow within the ecosystem and externally (e.g., using blockchain solutions, gold tokens could be traded outside the focal ecosystem). Finally, we focused on quotes involving governance, which, as clarified in Section 2.2, is essential to ecosystem orchestration. More specifically, we examined whether the stakeholder could only view information on the blockchain (visibility access) or view and add new information (exchange access). Additionally, we considered whether the stakeholder complied with external norms and rules (such as refiner standards that the gold refiners were required to meet). Lastly, for each implementation, this process was conducted iteratively to ensure that respondents’ insights were reflected in the visual map. As illustrations, Figures 2a and 2b provide visual maps for two distinct implementations.

Additionally, we did not discard the remaining instances of blockchain use. Instead, after developing visual maps for the eight instances, we juxtaposed them with relevant interview information. For instance, in the first interview, the respondent mentioned that a large manufacturing firm used a blockchain solution to track its carbon footprint and depict its sustainable supply chain. Although the intended benefit for the manufacturing firm would differ from that of the stock exchange, the two visual maps are structurally similar. Similarly, one respondent discussed the implementation of blockchain for a global non-profit organization. The information shared by the respondent was indicative of key aspects of ecosystem orchestration but lacked the details necessary to develop the visual map. For example, the respondent shared information about the stakeholders in the ecosystem but did not provide details about their roles. Likewise, the respondent also shared some information about access to the information (important for the governance formulation aspect of ecosystem orchestration). However, specific details (e.g., who had what level of access) were lacking for creating the visual map. As a result, the visual map for this case (see Appendix D) was less definitive.

While promising, our empirical approach faced a significant challenge: the visual maps are based on information from representative blockchain vendors. To that extent, our visual maps are unidimensional. For instance, the visual map of the blockchain used in the stock exchange instance could certainly be enhanced if we were to interview representatives from the stock exchange. Moreover, the intended benefit, as we have articulated, pertains to that of the hub firm. However, other stakeholders in the ecosystem may also benefit from being part of it. However, our study does not explicitly capture such collective benefits. Although we made an effort to augment the primary data through interviews with secondary sources such as press publications, we acknowledge this limitation explicitly in the study’s closing arguments.

## 4 Findings

## 4.1 Intended Benefit: Collation

The most basic use of blockchain involves aggregating data from multiple sources into a single, reliable repository. For example, in the case of a stock exchange, a blockchain solution was used to track the movement of gold from mines to vaults (Figure 2a). To this end, the exchange gathered information from key stakeholders in the supply chain, including gold refiners, quality assurance laboratories, and storage vault managers. This information was then stored on a private blockchain. This idea was highlighted by the respondent with the following quote:

It is more of an internal audit system. You could look at it as where all the details from all the future onboarded refiners, as well as the current refiners, it’s stored on a single tamper-proof database., & you can basically log the information in real-time. Meaning when a refiner imports raw gold, converts into finished bar, that information is logged in real time along with sourcing details, along with [an] independent lab verifying its validity or authenticity of the transaction. So, you have the entire chain that is logged in real-time, which can be used for an internal audit system.

![](/api/attachments/3MXDFAPW/fulltext/images/2f8eeca2cce02dd0c6dd58c13a87a9f156e956c7a87654027adbe1b213e6a609.jpg)  
Figure 2a: Visual Map for The Stock Exchange Use Instance

The respondent reported a similar use in the case of a coffee producer (Appendix Figure E1), where the organization sought to trace coffee beans from farming/growing to delivery. To this end, they used a blockchain solution to collect real-time information from multiple stakeholders, including farmers and transporters. This information was stored on a private blockchain, providing visibility into the goods’ movements to all stakeholders across the supply chain. The respondent’s corresponding quote highlights this fact.

Farmer-producer companies showing I’m selling two tons of coffee, which could be coming not from one farmer in this case, multiple farmers aggregate the product, and still traceability is maintained, they sell, let’s say, 30 bags to [X], [X] receives it in their warehouse, the entire bag., & they show that digitally on our platform.

Together, these instances reveal a common purpose: deploying blockchain applications to consolidate information dispersed across multiple stakeholders in the supply chain. Therefore, we combined these uses into a first-order category called spatial data aggregation. Further, data aggregation can help provide a temporal view of a single stakeholder or key resource as it moves through the ecosystem. The respondent from the blockchain implementation for the premium coffee maker explained it in the following quote:

So, every point in time, as the goods physically moved from one location to the other location. It is digitally orchestrated on our system there by appending to the traceability of that particular product.

Therefore, we combined such uses into the first-order category called temporal data aggregation. It is, however, important to note that organizations may use the same blockchain application for uses under both types of first-order categories (e.g., while our quotes do not indicate this, it would be possible for the stock exchange to assess the quality of operations of a single refiner over time). Finally, we combined these two categories into the first intended benefit archetype, termed collation. As its name suggests, this archetype refers to organizations that leverage blockchain applications to consolidate information from multiple sources. As we later elaborate, this archetype of intended benefit is a starting point, playing a role in other, more complex archetypes.

## 4.2 Intended Benefit: Representation

Our respondents also reported instances in which blockchain technology created a digital equivalent of an existing artifact or resource. Note the distinction of such uses from those reflected in collation, where information was merely aggregated. Consider the following quote from the respondent in the patent management case. The respondent stated that a patent was converted into a nonfungible token (NFT) and traded.

We have implemented a tokenization of patents, where each patent with certain attributes and metadata is tokenized as a non-fungible token, and it’s put on the blockchain, creating a new asset class. This asset class is an information product with an authentic track and trace of the ownership of patents, where it has changed hands, and the current owner captured in a way that can be trusted and is publicly available. ... So, trading did happen earlier also, but it was very opaque., & it was very difficult and was extremely complicated., & it wasn’t transparent, in the sense that it is possible today, that if I choose to, I can put it on an open bid, which was something that you’ve not heard of, right, like I can run an English or Dutch auction on my patent, if I want to. I mean, I’m saying it’s possible. I’m not saying it is happening, I’m saying it’s possible, probably you’ve not heard of that happening earlier, because it just technology didn’t permit it. ... So suddenly, the market becomes accessible to a lot more players.

We interpret this case as involving a non-digital asset (i.e., a patent) and its digital equivalent, which have a codependency. Namely, the NFT of the patent is only valuable because a real patent backs it, while a patent becomes an efficiently tradeable asset because it is represented as an NFT. We characterize such cases as codependent digitalization. A respondent from the stock exchange case made a similar point, stating that, once gold-backed tokens are created, they can be traded:

Tokens backing those bars can be created and traded globally, if you want it on a large public blockchain like Ethereum either for semi-regulated cryptocurrency or otherwise, just with cryptocurrency.

Another related set of uses we noted involved blockchain implementations that created a digital representation of an abstract market characteristic. For instance, in the following quote, the respondent in the automobile manufacturing case explained how their platform works with brands, allowing them to create NFTs and foster customer loyalty. In such instances, there is no direct mapping between a non-digital resource and a digital representation. Instead, digital representation manifests characteristics that operate in an ambient manner (e.g., customer loyalty). Therefore, we group these uses into ambient resource manifestation, another first-order category.

So, you kind of sell NFTs to carve out your most loyal and most fans in your customer base who are willing to engage with the brand in a deeper way and be rewarded in some way. … there may or may not be a physical product behind this. … Through our B2B2C platform, we are offering brands an ability to launch their NFTs and tap the consumer surplus. For example, a customer has launched their campaign on our platform. So, what they are doing is they are selling each NFT for an amount that goes towards [the] planting of trees.

These categories (e.g., codependent digitalization and ambient resource manifestation) were combined in the third intended benefit archetype, which we termed representation.

## 4.3 Intended Benefit: Replacement

Our interviews also revealed instances where blockchain applications rendered physical artifacts from existing processes redundant. For example, consider the following quote from a case of blockchain implementation at an automobile manufacturer. Specifically, the quote discusses using a blockchain to maintain car health and usage data.

Cars have a lot of data that they generate, right? And this data is being stored by the OEM. The problem really is that in the past, you have seen other companies come up with solutions with OBD ports, where they can put something in the OBD port and then capture this data, which for us seems like a redundant thing to do because the OEM is already sitting on a boatload of data. So, the way we kind of designed the solution was to say, no external hardware needed, right? The OEM is sitting on the data; just make sure that you take the consent of the end user, and then enable secure sharing of the data between the OEM and its partners ... the idea was that multiple parties should be able to share data with each other securely, and in a way which is auditable.

In this quote, the blockchain solution rendered the onboard diagnostic (OBD) port, a physical artifact within the vehicle’s infrastructure, redundant for data capture. Instead, a blockchain solution continuously captures car health data and stores it in a single repository. A similar example was highlighted by the respondent in the context of the agriculture loan approvals using blockchain. More specifically, the following quote from the respondent clarified the role of blockchain-based receipt of warehouse stock as a more credible alternative to non-digital receipts:

Earlier, farmers used to do it only with local cooperative banks., & whatever banks commercial banks tried to get in burn their fingers because some dishonest farmers or traders would want to just say that this is a warehouse receipt, lend against, there’s nothing in the warehouse, or you go to three or four different banks against the same receipt, even if there is something in the warehouse. So many banks stayed out of it. So, what [Y] is trying to do is keep the warehouse receipt on chain for two purposes. One, it’s authenticated. So, you can look at the warehouse receipt and say that this is genuine, as against the paper, which seems genuine but can be forged. Second is a single mortgage.

In these examples, it is clear that the organizations deployed blockchain applications to render physical artifacts redundant. Therefore, we combined such examples into a first-order category termed physical artifact redundancy.

Additionally, we found instances in which organizations used blockchain applications to replace a stakeholder in an existing process. Consider the following quote from the respondent in the accounting and transaction settlement case. The quote highlights how the blockchain implementation and the underlying consensus mechanism eliminated the need for a notary, a key stakeholder. Therefore, we characterized such uses of blockchain as a separate first-order category, called process stakeholder redundancy.

We have built a protocol using blockchain that supports triple-entry accounting. ... So, in double-entry accounting, you have two parties who transact between them, there’s a debit and credit on either side, in triple-entry accounting, there’s a third party who becomes the notary for the transaction., & that notary in our protocol, the way it works is the notary puts it out for consensus within the network., & that verifies the consensus and then publishes it to the ledger. So, by doing that, we are able to do real-time reconciliation, realtime audit, and real-time settlement.

The combination of these two first-order categories led to the third archetype of intended benefits: replacement.

The above instances also reveal at least two distinct pathways through which organizations seek replacementbased intended benefit from blockchain applications. First, an organization may directly move from collation to replacement. For example, in the case of an automobile manufacturer, the blockchain solution replaced a hardware component (i.e., OBD) commonly used in cars. A specialized blockchain-backed feature replaced this component in the auto manufacturer’s existing car vehicle infrastructure. However, this blockchain solution (1) collated data from multiple systems within the vehicle (collation), (2) stored this data with the auto manufacturer, and (3) shared the data with third-party entities, such as insurance companies, provided the concerned user approved the sharing. Thus, the automobile manufacturer sought replacement after collation (See Figure 2b).

![](/api/attachments/3MXDFAPW/fulltext/images/bf537d61dd60a4ab663dadc3d1dfcfe691babff3de83b7d981b17e4ae3fc4cd3.jpg)  
Figure 2b: Visual Map for the Car Manufacturer Use Instance

The second pathway for replacement follows collation and representation. In the case of agricultural loan intermediaries, the blockchain solution was utilized to provide a digital token in exchange for loan collateral (Appendix Figure E2). The information pertaining to the digital token was collected from various stakeholders (e.g., warehouse managers and farmers) and stored on the blockchain (collated). This token served as a digital equivalent of a traditional physical document, endorsing the borrower’s ownership of assets (representation). This digital receipt, when presented at a lending institution, unequivocally suggested that the borrower had secured assets of a specific value. As a result, the representation of the receipt by a digital equivalent replaced an existing component of agriculture lending (replacement).

## 4.4 Intended Benefit: Extension

Lastly, we identify extension as the final intended benefit archetype. Under this category, organizations utilize blockchain solutions to develop new offerings or enhance existing ones. For example, the blockchainbased news and media content aggregation app collated content from multiple sources (Appendix Figure E3). Although through collation, the app offered the usual benefits of efficient access to more comprehensive news content, it faced a challenge: Readers were reluctant to complete the registration/subscription process to consume it. In response, the blockchain solution introduced an access token that could be purchased with fiat currencies. Once purchased, the access tokens served as prepaid wallets, eliminating the need for users to undergo a cumbersome registration process. The respondent expounded on this process in the following quote:

We’re working with a partner who has digitized most of the publishers ranging from [A], [B], [C], [D] around the world for last 20 years, okay, they run their mobile apps., & they had basically two pain points. News authenticity, content authenticity, which is a problem. A bigger pain point is around the paywalls. So, we have built a Super App tokenized-access tokens, so I subscribe to it’s like a subscription network, but now it’s decentralized. There is no dispute resolution., & because there is no dispute resolution, which has to be done explicitly. It’s more efficient and low cost. More money goes to publishers. So, let’s say I bought \$100 worth of access tokens on the blockchain., & every time I watched the New York Times, let’s say reviews, a cent a watch 100 times is \$1. So, it gets automatically settled or accounted for. So, the thing is, our publishers just want to get paid on a monthly basis, daily basis. They don’t really care how much it is; they want cash registers to ring.

We bucketed such cases in which the blockchain solution enabled automatic settlement between two or more parties, often through a smart contract, leading to our next first-order category, termed contract automation.

We also found instances where organizations used a blockchain solution to enhance their current offerings directly. For example, in the case of an automobile manufacturer, the blockchain solution enabled them to add previously excluded stakeholders (i.e., insurance providers).

What that means is that every vehicle who signs up for the passport, maintains the data about that vehicle in an immutable ledger, which is, of course, the blockchain., & the consent after being taken from the end user, the end user has control of the data. So, the hypothesis is that the custodian of the data is [company], but the owner of the data is the end user, i.e., the individual., & if they so choose, they can share the data right now with one of two insurance companies, and the third insurance company to be added., & the value proposition in this case is usagebased insurance. Right, you can basically now allow the insurance company to validate the risk profile of the driver based on sensor data from the car, which is stored in the passport and is immutable, auditable, digitally signed time stamp., & each one of them can assign a driver score, which can then be used for offering premium discounts to the end user. So that’s a great use case where we are using the platform.

Similarly, in the case of blockchain implementation for a patent marketplace, the respondent highlighted the need to include a wider set of stakeholders through a public blockchain to ensure that patent ownership and dependencies can be readily tracked (Appendix Figure E4). Thus, the patent marketplace decided to adopt a new blockchain solution to expand the ecosystem.

They realize that [existing solution] doesn’t give them the benefit of public visibility, which was very essential in this case, because you want to track ownership, but not on a private chain, you want to track ownership on a public chain, so that you know, it’s visible and it’s recorded, and it’s immutable in a certain way.

To this end, the marketplace created a token for each patent. The respondent clarified this point in the following quote:

We have implemented a tokenization of patents, where each patent with certain attributes and metadata is tokenized as a nonfungible token, and it’s put on the blockchain, creating a new asset class. This asset class is an information product with an authentic track and trace of the ownership of patents, where it has changed hands, and the current owner captured in a way that can be trusted and is publicly available. So, if somebody has to challenge it, that’s also, you know, feasible. So that’s the first part. So tokenizing patents immediately made it tradable. So, that was one good input, effective way of doing it now. Say we can trade it; we have the history of changing hands, and you have all the data associated with it.

We combined these two cases (i.e., an automobile manufacturer and a patent marketplace) because they shared the common attribute of expanding the existing ecosystem. Such expansion, we argue, would result in newer value flows. Grouping these two cases created our subsequent first-order category, termed ecosystem expansion. Finally, we note that the newly created firstorder categories—contract automation and ecosystem expansion—have a common thread: They capture instances in which organizations enhance their existing businesses by directly adding new workflows. Therefore, we grouped them and named the fourth resulting intended benefit archetype extension.

Further, much like in the case of replacement, we found two distinct pathways for extension. According to the first pathway, organizations transition directly from collation to extension. For example, in the case of a news and media content aggregation application, beyond content collation, the aggregator application utilizes a token to enhance its current offering (extension). Similarly, the second pathway for extension involves a shift from collation followed by representation. As in the case of public blockchain solutions involving patents, patent tokenization (representation) makes them tradable, significantly simplifying an existing investment device.

In summary, the discussion so far has revealed four distinct intended-benefit archetypes and their interdependencies. Namely, we found collation to be the foundational intended benefit that enables the rest. Further, we found multiple pathways by which organizations attempt to achieve higher-order intended benefits (i.e., replacement and extension). These findings are captured in Figure 3.

## 4.5 Orchestrating Blockchain Ecosystem: Side Inclusion

Side inclusion, as a strategic consideration, concerns a fundamental conflict within platforms. Namely, adding more sides can offer benefits from network effects, but more sides may also lead to more conflict (Hagiu, 2014). Thus, when orchestrating ecosystems, organizations need to be watchful. However, the visual maps reveal a limited presence of such conflicts. A possible reason is that, in our use cases, blockchain was deployed as a foundational technology that supported the organization’s core business (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2017). With limited conflict between sides, we found that organizations added stakeholders based on their specific roles in managing information flows. Namely, we identified three such roles: information provider, information recipient, and information custodian. These roles were indirectly referred to by one of the respondents in the following quote:

Of course, the benefit for regulators or buyers is buyers know the quality of the bar as well as the sourcing details of the bar that they are buying. So, you have benefits for the stock exchange, buyers, and refiners. Regulators, of course, can see a real-time view into all the data, all the transactions that are happening from onboarded accredited refiners in real time.

![](/api/attachments/3MXDFAPW/fulltext/images/85836e2656603e932f8d143b47327bdae628975c4f6c9ea8960517310a6512a4.jpg)  
Figure 3. Intended Benefits and Underlying Hierarchy

Further, these roles did not always map onto the roles in the supply chain of the concerned organization. For instance, in the stock exchange case, upstream stakeholders (i.e., gold refineries and labs) served as information providers, while downstream stakeholders (i.e., jewelers and retail buyers) served as information recipients. In contrast, the case of the car manufacturer depicted a much fuzzier mapping in which the downstream stakeholder (i.e., the car customer) served as the information provider, and a stakeholder outside the value chain (i.e., the car insurance company) served as the information recipient.

The visual maps also reveal that the information custodian role was largely supplementary, as no stakeholder performed this role in isolation. In each case, the information custodian was either the information provider or the consumer. Further, based on the nature of the information, the information custodian’s role varied in its centralization. For instance, in the case of coffee production, the information pertained to the product. As a result, the information custodian role was centralized, allowing the coffee producer near-total oversight over the information flowing through the blockchain (Appendix Figure E1). In contrast, for the car manufacturer, the information pertained to customers. As a result, the custodian role was divided between the car manufacturer and the customer (Figure 2b). This distinction was acknowledged by the respondent in the following quote. In summary, when orchestrating ecosystems, organizations include stakeholders based on the roles they play.

The data about that vehicle is in an immutable ledger, which is, of course, the blockchain., & the consent after being taken from the end user, the end user has control of the data. So, the custodian of the data is the OEM, but the owner of the data is the end user, i.e., the individual.

## 4.6 Orchestrating Blockchain Ecosystem: Design Selection

As with side inclusion, the tension underlying design selection involves conflicts among stakeholders (Hagiu, 2014). However, our visual maps reveal a much more elaborate approach to including design features. Namely, we observed a layered approach in which all organizations sought to achieve information immutability and decentralization. Thus, the relevant design features (e.g., a consensus mechanism) were essential for each of the four archetypes of intended benefits. For instance, the efficacy of a “do not disturb” list, which all telecom companies were expected to follow and would be penalized for noncompliance, required a decentralized, tamper-proof list (Appendix Figure E5). The following quote conveys this point:

I [want to] real-time update my systems on Telco side., & that basically gives information about consent to the registries of everyone, not just a central [but] to every node in the blockchain., & thereby, everyone else also have information that the customer doesn’t want to receive a phone call. Now, that’s one big benefit. If you look at the, if you look at the second benefit in the same use case would be enforcement of certain guidelines.

More importantly, the visual maps reveal three other objectives that informed design inclusion. First, organizations sought boundary-spanning activity. In prior IS research, this concept has attracted significant attention. It refers to the act of crossing designated boundaries to enable critical resource flows, such as knowledge (Levina & Vaast, 2005). Along similar lines, boundary spanning in our context pertains to a given information resource, typically maintained on a single blockchain, being usable across multiple external environments, including those on other blockchains. For instance, in the case of the stock exchange, while the primary objective was to make the gold sourcing information available across the supply chain (collation as the intended benefit), the blockchain solution also created digital tokens for gold bars (Figure 2a). Owners of these tokens could trade them outside the permissioned blockchain of the stock exchange. However, such a trade necessitated that the information about the gold bar be maintained on the permissioned blockchain and be accessible on an external permissionless blockchain. To this end, the blockchain solution implemented an information retrieval feature that assigned each gold bar a unique serial number. This number served as a key, granting access to specific information for those on the permissionless blockchain. The respondent communicated this process in the following quote:

For the gold side, you tokenize the gold that you know is responsibly sourced and sustainably produced…it is of a certain purity and quality, and the bars are also of a certain purity., & those backed tokens backing those bars can be created and traded globally if you want it on a large public blockchain.

Second, organizations sought embeddedness, wherein a blockchain-backed resource can be seamlessly deployed within existing digital environments. For instance, in the case of content aggregator apps, the blockchain solution allowed readers to pre-purchase digital access tokens (Appendix Figure E3). These tokens would then be automatically deducted as and when the reader consumes premium content. However, the organization faced significant infrastructure costs when implementing this solution. The blockchain solution addressed this concern by using a subnet feature, which allows the infrastructure of a permissionless blockchain to be “cordoned off” to ensure security and privacy. Once created, this digital boundary would operate as a permissioned blockchain on top of a permissionless blockchain. Thus, a new token could be easily deployed on the existing infrastructure of a permissionless blockchain. Along similar lines, the blockchain solution developed for the car manufacturer was embedded in the car manufacturer’s existing mobile application (Figure 2b). As a result, the solution was part of the overall digital interface between the customer and the car manufacturer. The following quote conveys the point:

## Our customer is the OEM. … Even the end user is our customer because the passport is issued by us, to the customer inside the OEM app.

Lastly, design features were also included to achieve the efficiency of completing a transaction. This notion is consistent with the classical view that platforms reduce transaction costs, and this objective can drive the inclusion of design features (Hagiu, 2014). In addition, efficiency is the foremost value blockchain can offer organizations (Kohli & Liang, 2021). For example, in the telecom industry, where the customer requested a “do not disturb” service, the blockchain’s baseline features ensured compliance, as the list was maintained in a decentralized ledger (Appendix Figure E5). However, if a telecom provider were to call a customer on the “do not disturb” list, the subsequent penalty would be enforced via a smart contract. Similarly, the blockchain solution for real-time reconciliation of financial transactions used a miningless protocol, which significantly improved transaction throughput (Appendix Figure E6). The respondent indicated this point in the following quote:

We were able to do real-time reconciliation, real-time audit, [and] real-time settlement. … We built a POC for a very large MNC bank in India and demonstrated [a] 98% reduction in OpEx for reconciliation.

For this bank, the way the proof of concept was for four lakh cheques a month and they will typically have 12,000 exceptions and less than 50 or 100 delinquencies a day for that period, I mean, whatever the period was. We eliminated the exceptions completely. So, they had to only deal with the delinquencies.

In summary, visual maps reveal that immutability and decentralization are the baseline objectives for including design features, boundary spanning, embeddedness, and efficiency, which act as “second-order” imperatives.

## 4.7 Orchestrating Blockchain Ecosystem: Governance Formulation

The third and final strategic consideration for platforms pertained to formulating specific governance norms. In concurrence with Hagiu (2014), the visual maps reveal two objectives that guided governance formulation as a strategic consideration. First, user organizations wanted to assess a stakeholder’s legitimacy to be on the platform, at times with formal guidelines. In the stock exchange instance, each refiner had to meet refiner standards set by the stock exchange. In other instances, the assessment of a stakeholder prior to onboarding was much more implicit (i.e., driven by their relevance given the intended benefit of the blockchain solution in that instance). In the case of the car manufacturer (Figure 2b), the intended benefit was collation followed by replacement. Therefore, stakeholders active on the permissioned blockchain included car owners (information producers and custodians), the car manufacturer (information receiver and custodian), and the insurance companies (information receivers). Other stakeholders, such as component manufacturers and showroom owners, were not involved. In conclusion, the organizations either explicitly or implicitly restricted stakeholder onboarding.

So, depending on what outcome you want to achieve, you bring in the right participants and make sure that the data across the chain is connected, digitized, and then it’s also able to give you confidence in the claim that ultimately is being made for the chain. Right, so it’s very important to select the right participants in the chain to get the right credible outcome.

That also depends on who are meeting the stock exchange refiners standards because it is not a system where we can deploy it at any refinery.

Second, under governance formulation, the organizations sought explicit access norms for onboarded parties (Hagiu, 2014). The visual maps reveal two distinct types of access control: visibility access, controlling passive interactions such as viewing information, and exchange access, regulating stakeholders’ ability to use the information. For example, in the case of a blockchain solution offering realtime reconciliation of financial transactions (Appendix Figure E6), the regulator was able to view the transaction data for auditing purposes. As a result, the regulator only had visibility access. Likewise, in the case of the stock exchange, the regulator was able to view the gold sourcing information (Figure 2a). The following quote clarifies how visibility access was operationalized.

There are certain access rights and permissions that we have customized for refiners., & the lab, as well as the logistics transport provider as well. For example, a logistics provider moving the finished bar from the refinery to the vault or moving the raw gold from the customs to the refinery is not going to be able to see details on sourcing certification, etc., because they don’t need to have access to that information.

Next, concerning exchange access, we noted at least two patterns. First, in many permissioned blockchain use cases, there was a central entity (e.g., a stock exchange or a coffee producer) that held significant exchange access. This point connects back with the earlier discussion on the information custodian as a role. However, when the information pertained to customers, the exchange access was divided. For example, in the case of a car manufacturer, the car user was able to view the information generated by the car and permitted the manufacturer to share it with the insurance company. At the same time, the car manufacturer had complete access to information from all vehicles and could share information from specific users after receiving the necessary consent (Figure 2b). ## 5 Conclusion

## 5.1 Summary of Key Findings

Our first finding is the four distinct archetypes, which constitute a generalized set of intended benefits from blockchain. We categorized these archetypes according to how organizations intended to leverage information flows through the blockchain solution, ranging from passive information collation to more active extension of current offerings. Furthermore, we found a hierarchy in which collation serves as a foundational intended benefit, paving the way for more advanced ones, such as extension and replacement. Lastly, we reported key attributes that hub firms must consider and configure as they orchestrate blockchain ecosystems. Collectively, these findings have important implications, which are discussed subsequently.

## 5.2 Implications for Research

Our first contribution is to the research on blockchain ecosystem orchestration. As our literature review indicates, most prior research has examined such ecosystems in community settings, where diverse and distributed actors come together to form a blockchain artifact (Miranda et al., 2022). In contrast, our study examines organizational contexts wherein an existing organization orchestrates a blockchain-based ecosystem comprising existing supply chain actors, regulators, and customers. This distinction is crucial for two reasons. First, in traditional blockchain instances, the absence of a “hub” firm introduces a different set of governance and incentive problems (Andersen & Bogusz, 2019), which may not be relevant when the hub firm is present, as is the case in our study. Thus, our study provides a nuanced understanding of how ecosystems can be orchestrated when the hub firm serves as the primary driver of the blockchain solution. Second, our study addresses the concern in the blockchain literature that the path to blockchain’s business value is unclear (Dozier & Montgomery, 2020). This lack of understanding can be limiting because “identification of need and value proposition is important” (Angelis & da Silva, 2019, p. 312) for organizations to adopt emerging technologies. To this end, our study elaborates on how existing organizations can construct value propositions by drawing on our archetypes. To that extent, our study complements recent efforts that focus on stages, such as the business model, which may follow the identification of value propositions (Chong et al., 2019). We call for further research examining blockchain in contexts where a hub firm promotes the solution, as this could expand the current understanding of how blockchain can provide value to existing organizations (as opposed to emergent and distributed communities) (Huang et al., 2018).

Our next contribution is to the literature on blockchain ecosystems. As reviewed in Section 2.1, the majority of blockchain ecosystem studies assume a functioning blockchain ecosystem, encompassing the complexities of such orchestration without going into the inner workings. Our study unpacks this “black box” by reporting the intended mechanisms and benefits, and the crucial attributes and processes that hub firms must address when orchestrating the blockchain ecosystem. On a related note, our study also contributes to the limited yet promising research on blockchain ecosystem orchestration, which has prioritized context specificity by focusing on a single industry (Papadonikolaki et al., 2023) or emphasizing a single intended benefit (Gligor et al., 2022). However, we cast a wider net, examining blockchain ecosystems from varied industries. Given such breadth, we present a more generalized set of configurable attributes (i.e., side inclusion, design selection, and governance formulation). This represents a significant departure from prior work, which, due to its narrow focus on specific industries, provides more precise configurations of resource bundles (Gligor et al., 2022).

That said, our work can be significantly augmented by including the element of time. Given that ecosystems evolve, it is possible that certain stakeholders and value flows are added subsequently. However, in our study, we examined blockchain ecosystem orchestration without such a longitudinal view. Therefore, we call on future research to examine how ecosystem orchestration unfolds, which may reveal more common patterns. (Leong et al., 2019)

Next, in articulating side inclusion, we also expand the current understanding of stakeholders’ roles in an ecosystem. Prior work has considered stakeholders through a centrality lens, in which complementors are viewed as less central than orchestrators (Cozzolino & Geiger, 2024). This classification is reasonable, given that prior work focuses on the evolutionary nature of ecosystems, where stakeholders may shift from the periphery to the center (Shi et al., 2024). We diverge from this approach by defining stakeholders through an information lens. Namely, we identify three roles that stakeholders can adopt based on their use, access, and control of information within the ecosystem. This classification, we believe, is crucial for ecosystems organized around digital information artifacts (in our case, blockchain solutions) because information flow is essential to stakeholder activities. In continuation of the argument above, this finding could be further enhanced by incorporating a longitudinal perspective of ecosystem orchestration, which may reveal how information-based roles emerged and evolved within a given ecosystem. (Li et al., 2022)

Our next contribution is to the literature on design choices for digital artifacts in ecosystem settings. Prior work, although not directly related to blockchain, has examined how design choices influence behavioral and economic outcomes within the relevant ecosystem (Ye et al., 2014). Thus, the focus has been on the consequences of design choices (Cavusoglu et al., 2016). In blockchain settings, influential design features include smart contracts, which facilitate the efficient enforcement of rules (Miranda et al., 2022). We complement this literature by assessing specific design selection attributes (i.e., boundary spanning, embeddedness, and efficiency) involved in orchestrating the ecosystem. To that extent, our study considers design selection as the goal, rather than the input, as much prior work has done. On a related note, the identified attributes expand the pool of blockchain design choices beyond those defined solely in technological terms, such as immutability and transparency. In this vein, we call for future research to continue expanding blockchain’s features and affordances, a research direction that is becoming increasingly vital. (Rossi et al., 2019).

Our final contribution to research relates to governance in blockchain ecosystems. As discussed in Section 2.2, a prior understanding of governance during ecosystem orchestration is essential, particularly regarding its structural aspects (e.g., open versus closed ecosystems). However, blockchain ecosystems may be more complex, warranting multidimensional consideration of governance (Beck et al., 2018). To that end, our work adds stakeholder legitimacy (e.g., based on third-party standards) and information access as important attributes of governance in blockchain ecosystems. These attributes are particularly salient because we focused on blockchain ecosystems involving hub firms engaged in controlled business transactions, rather than on more democratized collectives such as DAOs. Further research could expand on our identification of the three attributes by considering the perspectives of other stakeholders, an important avenue given the ecosystem nature of blockchain technology. (Tiwana et al., 2010).

## 5.3 Implications for Practice

Our study informs practice in several ways. First, given the variety of blockchain ecosystems, we offer direct use cases alongside more generalized archetypes of intended benefits. Managers should find such details helpful for assessing the need and viability of a blockchain solution for their business problems, particularly because the path to business value from blockchain is unclear (Dozier & Montgomery, 2020). Second, a more general understanding of intended benefits may enable managers to envision how blockchain can go beyond the typical claims of transparency, traceability, and immutability. Finally, breaking down the orchestration of blockchain ecosystems into specific attributes could allow managers to focus on these attributes, which are crucial for ecosystems driven by an established hub firm (Schmidt & Foss, 2025).

## 5.4 Limitations

Our findings should be viewed in light of certain limitations. First, our findings, although based on varied instances of blockchain use, need further assessment for generalization. For instance, the user organization’s legacy and market power may shape how it orchestrates the ecosystem using a blockchain solution. However, in our study, this property was not taken into consideration. Next, while our interviews captured the operations of a given blockchain solution, this data was conveyed at a single point in time (we interviewed each set of respondents only once). Therefore, the notion of time was largely excluded from our analysis. Next, our emphasis on breadth (i.e., covering several sectors) limited our ability to “deep dive” into a single sector (see Du et al., 2019). Our penultimate limitation is that our analysis was based on the responses of blockchain solution providers. However, in IS research, especially involving formative technologies, multiple perspectives can offer more nuanced insights. Finally, given our “process view,” we were not able to precisely identify contingencies that would have allowed the intended benefit to translate into an actual benefit.

## Appendix B: Interview Protocol

Criteria used for the selection of blockchain implementations: First, we focused on organizations that provide blockchain-based solutions. The rationale for this filter was to gain a broad view of blockchain use cases and implementation challenges. Second, we sought organizations that had delivered at least one live blockchain use case. This filter is crucial to ensure that findings are based on cases executed in practice, rather than envisioned in a controlled or “sandbox” setting, which may not lead to a clear identification of value to the customer (Dozier & Montgomery, 2020). Third, we preferred organizations with use cases outside the “cryptocurrency” space. While important and insightful, such cases have already attracted significant research attention. We conformed to this criterion in all except one responden organization. Lastly, organizations that were eventually contacted and satisfied the above criteria were identified through snowball sampling and referrals.

Questions and underlying rationale: The idea of a protocol is to help “standardize agenda” (Yin, 2002, p. 66). However, because we were interviewing stakeholders working in an evolving setting (i.e., Blockchain), we had a small set of common questions and questions based on specific information shared by the respondent. Lastly, we note that all interviews were conducted online and recorded for transcription and analysis.

Questions common across all interviews: Our first question aimed to uncover the background concerning how and why the focal interviewee’s firm operates in the evolving space of enterprise blockchain. This question, in some of the interviews, also highlighted critical pivots whereby the firm began offering solutions distinct from the ones it initially started with:

## 1. How did you/your firm decide to venture into Blockchain technology?

Given our interest in specific blockchain implementations, we followed up with a question focused on a client organization’s use case. In some cases, the interviewee revealed the use case, while in others the interviewer already knew about it (e.g., through secondary data). Given that the subsequent question was framed as per the use case, there was no standard statement. Some examples are provided below.

## 2.1 So, what is triple-entry accounting?

## 2.2 You can choose two or three of the most interesting applications or engagements and talk to take us through how it started, what the benefit is to the customer.

Having identified the specific use case, the interviewees were probed on the basic motivation for selecting blockchain as the solution. This question was important, given that blockchain requires the involvement of multiple organizations and is therefore a more complex endeavor than typical intra-organizational information systems. Once again, because the question was specific to the use case, the precise framing varied; however, the general template included the following text.

## 3. What is the reason to consider implementing blockchain? Why is blockchain the right technology? Was the idea to reduce cost or improve customer service (or any other)?

Building on the interviewees’ responses, they were further asked about the type of blockchain used for the particular use case. Understanding the type of blockchain used was essential to assessing how the selected blockchain aligned with the organization’s business objectives and the related technical requirements. Hence, the following question was asked:

## 4. What is the kind of blockchain technology that you are using (public, private, permissioned/consortium)? Please describe. If a consortium, who are the members, and what is the governance mechanism?

Building on the interviewees’ responses, we probed further to understand the challenges encountered in implementing the specific blockchain. With this question, we aimed to identify the technical barriers, as well as the organizational and ecosystem impediments, surrounding the implementation of blockchain. Moreover, the question was directed to capture the holistic view of the blockchain implementation. While we directly asked about the challenges and steps involved in blockchain implementation (5), in some instances, we supplemented this by asking follow-up questions (a, b, c, d, and e) for further elaboration and clarification.

5. What are some challenges faced in implementing blockchain? Take us through the steps in your organization’s blockchain implementation?

a. Data integration issues faced, if any?

b. Some of the changes that you have to bring into the organization?

c. Any other requirements that you had to fulfil for implementing blockchain?

d. Do you need to cooperate with others to realize the benefits of blockchain fully?

e. How did you put together the team for implementing and using blockchain?

Following that, we asked the interviewees about the benefits of using blockchain for their customers, and in some instances, their customers’ customers. Given that the previous question focused on implementation challenges, this question was crucial for understanding the value proposition for customers and how technology providers articulate it to their customers. The question was framed as follows:

## 6. What are the principal benefits of using blockchain for your customers?

Subsequently, the interviewees were asked about the investment requirements and sustainability of the blockchain-based business model. Although blockchain technology is still in its nascent stage, the objective of this question was to examine the long-term feasibility and economic viability of blockchain-based businesses. Therefore, the question posed was:

## 7. Why do you think blockchain is a sustainable business model? What are the investment requirements?

Thereafter, the interviewees were asked about the factors limiting the scaling of blockchain. The purpose of this question was to understand why blockchain adoption remains limited and what innovations (specific to the domain in which the interviewee operates) are required to accelerate its growth, thus providing an extensive view of blockchain use cases from both present and future perspectives.

## 8. What are the impediments to scaling blockchain?

8.2 Its impact on the market you operate in.
