# Media Affectivity: Theorizing The Role of Digital Media Capabilities in Aligning Team Members’ Affective States

Maylis Saigot,<sup>1</sup> Rob Gleasure,<sup>2</sup> Ioanna Constantiou<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>UQ Business School, The University of Queensland, Brisbane, Australia, m.saigot@uq.edu.au <sup>2</sup>Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Denmark, rg.digi@cbs.dk <sup>3</sup>Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Denmark, ic.digi@cbs.dk

## Abstract

The recent shift toward digital workplaces and online collaboration highlighted the important yet often overlooked implications of affective processes among digitally connected team members. To understand and empathize with one another, team members must often find a way to improve their “affective alignment”—that is, the degree to which each team member’s affective state is responsive to the affective states of others in the team. In this article, we propose a new theoretical framework: media affectivity, which posits that a medium’s affective channels, affective representations, and affective inflection enable and constrain the emergence of affective alignment in teams. Media affectivity helps to understand why teams may feel more or less connected when using different digital media, even when the communicated information is complete, accurate, and mutually understood. Media affectivity further challenges some of our existing assumptions about digital media, including what it means to be “co-located,” what it means to be “synchronous,” and what it means for media to be “embodied.” At a deeper level, media affectivity highlights the need to consider how digital media impact team members’ affective alignment, not only to explain cognition and decision-making but because affective alignment is itself a valuable outcome

Keywords: Digital Media, Emotional Contagion, Online Teams, Future of Work

Robert Gregory was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on January 23, 2024, and underwent three revisions.

## 1 Introduction

In 2022, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) embarked on the pioneering Psyche mission, aimed at unveiling the secrets of a metalrich asteroid nestled between Mars and Jupiter (NASA, 2022a). As the planned launch date approached, unforeseen delays triggered an inquiry by an independent review board (IRB) (Potter, 2022). The IRB report on the Psyche mission identified significant communication issues, particularly regarding informal exchanges (NASA, 2022b). Remote work due to COVID-19 increased teams’ reliance on advanced digital communication media and reduced the spontaneous, face-to-face interactions that typically convey subtle communications among team members (NASA, 2022b). Teams’ reliance on digital media did not appear to damage their exchange of factual or technical information. The report suggests that teams generally shared such information in an accurate, timely, and complete manner. Yet, despite the effective flow of factual and technical information, the IRB report indicates that many team members felt nervous about specific details of the launch but that this nervousness was not communicated among the teams involved (NASA, 2022b). Had team members realized their colleagues were nervous, those team members might have slowed down the process until everyone felt happier and more confident, and this slowdown could have preempted several setbacks. Thus, it appears from the report that it was not enough for team members to use digital media that could communicate accurate, timely, and complete information. They also needed to know what other team members were feeling, not necessarily to help interpret facts and technical details but because those feelings were, themselves, valuable information.

The challenge illustrated by the NASA example is that the discussion of digital media—the set of digital technologies that virtual or hybrid teams use to communicate directly with one another—has largely focused on cognitive elements in modern workplaces, such as developing shared mental models and reducing uncertainty (e.g., Dennis et al., 2008; Kock, 2009; Rafaeli et al., 2012). Yet, as the NASA case illustrates, communication also includes a distinct affective layer that allows individuals to detect and respond to each other’s affective states (de Waal, 2012). This convergence on shared affective states, which we refer to as affective alignment, creates a sense of togetherness and empathy as individuals come to experience similar physiological influences (Hatfield et al., 2014). Once these individuals have become affectively aligned, team members become more sensitive to changes in each other’s affective states, and they can respond when they detect an unexpected or undesirable state (Tee, 2015).

Achieving this alignment is difficult, however, as the affective capabilities of many digital media—the features that allow for the expression and perception of emotion—are limited, making it difficult to communicate these states effectively (Brady et al., 2023; Byron, 2008; Kramer et al., 2014). This problem has become more relevant in recent years, as many organizations have shifted to flexible work models to help team members avoid stressful commutes and find a better work/life balance (Diab-Bahman & Al-Enzi, 2020; Malhotra, 2021). Despite the wide range of digital tools that allow individuals to communicate efficiently and accurately, multiple studies suggest that since this shift, team members have felt less connected to their colleagues (Mihalache & Mihalache, 2021; Yuan et al., 2021) and more lonely in general (Fonner & Roloff, 2010; Sardeshmukh et al., 2012; Weinert et al., 2015).

This growing sense of disconnection and loneliness among teams, even as the digital tools they use become more powerful and efficient, is a major challenge for modern organizations. This paradox persists, we argue, because our dominant frameworks for understanding digital media (like media richness, synchronicity, naturalness, etc.) steer our focus toward cognitive effectiveness, leaving us ill-equipped to explain the emergence of affective alignment. These theories cannot sufficiently differentiate between the conscious communication of symbolized affect (e.g., an emoji) and the basic, often unconscious, affective information that underpins the very feeling of social connection. Without a framework dedicated to this foundational layer, we lack the language to diagnose why technologically rich collaboration can feel so emotionally impoverished.

To build this necessary theoretical foundation, we ask: How do different digital media capabilities enable and constrain affective alignment among team members? We focus on the foundational mammalian affective communications described by de Waal (2012) and de Waal and Preston (2017), and develop “media affectivity” as a dedicated framework to explain this specific outcome. This foundational layer of affective communication typically occurs subconsciously, allowing individuals to detect and respond to the affective states of others, without necessarily realizing what they are doing.

To maintain this sharp focus, our framework is intentionally distinct from cognitive-affective models like Te’eni’s (2001), which examine how emotion is strategically used to influence cognitive tasks. Instead, we develop “media affectivity” as a dedicated theoretical framework to explain the specific outcome of affective alignment. It is not intended as a general theory for all affective phenomena in computer-mediated communication. For instance, while our framework considers the availability and use of emoticons as a capability, it does not prescribe principles for the visual design of a specific emoticon. Its primary concern is how such capabilities facilitate the foundational, often subconscious, layer of affective communication that underpins social connection, rather than the strategic use of emotion to influence cognitive outcomes.

Building this framework of media affectivity requires two important steps. First, we present the underlying theoretical logic for our framework—the logic that explains behaviors and provides the why and how for the framework (Hedström & Ylikoski, 2010; Sutton & Staw, 1995; Weber, 2012). In this study, this underlying theoretical logic is based on affective alignment, which we describe in more detail in the following section. Second, we differentiate and relate key affect-related digital media capabilities for our framework development. We then discuss how this framework contributes to existing research on digital media and online teams.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Digital Media and Virtual/Hybrid Teams

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, many online teams relied on text-based digital media, such as email or instant messaging (Cummings et al., 2009; Kock, 2001). More recently, and especially in the years since the COVID-19 pandemic, many teams have adopted additional digital media for more advanced distant communication (Paavola et al., 2025; Vakeel et al., 2025). The technical and functional capabilities of this ecosystem are diverse. Some media are designed for asynchronous knowledge transfer, operating on a client-server model where text and files are transmitted and stored centrally, creating a persistent and searchable repository for the team (e.g., email, Jira, Trello). Others are built for synchronous collaboration, relying on low-latency data synchronization to enable the real-time processing of text in chat spaces (e.g., Slack, WhatsApp) and the cocreation of shared digital artifacts on collaborative documents or whiteboards (e.g., Google Docs, Miro). Finally, video conferencing tools (e.g., Zoom, Microsoft Teams) are designed to encode, transmit, and process multiple, simultaneous packet-based streams of audio-visual data, allowing for the communication of rich cues like vocal tone and facial expressions to create a sense of shared presence.

Much of the existing literature on media capabilities has focused on the elements of face-to-face communication that are lost when groups use digital media (Raghuram et al., 2019), and the subsequent threat of misinterpretation. Notably, when groups are not face-to-face, they have limited capability to provide immediate feedback, transmit multiple cues, use natural language, or provide personal focus (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Such limitations of “lean” media make it more difficult to adjust perspectives, resolve differences, and achieve consensus (Treviño et al., 1987). Building on the distinction between “lean” and “rich” media, Kock (2004, 2005) proposed a “media naturalness hypothesis,” in which he suggests humans prefer communication that resembles face-to-face communication because our biological apparatus has evolved to prime us with the symbolic tools and heightened physiological alertness for this mode of communication.

Several studies of digital media have also sought to illustrate the importance of affective communication, i.e., communicating information about an individual’s affective state, alongside other types of communication. In perhaps the most comprehensive theorization of the cognitive-affective perspective, Te’eni (2001) demonstrated that affective communication often performs a distinctive role in facilitating cognitive processes, such as motivating other group members to perform tasks and focusing attention on particular objects or situations. Other studies have further shown that affective communication can also be used to address surrounding issues in a group, such as well-being, selfefficacy, and self-care management (e.g., Kashian & Mirzaei, 2019; Kumar & Alok, 2022; Torro et al., 2022).

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the primary focus of existing literature has been on how the functional capabilities of digital media support cognitive processes and task-oriented outcomes. This focus, however, leaves a critical gap in our understanding. It overlooks the foundational process of affective alignment, which creates the basis for social connection itself, even without symbolic communication. The existing literature, therefore, can explain why a team successfully completes a project using Jira and Slack, but it is ill-equipped to explain why that same team might feel disconnected and isolated in the process. Conversely, it struggles to diagnose why another project might stall or fail when the root cause is not a technical or cognitive breakdown, but the very disconnection and isolation that these theories overlook. The next section discusses affective alignment in more detail.

## 2.2 An Affective Alignment Perspective

Affective alignment describes the extent to which each team member’s affective state is responsive to the affective states of others in the team. <sup>1</sup> This phenomenon has been described under various terms, including affective state matching (de Waal, 2012), emotional contagion (Hatfield et al., 1992), and mood contagion (Nummenmaa et al., 2008). Each of these terms describes the process by which changes in the affective state of one member of the group produce changes in the affective states of others. We prefer the term “affective alignment” in this study, as there are situations where team members’ affective states are aligned but different.

Before describing how affective alignment takes place, it is important to clarify what is meant by an “affective state,” as this concept is often used without providing a clear definition (Forgas, 2008). George (1996) explains that “affect is a broad, generic term that covers both the intense feelings and reactions people have, which are commonly referred to as emotions, and the less intense, but no less important, feelings often called moods” (p.145). Thus, when we refer to an “affective state,” we are describing the underlying experience of a continuously changing physiological state, influenced simultaneously by an individual’s response to specific events and stimuli, as well as that individual’s social environment, previous affective states, and unique personality.

The extent to which affective states can be separated from cognition has been contentious for some time, and there is compelling evidence that the experience of affect is deeply entangled with psychological and interoceptive processes (cf. Barrett, 2017a). However, it appears that while the experience of affect often involves a strong psychological component, affective states have physiological bases that tend to be, at least to some extent, consistent across individuals (Panksepp & Watt, 2011). For example, while humans experience fear differently across a variety of instances, these instances likely share an increased heart rate as a common feature (Barrett, 2017a).

Building on this definition of affective states as continuously changing physiological states, de Waal (2012) presented a framework to show how groups rely on affective alignment to facilitate other types of communication, arguing that, because individuals respond to one another at an affective level, this creates a foundation for more conscious interaction. That framework suggests these mechanisms can be conceptualized across three distinct layers.

Affective alignment occurs at the first layer. Individuals who interact with one another communicate and respond to each other’s affective states via (often unconscious and unintentional) “contagion” processes (Hatfield et al., 1992). Thus, individuals can find themselves experiencing specific affective states because of the affective states of others in their environment. Affective alignment has been observed among friends and family members (Parkinson, 2020), among colleagues in the workplace (Barsade et al., 2018), and even among individuals reading each other’s social media posts (Goldenberg & Gross, 2020).

Existing literature suggests a strong connection between the degree of affective alignment and the closeness of groups (Benner et al., 2009). The more that individuals can match each other’s affective states, the more natural their interactions become. For example, team member A may unconsciously notice that their friend, team member B, is experiencing stress, and team member A may begin to feel stressed as a result. Their shared stress state helps them to feel connected, even if they are not aware that affective alignment has occurred.

There is also evidence that the opposite occurs, that is, that an individual who wishes not to feel close to another can do so by rejecting this transfer of affect, thus allowing that individual to create social distance and interact in ways that would be difficult if they were experiencing matching affective states (see Ahmed, 2013; Cameron et al., 2019; Fuchs, 2019). Wróbel and Imbir (2019) explained that, although this nonmirroring of affect may result from conscious affiliation goals, it can nonetheless occur automatically and unconsciously via the same basic mechanisms of affective alignment. Those authors explain that when individuals perceive that they are socially close to others, their associative physiological network provides the necessary priming for affective externalization and internalization. When they perceive social distance, the opposite occurs. They explain, “propositional knowledge about the sender’s nonaffiliative intent (e.g., the sender is immoral, belongs to an outgroup, has competitive intent, or is angry with the receiver) activates corresponding nodes in the associative network (e.g., wickedness, disliking, rivalry), which in turn block the automatic perceptionaction link. As a consequence, the receiver’s congruent reactions may be inhibited.” (Wróbel & Imbir, 2019, p. 446). The resulting lack of mirroring can then become important affective information in its own right. In an online team, for example, team member C may not experience the transfer of stress from team members A and B, because C is frustrated by A and B’s lax work behaviors in the past. Team member C’s mismatched affective state enables and signals their disinterest in working together to address the causes of the present stress. Thus, affective alignment at this foundational level occurs when individuals are responsive to the affective states of others, but affective alignment does not necessarily require that individuals adopt the same affective states.

The middle and upper layers of affective communication build on affective alignment, though they are distinct from it. At the middle layer, individuals may use their understanding of others’ affective states to engage in “sympathetic concern, whereby they may attempt to create more desirable affective states in others. This attempt to change affective states typically implies a shift towards intentionality and the use of symbolic affective communication, as individuals move from perceiving an affective state to evaluating it as part of mutual awareness. In an organizational context, this shift often occurs when team members use their understanding of others’ affective states as a sign that they need to adjust their attitude or behavior. For example, team member A may experience a transfer of stress emotions from team member B, and this transfer of emotion may help team member A recognize that team member B is stressed. Team member A may choose to first mirror team member B’s expression, but then smile, thus prompting team member B to also smile, and so helping both individuals to feel a sense of connection while also becoming less stressed.

The third layer is most conscious and intentional, and it occurs when individuals engage in “perspectivetaking” by interpreting the affective information they receive from others, such that they can imagine how others are likely to interpret situations and interactions. This type of communication adds complexity to communication, as it requires that affective information is integrated with cognitive information (Te’eni, 2001). Yet, this combination of cognitive and affective information is important to understand how others make judgments (Weiss et al., 1999; Weiss &

Cropanzano, 1996), to coordinate behaviors (Barsade et al., 2000; Bartel & Saavedra, 2000; Spoor & Kelly, 2004), and to develop meaningful interpersonal relationships (Barsade & Knight, 2015; O’Leary et al., 2014; Schinoff et al., 2020). Team member B, having established that team member A is stressed, may ask them to elaborate on the reasons for that stress, so that they can improve their shared understanding and work together more effectively.

This study focuses on affective alignment, that is, the first, foundational layer of affective communication, for two main reasons. First, we focus on affective alignment because it is neglected by other theories of digital media. Second, and more significantly, we focus on affective alignment because it is important. Without understanding affective alignment and the way it is impacted by digital media, it is difficult to explain why team members who interact through digital media may feel disconnected and lonely (Mihalache & Mihalache, 2021; Yuan et al., 2021). Further, because affective alignment provides the necessary preconditions for the other layers, without analyzing affective alignment, it is difficult to diagnose breakdowns in communication, such as the one described at the beginning of this study at NASA.

The following sections dive deeper into the two mechanisms of affective alignment: affective externalization and affective internalization.

## 2.2.1 Affective Externalization

For affective alignment to take place, affective information from one team member must be perceptible to others. Affective externalization describes the physiological and behavioral mechanisms that allow an observer to perceive the affective state of some other individual (consciously or non-consciously). The nature of these affective externalizations varies. Some affective externalizations are unconscious physiological responses that can be observed independently of other communication, including changes in pupil dilation (Kret et al., 2014), facial color (Montoya et al., 2005), hormonal levels, heart rate, and sweating (Kret, 2015; Prochazkova & Kret, 2017). Context is important for understanding the meaning of these affective externalizations, but even without knowing what someone is talking about, these affective externalizations contain some perceived indication of the sender’s affective state. The degree of information about affective states varies in these affective externalizations. However, each is difficult or impossible to conceal entirely in a face-to-face interaction.

Other affective externalizations in the foundational layer are more verbal. For example, humans tend to talk about events that trigger a strong emotional response (Christophe & Rimé, 1997; Derks et al., 2008; Rhee et al., 2020). Verbal cues can also contain affective externalizations through a particular choice of words, tone, manner, voice shakes, etc. (Cheshin et al., 2011). Affective externalizations may also be inferred from the act of talking or not talking, rather than only the content of the verbal cues, as individuals may perceive another person’s unwillingness to speak as a signal that they are apathetic or that they are afraid of revealing emotion unintentionally if they speak (Cheshin et al., 2011).

Affective externalizations also include a range of nonverbal and semi-involuntary communications. Perhaps the most obvious of these is a change in facial expression (Vick et al., 2007). The ability to communicate basic emotions such as joy, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, and disgust using facial expressions is well-established (Du et al., 2014). Other changes may be more subtle. For example, a change in body posture may act as an affective externalization, as it can signal that a team member feels relaxed, excited, or threatened (Prochazkova & Kret, 2017). Likewise, eye gaze may communicate trust, discomfort, engagement, or boredom (Kret, 2015). While many of these displays usually occur naturally and without conscious intention, once they surface, they are often readily accessible to our awareness. Being aware of these affective externalizations means they can be controlled and reduced, enhanced, or even faked (Kret, 2015). This is especially common in social situations with stronger norms about affective externalizations (Rafaeli & Sutton, 1987), where the performance of specific emotions may be required—a phenomenon often referred to as emotional labor (see Diefendorff & Gosserand, 2003; Grandey & Gabriel, 2015; Morris & Feldman, 1996).

## 2.2.2 Affective internalization

Affective alignment not only requires that team members make information about affective states perceptible to others; it also requires that team members who receive information about affective states do something with that information. Thus, affective internalization refers to the set of physiological and behavioral mechanisms that lead an observer to shift from being exposed to somebody else’s affective externalization to experiencing a change in their own affective state. As noted previously, this change may involve adopting a similar affective state to the one observed, or it may not, depending on context. Those mechanisms that drive emotional contagion commonly involve two unconscious processes: mimicry and category activation (Elfenbein, 2014; Parkinson, 2020).

Mimicry can be differentiated according to three types: motor, autonomic, and verbal (Feldman, 2012; Hatfield et al., 1993; Palumbo et al., 2017; Provine, 1986; Simpson et al., 2014). Motor mimicry refers to the recreation of facial expressions and movements, including muscles (Simpson et al., 2014), eye contact (Feldman, 2012; Wang et al., 2004), yawning (Provine, 1986), or crying (Simner, 1971). Autonomic mimicry occurs mostly through mechanisms such as physiological linkage and pupil mimicry (Prochazkova & Kret, 2017). Physiological linkage includes heart rate, breathing movements, and temperature synchronization (Feldman, 2012; Palumbo et al., 2017). Vocal mimicry occurs in the use of syntax, linguistic features (including prosodic and phonologic features), words, and clauses (Chartrand & van Baaren, 2009). When team members mimic affective externalizations, they receive physiological feedback that translates these sensations into affective alignment. For example, mimicking someone else’s smile can make the mimicker feel joy (Barsade et al., 2018).

Category activation occurs when individuals are exposed to affective externalizations from others, and this exposure primes that individual to experience a similar affective state (Goldenberg & Gross, 2020; Lindquist & Barrett, 2008; Niedenthal et al., 2009). This form of affective internalization uses both physiological and behavioral mechanisms and is often reinforced by a natural human tendency to verbalize emotional experiences (Christophe & Rimé, 1997; Rhee et al., 2020). Category activation differs from mimicry in two main ways: the observer neither needs to (1) recreate the affective externalizations nor (2) directly observe the affective externalizations. Instead, the observer only needs to become aware of the expresser’s affective state. In practice, this means that indirect affective externalizations activate a person’s concept of the observed emotion’s category. This mechanism helps to explain why affective alignment can sometimes occur without in-person interaction (e.g., Belkin, 2009; Cheshin et al., 2011), without direct interaction (e.g., Ferrara & Yang, 2015; Goldenberg & Gross, 2020; Li et al., 2017), or even without any interaction at all (Huntsinger et al., 2009).

## 3 Media Affectivity: A Framework to Explain Affective Alignment in Online Teams

Having defined affective alignment and described the two mechanisms responsible—affective externalization and affective internalization—we now focus on theorizing how digital media enable and constrain these two mechanisms. Specifically, we present media affectivity as a theoretical framework that describes the degree to which digital media allow an online team to achieve affective alignment.

A theoretical framework requires an explanatory logic, i.e., the why and how that makes that framework more than a list of constructs (cf. Sutton & Staw, 1995). Hedström and Ylikoski (2010, p. 54) summarize this role of mechanisms, stating that “the primary epistemic … of science is to understand phenomena, and this is precisely what mechanisms provide.” For media affectivity, affective externalization and affective internalization provide this explanatory logic. They help us to understand how and why team members respond to each other’s affective states when interacting via digital media. A theoretical framework also requires a clearly defined set of “composite things” (Weber, 2012) to describe the who, what, where, and when of the framework. For media activity, defining these composite things requires that we identify the types of digital media capabilities that enable and constrain the mechanisms of affective alignment in online teams.

The set of digital media capabilities available to online teams is vast. We therefore performed a literature review to identify and group digital media capabilities that are relevant for affective externalization and affective internalization. Appendix A describes the literature review in detail, including the keywords, databases, tools, and processes used. To summarize this process, we gathered a sample of 258 papers and coded them using Gioia et al.’s (2013) method to identify key technology capabilities that could impact affective externalization and affective internalization. We developed first-order concepts from recurring ideas in the sampled literature, such as gestural and braincomputer interfaces and physiological signals. This yielded 23 first-order concepts, which we grouped into six second-order themes using the logic of affective alignment. For instance, the first-order concepts mentioned above were grouped under the theme “bodily affective cues.” We then identified three aggregate dimensions that grouped the second-order themes into conceptually distinguishable categories. Figure 1 illustrates the breakdown of these three categories of media capabilities, and Table 1 provides definitions.

The three high-level categories are affective channels, affective representation, and affective inflection. Each of these three categories plays an important role in both affective externalization and affective internalization. Figure 2 presents a visual summary of the framework.

Beginning with affective externalization, affective channels—which describe the capabilities of the medium that impact the types of affect-related information users can express or view when using the medium—gather information about individual team members’ affective states. While there are other ways for affect-related information to enter the system, affective channels provide the primary means to capture moment-to-moment changes in the affective states of team members. In a project management tool like Jira, for instance, a developer may externalize frustration through these channels by updating a ticket’s status to “Blocked” and adding an angry emoji (??) in a comment.

Next, the medium’s affective representations—which describe the capabilities of the medium that present users with contextual information about their own and others’ past and present affect-related behavior— capture these externalizations and consolidate that information into a more stable and integrated form. These affective representations therefore allow for an accumulation of affective externalizations and the ability to highlight changes in affective states. The Jira ticket, now permanently displaying the “Blocked” status on the shared sprint board, becomes a persistent social representation for the team. It also acts as a selfrepresentation: Each time the developer sees their own red, “Blocked” ticket, the persistent visual cue can revive their initial frustration, influencing their present-time appraisals.

Finally, affective inflection <sup>2</sup> —which describes the capabilities of the medium that influence the affective states of its users, beyond what occurs in the direct interactions among team members—builds on these representations by organizing them. This resulting affective inflection may include bottom-up organization, based on the affective externalizations from team members, and top-down organization, based on the design of the digital media. For example, Jira’s design (aesthetic style) may constrain expression to a formal set of statuses, while its workflow automation could enact affective weighting by automatically changing the ticket’s color to red and escalating its priority, thus amplifying the urgency of the user’s expression.

Moving onto affective internalization, the process is initiated by affective inflection, which now acts to prioritize the organized affective state for other team members. This is achieved through system-level mechanisms that amplify certain affective cues, such as by triggering automated notifications or using visual design choices like color to convey urgency and meaning. In the Jira example, the prioritization aspect of inflection occurs when the workflow sends an urgent, system-generated notification to the sprint lead. This notification directs the sprint lead to the affective representation—the bright red, high-priority ticket on the team dashboard—which communicates an accumulated and prioritized view of the problem. This representation, in turn, causes the sprint lead to engage with the affective channels themselves by clicking into the ticket. There, they read the frustrated comment and see the angry emoji, allowing them to internalize the specific, momentary affective state that triggered the event.

The following sections describe each category of digital media capabilities in more detail, including specific examples to illustrate how they impact affective alignment by enabling and constraining affective externalization and affective internalization.

## 3.1 Affective Channels

Digital media offer various affective channels that allow team members to create affective externalizations. These affective channels can be categorized into two main types: speech affective cues and bodily affective cues.

Speech affective cues refer to the set of written or spoken symbols that indicate an affective state, which team members can both produce (externalize) and perceive (internalize) when interacting online. Obvious examples are loaded and valenced words that reference specific emotions, such as “delighted,” “afraid,” etc., or text-specific affective externalizations such as exclamation marks or emoticons (emojis) (Cheshin et al., 2011; Goldenberg & Gross, 2020). Many other examples are less obvious. Team members can produce affective externalizations using punctuation, repeated characters, and verbosity (Park, 2007; Riordan & Kreuz, 2010). Other examples may be even more subtle and/or inadvertent. Team members can create affective externalizations with speech affective cues based on message length, conversational cadence, and affective connotation (Cheshin et al., 2011). For example, an individual who responds with one-word messages may communicate hostility. Speech affective cues thus encompass a wide range of features to capture and present speech or text, not only in terms of words and symbols, but also in terms of when speech was created, viewed, amended, or referenced.

Bodily affective cues describe the extent to which digital media allow team members to use bodily manifestations as affective externalization. Some of these bodily cues are intuitive to understand, such as facial expressions, gazes, and body movements (Dael et al., 2012). For example, a team member may externalize relaxation and happiness by smiling, sitting back, and looking at another team member in the eye. Alternatively, a team member may express frustration by frowning and refusing to look at another team member. Bodily affective cues also include involuntary physiological signals like blushing, muscle clenching, pupillary dilation, or micro-movements in facial muscles, which indicate happiness, fear, or surprise, for example. These signals can be internalized without team members realizing, as observers can involuntarily emulate the underlying physiological states of others not physically present based solely on such visual cues (cf. Cooper et al., 2014; Nummenmaa et al., 2008; Palumbo et al., 2017).

Digital media can also introduce new channels for bodily affective cues, beyond what is typically experienced in face-to-face interaction. For example, Wang et al. (2004) developed an online chat platform where the galvanic skin response (GSR) of each team member, a common indicator of excitement or stress, was recorded and made available to others, along with self-reported emotional tags. Similarly, Liu and colleagues conducted numerous studies (e.g., Liu, 2019; Liu et al., 2017, 2019) where they communicated physiological data among team members to stimulate affective externalization and affective internalization. Team members report that the communication of bodily affective cues, such as heart rate and facial expression, feedback can foster social connection among the team (Chanel & Mühl, 2015; Hassib et al., 2017; Liu, 2019; Slovák et al., 2012). For example, Janssen et al. (2010) found that when it comes to influencing social behavior, visualizing one another’s heartbeat is comparable to bodily cues often found in intimate physical spaces, such as eye gaze or interpersonal distance.

## 3.2 Affective Representation

Affective representation refers to the way digital media capture and describe team members’ affective states. Affective representation comprises two crucial digital media capabilities: self-representations, which continuously reflect each team member’s affective externalization back to themselves, and social representations, which continuously reflect team members’ affective externalizations to others. Unlike the episodic transmission of affective states via affective channels, these affective representations provide a continuous stream of affective externalizations and prompts for affective internalization.

Social representations are perhaps the most intuitively important for affective alignment. Social representations present an accumulation of information about team members’ affective states. This accumulation is important, as it allows teams to develop affect-related norms, which make it easier to keep track of others’ changing affective states in light of shared events (Parkinson, 2020). Richer social representations therefore make it more likely that team members will feel like they are part of a connected social system, and thus more likely that they will respond to each other’s affective externalizations (Bartel & Saavedra, 2000; Cramton et al., 2007; Rafaeli et al., 2012). Social representations can have a further indirect impact on affective alignment without necessarily capturing any specific affective state, e.g., by displaying other team members’ current location, local time, or weather conditions. This indirect effect occurs because these social representations contribute to feelings of social presence and connectedness (Liu et al., 2019), which prime team members for affective externalizations and affective internalizations via the resulting synchronization of physiological activity across individuals (Järvelä et al., 2016).

Self-representation capabilities provide team members with a visualization of their own affective states. As with social representations, some of the selfrepresentation capabilities that impact affective alignment are not overtly affective in nature (John et al., 2025). For example, some digital media allow users to observe their own faces (e.g., self-view on Zoom). Observing one’s own face could be useful for a variety of reasons, such as making sure the camera is working and positioned correctly. However, it also provides the viewer with the ability to observe their own facial expressions, posture, pallor, etc. The resulting heightened self-awareness has been linked with greater susceptibility to affective externalization and affective internalization, due to increased activity in neurological regions associated with affective processing (Wang et al., 2024). More affect-specific self-representations have also been developed. For example, Howell et al. (2018) sought to investigate data-driven self-reflection by developing Ripple, a shirt enhanced with subtle electrodermal activity signaling that changed color in response to skin conductance (e.g., when the wearer becomes excited, the shirt’s stitches turn red). They found that participants wearing Ripple felt a stronger connection between their own affective states and bodies and engaged in more intimate conversations. Relatedly, Chanel and Mühl (2015) found that providing team members with private biofeedback to indicate their own affective state encourages those individuals to become more open to affective externalization and affective internalization with other team members.

Another component of self-representation is the persistence of affective communications (e.g., chat history can remain available indefinitely or for a fixed amount of time before being erased). Tat and Carpendale (2006) explored the role of communication persistence using CrystalChat, a visualization tool that lets users explore their chat history to help them reflect on their past social behavior. The study found the system triggered (1) the surfacing of memories, thereby encouraging storytelling around these memories, and (2) the realization of social habits, which can influence future social interactions. Viewed in terms of affective externalization and affective internalization, it appears that the key to these impacts was the ability to recall and recreate previous affective states, even when significant time had passed. This ability to transcend time may be important, as existing research has shown that time influences how well individuals recall past affective states, and that this ability to recall past affective states influences their present-time appraisals and decisions (e.g., Fredrickson, 2000; Levine et al., 2001). These ideas have inspired multiple design-based explorations of “affect bookkeeping” as a form of self-representation, in which team members record and revisit affective states as part of routine reflective practices (Daily & Picard, 2007; McDuff et al., 2012; Ståhl et al., 2009). Each of these examples appears to offer the type of contextualized self-representation that primes individuals for affective externalization and affective internalization (John et al., 2025), partly because they prime the affective internalization mechanism of individuals and partly because such multisensorial representations can blur self-other boundaries (Tsakiris, 2017).

## 3.2.1 Affective Inflection

Affective inflection in digital media refers to the overarching influence that a platform exerts on the affective states of its users, beyond what occurs in the direct interactions among team members. While affective channels and affective representation facilitate affective externalizations and affective internalizations within a team, affective inflection provides a common influence that acts on different team members’ affective externalizations and affective internalizations simultaneously. This capability is constructed through two key elements: the aesthetic style, which encompasses the aesthetic and symbolic design choices that guide and constrain affective communications, and affective weighting, which describes the prioritization and emphasis of certain affective states through content sorting and recommendation systems. <sup>3</sup> Together, these two elements of affective inflection create a pervasive backdrop that transcends individual relationships, subtly guiding affective externalization and affective internalization towards particular affective states.

The aesthetic style describes the degree to which the medium supports affective externalization and affective internalization through its aesthetics, and the degree to which it supports symbols for some types of affective externalization and affective internalization and not others. Intuitive examples include the colors, fonts, shapes, and images that an interface presents to users (Cai et al., 2008; Longstreet et al., 2021; Lundholm, 1921; Romi, 2012). For example, color schemes with shorter wavelengths have been associated with more positive and lower arousal affective states (e.g., Babin et al., 2003; Crowley, 1993; Valdez & Mehrabian, 1994; Yildirim et al., 2007). Other examples may be more subtle. For example, a videoconferencing tool might include a “like” button in its chat window but forego a “dislike” button. This decision to include a “like” button but not a “dislike” button means it becomes easier to externalize positive emotions (because there is a means to do so) and to internalize positive emotions (because they become easier to detect). Torre and Lieberman (2018) showed that the added ease of labeling and communicating such positive emotions increases their physiological salience, with the result that people implicitly self-regulate their affective states to focus on positive affective states. Therefore, even without using the “like” button on a videoconferencing tool, each team member may become more primed to recognize positive emotions in themselves and others when using that videoconferencing tool. The result is that affective states across the team may converge without even interacting, as each member is subject to similar parallel influences.

Note that the aesthetic style is distinct from the affective channels described earlier, as the aesthetic style describes a medium’s prioritization of specific affective states, rather than its ability to facilitate affective externalizations and affective internalizations via different modalities. For example, Zoom lets users react with emojis during calls, but the shortcut prioritizes positive displays such as clapping hands, a thumbs up, smiling faces, etc. This creates a positive bias in how participants of the call interact—they must expend more effort to create a negative or ambivalent affective externalization. In the future, platforms may implement more exploratory features to influence the moods of their users. So far, researchers have considered the effects of embedding smile reminders (e.g., Moore et al., 2017), emotionally adaptive artifacts (e.g., Farahi, 2018), AI-enabled emotion regulation chatbots (Peng et al., 2019), and visual features of digital media such as lighting and colors (e.g., Barsan-Pipu et al., 2020) on users’ affective states and responses. Collectively, these features promise to support affective alignment, as they help to stimulate recognizable affective states among team members, and thus create a baseline of social connection that enables ongoing affective externalization and affective internalization (cf. Parkinson, 2020).

The affective weighting of a digital medium impacts affective externalizations and internalizations by prioritizing certain types of content or creating/reinforcing norms about what types of affective states are most appropriate. The principles governing this algorithmic prioritization were largely developed and studied in the context of large-scale social media. Research on these platforms has shown that content containing high-arousal emotions like anger or outrage is more likely to go viral, as algorithms are often designed to amplify content that drives engagement (Berger & Milkman, 2012; Brady et al., 2020). Platforms such as X and Facebook are particularly prone to this type of pattern (e.g., Huszár et al., 2022; Milan, 2015; Milli et al., 2023; Rathje et al., 2021). For digital media that monetize engagement, this creates an incentive to adopt an affective weighting towards these more “viral” emotions.

The same engagement-driving principles from social media are now beginning to appear in organizational collaboration platforms. A prominent example is the rise of AI-powered features like intelligent summaries or “meeting recaps” in workplace software (e.g., Microsoft Teams). These AI systems don’t simply produce verbatim transcripts—they algorithmically determine which highlights and sentiments from a meeting are “important” enough to surface for everyone. Notably, Microsoft’s Teams Premium Intelligent Recap feature is explicitly designed to analyze conversations and even report on the overall sentiment of meetings (Microsoft, 2025b). According to Microsoft’s documentation, the AI-generated recap will categorize the tone of the meeting (positive, negative, or neutral) and show how sentiment trended over time during the discussion (Microsoft, 2025b). In practice, this means the algorithm is actively detecting emotional cues in participants’ language. To decide what makes it into the summary, it must weigh the affective intensity or significance of statements— effectively performing a form of affective weighting.

For instance, an AI summary might prominently highlight a comment like “I’m really concerned about this deadline” if it deems that remark critical to the meeting’s outcome, while omitting mundane status updates. By calling out the expressed concern (an anxious or high-arousal affective state), the tool elevates that emotion for all team members who read the recap. The stated goal of these features is productivity and clarity, but they demonstrate that the technical capability for affective prioritization is already being deployed. Similarly, Microsoft’s own Copilot for Teams emphasizes not just factual highlights but also the sentiment behind what was said—essentially signaling to users which emotional undercurrents mattered in the conversation (Microsoft, 2025b). In this way, platform-level choices are guiding how affect is communicated and internalized within teams. What gets algorithmically summarized (e.g., an employee’s frustration or urgency) becomes highly visible, whereas more neutral or routine statements may fade into the background (John et al., 2025). This selective surfacing of emotion mirrors, on a smaller scale, the dynamics seen on social media feeds.

Platform designers rarely reveal how their systems process or prioritize emotion, echoing the opacity that has long characterized social media algorithms. Facebook, for instance, has acknowledged not publishing how it weights engagement signals—a safeguard against gaming the system, but one that conceals which emotions are amplified (Merrill & Oremus, 2021). A similar opacity now surrounds enterprise platforms. Tools like Microsoft Teams’ Intelligent Recap and Viva Insights show that the technical feasibility for affective weighting is already built in: they detect sentiment, tone, and behavioral patterns to decide which elements of communication are worth surfacing. What remains undisclosed are the affective parameters that guide these choices— whether stress, dissatisfaction, or enthusiasm are given greater weight. This capability can make signals of strain or urgency more visible to teams, and in doing so it is developing a capability to shape what emotional registers dominate collaboration (Microsoft, 2025a).

These examples illustrate that affective weighting is already built into workplace technologies. AI tools that summarize meetings or analyze team communication rely on models that decide which cues—such as expressions of concern, stress, or enthusiasm—deserve attention. These systems do not just report; they shape what becomes visible and shared.

## 4 Discussion

This study argues that affective alignment is an important aspect of the coordination of online teams, emerging over iterative cycles of affective externalization and affective internalization via the digital media capabilities available to team members. Previous studies have demonstrated that these types of affective processes are an important contributor to individual well-being (Marroquín et al., 2017; Niven et al., 2009), to the formation and maintenance of online relationships (Barsade & Knight, 2015; Schinoff et al., 2020), and to the general cohesion of online groups (Barsade et al., 2018). Further, previous studies have also acknowledged that these types of affective processes lay a foundation for cognitive and taskspecific attitudes and behaviors (e.g., Kashian & Mirzaei, 2019; Kumar & Alok, 2022; Te’eni, 2001; Torro et al., 2022). The following sections explore the contributions of media affectivity and describe how it can provide fertile ground for further theorizing into the relationship between digital media and affect in online teams.

## 4.1 The Unique Contribution of Media Affectivity

The unique contribution of media affectivity is its focus on the emotional connections and shared affective states among team members, a crucial aspect of collaboration often overlooked by existing theories. It achieves this by being designed to explain a different outcome: affective alignment, the foundational physiological and emotional responsiveness between individuals that underpins team cohesion. In contrast, prominent frameworks like media richness and media synchronicity center on cognitive outcomes, such as reducing uncertainty or improving task effectiveness. This distinction is visually represented in Figure 3, which maps prominent theories along a spectrum from affective to cognitive focus using de Waal’s (2012) layers of affective communication as a conceptual guide. By explaining affective alignment, our framework can address phenomena that other theories cannot, such as why a team may feel disconnected even when taskoriented communication appears to be flawless, as seen in the NASA example. For readers interested in a detailed, side-by-side comparison of these theoretical distinctions, a detailed discussion is provided in Appendix D.

Furthermore, by centering on this foundational affective layer, our framework challenges some of the field’s core assumptions about digital media. For example, where media naturalness theory holds up face-to-face interaction as the “gold standard,” media affectivity shows that digital media can introduce new, non-naturalistic affective channels, like sharing biometric data, that can enhance affective alignment in ways that have no face-toface equivalent. Similarly, it reframes concepts like “synchronicity” and “colocation,” arguing that they must be understood not just as technical properties of a medium, but in relation to the highly dynamic and perishable nature of human affective states.

## 4.1.1 From Similitude to Embodiment in Digital Media

Media that recreate the visible and audible information present in face-to-face interaction have typically been the gold standard for communication in existing studies of digital media. This makes sense, as face-to-face interaction combines a wide range of symbolic channels, all assembled in a structure that humans have evolved to process efficiently. This means that team members can communicate with words, gestures, posture, intonation, etc., and other team members can be relatively confident they understand them as intended. For these reasons, while textual or visual information is viewed as an effective way to transmit information from one team member to another, face-to-face-like interaction is seen as the most effective means of converging on a shared understanding (Dennis et al., 2008).

At first glance, the same logic seems to apply to the communication of affect, meaning that media affectivity should also aspire to the gold standard of similitude with face-to-face interaction. However, the literature review revealed a new category of technologies that challenges this logic. These are technologies that capture biosignals from users and use the data to shape the interaction. For example, Apple’s Digital Touch lets Apple Watch users share their heartbeat and other affective signals with other users (Apple Support, 2023). Emotion-aware technologies use and process affective input to enhance user experience (Harley et al., 2017). Users can leverage those affective capabilities to improve emotional control, awareness, and well-being (Orlando et al., 2021; Peake et al., 2018). This departure from the principle of similitude reinforces the distinction between the cognitive layer of communication and the foundational affective layer (de Waal, 2012). More importantly, it also highlights opportunities to design innovative new media to enable media affectivity, even if it means those media become less comparable to faceto-face interaction. For example, viewing a number that indicates another team member’s heart rate does not resemble face-to-face interaction, and yet it seems to boost affective alignment because it helps to integrate physiology into team members’ interactions (Hassib et al., 2017; Slovák et al., 2012).

## 4.1.2 Synchronization and the Perishability of Affect

The concept of synchronicity describes a medium’s ability for real-time, intuitive feedback that allows individuals to identify and resolve divergences quickly (Dennis et al., 2008; Riordan & Kreuz, 2010). Such rapid feedback further informs team members when others are available and ready to provide assistance, thus facilitating informal interactions and ad hoc plan changes (Schinoff et al., 2020). Synchronicity has therefore been acknowledged as an important aspect of online teams for some time. However, media affectivity demands that we reconsider what is meant by synchronicity.

The main assumption that we challenge is that synchronicity is a quality of the medium. Existing literature focuses on mental models, and these mental models tend to remain stable for prolonged periods, often changing only when individuals are actively challenged on their assumptions or when individuals are asked to collaborate with new people (de Graaf, 2019; Kude et al., 2019). Hence, existing theories of digital media generally assume that once individuals can share information effectively, then each team member’s mental models can find a stable configuration.

However, unlike mental models, affective states are perishable. This is one of the reasons that existing research often differentiates fleeting emotions (a major component of affective states) from moods and personality—because emotion, by its nature, is fleeting (Frijda, 1993). For affective alignment, a delay in communication thus presents a new concern. If a team member is experiencing some affective state and communicates using a medium that does not reach other team members immediately, the sender’s affective state could have passed by the time another team member receives any affect-related information. The quality and completeness of affect-related information are not sufficient to overcome this problem. Instead, because the affective states of team members are so dynamic, synchronicity must be considered in relation to the affective states of team members at a given point in time. Thus, synchronicity becomes more than a capability of a digital medium; it is a capability of team members relying upon that digital medium.

## 4.1.3 “Location” as an Affective Component of “Co-Location”

Colocation refers to the extent to which individuals interact within the same social environment. Media naturalness argues that colocation is important because we have evolved to interact when sharing a physical location; thus, our cognitive systems are most efficient when we are physically co-located. However, digital media also create what can also be considered spaces or “locations,” that is, social and material assemblages that connect individuals with digital representations of objects and other people, bound in systems of practice (Coyne, 2010; Faraj et al., 2016; Grewal et al., 2020; Nah et al., 2011).

Media affectivity suggests that the “location” in “colocation” also plays an important role. A location’s light, sound, temperature, etc., may each have a significant impact on affective alignment, as these aspects of a location may elicit shared affective states among team members, such as joy or stress. The same locations may also elicit diverging affective states among team members. These divergences could occur because each team member may have contrasting associations or previous experiences in a location. For example, if a specific video conferencing tool has positive associations for one team member and negative for another, then team members may feel socially disconnected from one another when interacting using that tool. The shared “location” created by digital media must therefore be understood, not only in terms of the affective states they elicit in individual team members, but also in terms of the consistency of those affective states across team members.

Rethinking digital media in this way allows for deeper reflections about the role of digital “location” for online teams. The American architect Frank Lloyd Wright once wrote: “Whether people are fully conscious of this or not, they actually derive countenance and sustenance from the atmosphere of the things they live in or with. They are rooted in them just as a plant is in the soil in which it is planted” (Wright, 1954, p. 135). Contemporary architects widely acknowledge the need to balance functional considerations with an understanding of affect when creating social spaces using physical materials. Those architects appreciate that those social spaces provide more than a material structure for individuals; they set a “tone” that impacts how individuals feel when they occupy those spaces, how they interact, and how they integrate those spaces into their daily lives (Kock & Lynn, 2012; Pallasmaa, 2016). Media affectivity takes this idea further. It demands that we consider “location” not only in terms of whether it elicits positive or negative affective states but also in terms of how similar those elicited affective states are likely to be across team members.

## 4.2 Future Research Directions

Media affectivity will help researchers in the future to analyze how different media impact affective communications. In particular, we highlight four areas where media affectivity could be applied in the future to pursue new phenomena and research questions.

First, we encourage researchers to study affective alignment using media affectivity. This study began by describing the postponement of the NASA Psyche mission. That story illustrates why affective alignment is important and why it must be considered separately (at least partly) from other types of social coordination in online teams. The independent review board, after investigating the incident, noted that “remote and hybrid work must be minimized on Psyche to give the team the best opportunity to coalesce in a short time” (Zurbuchen & Leshin, 2022, p. 3). Building on de Waal (2012) and others, we argue that such a coalescing first requires a capacity for affective alignment at a basic physiological level. Years of research have focused on shared cognition and knowledge management, and these studies have been highly valuable to moving the field forward. Media affectivity focuses on something that precedes this type of connection yet contributes to both the individual work experience of team members and the relationships among them.

Second, we argue that media affectivity has important implications for understanding workers’ mental health.<sup>4</sup> The move to online work and digital workplaces has created a stronger reliance on technology, and this has created new threats to team members’ well-being. One well-known threat is the possibility of technostress (Fonner & Roloff, 2010; Weinert et al., 2015)—the stress created by users’ inability to cope with or get used to ICTs (Tarafdar et al., 2007). Another example is the concept of “Zoom fatigue,” which emerged in recent years from a combination of stressors, including mirror anxiety, the sensation of being physically trapped in the Zoom frame, and hypergaze (Fauville et al., 2021). Concepts such as technostress and Zoom fatigue may have important repercussions for team members, as they are linked with dissatisfaction, fatigue, anxiety, and overwork, and subsequently decrease individual productivity and organizational commitment (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2007). Technostress is also linked to burnout, which occurs when a team member experiences work overload, insufficient reward, a breakdown in community, and a conflict in values—all in all, a failure to recognize the human nature of work (Maslach & Leiter, 2008). Media affectivity can help to explain why certain media may enable more or less problematic experiences. Media affectivity may even help designers and managers nurture positive affective states; states that have the power to correct the after effects of negative experiences (Cavanagh & Larkin, 2018; Fredrickson et al., 2000).

Third, media affectivity may help to isolate the impact of digital media on online teams’ affective states from other influences, such as the personalities and affective traits of individual team members. For example, an individual team member may feel a lack of connection to their team or role. Media affectivity makes it possible to investigate if this problem is a result of the media that the team uses or whether it is because of their role or interests. This distinction can allow managers to make more thoughtful and precise changes, such as relocating the team member to another role or simply switching up the media through which the team communicates. Media affectivity may also help to explain why specific relationships are stronger or weaker among team members. Different personality traits result in differentiated affective appraisals, experiences, and reactions (George, 1996). For example, individuals scoring high on neuroticism typically use more angervalenced words in their emails (Akbar et al., 2019). Moreover, dispositional traits can have important consequences for someone’s ability to perceive others’ affective externalizations and internalize them, making them more or less susceptible to affective alignment (Bond & Raleigh, 1999). Media affectivity may therefore provide an important lens through which to understand and resolve interpersonal conflict.

Fourth, we encourage researchers to consider the impact of social AI agents in digital workplaces in terms of affective alignment. Our research highlights fundamental challenges for such social AI agents. Affective alignment relies, in part, on the communication of affective information among team members. Some of this affective information can already be emulated well by social AI agents. For example, there are already sophisticated tools to computationally detect affect-related linguistic features, and social AI agents can adopt particular linguistic styles when communicating. Other affective communication is also already conceivable for social AI agents with realistic avatars, such as matching eye gaze, detecting and mimicking facial expressions, and even creating responsive pupillary dilations. However, some of the affective information that team members externalize and internalize unconsciously to allow affective alignment appears to require that those team members have a physical body; information like heart rate, breathing movements, body temperature, hormonal levels, and sweating (see Feldman, 2012; Kret, 2015; Palumbo et al., 2017; Prochazkova & Kret, 2017). Whether or not social AI agents can emulate this level of affective information is less clear.

If emulating these unconscious affective externalizations and internalizations is impossible, or at least impossible in the near term, then there are several potential problems for organizations. Perhaps team members will struggle to coordinate with social AI agents. Perhaps the introduction of social AI agents into teams will make team members feel lonelier and more isolated, as team members experience human-like interaction but without the basis for empathy, sympathetic concern, and perspective taking. Perhaps social AI agents will be actively disliked, as their lack of response to affective information will create an instinctive sense of intended social distance among other team members. Perhaps social AI agents will inadvertently communicate apathy, and this apathy will spread via contagion among team members. We call for a more thorough investigation of such possibilities through the lens of media affectivity and affective alignment.

## 5 Conclusion

This study addresses the growing need to theorize the relationship between digital media and affective alignment. Such theorization is important to inform the design of systems that foster empathy and allow individuals to feel connected. Specifically, we asked: How do different digital media capabilities enable and constrain affective alignment among team members? We propose a theoretical framework of media affectivity that explains how digital media enable and constrain the emergence of affective alignment among team members. This framework outlines the affective mechanisms involved and the media capabilities that mediate the flow of affective information. We argue that media affectivity can help to make sense of why some teams are better able to form strong relationships and why some team members may suffer from issues relating to social and mental well-being.

## Appendix A: Literature Review

Because many of the concepts relevant to this model are scattered across multiple literature streams, this study requires a broad, multidisciplinary search for literature. To increase coverage and facilitate access to siloed concepts (Raghuram et al., 2019), we performed a literature review, following the three-step strategy outlined in Table A1.

For the first step, we conducted a systematic search to find existing research at the intersection of communication media and emotion/affect, following a process adapted from Okoli & Schabram (2010) and vom Brocke et al. (2015). We defined two areas to start our search: (1) affective alignment and (2) digital media capabilities relevant for affective communication. We created two search strings that captured these terms (see Affective Alignment and Media Capabilities sections below). We ran the search on Scopus and iteratively excluded keywords and subject areas to refine the search. For instance, we realized we needed to exclude terms related to “viruses” due to the conflicting meaning of “contagion,” and terms related to animal research to focus our search on human studies. Because this topic has been extensively studied, we restricted document types to book chapters and peer-reviewed journal articles that had either received at least 10 citations or were published in 2022 or later. Because digital media capabilities are continuously changing, we were more inclusive of outlets for this topic because we wanted to capture studies of emerging technologies, so we also included conference papers and removed the citation cap. The maturity of the affective alignment literature meant we could also restrict the search to the title, as the terminology is relatively stable. Terminology was less stable in the digital media literature, so we also included abstract and keywords in the search.

The searches returned 460 (affective alignment) and 467 (media capabilities for affect communication) documents. Next, we manually screened the titles and abstracts. We removed 297 (affective alignment) and 223 (media capabilities) documents that were either out of scope, editorials, retracted, or not publicly accessible (conference abstracts). Within each corpus, we then ranked the documents’ topics according to a star system, with one star being a poor fit, two stars being a medium fit, and three stars being an ideal fit (see Table A2 for criteria). At this point, we were left with 52 3-star documents for affective alignment and 111 3-star documents for media capabilities. These became our preliminary literature sample. Among those articles, we identified 17 key documents that were most relevant for answering the research question.

In the second step of our search, we used a snowballing technique to identify articles like these 17 key documents that might not have been captured during our initial search. We performed backward and forward searches (Webster & Watson, 2002), as well as a similarity search using connectedpapers.com.<sup>5</sup> These three methods added another 95 articles (bringing our total literature sample to 258 papers) that were selected because they brought clarity to (1) digital media capabilities that are likely to impact the exchange of affective information or (2) the underlying mechanisms that explain and drive affective alignment.

For the third step, we coded the sampled 258 papers to identify key concepts following the method proposed by Gioia et al. (2013). We developed first-order concepts based on recurring concepts in the sampled literature. For example, we noted that several papers mentioned the importance of detection of eye-gaze as a social indicator of affect, while other papers mentioned facial color, others mentioned discernible hormone releases, others mentioned heart rate, and others mentioned skin conductance. Because multiple papers in our literature sample focused on each of these concepts, we added the code of physiological signals to our first-order concepts. The result of this phase was an extensive list of 23 concepts, so we identified six second-order themes that could be abstracted from these first-order themes, grouping them and describing them using the logic of our guiding theory: affective alignment. During this time, we continuously iterated back and forth to consider alternative concepts, themes, and labels. As one example of a second-order theme, returning to the example of physiological signals, we observed that there were multiple similar codes that represented a form of “bodily affective cues,” that is, they described physiological changes that typically occur when an individual experiences some particular affective state or states. We next identified three aggregate dimensions that each described digital media capabilities.

## Appendix C: Controversies Surrounding the Experience of Affect

The idea that affect is communicated among individuals assumes that core affect is, at least partly, comparable across individuals. This assumption is not without controversy. Several studies have argued that interoception plays a key role in psychologically constructing emotions (e.g., Craig, 2002; Wiens, 2005). This means emotions can be felt differently among individuals and over time, as core affect is continuously constructed based on interoceptive cues rather than just as periodical responses to stimuli (Barrett, 2017b). This explains why different people (or the same people at different points in time) sometimes feel different emotions although they experience similar bodily sensations. While this new knowledge restricts the scope of mimicry, there is still substantial evidence of effective mimicry processes in emotional experiences—indicating that mimicry and interoception are not opposites but most likely coexist. In fact, advances in neuroscience unveiled the existence of mirror neurons in monkeys, constituting a mirror neuron system.

More recently, studies have confirmed the existence of a mirror system (MS) in the human brain as well (Iacoboni, 2009; Wild et al., 2003). These unique neurons are increasingly associated with mood contagion (Nummenmaa et al., 2008). Effectively, the MS gets activated both when performing a goal-oriented action (e.g., grabbing an apple with the hand) and when observing the same action performed by someone else (Rizzolatti et al., 2001). The activation of the mirror system may have two main consequences: (1) it enables the observer to understand the observed behavior, and (2) it facilitates the observer’s somatic and autonomic responses to imitate the observed behavior (Brass & Heyes, 2005; Rizzolatti et al., 2001). Several fMRI studies have investigated the role of the MS in the spread of emotions during human social interaction and found that the perception of a specific emotion fired up the neurons that are responsible for generating this same emotion (see Prochazkova & Kret (2017) for a review). An interactive emotional contagion model (Nummenmaa et al., 2008) integrates these various mimicry processes into a cohesive framework that articulates how the different motor and neural elements of emotional contagion work together. Importantly, evidence shows that emotional empathy recruits the mirroring systems more strongly than cognitive empathy does. These findings suggest that it is easier for individuals to simulate others’ affective and cognitive states internally when they are exposed to affective situations compared to non-affective situations because the mirror neuron system seems to be more sensitive to emotionally loaded triggers (Nummenmaa et al., 2008).

## Appendix E: Developing A Layered View of Affect and Cognition in Online Teams

There are many different theories and frameworks in IS literature for studying online teams that integrate affect and cognition. Some of these theories and frameworks describe the role of communicating affective states to better communicate other information. One notable example of such a theory is Te’eni’s (2001) cognitive-affective model of organizational communication for designing IT. Other studies describe the need to communicate affective states to strategically change the affective states of others and make them more willing to engage. Notable examples include Fehrenbacher’s (2017) study of affect infusion and detection through faces, and Rutner & Riemenschneider’s (2015) study of emotional labor and conflict management. These theories and frameworks provide valuable insights, so it is important to understand how they can be integrated with media affectivity’s focus on affective alignment at a physiological level.

Returning to de Waal (2012), we can consider the social role of affective states across three layers (see Table E1). At the foundational first layer, team members use affective channels, affective representation, and affective inflection for affective alignment, which has been the focus of this study.

At the middle layer is sympathetic concern, which refers to “concern about another’s state, and attempts to ameliorate this state” (de Waal & Preston, 2017, p. 500). At this layer, team members may become consciously aware of other team members’ affective states and use affective channels, affective representation, and affective inflection to strategically change those affective states. For example, if the affective channels include video of each other’s faces, then one team member observes from another’s face that they are anxious, and they may choose to smile to help alleviate that anxiety. If the affective representation allows individual team members to indicate whether they are busy or free, then another team member might choose to change their representation to show that they are free to indicate that they are not stressed. If the affective inflection of one digital medium tends to prioritize urgent content, then another team member might suggest the team switch to a different medium more associated with casual interaction. It is at this layer that we can position the work of Fehrenbacher (2017) and Rutner & Riemenschneider’s (2015). Integrating media affectivity means we can understand not just how team members try to influence each other’s affective states at this layer, but also how they become aware of those states in the first place.

At the upper layer is perspective taking, which refers to “the capacity to take another’s affective perspective: for example, understanding their specific situation and needs, separate from one’s own, which still requires access to personal representations of the other’s state.” (de Waal & Preston, 2017, p. 449). When team members engage in perspective taking, they may adjust the details and instructions they share with other team members, based on their understanding of other team members’ affective states, as inferred from affective channels, affective representation, and affective inflection. At this layer, the goal is more than influencing the affective states of the team; it is about treating those affective states as an enabler of other outcomes. For example, if the affective channels include video of each other’s faces, then one team member might ask a sensitive question about an upcoming deadline, control their own facial expression to conceal their personal feelings, and observe the facial reactions of others to help interpret their responses. They might change their affective representation to indicate they are free for calls as a way of inviting other team members to approach them individually if they wish to voice concerns, and they might look at others’ affective representations to sense whether they want to avoid such individual approaches. They may also observe the preferred media of other team members to determine how they are feeling and to use specific media strategically for certain types of communication. It is at this layer that we can position the work of Te’eni (2001). Integrating media affectivity allows us to dive deeper into the features of digital media that enable and constrain affective complexity in a team.
