# Expanding the Scope of IS Research Impact: A Framework and Guidelines

Hind Benbya,<sup>1</sup> Mareike Möhlmann,<sup>2</sup> Jan vom Brocke<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Western Sydney University, Australia, H.Benbya@westernsydney.edu.au <sup>2</sup>Bentley University, USA, mmoehlmann@bentley.edu <sup>3</sup>University of Muenster, Germany, jan.vom.brocke@uni-muenster.de

## Abstract

Given its sociotechnical focus, information systems research has the potential to generate diverse forms of impact and advance society’s grand challenges. But producing research impact beyond academia remains a difficult endeavor. In this editorial, we discuss the multifaceted nature of IS research impact and offer a framework and guidelines for researchers to plan, deliver, measure, and communicate research impact across academia, business, and society, while considering the research context.

Keywords: Research Impact, Societal Impact, Business Impact, Grand Challenges

Dorothy E. Leidner was the accepting senior editor. This editorial was submitted on June 23, 2024, and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Information systems (IS) researchers are increasingly required—and at the same time have the opportunity—to demonstrate the impact of IS research<sup>1</sup> on organizations, businesses, and society, in addition to contributing scholarly knowledge to IS and related fields. Impact occurs when research generates considerable academic contributions, produces changes for organizations or businesses in the real world, or initiates benefits outside of academia by addressing community or societal needs. It can take different forms and influence diverse stakeholders in areas such as health, well-being, culture, society, commerce, and the environment.

The shift from pursuing academic research relevance to seeking to also make a difference outside of academia is changing both IS researchers’ focus and the expectations of policymakers, practitioners, and the general public. <sup>2</sup> For instance, national assessment programs, governments, and funding agencies are now placing a strong emphasis on dissemination and impact evaluations.<sup>3</sup> Thus, to secure support for public research funding, IS researchers need to look back to identify the contributions made by their research and also look forward to predict what impacts their research will have.

University and business school rankings have also evolved from being based on traditional research and reputation metrics to including impact as a core dimension of evaluation. For example, the Times Higher Education Impact Rankings assess universities’ social and economic impact based on an evaluation of their contributions to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) <sup>4</sup> through their research, teaching, outreach to businesses and policymakers, and the stewardship of their own resources (primarily their personnel and properties). Business school accrediting bodies such as the Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB) and the European Foundation for Management Development (EFMD) have revised their standards, placing impact at the core of business schools’ missions. For example, AACSB’s vision of societal impact is that business schools should contribute their specialized expertise to help mitigate some of society’s most pressing economic, social, human, and environmental problems, requiring business schools to report their contribution to societal impact across diverse standards.<sup>5</sup> In 2020, the AIS established the AIS Impact Award. Rosemann et al. (2026) provide an account of the award committee’s perspective on measuring research impact, which has also informed AACSB in its development of guidelines for assessing business school research impact.

In the IS discipline, research holds significant potential for impact, not only within academia but also in broader real-world applications. However, assessing and driving such impact poses significant challenges. The existing systems of incentives and key performance indicators often fall short in capturing the diverse pathways to impact and maximizing the tangible benefits of IS research. Our goal is to introduce a comprehensive framework and specific guidelines that broaden the understanding of IS research impact. Through our framework, we not only conceptualize impact but also provide guidance on planning, delivering, measuring, and communicating impact. This process is inherently not linear and involves active engagement with impact partners and continuous adjustments aimed at generating meaningful and lasting results.

## 2 Perspectives and Areas of Research Impact

## 2.1 Different Perspectives on Research Impact

The increasing importance of generating research impact beyond scholarly contributions has led to diverse national approaches and frameworks for impact assessment, including the Research Excellence Framework in the UK. the Evaluation of the Humanities in Norway (Research Council of Norway), the EU project IMPACT-EV, and the Australian Excellence for Research Initiative. Overall, case studies and applying conceptual frameworks are the predominant methods for measuring the social outcomes of research. In addition to such national frameworks, many universities have developed research impact toolkits to guide their researchers’ plans to evidence and articulate the impact of research.

A major challenge to advancing research impact, however, is that impact carries different meanings for different stakeholders and organizations (see Table 1). It unfolds across various areas in a nonlinear way, often materializes long after the research, and is assessed differently across disciplines. The perception and scope of research impact within the IS discipline have evolved over time. In early works, discussions on impact were more limited, with the primary focus being on IS for individuals and organizations (Gable et al., 2008; Nunamaker et al., 2017). For example, Gable et al.’s (2008) IS-impact measurement model conceptualized impact in terms of the potential of IS managerial, methodological, and technical capabilities, as well as IS practices and artifacts, to benefit individuals and organizations.

However, there has been a shift towards a broader understanding of impact—beyond individuals and organizations. To this end, Nunamaker et al. (2017, p. 337) described how “many definitions of IS limit its scope to IT support for organizational managers. Yet managers are only a fraction of those served by information systems … [instead] information systems involve many technological, social, political, psychological, and economic concerns.”

## 2.2 Areas of Research Impact

As noted earlier, with technology and its relationship with society, organizations, and the economy becoming ever more complex and nuanced (Benbya et al. 2020), IS researchers have a unique opportunity to make meaningful contributions that extend well beyond traditional academic boundaries. This development highlights the discipline’s evolutionary focus from academic impact to also addressing broader organizational and societal issues.

To expand the scope of IS research impact, we suggest that IS academics design their research to reach high impact for academia, business, or society. Research at the intersection of academia and business, as well as academia and society, offers considerable opportunities. Likewise, while developing a high cross-area impact strategy that aims to achieve significant impact across all three domains (academia, business, and society) is undeniably challenging, it remains an important aspirational objective to which individual research projects can contribute incrementally (see Figure 1).

## 2.2.1 Impact on Academia

Academic impact within the IS discipline refers to the extent to which research in this field contributes to, influences, and advances the body of knowledge and understanding related to IS and its neighboring research disciplines (Galletta et al., 2019; Wickert et al., 2020). One key aspect is the contribution to developing, refining, or challenging IS theory. Academic impact may also refer to research that introduces novel methodological approaches for studying IS phenomena.

The expectation for IS scholars to demonstrate academic impact with their research has existed for a long time and has well-established best practices. Academic impact is often measured by metrics such as citations in high-quality academic journals. Key metrics for gauging journal quality include the journal’s ranking, selectivity, citation count (e.g. via Google Scholar), and h-index (Hirsch, 2005). A number of lists are used to rank academic journals. For example, the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), the flagship journal of the Association for Information Systems (AIS), is listed on the AIS Senior Scholars’ List of Premier Journals in the IS field. Other lists that are commonly used to determine research excellence include the UT Dallas List of leading business journals and the Financial Times 50 list of top business journals. These lists also play a key role in faculty promotions and tenure cases (Galletta et al., 2019).<sup>6</sup>

Additionally, a journal’s impact factor is another measure used to indicate that a journal publishes research that significantly advances the field’s understanding (Galletta et al., 2019). For example, the high quality of JAIS is indicated by its high impact factor—in 2023, the 3-year impact factor of JAIS was 7.0, and the 5-year impact factor was 7.4.<sup>7</sup>

Journal-based measures have been questioned as indicators of academic impact (e.g., Davison & Bjørn-Andersen, 2019). Across disciplines, increasing concerns have been raised regarding citation cartels, conflicts of interest violation, hyper-authorship practices (e.g., Hanson et al., 2024; Plevris, 2025), and, more recently, AI-fabricated papers (e.g., Elali, 2023; Nicholas et al., 2025). The AIS has established governance mechanisms to address these developments and, in particular, has initiated an AI task force to provide continuously evolving guidance for the responsible and ethically sound use of generative AI in scholarly work. Marabelli et al. (2026) reported on a panel addressing the challenges and opportunities of generative AI in scholarly research involving publishers and editors-in-chief of major AIS-related journals.

Beyond journal-based measures, academic impact may refer to whether research has been integrated into curricula, adopted in undergraduate or graduate courses, created measurable student competency improvements, or increased long-term student success, based on job offers or academic placements (see Galletta et al., 2019; and Wickert et al., 2020, for additional indicators for quantifying impact).

## 2.2.2 Impact on Business

Another type of research impact refers to the tangible benefits and positive changes that research contributes to businesses and organizations—often referred to as implications for management practice, which concerns the influence, transformation, or modification in how managers think and act (Nunamaker et al., 2017; Pan & Pee, 2020; Wiener et al., 2018; Wickert et al., 2020). Early considerations of impact in the IS discipline primarily focused on this type of impact (Gable et al., 2008). Researchers seeking to generate practical impact often focus on conventional management practices, the establishment of connections to existing practitioner frameworks, and explanations of how practitioners could benefit from research insights (Wickert et al., 2020).

One research example of impact for business comes from Edelman et al. (2017), which uncovered evidence of racial discrimination on Airbnb. This study clearly generated impact that led to tangible changes on the platform. Airbnb acknowledged the research by implementing a series of commitments to change, incorporating some of the recommendations proposed by a Harvard Business Review article (Fisman & Luca, 2016) building on research by Edelman et al. (2017). In a subsequent podcast, <sup>8</sup> one of the coauthors of the 2017 article, Harvard Business School professor Michael Luca, explained that Airbnb has adopted several of these proposed changes, applying them broadly across the platform.

This type of impact extends to public and governmental organizations. IS research can contribute by shaping regulatory frameworks, informing public policy, and establishing standards and best practices (see Galletta et al., 2019; Wickert et al., 2020). For instance, the policy impact of IS research refers to the influence of insights, findings, and innovations derived from IS studies on the development, modification, and enforcement of policies across various domains (King & Kraemer, 2019), as exemplified by references to IS research in relevant public policy or regulatory frameworks. While direct policy changes attributable to IS research may be rare and challenging to measure (King & Kraemer, 2019), a substantial body of IS research has analyzed and recommended policy improvements. The cumulative effect of these contributions has the potential to drive meaningful policy change.

One example of impact in this area is Roman Beck, Christoph Mueller-Bloch, and John Leslie King’s work on governance in the blockchain economy (Beck et al., 2018). This article was awarded a 2021 AIS Impact

Award for providing guidance on governance (including in risk and regulatory contexts) supporting the effective, efficient, and acceptable use of distributed ledger technology systems.<sup>9</sup> Another notable example is Edgar Whitley and Roser Pujadas’s work on consumer privacy regulation in UK financial services, which was also awarded a 2021 AIS Impact Award. Their research uncovered gaps in the regulation of open banking by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), a UK financial regulatory body.<sup>10</sup> Specifically, they revealed that certain aspects of the open banking ecosystem did not adhere to the FCA’s principles for business, particularly regarding the fair treatment of customers. In response to these findings, the FCA revised its regulations in 2019 to align with the research, enhancing the overall customer experience within open banking. Consequently, the more than five million customers currently utilizing open banking in the UK are benefiting from strengthened protections and improved, consent-driven controls over their financial data usage. Since May 2014, Whitley has served as co-chair of the UK’s Privacy and Consumer Advisory Group,<sup>11</sup> providing guidance to the government on matters of data security and trust.

The interdisciplinary nature of IS research, straddling the fields of business and STEM, further underscores the potential for generating impact for organizations and businesses, particularly through patent generation and the commercialization of research—as showcased, for example, in the founding of spin-off companies (Nunamaker et al., 2017). Encouraging the collaboration between universities and industry to develop commercialization pathways and fostering innovation within academic programs are critical steps to maximize the return on investment in IS research (CABS, 2026). This approach not only amplifies the economic contributions of IS research but also ensures that its potential for innovation and economic development is fully realized.

## 2.2.3 Impact on Society

Business and management scholarship and research, and IS research more specifically, can also generate societal impact and is highly relevant to addressing the “grand challenges” currently faced by society (Abbasi et al., 2023; CABS 2026; Wickert et al., 2020). Numerous thought leaders in the IS field have underscored the importance of achieving this type of impact. For example, M. Lynne Markus’s essay on impact in IS research in Galletta et al. (2019) calls for a stronger focus on “social or causal processes or mechanisms rather than causal effect sizes” (p. 49).

Societal research impact refers to the real-world benefits that research contributes to society, including improvements in individuals’ well-being and improvements in services benefiting the broader society (Galletta et al., 2019; Wickert et al., 2020). Impact is realized, for example, when research demonstrates how humans can be protected from the potential negative effects of technology or how technology can be leveraged to improve people’s quality of life.

Several studies advocating for societal impact have highlighted the United Nations SDGs (Abbasi et al., 2023; Galletta et al., 2019), which offer a collective vision for peace and prosperity for people and the planet. They outline important priorities, including the alleviation of poverty, hunger, and inequality, and the promotion of health and well-being, serving as a guiding blueprint for impactful research and initiatives.

Examples of impactful IS research in this area are manifold. One case of significant achievement is the work of Kristin Braa and Jørn Braa, who received a 2023 AIS Impact Award (Societal Impact category) in recognition of their nearly three decades of contribution to DHIS2, an open source, web-based platform most commonly used as a health management information system (HMIS). The Braas’ work has been published in top IS journals, and their efforts have led to the widespread adoption of the open-source DHIS2 software platform across more than 80 low- and middleincome countries. This platform supports the effective management of public-sector programs in areas such as health and education. Jørn Braa has been a pivotal figure in Oslo’s HISP<sup>12</sup> since its inception in 1994 in postapartheid South Africa. The initiative started as an action research collaboration between the University of Oslo and the University of Western Cape, aimed at strengthening the national health system through digital technology. Since then, his continued involvement in action research has been crucial in the development and implementation of both DHIS v1 and DHIS2.

Another case of significant achievement is the work of Manoj Thomas and Yan Li, who received a 2023 AIS Impact Award (Societal and Economic Impact) honoring their initiative, “Techies without Borders,”<sup>13</sup> a continuing medical education solution combining cloud technologies with edge computing to ensure reliability in resource-constrained environments. The project has attracted significant media attention and is active in countries such as Botswana, Brazil, and Haiti, among others. For example, the nonprofit’s ICT 4E initiative aims to address computer literacy in Haiti and help participants to obtain jobs that may bring them and their families out of poverty. Related publications are grounded in design science and have appeared in journals such as Information Systems Research.

Societal impact extends to environmental impact. The scope of IS research is broad and impactful, and numerous recent studies have addressed critical topics such as digital sustainability, green information systems, and the societal impact of AI (Kotlarsky et al., 2023; Leidner et al., 2022; Saldanha et al., 2022; Seidel et al., 2013; Benbya and Melville, 2025). To this end, the seminal work of Richard T. Watson, Marie-Claude Boudreau, and Adela J. Chen on information systems (Watson et al., 2009) laid the foundation for the emergence of the field of green IS. The JAIS special issue “Digital Sustainability and Information Systems Research: New Challenges and Theoretical Perspectives” underscores the field’s sustained and growing interest in these vital issues. This emphasis on societal impacts reflects a broader understanding of the role of IS in driving positive change.

By examining these topics, IS research can contribute to a more human-centered, sustainable, and equitable use of digital technologies, demonstrating the field’s commitment to not only advancing technological capabilities but also ensuring that these advancements are leveraged for the greater good.

## 3 Operationalization of Research Impact

Identifying suitable metrics and collecting data presents its own set of challenges. Impact evaluations often require researchers to provide concrete, verifiable, and externally referenced proof of impact. For instance, the Research Excellence Framework (REF) in the UK mandates the submission of supporting evidence in conjunction with impact case studies<sup>14</sup> that detail the extent and importance of the impact achieved. Similarly, Australia’s Engagement and Impact assessment insists on a description of the impact, underpinned by clear evidence, such as costbenefit analyses (Pan & Pee, 2020).

Despite these challenges, a variety of indicators can effectively be used to measure impact. As discussed above in Section 2.2.1, in assessing impact for academia, emphasis is often placed on the quality of journal articles and books and on educational influence. Impact on business and society plays out differently and involves a distinction between use and applied contribution (e.g., practice changes, references in public policy/regulatory frameworks, and the commercialization of research), thought leadership (e.g., presentations at industry or policy/government conferences, meetings/partnerships with leaders), dissemination and visibility (e.g., news media coverage, media interviews, citations in practitioneroriented publications, reports) and positive effects on people’s lives (e.g., creation of sustainable and environmental outcomes, improvements in wellbeing). The positive influence of IS research in enhancing people’s lives represents perhaps the most sought-after form of impact, yet it is simultaneously the most challenging to quantify.

Given the complexity of IS impact pathways, the use of research is often considered to be an intermediate outcome that will eventually lead to changes in end outcomes, directly or indirectly, after extensive use. Thus, the dissemination, usefulness, and use of research results are central to assessing impact at the intermediate level.

The framework outlines a structured approach, with overlapping stages to consider dynamics and feedback loops, and provides guidelines for IS researchers to design research impact. Rather than following a linear sequence, the different steps occur repeatedly, interactively, and at varying levels of abstraction. Ongoing IS advancements are one reason that iterative adoptions of an impact strategy are needed. For example, after a research project begins, an organization may decide to launch a new technology application or involve employees from different departments working with it. These changes can shift the project’s direction, requiring researchers to rethink its initial scope. Generally, delivering and measuring impact are cyclical activities, as different research components are implemented and assessed formatively in terms of their impact. This assessment often involves engaging with key impact partners to gather qualitative feedback on how specific research steps and contributions are perceived. Since delivering research impact is an adaptive process that learns from measurement and communication outcomes, both research activities and impact objectives will be adapted during the process.

The following section traces this core process, in which seeds for impact emergence are planted in each stage and respective measures are taken. For each step in the core process, we offer IS researchers specific guidelines to design a research impact strategy (Figure 2).

## 4.1 Planning Impact

Effective research begins with the identification of key research objectives that address important, actionable problems for academia, business, or society. To maximize its significance, IS research should be designed with a clear plan for impact, specifying the areas it aims to contribute to and the objectives it seeks to achieve. Our first guideline (G1) concerns planning research impact:

## G1: Specify the impact objectives

As explained in the above sections, the research scope may span one or multiple impact areas and may even pursue several objectives simultaneously. The different impact areas provide a useful framework for articulating, discussing, and scoping the intended outcomes. When addressing one or more impact areas, it is essential to align research with current discourse and demonstrate its relevance and significance.

Be specific: For each impact area and its corresponding criteria indicators, researchers should define the specific objectives. These refer to the desired changes facilitated by the research outcomes, such as reducing energy consumption, promoting inclusion, or enhancing efficiency. These objectives are typically defined with reference to both an impact entity (such as social distance) and an impact direction (such as an increase or decrease) while accounting for context. A good objective specifies the impact level—determining whether it aims to influence individuals, groups, organizations, or markets. The selected impact level typically guides the research methodology. For example, research in the domain of green IS has examined sustainable behavior at the individual level using personal usage data (Tiefenbeck et al., 2018), sustainable practices at the organizational level through an in-depth case study with a global software company (Seidel et al., 2013), and the implementation of green IS strategies based on a global survey of senior IT executives (Löser et al., 2017). While developing specific objectives is recommended, these should not be seen as static. Given that iterative changes and adaptations of the research project may be necessary, objectives may need to be updated throughout the research process.

Contribute to real-world challenges: To maximize impact, it is crucial to identify research objectives that contribute to real-world challenges early in the research process. Researchers should identify the most pressing sociotechnical problems businesses or society face and then apply IS theories and methods to inform relevant stakeholders about contemporary and emerging issues. Contributions using this approach may include generating actionable recommendations, developing new frameworks that can be adopted by organizations or policymakers, reframing existing challenges, and illustrating best practices and their outcomes. This can help impact partners make informed improvements and change their practices effectively.

Collaborate with impact partners to define scope: Collaboration with impact partners, such as businesses, public policy bodies, or non-governmental organizations, is often essential for defining meaningful research objectives and securing access to the necessary resources. Such partnerships frequently serve as a prerequisite for implementing impactful research and can help IS researchers identify practitioner needs, guiding the formulation of impact objectives. Here, instead of exploring a problem and considering how our theories may help, the focus is more on a problemsolving approach, whereby researchers and organizational/societal actors work together to coproduce new solutions to sociotechnical phenomena. This approach goes beyond interactions between IS scholars and organizational leaders, as both are active participants in all research design phases. Typically, it involves an iterative research process that capitalizes on learning by both researchers and organizational actors, producing not only process changes within the organization but also potentially introducing new technology-based artifacts.

## 4.2 Delivering Impact

Building on the objectives set during the impact planning step, the research activities and delivery of impact should align with the intended areas of impact— academia, business, and society—while actively engaging with impact partners. By maintaining a clear connection between the research process, outcomes, and the desired objectives, this phase ensures that the work not only generates valuable insights but also fosters contributions for business or society, whether by influencing stakeholder behaviors, informing policies, or driving organizational innovation. This step of the core process involves conducting research to ensure a meaningful impact on the design, use, consequences, and evolution of digital technologies. Our guideline for delivering research impact is as follows:

## G2: Apply impact-sensitive research

Delivering impact should aim to deliver tangible results to organizations, businesses, and society. During this stage, scholarly IS research methods are applied in alignment with the intended impact established at the beginning of the research. Thus, any research method, be it design science research (Hevner et al. 2004), quantitative research (e.g., Edelman et al., 2017), qualitative research (e.g., Möhlmann et al., 2023), or other types of research, such as mixed methods research, can generate meaningful impact (see Table 1). By using the term impact-sensitive, we emphasize that research should adhere to academic standards while also considering impact goals. This means continuously assessing potential research impact and, where possible, adjusting the research process to enhance research impact. In design science research, for instance, a methodology has been developed that is geared towards dealing with complexity by continuously designing and validating intermediate research results, such as the problem statement, the design requirements, the solution design, the proof of concept, and the proof of use (Tuuanen et al., 2024).

Seek use cases of new technologies: To implement impact-sensitive research, we recommend that IS research go beyond studying new technologies in isolation and actively seek new research opportunities by identifying where these technologies can address real-world challenges. Engaging with organizations and society to explore practical use cases can help to uncover gaps, limitations, and unexpected impacts, opening new avenues for inquiry. By focusing on how technologies function in practice—what they solve, where they struggle, and how they evolve—researchers can generate insights that drive both academic progress and responsible innovation.

Maintain engagement with impact partners: Typically, impact-sensitive research is an iterative process that capitalizes on learning by both researchers and organizational actors and produces not only process change in the organization but also technological solutions. It involves fostering partnerships with enduser organizations such as industry groups, consortia, associations, policymakers, and the public. By actively engaging in such communities of practice, researchers can establish a mutually beneficial relationship that fuels a virtuous cycle. Engaging with impact stakeholders can provide important feedback that can be used to plan and define impact criteria and further deliver impact. It also facilitates access to leaders for interviews, case studies, and datasets, enabling the creation of valuable knowledge that can be swiftly disseminated back to practice. Studies have examined organizational models for high-impact research and found long-term and close collaborations in which academia and practice jointly engage on real-world challenges to positively influence research impact (Benbya & McKelvey, 2011). Emphasizing this collaborative dynamic, vom Brocke et al. (2021) proposed the metaphor of a “dance” between academics and practitioners and, based on a rich empirical case, derived principles for such a dance across time and space in order to enhance the practical relevance of research projects.

One approach includes industry-led collaborative research programs (e.g., CRCs<sup>17</sup> in Australia). Such programs aim to solve industry-identified problems and create solutions (e.g., technical artifacts, algorithms, new methods, products, and services) that are jointly financed by industry consortiums, universities, and governments and have the potential to drive diverse forms of business and/or societal impact (e.g., new job creation, spin-offs, solutions to grand challenges). Given the complexity of such research programs, which often require multidisciplinary research expertise, diverse actors, and long time frames, they can only be pursued if universities are committed to the project and align incentives and promotion criteria to this genre of research.

## 4.3 Measuring Impact

Assessing research impact requires effective measurement. However, it is important to be realistic— quantifying the precise impact of a single research contribution is rarely feasible. Even when data is available, tracing it directly back to research findings can be challenging due to the influence of multiple external factors. Nevertheless, impact can be continuously evaluated based on predefined criteria and the judgment of key stakeholders. Our guideline for measuring impact is as follows:

## G3: Assess a wide range of impact criteria

In Section 3 and Table 2, we introduce example criteria, referring to indicators that help to measure and assess the degree to which IS research has proven impact. Identifying impact criteria and the extent of impact are essential for operationalization, whether through quantitative or qualitative measures. This may include improved access to digital technology, energy consumption levels, or reduced emissions. While quantifying the exact extent of impact can be challenging, using a range of clearly defined criteria can facilitate high-quality assessments and contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation process.

It is important to recognize that the scope of impact can vary significantly, both in depth (for example, comparing a single design guideline to a comprehensive policy) and in breadth (such as the size of the community benefiting from the research). These distinctions in depth and breadth are critical considerations when evaluating entries for the AIS Impact Award.<sup>18</sup>

Monitor continuously: As discussed previously, defining criteria for assessing IS research impact ideally occurs at the outset. However, given the iterative nature of impact strategies, the scope of a project may evolve slightly during the impact delivery phase. Consequently, the actual assessment of impact criteria may diverge somewhat from the initial plan—for instance, by incorporating additional criteria into the evaluation. While our model emphasizes measuring impact as a core activity following research implementation and delivery, as impact can only be evaluated once it has been realized, we believe that measuring impact should not be a static endeavor. Instead, IS researchers should prioritize the continuous assessment of potential impact throughout the research process. The more we assess such criteria, especially beyond the academic area, the more we will be able to recognize contributions that make an impact, e.g., on society, policy, and the economy. These can be used, for instance, in tenure processes and in grant applications.

## 4.4 Communicating Impact

Usually, researchers who are concerned about impactful research share their findings once impact has been delivered and measured. At the same time, changing stakeholder requirements and technological advancements necessitate ongoing dialogue with prospective stakeholders. This proactive approach ensures that research remains relevant and impactful. Our guideline for communicating research impact is as follows:

## G4: Maintain an impact dialogue

We advise researchers to actively engage with potential impact partners and end users—maintaining a continuous dialogue, learning about emerging challenges, sharing ideas to address these challenges, and collaboratively establishing processes for planning and delivering impactful research. It is essential to continuously build relationships, engage in dialogues, and communicate impact to relevant stakeholders.

Translate beyond the target organization: Communicating impact may involve academic publications in journals or books, but also practitioneroriented outlets such as MIS Quarterly Executive (e.g. Amend et al., 2025), Harvard Business Review (e.g.,

Möhlmann & Henfridsson, 2019), MIT Sloan Management Review (e.g., Jarrahi et al., 2023), reports, and news media coverage. Communication may leverage various channels—such as social media or podcasts—to effectively reach different stakeholder groups. Thus, to extend impact beyond academia, communication strategies must be tailored to diverse audiences, as their preferences often differ from academic communication in terms of format, language, and medium.

Those seeking to create impact often rely on “translations” of research findings to reach decision makers, typically through brief summaries or press releases following publication. However, this approach alone may be insufficient for reaching intended users beyond the target organization. We encourage researchers to share their findings beyond the immediate target organizations, including at practitioner conferences, trade association meetings, on social media, and other forums where their intended audience is present.

## 4.5 Impact Context

When research or innovation makes a difference, it doesn’t happen in a vacuum, but it is shaped by a broader environment. This impact context includes social, organizational, economic, and technological factors that determine how research findings are both crafted and applied and the extent to which they influence real-world outcomes. We offer the following guideline:

## G5: Build impact capacity

For research to create impactful change, a supportive environment is needed. This could refer to building strong partnerships, communicating findings clearly, and accessing key communities and media channels. Institutions looking to enhance their research influence—like the AIS has done—should take deliberate steps to build their capacity for impact. For example, the AIS has addressed this by setting up an AIS industry advisory board and fostering strategic collaborations.

Because impact depends on context, it is crucial to consider the research environment from the very beginning and stay flexible as conditions evolve. A big part of this involves identifying stakeholders and understanding how they’ll engage with the research outcomes. Work in design science (Baskerville et al., 2015) highlights that keeping an eye on changes in stakeholder behavior is a key to measuring how research is making an impact over time.

Explicitly consider time and context: We recommend that IS research geared towards business or societal impact should position the research in time and context. In design science research, for instance, vom Brocke et al. (2020) have referred to the “ephemeral nature” of design knowledge and recommend close positioning of research in time and context. For example, it is important to recognize that the scope of the impact objective may evolve over time, especially in iterative or long-term research projects, where the digital technology being studied is also subject to updates and changes. Take societal impact, for example. During the COVID-19 pandemic, social distancing was a key objective, supported by tracking apps. However, over time, perceptions regarding the benefits of social distancing have shifted, and in many cases, the creation of a sense of community has become a more important goal. This highlights the importance of explicitly defining impact objectives while considering the broader context and how priorities evolve over time.

Foster an impact ecosystem and mindset: Building an impact ecosystem begins with a fundamental shift in researchers’ mindsets. Embracing a perspective that appreciates diverse forms of impact can transform research into a potent catalyst for tangible change. For this transformation to take root, universities and business schools housing the IS discipline must realign their incentive structures and promotion criteria to acknowledge both business and societal impact. This shift fosters a culture where academic endeavors not only enrich scholarly understanding through publications but also generate impactful research outcomes for different stakeholders (e.g., communities, business, society). Ensuring that future IS researchers (especially in doctoral qualifications) are educated about research impact frameworks and methods can also expand the scope of IS research impact. The AIS can support this process by creating educational resources on IS research impact and making these available to its members, e.g., in the AIS digital library.

## 5 Implications and Conclusion

In this paper, we explore the multifaceted nature of IS research impact and propose a comprehensive framework to help researchers plan, deliver, measure, and communicate their impact effectively. Our main contribution is the development of a respective framework and a set of practical guidelines that offer hands-on recommendations for implementing a research impact strategy. Our work builds on and extends prior studies (e.g., Agarwal & Lucas, 2005; Gable et al., 2008; Lucas et al., 2013; Nunamaker et al., 2017).

Much of the existing research has emphasized the impact beyond academia, particularly on technology users and managers. For example, past research published in JAIS has largely conceptualized IS impact in terms of its potential to benefit individuals and organizations (e.g., Gable et al., 2008; Nunamaker et al., 2017). However, as we highlight in this paper, the scope of IS research impact has progressively expanded in recent years to address societal, political, and environmental concerns. Nowadays, the concept of impact in IS encompasses societal relevance, capturing tangible benefits, including enhancements to well-being that benefit individuals and communities at large (Galletta et al., 2019; Wickert et al., 2020). This impact becomes evident when research can be used to help safeguard the human experience from the potential adverse effects of technology or to illustrate how technology can be harnessed to enhance quality of life. To this end, one of our guidelines focuses on the recommendation to assess a wide range of impact criteria to operationalize these varying types of impact. This expansion reflects an increasing interest in how IS can contribute to solving grand challenges (Abbasi et al., 2023). Reflecting this shift, JAIS has issued calls for special issues on topics such as “Data Science for Social Good,” “Digital Sustainability and Information Systems Research,” and “Digital Responsibility—Social, Ethical, and Ecological Implications of IS.” In our framework, we capture this broadened and evolving understanding of the potential contributions of IS research to also include impact for society.

The quest to generate impactful IS research with demonstrable benefits for society and the economy has become an increasingly relevant priority in academia. As an applied field, IS is concerned with the phenomena that occur at the intersection of social and technical systems (Sarker et al., 2019). Thus, it has the potential to not only shift understanding and advance knowledge about society’s grand challenges, but it can also contribute solutions to real-world problems.

We view the expansion of the scope of impact as an opportunity to offer constructive alternatives to publication-based notions of impact and the associated, increasingly visible challenges related to gaming the system. The more consistently we evaluate scholarly work based on its tangible contributions to business and society—rather than relying primarily on meta-indicators such as journal rankings and citation counts—the more meaningful and sustainable the advancement of our discipline will become. This is not a call for a departure from publication-based indicators, but rather a call to complement and rebalance them with criteria that capture real-world value creation. In a world increasingly defined by human-machine collaboration, this broader understanding of impact is essential to securing digital sovereignty and ensuring that our discipline remains scientifically robust and societally relevant.

This editorial provides a framework to expand the scope of IS research impact. It discusses the multifaceted nature of IS research impact and provides guidelines to plan for research impact by identifying a process by which research can achieve its impact. It can be used by researchers to plan, deliver, measure, and communicate research impact while considering the research context.
