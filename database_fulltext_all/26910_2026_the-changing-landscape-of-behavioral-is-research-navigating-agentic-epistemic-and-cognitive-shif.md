# The Changing Landscape of Behavioral IS Research: Navigating Agentic, Epistemic, and Cognitive Shifts

Varun Grover,<sup>1</sup> Monideepa Tarafdar <sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Arkansas, USA, vgrover@uark.edu <sup>2</sup>University of Massachusetts Amherst, USA, mtarafdar@umass.edu

## Abstract

This editorial essay examines how contemporary artificial intelligence is reshaping the intellectual terrain of behavioral Information Systems (IS) research. We argue that AI gives rise to three interrelated shifts that warrant systematic attention: an agentic shift, in which action, delegation, autonomy, and accountability are redistributed across human-AI ensembles; an epistemic shift, in which knowledge and judgment increasingly operate under conditions of opacity and probabilistic performance; and a cognitive shift, in which human thought is progressively recomposed through repeated interaction with systems that generate, frame, and refine thought-like outputs. We outline a research agenda comprising several research problems within each shift that IS researchers need to address. We also discuss broader implications of these shifts for theory, methods, and society. Taken together, these shifts challenge several assumptions that have long underpinned behavioral IS research, including treating technology as a bounded artifact, expecting intelligible causal pathways, and assuming the relative stability of the relationship between human cognition and its support by IS.

Keywords: Behavioral IS Research, Agentic Shift, Epistemic Shift, Cognitive Shift, Agency, Cognition, Knowledge, Predictive AI, Generative AI

## 1 Introduction: The Box Breaks— and Thought Liquifies

For much of its history, the information systems (IS) field has flourished by studying technologies that can be neatly boxed. Whether mainframes, enterprise systems, mobile applications, or platforms, technologies were conceptualized as artifacts with identifiable boundaries, predictable functionalities, and largely deterministic behaviors. Humans adopted them, used them, resisted them, or misused them. Organizations implemented, aligned, and governed them, and sometimes failed in their use of them. Our theories, whether borrowed, adapted, refined, or developed natively, were well matched to this world.

Even as digital technologies grew more complex, more networked, and more generative, the dominant logic of

IS theorizing remained stable. Technologies, whether conceptualized as tools or relationally, were still primarily treated as things to be used. Human behavior was still framed around use. Although IT applications could predict patterns from past data, cognition was still assumed to reside primarily in the human mind. The outputs from IT applications were still evaluated through human-interpretable reasoning. And while IS scholars did study automated behaviors, for instance, in robotic applications, agency—when granted to technology—was typically metaphorical rather than literal because automation was largely programmed and predictable.

## That world no longer exists.

Digitalization has already strained the box. As argued elsewhere (Grover & Lyytinen, 2023; Piccoli et al.,

2024), digital technologies differ ontologically from prior IT because their fundamental unit is not hardware or software, but digital objects composed of bitstrings, capable of decoupling, representation, generativity, and deep embeddedness in social and organizational life.<sup>1</sup>

Digital resources metaphorically liquify the box, can travel across contexts, recombine in unanticipated ways, and reshape value propositions, identities, and institutions (Baiyere et al. 2023). However, artificial intelligence (AI), by which we mean machine learning-based predictive applications and transformer- and GAN-based generative applications, adds new capabilities that are even more remarkable. If digitalization liquified the box, AI liquifies thought itself.<sup>2</sup> AI is not merely a faster calculator, a better decision support system, or an advanced form of automation; it is a general-purpose technology that appears to produce knowledge on demand, and increasingly intelligence on demand, through nondeterministic, opaque, and adaptive processes operating over massive and changing data corpora. It learns, acts, and surprises. And in doing so, it forces us to confront questions with no close parallel in previous technological revolutions. Even businesses are realizing they need “first principle thinking” when redesigning their IT infrastructure for AI rather than just layering on another set of applications (Schrage & Kiron, 2025).

This editorial argues that AI introduces three profound shifts—an agency shift, an epistemic shift, and a cognitive shift—that together challenge many of the foundational assumptions of behavioral IS research. These shifts are not incremental extensions of existing frameworks; rather, they represent qualitative breaks that require us to rethink how we conceptualize the role of humans, their behaviors, the outputs from AI applications, knowledge, and human cognition in sociotechnical systems. What is common across these shifts is that they all chip away at our “humanness” in different ways. The purpose of this editorial is to send a clear message to the field: AI is different, and studying it as if it were “just another technology” using atomic adoption, implementation, and value models invites substantial conceptual and empirical errors. Below, we describe the three shifts and their theoretical implications for behavioral IS research.

## 2 The Three AI Shifts Within the Digital Ontology

Before turning to AI specifically, it is worth briefly situating this discussion within the broader trajectory of digitalization. The move from traditional IT to digital technologies is not merely about increased connectivity or computational power. It is about a change in ontology. Digital objects—defined as objects whose components include one or more bitstrings—can be pure or hybrid, material or immaterial, but they share properties that distinguish them fundamentally from prior artifacts (Faulkner & Runde, 2019). They are modular, recombinable, representational, and increasingly generative. Digital resources encapsulate value and capabilities behind programmatic interfaces, enabling unprecedented recombination and scale (Piccoli et al., 2024). In addition to having these properties, AI technologies, as a class of digital technologies, are probabilistic, produce variable (non-deterministic) results, and generate context-sensitive outputs that mimic aspects of human cognition. These properties are destabilizing long-standing assumptions in IS research. Behavior cannot be reduced to adoption or use, context can no longer be cleanly separated from artifact, and value cannot be traced linearly from design to outcome. And yet, if our theorizing remains somewhat anchored in what might be called a “scripted” approach—identify a gap, borrow a familiar theory, adapt it modestly, test it rigorously, and refine incrementally—we will miss the essence of digital phenomena (Grover & Lyytinen, 2023). That approach worked well in a world where technologies were bounded, stable, and largely predictable. However, digital artifacts are now embedded in continuously evolving sociotechnical fabrics and deliver outcomes that are generative and cannot be fully anticipated ex ante.

In this essay, we describe three AI-driven shifts and their implications for behavioral research in the IS field. We view behavioral IS research broadly as the substantial portion of IS research that focuses on how humans and human enterprise interact with IS and argue that these shifts are fundamental to this interaction.<sup>3</sup> The agency shift challenges who acts, the epistemic shift challenges how humans know, and the cognitive shift challenges how humans think. Why do these three shifts form the cornerstone of the emerging landscape of IS behavioral research? The agency shift indicates whose behavior we want to study when the AI has agency. The epistemic shift indicates how we design, understand, and interrogate AI (knowledge) outputs. The cognitive shift shows how we behave when we conduct cognitive tasks jointly with AI. The implications of these shifts raise several novel research questions that can be pursued as part of an AI research agenda that is particularly relevant to behavioral IS research.<sup>4</sup>

## 2.1 The First Shift: Agency—From Humans Using Technology to Human-AI Ensembles

## 2.1.1 Agency in the Pre-AI Era

Historically, IS research has treated agency as residing primarily in humans. Technologies might constrain, enable, or shape action, but they did not act on their own in any meaningful sense. Even when we spoke of “delegation” to technology, the delegation was understood as executing predefined rules or routines specified by humans. Therefore, most behavioral IS research assumed an organizational imperative where the primary agency resided with users, developers, and managers as individuals and collectives. Any transfer of agency to technology (i.e., automation and robots) was largely deterministic based on prescribed conditions.

## 2.1.2 Agency in AI

The most visible and perhaps most unsettling shift introduced by AI is the agency shift. Modern AI systems, that is, those based on machine learning and generative models, exhibit behaviors that are not explicitly programmed, not fully predictable, and not always traceable to specific human decisions. They recommend, classify, generate, negotiate, and increasingly act autonomously within organizational processes. They do so probabilistically, adapting to data and feedback in ways that blur the line between execution and judgment.

IS research has traditionally used the term “affordances” to describe the action potential of IT in relation to users’ goals and intentions. So, while traditional IT can be seen as a bundle of functions, AI systems are largely a bundle of affordances. Functional systems execute prespecified routines with clearly bounded capabilities. They need humans to trigger, direct, and interpret outputs. Affordance systems do not simply execute functions; they generate action possibilities by interpreting context, inferring intent, and producing outputs that were not explicitly specified at design time. Affordances are relational. They emerge from the interaction between the actor and the artifact. When AI systems generate code, summarize legal documents, propose diagnoses, or negotiate contracts, they expand and reshape the space of what can be done. Their outputs are not fixed endpoints but invitations to further action. They move workflows forward agentically. This distinction matters because functions presuppose human direction, while affordances presuppose distributed agency. Functional systems need humans to drive the process, whereas affordance systems, especially agentic AI systems, need outcome specifications, permission structures, and governance boundaries.

The shifting of agency has profound implications for a field that has historically viewed IS as something that can be designed, implemented, and used as humans desire. Thus, AI shifts the locus of behavioral IS inquiry from “How do humans use systems?” to “How do humans configure, supervise, and co-act with systems that generate unpredictable action possibilities?” Affordances are not new to IS research. What is new is that AI systems generate and recombine affordances dynamically and probabilistically, without full human specification, thereby altering the structure of agency itself.

With this shift, we not only have human agents but also human-agent ensembles and multi-agent systems. Three interrelated aspects of this shift are: accountability, autonomy (and control), and delegation. ## 2.1.3 Aspect: Accountability

By accountability, we refer to the locus of responsibility vis-à-vis AI systems (e.g., Recker et al., 2025). In prior IT systems, even automated ones, outcomes could ultimately be traced to rule specifications, parameter choices, or human design decisions. The chain of responsibility was often complex—but it was, in principle, reconstructable. AI systems disrupt this reconstructability. Outputs are generated through probabilistic pattern recognition across massive training corpora. The causal pathway from input to output may be opaque even to system designers. As a result, responsibility becomes diffused across developers, deployers, users, trainers, and the system’s evolving data environment.

For example, when an AI system denies a loan, flags a medical anomaly, recommends a strategic move, or generates a persuasive narrative, where does agency lie? With the developer who developed and trained the model? The organization that deployed it? The user who accepted its recommendation? Or the system itself? Traditional theories of agency, control, and accountability strain under this ambiguity. Concepts such as locus of control, responsibility attribution, and role clarity become unstable when actions are distributed across human-AI ensembles rather than to individual actors or organizational units. As a result, the research problems this shift raises are fundamentally different from those raised earlier in the field.

Important research problems confronting behavioral IS research include understanding how accountability might shift from humans to AI systems, addressing the challenges of attributing accountability when AI systems make consequential decisions, and developing governance structures for managing accountability visà-vis AI applications.

## 2.1.4 Aspect: Autonomy

In the 1990s, the reengineering revolution required us to rethink our business processes to take advantage of the emerging IT of those days. Today’s reengineering requires a new kind of analysis of jobs, tasks, and the right human-AI ensemble to leverage AI effectively. Autonomy is the extent of a human’s control over the design, operation, and use of an AI application (Shneiderman, 2022). For IS researchers, the challenge is not simply to study “trust in AI” or “AI adoption” but to theorize how agency is perceived, negotiated, and divided within these ensembles. This includes examining role and identity conflicts between human and AI actors, perceptions of autonomy and control, and new forms of coordination and governance that emerge when machines no longer function as passive instruments.

It is useful to note that AI introduces a new form of autonomy—not mechanical autonomy, but inferential autonomy. <sup>5</sup> The system selects patterns, weights signals, and generates outputs without explicit procedural instruction. Humans may define goals, but not the path to achieving them. This creates a paradox: As systems become more capable of control, human control becomes more abstract. Control shifts from procedural direction (“follow these steps”) to guard rails and boundary-setting (“do not exceed these constraints”). Governance becomes about metacontrol rather than operational control.

In this context, the key research issue is no longer adoption or acceptance but negotiated autonomy. IS scholars are thus faced with research problems that address how much autonomy AI systems should have and at what point human oversight should constrain it, rather than overseeing and legitimizing it.

## 2.1.5 Aspect: Delegation

In traditional systems, delegation meant transferring the execution of predefined routines. In AI systems, delegation increasingly means transferring judgment, and sometimes even goal specification. Indeed, AI systems are conceptualized to undertake delegation both proactively (AI-led) and reactively (human-led) (Baird & Maruping, 2021). More fundamentally, the agency shift introduced by AI compels us to reconsider what it means to remain human in sociotechnical systems where judgment is increasingly delegated. If humans are positioned primarily as supervisors of machine decisions, intervening only at the margins or in exceptional cases, then agency risks becoming ceremonial rather than substantive. At some point, augmentation slides into abdication, not through malicious or deliberate intent, but through routinized deference. When action is distributed across human-AI ensembles yet responsibility remains human, individuals may retain liability without authority, judgment without control, and accountability without comprehension.

For IS research, the challenge is to theorize delegation not as a property that moves between discrete actors but as an emergent and contested feature of human-AI configurations, and to ask where, when, how, and for whom human agency meaningfully persists.

The agentic shift forces the IS field to confront a foundational question: If AI systems can act, infer, recommend, and even negotiate, what remains uniquely human in organizational decision-making? Our field was built on the study of how humans design and use technologies. We are now entering an era where technologies participate in judgment. This is not a minor extension of automation theory; it is a redefinition of agency itself. The challenge for IS research is not simply to measure trust, performance, or adoption but to theorize how agency is distributed, how accountability is sustained, and how human authority persists (or erodes) within intelligent ensembles. If we fail to grapple with this shift, we risk studying AI as if it were a faster spreadsheet rather than a new actor in the sociotechnical order.

## 2.2 The Second Shift: Epistemic: When Knowledge Becomes Opaque

The epistemic shift is distinct from the agentic shift. The agentic shift concerns who acts and how agency is distributed. The epistemic shift concerns how knowledge claims are generated, understood, evaluated, used, and legitimized. Even if humans retain formal authority and responsibility for any outcome, the basis on which they judge AI output may be fundamentally altered.

## 2.2.1 Epistemology in the Pre-AI Era

In prior IT systems, even if decision authority could be delegated (automated), epistemic evaluation remained human. Correspondingly, IS research has long relied on an implicit epistemological assumption: that system behavior, while complex, is ultimately understandable.

Even when technologies were sophisticated, their logic (programming) could be inspected, audited, rectified, and explained—at least in principle. This assumption underpinned our ability to evaluate system outputs, system quality, decision quality, and information quality, and to assess their alignment with users’ design objectives and goals.

## 2.2.2 Epistemology in AI

With AI, both action and justification may become opaque. Many AI systems operate as epistemic black boxes. Their outputs are generated through highdimensional statistical processes that resist simple explanation. Even developers may not be able to fully articulate why a particular recommendation was produced. As a result, users are asked to trust or distrust systems whose reasoning they cannot fully comprehend. <sup>6</sup> Importantly, opacity is not merely a technical limitation to be solved through better explainability tools. It is a structural feature of many AI systems, particularly those that learn from massive, dynamic datasets. Even “explanations” may be post hoc rationalizations rather than faithful accounts of underlying processes. The epistemic shift, therefore, strikes at the foundations of what it means to know, to justify, and to be accountable for decisions in organizational life.

Four interrelated aspects of this shift are: evaluating AI output claims, opacity, sensemaking, and epistemic reconfiguration. These are elaborated below and explained in Table 2, as they raise fundamental questions for IS research.

## 2.2.3 Aspect: Evaluating AI Output Claims

When an AI system diagnoses a medical issue or recommends a high-stakes acquisition target, should the user simply accept the recommendation? This raises a foundational issue for IS research of understanding what it means to evaluate knowledge produced by AI (Lebovitz et al., 2021).

For behavioral IS research, this indicates a structural epistemic inversion. Knowledge is no longer justified through shared reasoning processes but through empirical performance metrics that may be opaque to human cognition. Users cannot independently reconstruct the inferential pathway. Instead, evaluation shifts from reasoning-based validation to outcomebased validation: “Does it work?” replaces “Why or how does it work?”

## 2.2.4 Aspect: Opacity

Opacity refers to the non-transparent algorithmic logic underlying AI applications and stems from the probabilistic property of these algorithms (Bitzer et al., 2023). AI opacity does not merely introduce new variables into existing IS theories; it violates core epistemic assumptions underlying widely used models of adoption, trust, decision support, and organizational learning. For instance, in adoption models, belief formation presumes evaluability, which occurs through interpretation, explanation, and causal inference, not just exposure to outputs. Similarly, in IT trust models, trust is based on predictability and transparency, which are calibrated through use. Sociotechnical systems theory presupposes mutual intelligibility between social and technical subsystems as a precondition for joint optimization.

In behavioral IS research, AI opacity violates the presumption of mutual intelligibility. The technical subsystem may outperform the human (behavioral) subsystem while remaining epistemically inaccessible to it.

## 2.2.5 Aspect: Sensemaking

The epistemic shift forces IS research to reconsider the nature of sensemaking in AI-infused environments. Sensemaking refers to the process through which people seek to understand issues or events that are novel, ambiguous, confusing, or otherwise violate expectations (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014). Traditionally, sensemaking of IT output involved constructing coherent causal narratives that aligned observations (outputs) with expectations (computational logic). With AI, users may no longer ask, “Does this make sense?” but rather, “Is this statistically reliable?” The interpretive act shifts from causal reconstruction to confidence calibration

For IS scholars, this necessitates a shift from studying objective system accuracy alone to examining epistemic adequacy, trust, sensemaking, and interpretive practices. They would need to grapple with understanding how individuals and organizations rationalize AI outputs and reconcile conflicts between human judgment and machine recommendations.

## 2.2.6 Aspect: Epistemic Reconfiguration

The epistemic shift introduced by AI opacity raises equally profound questions about our humanity. To be human in organizational life has long meant not only acting but knowing why one acts, being able to justify decisions to oneself and to others through shared standards of reasoning. Opaque AI systems fracture this condition. When AI recommendations cannot be meaningfully interrogated, evaluated, or explained, human judgment risks becoming derivative rather than deliberative. Users may come to treat AI outputs as authoritative signals to be accepted or managed, rather than claims to be understood, assessed, and potentially rejected. Over time, this can alter the nature of sensemaking itself: Explanation gives way to acceptance, reasoning to reassurance, and justification to compliance. This is not simply a problem of trust calibration or explainability in design; it is an epistemological reconfiguration in which knowledge is produced without understanding, and action proceeds without intelligible justification (Jongepier & Keymolen, 2022). For IS research, the challenge is to examine how humans preserve or relinquish their role as epistemic agents when knowledge claims become opaque, distributed, and increasingly automated.

In summary, if the agentic shift asks who acts in AIinfused systems, the epistemic shift asks who knows what and on what grounds. AI systems may produce accurate and valuable outputs, but they do so through processes that resist human comprehension. This challenges a foundational assumption of organizational life: that responsible action requires intelligible justification. The task for IS research is not simply to improve the explainability of interfaces or measure trust in systems, but to theorize how knowledge itself is redefined when understanding is no longer a precondition for decision-making. In an era when intelligence can be generated without insight, the preservation of the human epistemic role becomes a central question for our field.

## 2.3 The Third Shift: Cognition—When Offloading Affects Humanity

If the agency shift challenges who acts, and the epistemic shift challenges how we know, the cognitive shift challenges how humans think.

## 2.3.1 Human Cognition in the Pre-AI Era

Digital technologies have long enabled cognitive offloading and, in doing so, have already reshaped human cognition. Calculators reduced the need for mental arithmetic; spreadsheets externalized calculation, modeling, and record keeping; and databases expanded organizational memory and computational capacity. These technologies did not merely “support” cognition in a narrow sense; they made humans and organizations more adaptable, capable, and efficient by distributing cognitive work across people and artifacts. In this sense, cognition in the pre-AI era was already sociotechnical and distributed rather than purely individual. Yet such technologies largely remained within narrower functional domains—storing, retrieving, calculating, and coordinating—while interpretation, judgment, and creative reasoning remained predominantly human. They extended cognition without fundamentally blurring the boundary between cognitive support and cognitive performance.

## 2.3.2 Human Cognition in AI

Contemporary AI systems do not merely store or retrieve information; they generate text, code, diagnoses, analyses, and creative artifacts that resemble the products of human reasoning. They increasingly perform tasks that require synthesis, inference, and evaluation—activities traditionally associated with expertise and professional judgment. As a result, humans interact with AI not simply as users, but as partners and even collaborators in cognitive work.

The cognitive shift is distinct from the epistemic shift. The epistemic shift concerns the legitimacy and evaluation of AI-generated knowledge. The cognitive shift concerns how engagement with AI alters the structure of human thinking itself. Even if knowledge claims can be validated and agency remains formally human, sustained interaction with AI may reshape attention, memory, skill acquisition, intuition, reasoning, and identity. The question is not merely whether AI outputs are trustworthy, but how working alongside AI transforms the cognitive activities, capacities, and developmental trajectories of human actors (Mineo, 2025).

Three interrelated aspects of this shift are: cognitive recomposition, cognitive coupling, and cognitive dependency and erosion.

## 2.3.3 Aspect: Cognitive Recomposition

AI systems increasingly perform synthesis, inference, pattern recognition, and creative generation—activities historically associated with expert cognition. When AI drafts legal briefs, generates strategic analyses, or produces scientific hypotheses, the human role can shift from first-order reasoning to second-order cognitive activities such as curating, prompting, evaluating, and refining AI-generated alternatives. This shift has an epistemic dimension because users must assess the credibility and adequacy of outputs generated under conditions of opacity. But it also has a cognitive dimension when such interactions occur over time. Repeated reliance on AI can alter how individuals allocate attention, decompose problems, interrogate and retrieve information, and develop expertise. In this sense, the change is not only in what people know, but in how they habitually think.

Cognitive recomposition, therefore, refers to the reorganization of reasoning processes through sustained interaction with AI systems (Dennis et al., 2023). For instance, users may spend less time on first-order generation and more time on second-order activities such as prompt framing, plausibility assessment, output integration, and uncertainty management.

Existing IS theories struggle to account for situations in which performance improves without corresponding gains in understanding or expertise is exercised indirectly through prompting, selection, and validation rather than direct cognitive effort. For instance, traditional IS theories, such as task-technology fit and cognitive fit, assume that improved performance is correlated with deeper understanding and skill accumulation. AI disrupts this assumption. Performance may increase even as underlying cognitive engagement decreases. Essentially, the fundamental nature of human cognition in the AI context is changing.

## 2.3.4 Aspect: Cognitive Coupling

As AI systems increasingly perform tasks that require synthesis, inference, and judgment, they move beyond supporting cognition to actively shaping it. Humans interact with AI, not as tools but as cognitive partners. This creates a form of cognitive coupling—a feedback loop between human cognition and artificially simulated cognition that is not actually so (Jussupow et al., 2021). Unlike traditional tools, AI systems influence not just speed and storage but also cognitive pathways, framing effects, lines of reasoning, and problem decomposition strategies. Cognitive coupling may amplify biases, narrow search spaces, or standardize reasoning patterns across organizations. Alternatively, it may expand associative reach and creative recombination.

## 2.3.5 Aspect: Cognitive Dependency and Erosion

Cognitive dependency refers to humans’ over-reliance on AI applications to the point where they offload key reasoning tasks to AI and passively accept algorithmic outputs without exercising judgment (Benbya et al., 2021). From an IS perspective, these are not abstract philosophical concerns. They have direct implications for employees’ training, performance, and motivation, and for workplace dignity. Cognitive offloading may increase efficiency in the short term while eroding deep expertise in the long term, particularly as AI automates tasks that develop professional judgment and expertise. However, cognitive dependency could also have positive implications if it opens up opportunities for higher levels of human thinking.

Studying these dynamics requires moving beyond narrow task performance metrics to examine identity, dignity, and the lived experience of working with intelligent systems. It also requires longitudinal investigation: cognitive shifts unfold over time, often invisibly, as habits and dependencies form.

Overall, the cognitive shift raises questions about what it means to remain human while using systems where thinking itself is increasingly shared with or delegated to machines. Cognitive offloading may empower novices, accelerate productivity, and democratize access to expertise. At the same time, it may hollow out deep understanding, erode experiential learning, and redefine mastery in ways that privilege speed and output over insight and judgment. These changes are not merely technical or ethical concerns; they go to the heart of human dignity, identity, and meaning at work. For IS research, engaging in the cognitive shift means grappling with how intelligent systems reshape not just what humans do, but whom they become as thinking actors in sociotechnical systems.

In summary, AI does not merely automate tasks; it participates in reasoning. Over time, this participation may recompose expertise, reshape intuition, and recalibrate the meaning of mastery. The central question for IS research is not whether AI improves performance, but whether it transforms the cognitive foundations of professional life. In environments where intelligence is always available on demand, the preservation of deep understanding, reflective judgment, and meaningful human agency becomes not a technical problem but a civilizational one. The cognitive shift compels us to examine not only how AI changes work but how it changes the human mind within work.

## 3 Broader Implications for Behavioral IS Research

Taken together, the agentic, epistemic, and cognitive shifts do more than expand the menu of topics for IS scholars; rather, they destabilize several background assumptions that have quietly organized behavioral IS theorizing. Much of our prior work presumed bounded artifacts,<sup>7</sup> intelligible causal pathways between inputs and outputs, and a relatively stable division between human cognition and technical support. AI unsettles all three. The artifact is no longer merely something to be used but part of an ensemble that co-acts with humans. Knowledge is no longer valuable only when it can be explained but also when it can be shown to perform under conditions of opacity. And cognition is no longer simply supported by technology but recomposed through repeated interaction with systems that generate, frame, and refine thought-like outputs. This has implications for both the theory and methods we use in our research, as well as for broadening its scope to the embrace of societal impacts as a core constituency of our work. Figure 1 summarizes these implications.

Theory: These shifts imply that IS theorizing must move in at least three directions. First, they highlight the need to embrace theoretical diversity, flexibility, and scaffolding beyond the traditional framing that foregrounds the use of technology to one that enables us to understand how to do things together with technology. For example, to investigate how humans work with and interrogate artifacts whose outputs resemble the products of human cognitive reasoning, we could draw from perspectives such as metacognition, which focuses on individuals’ epistemic approaches to learning. Second, the highly prevalent traditional model—adapt an existing theory modestly, test it rigorously—works best when phenomena fit comfortably within existing conceptual frames. AI-related phenomena do not. For instance, multi-agent AI systems constitute instability and generativity arising from dynamic, probabilistic, and interconnected outputs from different agents; human interaction with such systems cannot be neatly boxed into any one theory. We thus need thoughtful theoretical blending (Jiang et al., 2022) that respects AI’s fluid and complex attributes rather than forcing alignment with particular theories. Third, we need developmental theories of human capability that examine how AI changes not only task performance in the moment but also the long-term formation of expertise, judgment, identity, and dignity. IS, as a field, is uniquely positioned to do this work. We sit at the ontological intersection of technology, processes, organization, and human behavior, and we study how they transform one another. But doing so requires institutional flexibility—openness to non-traditional theorizing, tolerance for ambiguity, and a willingness to engage phenomena before they stabilize. For instance, theories in flux (Tremblay et al., 2021) and interesting patterns (Miranda et al., 2022) offer important intermediate theoretical products.

Methods: We need methodological diversity that extends the behavioral IS repertoire of experiments, surveys, interviews, and ethnographies. The three shifts give rise to novel phenomena that need descriptive studies to surface new concepts, constructs, and relationships. They also give rise to unpredictable effects; thus, we should engage in abductive reasoning that engages and grapples with anomalies. Furthermore, since AI-driven phenomena are built on algorithmic systems that generate data at scale, computational approaches (Miranda et al., 2022) that generate patterns without sacrificing theoretical abstraction are helpful. Finally, AI-driven phenomena and concepts may not stabilize (Tarafdar et al., 2026), necessitating longitudinal and contextually embedded studies that can trace emergent theoretical patterns commensurate with their fluid ontologies.

Society: The three shifts outlined above, agency, epistemic, and cognitive, do not stop at organizational boundaries. Together, they point to broader societal implications concerning power, dignity, and the place of humans in AI-mediated systems (Sabherwal & Grover, 2024).

Historically, human uniqueness and dignity have been grounded in bounded rationality: humans possess situated experience, partial but complex knowledge, and judgment shaped by lived context. These limitations are not weaknesses, but sources of value. In organizations, individual expertise, tacit knowledge, and experiential judgment confer authority, influence, and economic reward. At the societal level, distributed human cognition underpins pluralism, deliberation, and democratic governance.

AI unsettles this foundation. Contemporary AI systems are trained on vast, aggregated datasets that approximate forms of collective knowledge; as these systems improve, individual bounded rationality is progressively supplemented or displaced by machinemediated collective rationality. The epistemic and cognitive shifts described earlier thus scale upward: Knowledge production becomes centralized, reasoning becomes externalized, and judgment becomes increasingly mediated by infrastructures that are owned and governed by a small number of actors. This has consequences for societal and institutional power. As agency, knowledge, and cognition are redistributed across human-AI ensembles, economic and political advantages may accrue less to individuals and more to those who control data, models, and computational infrastructure. Productivity gains generated through AI may therefore be unevenly distributed, reinforcing existing asymmetries rather than democratizing opportunity. This dynamic is not inherent to AI itself, but to the sociotechnical arrangements through which it is developed and deployed.

The societal concern is not merely inequality in how benefits from AI are distributed but the erosion of human distinctiveness as a basis for participation, influence, and dignity. When judgment is abdicated, knowledge is opaque, and cognition is offloaded, individuals risk becoming interchangeable operators within systems whose logic they neither shape nor fully understand. In such a world, ideals of equality and justice—grounded in informed participation, deliberation, and accountability—may come under strain. IS scholars must thus grapple with how AI shapes power, legitimacy, justice, human flourishing, and participation in society. This necessitates studies that analyze state, national, and international institutions, government bodies, and societal movements. It also provides IS scholars with the opportunity to conduct policy-focused IS research, discussed elsewhere in this journal (Beck et al., 2025).

## 4 A Call to the Field

AI forces us to problematize concepts that strike at the heart of behavioral IS research: agency, knowledge, and cognition. It urges us to confront research problems arising from the rapid transformation of these concepts and, in turn, the upending of the human-technology relationship. These are not peripheral or distant issues; they define how humans and intelligent systems will coexist in organizations and society.

The challenge before us is not simply to study AI, but to study differently. To move beyond the box—and beyond thought as we have traditionally conceived it. This editorial is a call to develop theories that do justice to the profound ontological transformations underway in both the technologies we study and the sociotechnical phenomena stemming from their intersection with organizational and societal processes, without losing sight of the human values at stake.

If we rise to this challenge, IS research can play a central role in shaping an AI-infused future that augments and even enhances humanity rather than diminishes it. If we do not, other fields highly vested in AI will define the conversation for us.

The choice, as always, is ours.
