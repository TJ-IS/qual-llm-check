# Enhancing Universal Access to Financial Services: Affordances, Constraints, and Cross-Country Cultural Value Influences on Mobile Payment Apps Use

Oluwafemi Akanfe, $^{1}$ Rohit Valecha, $^{2}$ H. Raghav Rao $^{3}$

$^{1}$ The University of Alabama at Birmingham, AL, USA, oaakanfe@uab.edu $^{2}$ The University of Texas at San Antonio, USA, rohit.valecha@utsa.edu $^{3}$ The University of Texas at San Antonio, USA, hr.rao@utsa.edu

## Abstract

Mobile payment apps (MPAs) are often promoted by advocates of digital financial access (DFA) as a solution to increase universal access to formal financial services globally. However, MPA use remains lagging across many underserved countries, with varied reactions to digital financial services. This study takes a non-traditional approach, grounded in technology affordances and constraints theory, to explore the impact of DFA relational attributes and cross-country cultural values on MPA use. Analyzing over 34,000 online reviews from MPA users, we employed a two-stage research design: first, we utilized neural networks and natural language processing techniques to identify and quantify DFA relational attributes (affordances and constraints); second, we empirically examined the relationships between these DFA affordances and constraints and MPA use, incorporating cross-country cultural value data to explore how cultural values interact with these relationships. Our findings reveal that when an MPA operates in a traditional value-oriented culture, the positive relationships between the affordances (authentication and identification protection, technical and design, and nominal transaction fee) and MPA use are weaker, and the negative relationships between the constraints (security and credit application complexity) and MPA use are attenuated. However, in survival-value-oriented cultures, the positive relationships between the affordances (authentication and identification protection, and technical and design) and MPA use, and the negative relationships between the credit application complexity constraint and MPA use, are amplified. This study offers methodological, theoretical, and practical contributions to promoting universal financial access through designing and implementing culturally sensitive MPA usage strategies across countries.

Keywords: Mobile Payment Apps, Digital Financial Access, Affordances, Constraints, Cross-country Cultural Value, MPA Use

## 1 Introduction

“Universal access to financial services is within reach—thanks to new technologies, transformative business models, and ambitious reforms.” Jim Yong Kim (Former President, World Bank Group)

Universal access to financial services through digital technologies (World Bank Group, 2020; WEF, 2018), termed digital financial access (DFA), requires the promotion of mobile payment apps (MPA). MPA is a digital financial system that is easily installable on a mobile device and can offer efficient payment services to individuals (Kazan et al., 2018). This makes MPA ideal for DFA initiatives, as it provides access to financial services to those living in areas who otherwise lack access to traditional banking services. MPA has been shown to extend sustainable and tailored financial services to the financially excluded and underserved populations (Pazarbasioglu et al., 2020).

However, approximately 1.4 billion adults worldwide still lack access to formal financial services (Demirgüç-Kunt et al., 2021), and MPA use remains notably unbalanced and lagging across many countries, including emerging economies (Kar, 2021). For instance, while MPA use has successfully increased financial access in countries such as Kenya, China, and Bangladesh, its use continues to be abysmally low in other developing economies like Nigeria, South Africa, and Mexico (GPFI, 2020; Pal et al., 2021). Even in the US, its acceptance is not ubiquitous (Rathore 2025; Rooney, 2019). This global imbalance in MPA use poses a threat to DFA.

To realize the effectiveness of MPA, specifically for enhancing DFA, researchers and advocates of DFA initiatives have suggested that the inherent factors behind its use (CGAP, 2017) should be explored from the perspectives of affordances and constraints (Pal et al., 2021). Affordances and constraints are “relational attributes” that emerge from the interactions between users and the materiality of artifacts and are based on the users’ capabilities, i.e., capabilities referring to what a user can or cannot do when interacting with a system, service, or product (Leonardi, 2011; Matthews, 2019). As Leonardi (2011) points out, because of the relational property of affordances, the same technology may support different affordances.

Prior related studies have primarily relied on single or limited country samples (Hoehle et al., 2015; Pal et al., 2020, 2021) and case studies of individual MPAs (Lashitew et al., 2019; Zhou, 2013), utilizing survey methods to identify factors and strategies for promoting universal financial access, typically from the viewpoint of user intentions. Separately, existing research has documented DFA interventions that have been ineffective in varied cultural settings (Lashitew et al., 2019). While it may be obvious from prior literature that affordances will increase MPA use and constraints will decrease it (e.g., Pal et al., 2021; Picoto & Pinto, 2021), an exploration of user-artifact interactions will allow affordances and constraints in a particular context to be revealed. For instance, regarding the possible affordances and constraints, it is yet unknown what they are in the context of DFA and MPA use.

In addition, a more complex but inadequately examined issue lies in how MPAs align with the cultural value systems that guide everyday financial behavior within the DFA context. Achieving meaningful DFA requires understanding how the attributes of financial technologies, including MPAs, resonate with or conflict with the cultural value systems (Hofstede et al., 2010) that shape financial decision-making in diverse communities (Aksoy et al., 2013; Khan et al., 2022). Thus, the complex, relational nature of these attributes is sensitive to users' cultural contexts. Fayard and Weeks (2014, p.242) emphasize that affordances associated with technology do not exist in a vacuum but are closely linked to the web of cultural knowledge that gives them meaning. Cultural differences significantly influence how users perceive and interact with technology (Aksoy et al., 2013; Khan et al., 2022; Lashitew et al., 2019). Without accounting for cultural context, well-intentioned DFA initiatives may fail to achieve their objectives and could even exacerbate financial exclusion.

This paper focuses on DFA, specifically in relation to MPA, and utilizes real-world data sources to discover and enhance the understanding of what affordance and constraint attributes are important for the interaction between users and technology artifacts, and how the cultural context impacts such interactions. It draws on the existing studies and extends them to a multi-country analysis. It goes beyond traditional literature and recognizes the place of cultural value contexts on the influence of the relational attributes of affordances and constraints on MPA use. This stance aligns with the World Bank's longstanding emphasis on preserving and accounting for cultural values to overcome the obstacles to formal financial service access, thus enabling the creation of effective interventions (World Bank, 2014). The paper draws on alternative data sources, i.e., online reviews (e.g., Aggarwal et al., 2012; Kar, 2021) and non-traditional systematic approaches to advancing DFA (Pazarbasioglu et al., 2020) by addressing the following questions:

RQ1: What are the DFA affordances and constraints in the MPA context?

RQ2a: How do the affordances and constraints impact MPA use?

RQ2b: Given that DFA affordances and constraints vary across countries, how do cross-country cultural value dimensions impact the relationship between them and MPA use?

Against this backdrop, we turn to a data-driven exploratory research paradigm (Berente et al., 2019; Debortoli et al., 2016; Miranda et al., 2022) to address the above questions in a sequential two-stage study. In Stage 1, the exploration uses neural networks and natural language processing to identify and investigate DFA affordances and constraints. In Stage 2a, we model MPA use as a function of the direct effects of DFA affordances and constraints. Subsequently, Stage 2b explores the context-specific factors by examining the cross-country cultural value differences that interact with the DFA relational attributes and MPA use. Specifically, we focus on real-world online reviews of MPA users from the Google Play Store to identify the DFA affordance and constraint attributes inherent in MPAs, and pool the online review data with countries' cultural values information from the World Values Survey (WVS) datasets (Haerpfer et al., 2022). The study draws on the technology affordances and constraints theory (TACT) (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013; Majchrzak et al., 2016) to guide the identification of the DFA affordances and constraints and cultural values literature (Aksoy et al., 2013; Hong et al., 2016) to contextualize the influence of cross-cultural values.

This study advances the literature on MPA use within the context of universal financial access. By integrating DFA relational attributes with cross-country cultural values within the TACT framework, our study complements extant research (e.g., Lashitew et al., 2019; Pal et al., 2021), offering a new outlook on DFA initiatives for enhancing financial access. Methodologically, we combine neural network and natural language processing approaches for content analysis and quantifying DFA attributes in MPAs across various countries, thereby expanding our understanding of user behaviors with technology relational attributes in diverse cultural contexts. Practically, the research will benefit universal financial access efforts by providing digital financial service stakeholders and MPA developers with insights to refine their strategies and tailor products to meet the diverse cultural needs of users. In particular, it supports the aspirations of the World Bank (World Bank Group, 2022), which emphasizes “geospatial mapping initiatives, as well as approaches to measure digital financial service-related data for financial inclusion purposes.” By recognizing DFA attributes valued in different cultural landscapes, governments can tailor policies, encourage specific MPAs or digital financial services, foster a more inclusive ecosystem, and balance innovation with consumer protection in the evolving digital finance sector.

The rest of the study is structured as follows: The next section reviews the literature concerning the role of MPA for DFA, explains the theoretical context, and highlights existing gaps. The subsequent section describes the two-stage study contexts involving research design, data collection, hypothesis development, methodologies, measurements, and analysis. Finally, we discuss the findings and end the study with contributions to theory, method, and practice.

## 2 Literature and Theory

## 2.1 Digital Financial Access and Mobile Payment App

The derivation of the DFA concept stems from the overarching concept of universal access to financial services. Universal access represents the delivery of useful, affordable, effective, and sustainable formal financial services to all—fairly and safely, especially disadvantaged groups (Claessens, 2006). DFA refers to making digital means available to financially excluded and underserved groups with a range of formal financial services that are tailored to their needs and have a long-term societal well-being focus (Pazarbasioglu et al., 2020).

In enabling DFA across countries, MPA is often considered a potential digital tool for increasing access to formal financial services (Senyo et al., 2022). MPA offers a range of features, such as person-to-person (P2P) transfers, bill payments, and online and offline purchases. Studies have shown that MPA use has increased access to financial services for the unbanked or underbanked, including low-income individuals and rural populations (Ozili, 2020; Pal et al., 2021). In addition, extant studies have found that MPA enables the financial participation of segments of a population that lack access to formal financial services and has contributed to the economic transformation of developing economies in Jordan and Kenya (David-West et al., 2021). However, despite several claims of MPA's capability to increase formal financial accessibility among underserved groups, its use remains relatively unbalanced across some developing nations (Pal et al., 2020).

A prior study has suggested exploring the role of affordances and constraints on MPA use to realize its effectiveness in increasing formal financial access for underserved populations (Pal et al., 2021). Particularly in developing countries, where technology usage patterns are lagging, understanding the affordances of technology—the actionable potential they present within their social and contextual settings—is crucial (Wyche & Steinfield, 2016).

## 2.2 Technology Affordances and Constraints Theory

Lancaster (1971) suggests that consumer product demand is driven by its attributes rather than the product itself; this study similarly views MPA as an IT product whose demand is shaped by certain attributes. To explore these attributes in the context of DFA, we draw on the TACT framework (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013) to offer a structured lens for investigating the specific attributes influencing MPA use. This theoretical framework is grounded in two main concepts: affordance and constraint (Pal et al., 2021).

Affordance is a concept that helps people understand and describe the use of technology (Piccoli, 2016). It is the actionable potential of technology features that helps individuals achieve their goals, which often triggers positive user reactions (Abhari et al., 2022; Anderson & Robey, 2017). IS research identifies affordances as technology features that users perceive as means to achieve specific goals (Karahanna et al., 2018; Leidner et al., 2018). In the context of MPAs, these affordances, like streamlined transaction processes, instant financial management, and seamless integration with other digital platforms, facilitate user engagement and redefine consumer financial interactions (Pal et al., 2021). This perspective resonates with Leonardi's (2011) view that affordances are relational, depending on users' perceptions and objectives. Hence, MPAs create an environment where technology use transcends basic functionality, transforming into an active space for financial engagement.

However, Hutchby (2001) emphasized that technology is also perceived as imposing constraints, which can hinder goal achievement. These constraints, manifesting as obstacles in technology use, often lead to negative user experiences (Leonardi, 2011). Constraints can be diverse, ranging from technical issues, such as system incompatibility and reliability concerns (Norman, 2013), to sociocultural and psychological issues related to trust and privacy. Ultimately, the shared theme is that technology derives its meaning from how it is used (Orlikowski, 2000), depending on its context, its intended purpose, and related activities (Pentland et al., 2022).

To that end, the TACT framework integrates these concepts of affordances and constraints to provide a comprehensive understanding of the underlying attributes influencing technology use (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013). The TACT is valuable because it facilitates the identification of novel affordances and constraints associated with both conventional and emerging technologies, which offer insights beyond those provided by traditional theories. Gibson (1979/1986) defines affordances as the possibilities for action that an object provides or offers to a specific user within a specific context. It is the distinct context of use that gives the TACT framework its unique flexibility, which allows for a more detailed understanding of how these attributes shape the use of MPAs.

## 2.3 Cross-Country Cultural Values and Mobile Payment App Use

People's willingness to use technologies is not just because the attributes exist, but because those attributes make sense within their cultural context (Fayard & Weeks, 2014). Cultural values, which are defined as enduring beliefs and guiding principles shared by members of society, are internalized through lifelong socialization and influence fundamental cognitive and behavioral processes, including attitudes toward technology use (Krishna et al., 2023). TACT reminds us that technological outcomes are never produced in isolation; users realize affordances or confront constraints only as they interpret a technology through the lens of their environment (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013). Because these perceptions are socially embedded, cultural values represent a critical layer of that context, shaping users' interpretations of and responses to technological attributes (Hoehle et al., 2015). As such, cultural values interact with how users assess both the affordances and constraints of digital financial services.

While infrastructural attributes (David-West et al., 2021; Senyo et al., 2022) and economic factors (Ozili, 2020; Pal et al., 2021) are widely researched factors contributing to uneven MPA use, an underexplored issue involves how MPA attributes align with the cultural value systems that guide everyday financial behavior within the DFA context. In many countries, financial practices such as informal lending (Burtch et al., 2014), rotating savings groups, and cash-based exchanges are impacted by cultural values that reinforce trust and mutual support (Krishna et al., 2023). If MPAs are perceived as undermining these norms, for example, by encouraging individualism or impersonal transactions, they may be rejected, even when technically available and affordable (Hoehle et al., 2015).

Although studies have recognized the importance of cultural values in shaping the reaction towards technology use (e.g., Hoehle et al., 2015; Picoto & Pinto, 2021; Zhang et al., 2018), much of this literature has either treated culture as broad contextual control or relied on frameworks grounded in organizational life. The Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness (GLOBE) study, for example, focused on leadership expectations within firms (Beugelsdijk et al., 2017) but does not shed light on the everyday technology choices of financially underserved consumers. Hofstede's measures were developed from employee responses within a single multinational corporation (IBM) and were designed primarily to capture cultural tendencies relevant to workplace behavior and organizational management (Picoto & Pinto, 2021).

As a result, other research opted for more recent cultural dimensions from the Inglehart-Baker's World Values Survey (WVS) (e.g., Aksoy et al., 2013; Burtch et al., 2014; Morgeson et al., 2011). The WVS is a comprehensive framework that captures beliefs about autonomy, trust, institutional authority, innovation, and social change across nearly 100 countries (Haerpfer et al., 2022). To ascertain that adopting the WVS would not overlook insights offered by earlier cultural models, a prior study tested the overlap between Hofstede's indices and WVS dimensions and found a strong correspondence (Aksoy et al., 2013), leading researchers to suggest that Hofstede's measures overlap to an extent with the WVS framework (Ayyagari et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2016). $^{1}$ However, the two frameworks differ in scope and theoretical orientation in this context. Hofstede's corporate origin limits their applicability in understanding how everyday users, particularly those in financially underserved populations, engage with digital technologies outside of formal organizational settings. In contrast, the WVS is grounded in sociopolitical theory and was explicitly designed to capture individual-level beliefs and value orientations across a wide range of life domains (traditional vs. secular-rational and survival vs. self-expression), including trust, autonomy, institutional authority, and openness to social and technological change (Haerpfer et al., 2022).

Given that this study focuses on how users interpret the influence of DFA affordances and constraints on MPA use in diverse cultural settings, the WVS offers a more appropriate lens to extend TACT in a cross-country cultural context. It enables the examination of which types of cultural values shape whether specific features of MPAs are perceived as affordances or constraints. Moreover, unlike corporate-based frameworks such as Hofstede's, WVS items explicitly target non-work life domains, making them better suited for analyzing everyday financial behavior (Haerpfer et al., 2022). Meta-analyses further indicate that WVS dimensions are more demographically diverse and offer a logical next step for investigating cultural regions beyond geographic boundaries (Taras et al., 2016).

Accordingly, we draw on the WVS dimensions as moderators that theoretically shape how users perceive and act on the relationships between DFA affordances and constraints and MPA use. Integrating the WVS dimensions with TACT fills a key gap in the literature by providing insight into the contextual factors that condition technology use in the DFA domain. The following section discusses the research design, including the data-driven exploration and the development of the research hypotheses.

## 3 Research Design and Data

Online activities continually generate datasets, providing opportunities to draw insights accessible through computational data-driven exploratory techniques (Abbasi et al., 2016; Bachura et al., 2022; Berente et al., 2019). Informed by this data-driven paradigm, this research is divided into two stages to address the objectives. Stage 1 focuses on data exploration that leverages neural networks and natural language processing techniques to investigate online reviews from multiple MPAs across different countries, aiming to identify and quantify DFA relational attributes (affordances and constraints). These attributes form the building blocks for Stage 2. In Stage 2a, we propose an empirical model to examine the relationships between DFA affordances and constraints on MPA use. Stage 2b refines this model by examining context-specific determinants, primarily focusing on how cross-country cultural variances might interact with the impact of DFA relational attributes on MPA use.

This study utilizes online reviews as a source of direct and context-rich insights into user experiences with the affordances and constraints of MPAs across diverse cultural settings. Online reviews offer real-world feedback that reflects how users perceive and interact with MPAs. Given that cultural values play a critical role in shaping how individuals interpret the affordances and constraints of technology use, analyzing online reviews allows us to refine the TACT constructs to better reflect user behavior and experiences across various cultural settings. Hence, we propose a cultural values adaptation to TACT to more accurately capture DFA relational attributes, enabling more effective and culturally sensitive interventions.

## 3.1 Stage 1: Exploratory Analysis

In the first stage, we perform content analysis to identify and quantify the DFA affordance and constraint attributes inherent in multiple MPAs. The following outlines the process involved.

## 3.1.1 Data Collection and Processing

Data: We retrieved online user review data from the Google Play Store to extract and measure the underlying DFA affordances and constraints from the shared experiences of MPA users across different countries. Online reviews offer a wealth of user-generated content that highlights real-world experiences and practical issues, making them valuable data sources (Abbasi et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022). The Google Play Store was chosen for several reasons: (1) As one of the top two app platforms, alongside the Apple App Store, it includes various categories of mobile apps compatible with Android devices across multiple countries, including hundreds dedicated to MPAs (Statista, 2026); (2) It has a global market share of over 70% (StatCounter, 2023), and it is representative of a diverse user base that enables the capture of a large volume of diverse reviews. This includes input from underserved individuals in less affluent regions who might otherwise remain difficult to reach due to the high cost of Apple devices. (3) Its users typically use a wider range of devices with varying hardware, leading to more diverse feedback. Focusing on the Google Play Store ensured variation in the type of data collected while providing a consistent platform context for analyzing relational attributes.

We extracted users' reviews for each MPA, along with supplementary digital trace data, including the number of downloads, review count, app storage size, number of likes, user profile information, user ratings, app release date, and the countries of operation, between May 2012 and December 2020. Specifically, we identified the country of the MPAs by referencing their headquarters, country of registration, and description of regional availability on their respective websites. This approach was necessitated by the inherent geographic ambiguity in user reviews, especially given the absence of geotagged feedback on platforms like the Google Play Store, where users often comment without specifying their location.

We acknowledge the risk of fake or biased reviews; however, Google actively combats this issue through a robust anti-spam system that combines human moderation with machine learning to detect and remove violations. $^{2}$ Hence, the Google Play Store offers trustworthy metrics, including the number of downloads, which yield a more accurate profile of an app's user base and minimize the single-source bias that would arise from relying on user review ratings alone.

In addition, while most mobile apps are designed for a global audience (Hoehle et al., 2015), the Google Play Store's search results may vary depending on the user's location. To mitigate potential location-based selection bias, we employed a virtual private network (VPN)-based multi-region approach, accessing the Google Play Store by simulating searches from multiple geographic regions. Specifically, we used a VPN service configured to connect through web servers in North America, Europe, Asia, and Africa. We then cross-validated our dataset by performing a global search using the Google localization ("gl") parameter, which is a URL modification that forces Google Play to display apps available in a specified country. This approach increases the geographic representativeness of the sample and minimizes regional sampling bias. The initial online reviews (retrieved in February 2021) included $N = 171,301$ users across 320 MPAs. Figure 2 shows an example of an MPA on the Google Play Store platform.

Text pre-processing: To ensure the quality of our MPA dataset, we conducted rigorous data-cleaning steps. In the preliminary step, we retained MPAs with downloads exceeding 10k and a minimum of 100 reviews. Our decision to exclude MPAs with fewer than 10k downloads and few comments is grounded in the need for data reliability, variability, and robustness. Apps with few comments and a limited user base might not comprehensively capture users' perspectives, and outlier opinions can disproportionately skew the results. By focusing on these apps, we reduced the noise from off-topic comments and could better discern prevalent user sentiments. Setting such thresholds is consistent with many studies on user-generated content that aim for data richness and noise reduction (Jabr et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021).

Subsequently, we removed stopwords, unusual or meaningless characters, and numbers that lacked significant information. Using the Python Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK), we then performed word lemmatization on the dataset to reduce inflectional forms and return a dictionary base of words. This process captured variations of words used by reviewers and accurately mapped them to the root or dictionary form of the words (Debortoli et al., 2016).

Consistent with Zhang et al.'s (2016) methodology, we eliminated reviews not written in English to prevent potential comprehension challenges and mitigate the risks of inaccurate translations of unfamiliar languages, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Although some non-English-speaking countries were included in our analysis, we limited the dataset to English-language reviews to ensure consistency in text preprocessing and analysis. English reviews are prevalent even in non-English regions, particularly because the MPAs in our final dataset operate internationally and offer English-language interfaces. These apps are often used by individuals living outside their country of origin to conduct transactions with users in their home countries. While this approach may not capture the full breadth of linguistic diversity, it provides a consistent and reliable basis for cross-regional comparison.

Additionally, to maximize the informational value of our dataset, we removed reviews with fewer than five words, as such reviews typically do not contain relevant information for further content analysis (Xia et al., 2021). We took this step to ensure the quality of reviews and the presence of only information-rich reviews in our content analysis. As a result, the final datasets consisted of $N = 34,592^{3}$ refined online reviews of 207 (65%) MPAs spanning May 2012 to December 2020.

Content analysis: While developers often describe MPA features on their product pages, online reviews offer a rich complementary perspective that highlights practical user experiences and emergent issues not always covered in official descriptions (Wang et al., 2022). This user-centric information is valuable for identifying affordances and constraints from the perspective of those directly interacting and engaging with the artifact, in contrast to the developers' perspective. $^{4}$ We followed a data-driven exploratory research approach adapted from Bachura et al. (2022), utilizing a neural network technique to identify DFA affordances and constraints from the MPA users' experiences from the cleaned dataset. This data-driven approach consisted of (1) building a word-to-vector model, (2) compiling a list of DFA terms, (3) querying the word-to-vector model using DFA terms to extract review keywords based on similarity, (4) reducing the dimensionality of DFA terms extracted into orthogonal components, (5) categorizing the components as affordances or constraints, (6) labeling the components, and (7) validating the components.

(1) Building a word-to-vector model: Word-to-vector (W2V) is an algorithm that takes a large corpus of text to produce numerical representations of words, also known as “word embeddings.” W2V converts review keywords into word vectors (word distributed weights in a sentence) and helps capture linguistic regularities and word associations (Jabr & Zheng, 2020). W2V helps in contextual understanding and mitigating the overrepresentation of terms (Wang et al., 2022), thereby addressing the potential of the volume of reviews for an MPA to inadvertently inflate counts for words related to DFA affordances and constraints. Specifically, it captures the context in which words appear rather than just their frequency, identifying the relevance of words based on their contextual usage within reviews (Liu et al., 2021). This approach ensures that words are weighted by their contextual relevance rather than their raw counts (Liu et al., 2021). To create the W2V model, we preprocessed the review text to remove punctuation, tokenize words, and convert words to lowercase. This process was completed using the genism library in Python (Řehůřek & Sojka, 2010). We used a vector dimensionality of 300 (Mikolov et al., 2013) in order to improve the model representation of subtle semantic relationships (Mikolov et al., 2013).

(2) Compiling DFA terms: Categorizing the relevant DFA terms from users' communicated experiences is analogous to a dictionary-based text categorization process requiring a domain expert to assemble a list (Debortoli et al., 2016). However, there were no open-class DFA domain dictionaries that adequately captured DFA terms at the time of this research. As a result, we adopted a financial access glossary from the Center for Financial Inclusion $^{5}$ to capture the DFA terms relevant for developing a DFA dictionary for neural network analysis. The glossary contains a list of terms for extending financial services to all, along with their definitions. Each definition was analyzed using text analysis techniques such as tokenization, part-of-speech tagging, phrase chunking, and dependency parsing. Subsequently, relevant terms were added to a dictionary using a minimum frequency cut-off of 0.70 (e.g., Godbole et al., 2010). In this case, the dictionary created serves as a DFA domain dictionary consisting of 125 terms, each representing relevant tags. For example, the items include “Access,” “Availability,” “Usage,” “Retail,” “Afford,” “Capture,” “Rate,” “Credit,” “Lend,” and “Limit,” among others.

(3) Extracting similar keywords: With the W2V model and the DFA terms list both completed, we analyzed each review in an iterative process. In this process, each review was split into its set of keywords. Then, for each DFA term, the W2V model was queried for the top 100 most similar keywords (following Bachura et al., 2022). The similarly occurring keywords provided the ability to score DFA terms as variables even when the keyword itself was not present. This resulted in an open-class DFA domain dictionary that we utilized to refine our understanding of the relevance of DFA terms in user reviews and to provide a representation of the content of user reviews for given DFA terms.

(4) Dimensionality reduction: We employed principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimensionality of DFA terms, and represent these terms as numerical vectors. The purpose of PCA was twofold: (1) to condense the DFA terms into smaller composite dimensions while retaining relevant information, and (2) to mitigate potential multicollinearity among the extracted DFA terms. PCA is a powerful statistical technique widely applied for dimensionality reduction in text categorization tasks to filter overlapping words and eliminate less relevant ones (Greenacre et al., 2022). The analysis condensed the DFA terms into six principal components (F1-F6), each containing a cluster of relevant DFA terms. Subsequently, we aggregated the vectors (i.e., numerical weights) for the DFA terms for each principal factor to form a single measure or component. See Appendix A for the details on the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the components.

(5) Categorizing affordances or constraints: We employed sentiment analysis to assign the vectors of each principal component to their corresponding sentiments - positive, negative, or neutral. By leveraging the SentimentR package in RStudio, we examined the review texts to gauge their sentimental undertones. Such computational sentiment analyses have shown efficacy in previous large-text research (e.g., Abbasi et al. 2010). We designated a sentiment score for each review, ranging from -3.5 to +3.5. Reviews with scores between 0 and 3.5 were labeled as positive, those scoring between 0 and -3.5 as negative, and those scoring exactly 0 as neutral. A breakdown of the sentiment distribution within the dataset is presented in Table 1.

Subsequently, we examined 300 random reviews to identify the PCA components and their corresponding sentiments and user ratings. We found that the components corresponding to positive and negative sentiments are distinct and can be distinguished by the user ratings. The components associated with the positive sentiment often correlate with user ratings ranging from 4 to 5, while those associated with the negative sentiment are associated with user ratings ranging from 1 to 2. However, when the components related to positive and negative sentiments are present in the same review, the user ratings are usually 3, indicating a neutral sentiment. (7) Validation: In order to ensure that the DFA terms aligned with the assigned labels, we validated the labels assigned to the components to ensure accuracy using Krippendorff's alpha interrater reliability method (Krippendorff, 2018). These judges were responsible for assessing the labels assigned to the six principal components derived from the analysis. The authors were not involved in the validation process. The judges were not allowed to communicate with any of the other judges while validating.

We began by randomly selecting a sample of 50 MPAs, each accompanied by 25 user reviews. The judges were provided with comprehensive explanations of the component labels as defined in the context of this study. Their task was to evaluate whether the DFA terms pertained to the component labels. The judges were asked to provide a label for each of the components based on the terms provided. When discrepancies arose between the judges' ratings, they engaged in detailed discussions to reach a consensus. This collaborative approach ensured that differing opinions were addressed, and an agreed-upon label was identified. Subsequently, the agreed-upon label was compared with that of the researchers.

## 3.2 Stage 2a: Effects of DFA Affordances and Constraints

The identification of the six components forms the building block for subsequent empirical models that explain the relationships between the DFA attributes and MPA use. In the second stage, drawing from existing literature, we propose the following hypotheses to address our research questions.

MPA use: Policy and consumer-finance research (BIS, 2015; Cámara & Tuesta, 2014; WEF, 2018) recognizes that access to financial services requires more than just availability; it begins with the ability to obtain and use relevant financial tools (Ferrata, 2019). Building on this foundation, we conceptualize MPA use as the initial stage of user engagement, where individuals begin to use MPAs by downloading them, which enables access to their financial service features and potential benefits. While downloading does not guarantee sustained or intensive use, it is a necessary precondition and indicates that users perceive the app as relevant to their financial needs. Moreover, using an app suggests it likely offers services or fills a gap not met by traditional banking methods. This aligns with the idea that MPAs enhance financial access by addressing barriers associated with traditional services, such as limited physical branch access, complexity, or distrust of formal institutions (Ozili, 2020). Therefore, our hypothesis leans on the idea that the DFA affordances and constraints will facilitate or hinder MPA use within a country’s cultural contexts.

## 3.2.1 Affordances

Affordances refer to the actionable possibilities that arise from the interaction between users and technological artifacts (Chemero, 2003). In the context of DFA, we discovered that four key affordances (remittance and transfer, authentication and identification protection, technical and design, and nominal transaction fees) emerged from the content analysis as enablers of MPA use.

First, the ability to send and receive funds across locations is a core function of MPAs, particularly valuable in regions underserved by traditional banking infrastructure. Such remittance and transfer capabilities promote financial access by facilitating personal and commercial transactions (Pal et al., 2020; UNCDF, 2017). Second, reliable authentication mechanisms such as passwords, PINs, and biometrics offer users confidence in the system's reliability. Effective identity verification fosters trust and encourages continued usage (Kar, 2021; Ogbanufe & Kim, 2018). Third, the design and technical features of an MPA, ranging from interface layout to navigation flow, directly impact usability. A user-centric design enhances accessibility, supports goal achievement, and encourages long-term engagement (Pal et al., 2021; Qasim & Abu-Shanab, 2016). Finally, the affordability of services plays a critical role; minimal transaction fees reduce financial barriers, particularly for cost-sensitive users, thereby increasing adoption (Mallat, 2007; Oliveira et al., 2016).

Together, these affordances may shape how users perceive and interact with MPA technologies, ultimately influencing sustained use. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1a: The remittance and transfer affordance is positively associated with mobile payment app use.

H1b: The authentication and identification affordance is positively associated with mobile payment app use.

H1c: The nominal transaction fee affordance is positively associated with mobile payment app use.

H1d: The technical and design affordance is positively associated with mobile payment app use.

## 3.2.2 Constraints

Majchrzak et al. (2016) define constraints as “ways in which an individual or organization can be held back from accomplishing a particular goal when using a technology or system.” In the context of MPAs, two prominent constraints emerged from our content analysis (security and credit application complexity), which can negatively affect use.

Security is a fundamental concern in digital finance, particularly when users must trust platforms with their sensitive financial information. Studies have consistently shown that perceptions of inadequate security, including fears of fraud, data breaches, or weak encryption, significantly reduce the willingness to use mobile technologies (Oliveira et al., 2016; Tu et al., 2021). Security features such as fraud detection and risk mitigation mechanisms are essential to user trust (Ogbanufe & Kim, 2018). However, even minor lapses or perceived vulnerabilities can erode confidence and discourage continued MPA use.

In addition, credit application complexity presents another barrier. Although digital finance platforms have enabled broader access to credit services, these systems often require extensive verification, such as income documentation, proof of repayment ability, and formal identification (Chauvet & Jacolin, 2017; Leon, 2015). For underserved populations, these requirements can be exclusionary, especially in contexts where historical biases or limited data on financial behavior lead to discriminatory lending practices (Bazarbash, 2019). As such, the complexity of accessing credit through MPAs can deter potential users and undermine the inclusive goals of digital financial access. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2a: The security constraint is negatively associated with mobile payment app use.

H2b: The credit application complexity constraint is negatively associated with mobile payment app use.

## 3.3 Stage 2b: Contextual Factor: Cross-Country Cultural Values

According to Hutchby (2001), as cited by Fayard and Weeks (2014), affordances associated with technology are deeply linked to cultural knowledge, leading to diverse outcomes in technology use. This cultural knowledge enables individuals to comprehend and make sense of the actions that can be performed with an object (Fayard & Weeks, 2014, p. 242). Therefore, extending TACT by incorporating cultural dimensions provides a better understanding of how technologies are utilized.

Although culture has long been studied through Hofstede's influential yet dated and Western-focused model and the later GLOBE project, concerns have been raised that both frameworks rely on narrow samples and miss key non-Western and non-organizational cultural factors (Aksoy et al., 2013; Beugelsdijk et al., 2017; Drogendijk & Slangen, 2006; Hoehle et al., 2015; Hong et al., 2016; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Tung & Stahl, 2018). Given these concerns, WVS appears to be an appropriate choice for examining cultural value dimensions in our study. The WVS provides recent and comprehensive data collected from a wide range of countries, allowing for a more globally representative analysis of cultural values (Burtch et al., 2014). The WVS captures a broad spectrum of cultural dimensions, including traditional vs. secular-rational values and survival vs. self-expression values (Haerpfer et al., 2022), which are represented on a map. Traditional values emphasize religion, family, and national pride, supporting traditional family structures and social norms. Secular-rational values prioritize rationality, secularism, and skepticism toward traditional authority. Survival values focus on economic and physical security, often associated with low levels of trust and tolerance. Self-expression values emphasize individual autonomy, self-expression, and quality of life, with higher levels of interpersonal trust and tolerance. These dimensions have been adopted in some IS (e.g., Aksoy et al., 2013; Burtch et al., 2014) and marketing (e.g., Aksoy et al., 2013; Morgeson et al., 2011) studies, but neither touches on how they influence the effects of relational attributes on DFA initiatives across countries.

Importantly, the WVS map creates orthogonal cultural value dimensions that indicate an inverse relationship between each set of values. That is, high scores in self-expression values correspond to low scores in survival values, while an emphasis on traditional values usually points to the opposite end of the secular-rational scale. This inverse relationship is espoused by Inglehart and Baker (2000, p. 24), who illustrate that traditional values emphasize the opposite of secular-rational values, and survival values emphasize the opposite of self-expression values. This inverse structure allows the capturing of broad cultural tendencies without redundancy.

Given these inverse relationships, we focus on the orthogonal WVS cultural values dimensions (Traditional and Survival values) as the moderators in subsequent hypotheses development and model them following the econometric model of Morgeson et al. (2011). This choice avoids redundancy among overlapping cultural factors and yields a methodologically efficient specification $^{6}$ .

Moderating role of traditional values: Traditional value orientation reflects society's emphasis on long-standing customs, respect for authority, and adherence to established norms (Inglehart & Baker, 2000). In such cultures, stability and continuity are prioritized over innovation and change. This orientation plays a critical role in shaping how new technologies, such as MPAs, are perceived and used. While MPAs are designed to promote DFA by offering convenient, efficient, and low-cost financial services, these benefits may not be fully realized in societies where cultural norms favor conventional financial practices.

On the one hand, DFA affordances, including remittance and transfer services, authentication and design, and minimal transaction fees, are intended to reduce barriers to financial access. However, in traditionally oriented societies, these features might conflict with deep-rooted preferences for face-to-face transactions, physical cash handling, and trust-based relationships with local financial institutions (Cheikh-Ammar, 2018). The unfamiliarity of digital solutions can trigger resistance, as individuals may perceive such innovations as disruptive to established ways of managing finances. This aligns with prior research suggesting that cultural adherence to tradition can slow the use of technologies perceived as undermining societal norms (Baptista & Oliveira, 2015).

Nevertheless, there is also a counterpoint to consider. Some users in traditional societies may recognize the practical benefits of MPAs, especially when these tools address inefficiencies in existing financial systems. However, even when benefits are acknowledged, the pace and extent of use are likely to remain cautious due to the cultural preference for continuity over rapid change (Aksoy et al., 2013). At the same time, DFA constraints, including concerns about security risks or complex credit application processes, may resonate more strongly with users in traditional cultures. These constraints could reinforce existing skepticism toward digital finance, as they align with culturally ingrained caution regarding unfamiliar systems. Users may view such constraints as confirmation of the risks associated with departing from conventional financial practices. As such, traditional value orientation may dampen the positive effects of DFA affordances by promoting resistance to change while simultaneously amplifying the negative effects of DFA constraints by reinforcing risk-averse attitudes. Therefore, we propose that:

H3a: The country's traditional value orientation (denoted by traditional) negatively moderates the relationships between the affordances—(i) remittance and transfer opportunities, (ii) authentication and identification protection, (iii) technical and design, and (iv) nominal transaction fee—and mobile payment app use.

H3b: The country's traditional value orientation (denoted by traditional) positively moderates the relationships between the constraints—(i) security and (ii) credit application complexity—and mobile payment app use.

Moderating role of survival values: In societies where survival values dominate, individuals prioritize economic security, physical safety, and social conformity over personal autonomy, innovation, and openness to change (Burtch et al., 2014). These value orientations often emerge from prolonged exposure to economic instability, insecurity, or societal risks, shaping a collective mindset focused on caution, risk avoidance, and maintaining familiar routines (Inglehart & Baker, 2000). Such cultural contexts play a critical role in influencing how people perceive and engage with new technologies, particularly financial technologies like MPAs that challenge traditional ways of managing financial transactions.

While it might be expected that survival-oriented societies would resist using digital financial tools due to lower trust in technology and institutions (Aksoy et al., 2013), the relationship is more context dependent. When MPAs offer immediate benefits, such as reducing transaction costs or simplifying financial processes, these features align closely with survival-driven priorities. Technologies that enhance control over financial decisions are more likely to be accepted in environments where predictability and safety are paramount (Baptista & Oliveira, 2015). For instance, remittance and transfer opportunities are especially valuable in survival-oriented societies where cross-border financial support remains a critical lifeline for households (Ratha et al., 2023). Similarly, strong authentication and identification protection features can alleviate common concerns about fraud and misuse (Kar, 2021), thereby raising trust in digital financial services within low-trust environments. In such cases, DFA affordances are welcomed as practical solutions that align with core cultural concerns about security and economic stability.

However, the same cautious outlook that encourages the use of DFA relational attributes also leads to increased sensitivity when technologies introduce constraints. Complex credit application processes or perceived weaknesses in security protocols can reinforce existing fears, thereby discouraging use. According to Keil et al. (2000), cultures where perceptions of technological complexity are often amplified tend to project avoidance behaviors even when the actual challenges or barriers are minimal. Consumers in these contexts are more likely to distrust innovations promoted by corporate or institutional actors, especially when such innovations require changes to familiar financial practices.

Survival-oriented cultures tend to discourage experimentation and favor maintaining established routines (Aksoy et al., 2013). Even when digital tools offer clear advantages, continued use may be slow if users perceive that these tools introduce complexity or demand adjustments in behavior (Hoehle et al., 2015). These indicate that survival values shape technology use by filtering both the opportunities and challenges presented by MPAs. Therefore, survival-oriented cultures increase receptiveness to attributes that align with their need for stability but simultaneously increase aversion to attributes that introduce complexity. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

H4a: The country's survival value orientation (denoted by $survival$ ) positively moderates the relationships between affordances—(i) remittance and transfer opportunities, (ii) authentication and identification protection, (iii) technical and design, and (iv) nominal transaction fee—and mobile payment app use

H4b: The country's survival value orientation (denoted by survival) negatively moderates the relationships between constraints—(i) security and (ii) credit application complexity—and mobile payment app use

Cross-country cultural values datasets: We pooled the online reviews dataset with the recent seventh wave (2017) WVS data, curated by Haerpfer et al. (2022). The WVS offers a secondary survey dataset consisting of the aggregate measures of cultural values of 100 countries and involves an average of 1400 respondents per country. The WVS includes hundreds of questions, and PCA showed that these questions predominantly load onto two orthogonal dimensions, accounting for 70% of the variation in cultural orientations across different countries. These dimensions are represented on the cultural map as (1) the x-axis, which scales from survival values to self-expression values, and (2) the y-axis, which scales from traditional values to secular-rational values, indicating opposite relationships. For example, on the x-axis, a country scoring +3 on self-expression values is represented as -3 in survival values. This inverse relationship means that by focusing on survival values, we inherently capture the country's relative position on the self-expression scale. The same principle applies to the y-axis, where emphasizing traditional values also reflects a country's position on the secular-rational scale.

Utilizing the WVS data, we focused on each country's aggregate cultural metrics of survival and traditional values. By focusing on survival values (x-axis) and traditional values (y-axis), we provide more targeted and simplified data interpretation. Including self-expression and secular-rational values as separate dimensions would result in redundancy, as these values are inversely represented on the same scales. Analyzing these values on a single scale avoids duplication and keeps the analysis streamlined.

## 4 Stages 2a and 2b: Method and Analysis

This section presents discussions on variable measurements, model formulation, and analysis.

## 4.1 Measurements

Dependent variable: In IS literature, technology use is traditionally defined by how an individual interacts with an IT artifact (Hoehle et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al.,

2016), extending beyond mere accessibility (Ferrata, 2019). However, obtaining precise app usage data remains a challenge on platforms like the Google Play Store, particularly when conducting cross-country analyses involving multiple apps. Given this limitation, the number of downloads represents an observable proxy to use as they reflect users' decision to obtain the app and, by extension, their intent to use it.

Downloads are systematically recorded across platforms and reported in standardized ranges (e.g., 1,000-5,000; 100,000-500,000), making them widely available and allowing for consistent comparison across apps and contexts. While downloading an app does not guarantee repeated interaction, it is a necessary precursor to use. Alternative measures, such as review count (which is incorporated to assess the robustness of the study's findings), may capture user active engagement (Huang et al., 2017), but they reflect input from a much smaller group of engaged users. Prior research notes that only a small fraction of users write reviews, and those who do are often motivated by particularly strong positive or negative experiences (Huang et al., 2017; Li & Hitt, 2008). Consequently, we operationalize our dependent variable, MPA use, through the number of downloads on the Google Play Store, as they provide broader population coverage and serve as a precondition to use by MPA users.

Independent variables: The independent variables are the DFA affordances and constraints, including remittance and transfer opportunities, technical and design, nominal transaction fee, authentication and identification, security, and credit application complexity. We measured the independent variables using the vectors computationally derived from the W2V algorithm described in stage one. The independent variables' measurements are based on the MPA users' online reviews retrieved in February 2021. Prior research has demonstrated the possibility of quantifying text data for explanatory variables used in an empirical study (e.g., Yue et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2018).

Moderating variables: We incorporated the country's cultural values as the moderating variable, using the dichotomy proposed by Haerpfer et al. (2022) in the WVS dataset. To operationalize this, we utilized WVS aggregate measures, focusing on traditional and survival value estimated scores for each country. These scores, as per the WVS, range between -3.5 and 3.5. To ensure coherence, we merged our online reviews dataset with the WVS, connecting through the country of MPA operation. Our final dataset synchronizes WVS estimated scores from 33 countries with our online reviews. A quadrant representation of these countries with matched data is depicted in Figure 3.

## 4.2 Analysis

The dataset collated to test our proposed hypotheses is a clustered structure, with individual reviewers' data nested within multiple apps (MPAs) and MPAs' data further nested within multiple countries. This dataset type presents a potential presence of cluster variability, mixed determinants, and contextual effects that might lead to misestimated errors in the analysis (Venkatesh et al., 2022). Therefore, we employed StataMP17 software to statistically examine the proportion of variance explained by cluster variability and the homogeneity of the variables by estimating the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) values. Our test result shows an MPA-level ICC score of 0.27 and a country-level ICC score of 0.59. While there is no universally accepted threshold for the ICC value, researchers often use the 0.60 threshold suggested by Glick (1985).

Consequently, we consider the multilevel model (MLM) to ensure our model has adequate statistical power to detect contextual effects in the analysis. The MLM model addresses these potential structural problems by decomposing the heterogeneity effects of the variables at a higher level of analysis and incorporating unique random effects (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Yoo et al., 2020). Since our dependent variable, the number of downloads, was measured at the MPA level and reported in ranges by the Google Play Store, we treated it as an ordinal variable. To analyze this structure, we employed ordinal logistic regression within an MLM framework. This approach allowed for the modeling of nested data (apps nested within countries) and accommodated the ordinal nature of the outcome variable (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Accordingly, we conducted the model analysis, as detailed in the subsequent steps.

## 4.2.1 Model Formulation

Given that the outcome variable was at the app level, we would typically be able to aggregate individual-level variable data to the app level using group means to evaluate the effects of the independent variables. However, such aggregation could result in skewed estimates. To address this, we adhered to Croon and van Veldhoven's (2007) guidance and regressed the app-level outcome (#downloads) against the adjusted group means for both individual-level and group-level variables. This approach benefited from the unbiased variable estimates and aligns with prior cross-country cultural value literature (e.g., Burtch et al., 2014). Before adjusting for group means of the individual-level variables, we assessed potential multicollinearity. The initial variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis revealed high multicollinearity involving secular-rational and self-expression values. This issue likely stemmed from the conceptual inverse relationship, where traditional values oppose secular-rational values and survival values oppose self-expression values (Inglehart &

Baker, 2000). Consequently, we excluded these variables from the model and reestimated the VIFs. The revised analysis showed that all VIF values were well below 5, which is below the commonly cited threshold of 10 (O'Brien, 2007). Detailed VIF results are presented in Table 5.

Subsequently, we applied the adjusted group means of the independent variables derived from the first step of model formulation to examine the variances within MPAs and between country levels. In this case, we estimated the independent variables, including the DFA affordances and constraints within different apps, and examined how the country's cultural value orientations moderate the effects of the independent variables on MPA use. This involved a two-level model that included the app level and the country-level interaction effects (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Specifically, we tested the interaction effects of cultural value differences (country level) on the direct relationships of the adjusted group means of the DFA affordance and constraint attributes and MPA use (i.e., #downloads). Furthermore, we integrated a fixed-effects model specification to account for unobserved heterogeneity, a technique consistent with IS studies employing quantified reviews for econometric specifications (e.g., Liu et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2018). The within-MPA model (App level) is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \# D o w n l o a d s _ {i j} = \beta_ {0 j} + \beta_ {1 j} R e m i t A _ {i j} + \beta_ {2 j} T e c h A _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {3 j} N o m T r a n s A _ {i j} + \beta_ {4 j} A u t h A _ {i j} + \beta_ {5 j} S e c u r i t y C _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {6 j} C r e d i t C _ {i j} + \beta_ {7 j} A v g T h u m b s U p C o u n t _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {8 j} A p p R e l e a s e d D a t e _ {i j} + L o g (\beta_ {9} A p p S i z e _ {i j}) + \\ \beta_ {1 0 j} A v g S e n t i m e n t _ {i j} + \beta_ {1 1 j} A v g W o r d C o u n t _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {1 2 j} S o c i a l M e d i a _ {i j} + \\ A p p D e s c A t t r i b u t e s [ \beta_ {1 3 j} S E I n d e x _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {1 4 j} F F I n d e x (A 2) _ {i j} + \beta_ {1 5 j} U C R a t i o _ {i j} ] + \alpha_ {i j} + \varepsilon \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

In the given model (Equation 1), the dependent variable represents MPA use (proxied by the number of downloads), where the subscript i represents each MPA and the subscript j represents each country. The coefficients $\beta_{1}$ through $\beta_{6}$ are the regression slopes (adjusted group means) for the independent variables. Each of these coefficients is indexed by a subscript i for each MPA and a subscript j for each country. We controlled for the average number of thumbs-up per app, the app release date, app sizes, average sentiment score, social media presence, and app developer description attributes (Security Emphasis Index, Financial Feature Index, and Usability Claim Ratio) for each MPA, incorporating them as their respective coefficients. Specifically, the app size ( $\beta_{9}$ ) is log-transformed for normalization purposes. The $\alpha$ terms delineate the app-level effects, while $\varepsilon$ represents the error terms or residuals.

We recognize that, apart from distinct cultural values, each country possesses unique, heterogeneous factors that could impact its use. We incorporated country-level fixed effects into our model to account for these unobserved variables through the subsequent multilevel econometric specification. Thus, the model specification for between-country (country-level) is presented as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \beta_ {(0 t h r o u g h 6) j} = \gamma_ {(0 0 t h r o u g h 6 0)} + \\ \gamma_ {(0 1 t h r o u g h 6 1)} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {(0 2 t h r o u g h 6 2)} T r a d t n a l _ {j} + \\ u _ {(0 t h r o u g h 6) j} \end{array} \tag {2a}
$$

and

$$
\beta_ {(7 t h r o u g h 1 5) j} = \gamma_ {(7 0 t h r o u g h 1 5 0)}\tag{2b}
$$

Substituting country-level equations (2a-2b) into the App-level Equation (1) yields the following reduced-form equation with the app-level control variables and a complex error term.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \# D o w n l o a d s _ {i j} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \gamma_ {0 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \\ \gamma_ {0 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {1 0} R e m i t A _ {i j} + \gamma_ {2 0} T e c h A _ {i j} + \\ \gamma_ {3 0} N o m T r a n s A _ {i j} + \gamma_ {4 0} A u t h A _ {i j} + \gamma_ {5 0} S e c u r i t y C _ {i j} + \\ \gamma_ {6 0} C r e d i t C _ {i j} + \gamma_ {7 0} A v g T h u m b s U p C o u n t _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {8 0} A p p R e l e a s e d D a t e _ {i j} + L o g (\beta_ {9 0} A p p S i z e _ {i j}) + \\ \beta_ {1 0 0 j} A v g S e n t i m e n t _ {i j} + \beta_ {1 1 0 j} A v g W o r d C o u n t _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {1 2 0 j} S o c i a l M e d i a _ {i j} + \\ A p p D e s c A t t r i b u t e s [ \beta_ {1 3 0 j} S E I n d e x _ {i j} + \\ \beta_ {1 4 0 j} F F I n d e x (A 2) _ {i j} + \beta_ {1 5 0 j} U C R a t i o _ {i j} ] + \\ [ \gamma_ {1 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {1 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * R e m i t A _ {i j} + \\ [ \gamma_ {2 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {2 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * T e c h A _ {i j} + \\ [ \gamma_ {3 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {3 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * N o m T r a n s A _ {i j} + \\ [ \gamma_ {4 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {4 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * A u t h A _ {i j} + \\ [ \gamma_ {5 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {5 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * S e c u r i t y C _ {i j} + \\ [ \gamma_ {6 1} S u r v i v a l _ {j} + \gamma_ {6 2} T r a d t n a l _ {j} ] * C r e d i t C _ {i j} + [ u _ {0 j} + \\ u _ {1} R e m i t A _ {i j} + u _ {2} T e c h A _ {i j} + u _ {3} N o m T r a n s A _ {i j} + \\ u _ {4} A u t h A _ {i j} + u _ {5} S e c u r i t y C _ {i j} + u _ {6} C r e d i t C _ {i j} + \alpha_ {i j} + \\ \eta_ {i j} + \varepsilon ] \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Consistent with Raudenbush and Bryk (2002), we centered the variables before estimating the model for a more straightforward interpretation of the results. In addition, the number of MPAs and within-country parameters varies across MPAs, and there are not many country-level units (in this case, 33 countries). Following Wood's (2011) recommendation, a restricted maximum likelihood expectation optimization was performed to obtain maximum log-likelihood estimates of the cluster variances.

## 4.3 Results

Table 6 presents the MLM estimation, which includes both app-level explanatory variables and country-level moderating effects. For the first set of hypotheses testing (H1a-d and H2a-b), we examined the direct relationships between the DFA affordances and constraints and MPA use. The positive relationships between all sets of DFA affordances, including technical and design, nominal transaction fee, authentication and identification protection, and remittance and transfer opportunities, and MPA use were statistically significant at a p-value < 0.001. The negative relationships between all the DFA constraints (security and credit application complexity) and MPA use were statistically significant at p < 0.001.

While the DFA affordance and constraint attributes had positive and negative relationships with MPA use, we found wide variations in their significance. For instance, our findings suggest that MPA users care a lot about authentication and identification protection (2.259) but comparatively less about the remittance and transfer opportunities (1.289), technical and design (0.483), and nominal transaction fee affordances (1.923). Similarly, MPA users appear to be more concerned about the security constraint (-4.085) than the credit application complexity constraint (-0.783). The results indicate that users' preferences vary significantly, and these preferences should be considered when designing and optimizing an MPA.

We further explored the moderating effects of country-level cultural values, specifically traditional and survival values, on the relationships between DFA affordances, constraints, and MPA use. To do this, we incorporated the app-level variables (Level 1) into the country-level estimation (Level 2). Given that the independent variables differed across various MPAs, we employed an unstructured covariance matrix in the full model to address the random effects, incorporating the independent variables at the country level (Level 2).

For H3a and H3b, we found that the country's traditional value orientation negatively influenced $(p < 0.001)$ the relationships among authentication and identification protection, technical and design, and nominal transaction fee affordances and MPA use. Based on their coefficients (-9.538, -13.099, and -17.360, respectively), we observed that the country's traditional value orientation dampened the positive relationships. However, the traditional value had no significant negative influence on the relationships between remittance and transfer affordance and MPA use. One reason may be that traditional value-oriented MPA users are less liberal and hesitant to appreciate the relational attributes that MPA offers that traditional payment systems do not.

On the other hand, the country's traditional value orientation positively and significantly affected the relationships between security (40.649, $p < 0.001$ ), credit application complexity (23.107, $p < 0.001$ ), and MPA use. In other words, the traditional value attenuated the negative relationship between these DFA constraints and MPA use. This could be because traditional values emphasize the importance of trust and interpersonal relationships, which can reduce the perceived threat of security and make the credit application process more user-friendly.

For H4a and H4b, we hypothesized that the country-level survival cultural value would moderate the positive and negative relationships among the DFA affordances, constraints, and MPA use. We found that the survival value positively and significantly influenced the relationships among authentication and identification protection (3.429, p < 0.001) and technical and design (3.173, p < 0.001) and MPA use. Furthermore, the country's survival value orientation had a negative and significant (-3.516, $p < 0.001$ ) influence on the relationships between the credit application complexity constraint and MPA use. In contrast, the survival value did not significantly influence the relationship between the security constraint and MPA use. The coefficient is positive, and the results contradict the hypothesis that the country's survival value negatively influences the relationship. Therefore, we may infer that while a country's cultural survival value may affect the relationship between DFA constraints and MPA use, it is not necessarily a unidirectional effect and may vary depending on the particular constraint being considered.

## 4.3.1 Robustness Tests

We conducted a series of robustness tests. First, we acknowledge the potential for endogeneity issues. $^{8}$ Frequent app updates often signal active development and improvement, typically driven by the need to fix bugs, add features, or enhance security (James et al., 2023) rather than influencing the number of downloads. However, these updates may affect the app's affordance and constraint attributes. Similarly, the number of libraries, which refers to the external software components integrated into an app, does not directly drive the number of downloads but may introduce functional complexity that impacts app stability, performance, and user experience (Derr et al., 2017).

We examined the triad of the number of updates released, the number of libraries, and the DFA affordances and constraints. Since the Google Play Store does not readily provide this data, we sourced information on app updates and libraries from AppBrain, a third-party app store platform. To address potential endogeneity in the MLM analysis, we employed a two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) approach, which is particularly suited for multilevel data structures. The 2SRI adapts the instrumental variable approach to account for correlated errors and random effects in nested data (Setia et al., 2020). The results in Table 8 (see Appendix B) show that the chosen instrumental variables (number of updates released and number of libraries) were statistically insignificant in the second stage of regression. This consistency across the initial mixed-effects model and the subsequent robustness check support our hypotheses. While the robustness check led to changes in the size of some coefficients, these changes did not undermine the fundamental relationships identified in the initial model. Additionally, the insignificance of the residuals for each independent variable, as indicated by their p-values, further validates our findings.

Second, since we hypothesize that DFA affordances and constraints are associated with MPA use (Pal et al., 2021), there is a possibility of reciprocal relationships where the number of downloads (a proxy for MPA use) could lead to increases in affordances and decreases in constraints. We analyzed seven models incorporating the explanatory variables and the instrumental variables (number of updates released and number of libraries), following the methodology of Wang et al. (2019) and Pal et al. (2021). For our analysis in Table 9 (see Appendix C), the chosen instrumental variables were expected to correlate with the independent variables without affecting the dependent variable (Pal et al., 2021). In Model 1, without using the instrumental variables, we assessed how affordances and constraints influenced MPA use, measured by the number of downloads, and found significant effects. Conversely, in Models 2-7, where we implemented the instrumental variable approach, the number of updates released and the number of libraries significantly influenced each affordance and constraint attribute with a p-value of less than 0.001, while MPA use did not show a significant effect on these attributes. These results suggest that reciprocal relationships between MPA use and affordances or constraints are not a concern in this study.

Third, we reestimated the model using the review count as an alternative measure of MPA use rather than the number of downloads employed in the main models because some stakeholders may perceive that review counts capture app usage patterns and market reach (Huang et al., 2017). The results presented in Table 10 (Appendix D) show that the primary relationships identified in our main analysis remain stable using the review count measure. Specifically, the affordances and constraints maintain their significant associations with MPA use. Moreover, the direction and significance of interaction effects involving traditional and survival values align closely with the main results, providing additional support for the validity of our findings.

Fourth, to address potential concerns about omitted cultural variables, we included the nine cultural dimensions from the GLOBE study (House et al., 2004) as additional controls. While our main model focused on the interaction effects of WVS cultural dimensions with DFA affordances and constraints, the GLOBE dimensions were entered as direct effects. This allowed us to examine whether broader cultural constructs captured through a different theoretical lens might account for the observed relationships. The results in Table 10 (Appendix D) show that none of the GLOBE dimensions were statistically significant predictors. Importantly, the WVS-based (survival and traditional values) interaction effects remained significant and directionally stable.

Fifth, to address possible concerns with estimating MPA use only at the app level, we conducted an additional country-level regression using aggregated data derived through the Croon and van Veldhoven (2007) adjusted group means approach. This was to ensure that the country-level estimates reflected the between-group component of variance while controlling for within-group effects, thereby providing a more reliable test of country-level relationships. With the app-level variables aggregated to their adjusted country-level means, we examined whether the moderating influence of cultural value dimensions remained consistent at the macro level. Using the review count, an alternative measure of MPA use, the results from this aggregated country-level model, as shown in Table 11 (Appendix E), similarly align with those of the main multilevel analysis.

## 5 Discussion

The analyses revealed some interesting findings. First, the positive relationship between the nominal transaction fee affordance and MPA use is weaker when the MPA operates in countries with a traditional value-oriented culture versus a survival value-oriented culture. This observation suggests that the cultural values prevalent in a given society may influence individuals' perceptions of transaction fees. In traditional value-oriented cultures, people may exhibit a higher degree of patience and trust in the payment process, which could potentially make them more tolerant of nominal transaction fees. Conversely, in survival value-oriented cultures, transaction fees may be viewed as unnecessary overhead or a luxury beyond the primary survivalist causes, such as physical and economic security.

Second, the negative relationships between the DFA constraints (security and credit application complexity constraints) and MPA use are weaker in countries with a traditional value-oriented culture. In such cultures, where there is a strong emphasis on trust and interpersonal connections, community endorsement and personal assurances may help people downplay concerns about security and credit application complexity, thus reducing the deterrent effect of these constraints on MPA use. On the contrary, in a survival value-oriented culture, the negative relationship between only the credit application complexity constraint and MPA use is amplified, though this is not the case when the security constraint is added. This suggests that in environments where survival concerns are more pronounced, users may be warier of the complexities and potential pitfalls associated with credit applications, leading to reduced MPA use when such constraints are present.

Interestingly, traditional and survival values appear to exert unexpected influences on the relationship between the remittance and transfer opportunities affordance and MPA use. Specifically, neither value shows a statistically significant effect on this relationship. In addition, the findings contradict the hypothesis that survival values amplify the effect of security constraints on MPA use. Instead, the results indicate that higher levels of survival values may attenuate perceptions of security-related limitations. One possible explanation is that individuals who prioritize survival values may view MPAs as essential tools for basic needs, such as transferring funds to family, thereby overlooking potential security risks in favor of use. This bidirectional and context-dependent influence highlights the complexity of cultural values in shaping technology use and points to the need for further research to clarify the underlying reasons.

## 5.1 Theoretical and Methodological Contributions

Our study's theoretical contributions stem from examining how DFA relational attributes and cross-country cultural values interact to influence MPA use through the lens of

TACT and the cultural value literature framework. Integrating these previously distinct areas of research offers a fresh perspective and contributes to the broader literature on DFA initiatives. This perspective broadens the application of the TACT and encourages the investigation of a wide array of attributes of DFA initiatives, including digital insurance services, credit scoring algorithms, biometric identification, and cryptocurrencies, among many others. Expanding upon the works of Berente et al. (2019), Miranda et al. (2022), and Debortoli et al. (2016), we demonstrate TACT as a flexible theoretical lens to uncover valuable, actionable insights from digital trace data and guide the use and advancement of DFA initiatives.

Second, building on the works of Aksoy et al. (2013) and Hong et al. (2016), our study highlights the potential for cross-cultural value comparisons within the TACT framework. This approach enables the examination of how technology use is shaped by variations in cultural norms, beliefs, and institutional arrangements across different countries. Analyzing these differences reveals how cultural values inform users' interpretations and use of technology. This cross-country lens helps surface the contextual factors that guide whether a given attribute is seen as an affordance or a constraint. In turn, it demonstrates that users do not experience technology uniformly; their perceptions are filtered through their local environments. Recognizing this variation provides a stronger foundation for context-sensitive technology design, policy recommendations, and empirical analysis.

From a methodological perspective, our study employs data-driven neural network and natural language processing approaches to perform content analysis and quantify the DFA affordance and constraint attributes inherent in multiple MPAs across multiple countries, utilizing online review data. This innovative approach allows the relationship between technology attributes to be captured and used on a global scale. The critical challenges we address include the need for accurate and timely data-driven insights into how different cultural and technological contexts influence MPA use. Consequently, by addressing such a critical societal challenge, the study plays an important role in contributing to the social good (Abbasi et al., 2023).

In addition, given that users are unlikely to form preferences based solely on an app or product's official descriptions (Wang et al., 2022), we derived DFA relational attributes from user reviews rather than from developers' descriptions provided on official web pages. Reviews are a valuable source of information because they reflect what users find important. Unlike developers, who may highlight insignificant features, reviewers focus on attributes that truly matter to them (Wang et al., 2022). Consequently, app attributes viewed as insignificant by reviewers were considered neither affordances nor constraints. This approach ensured that our analysis would align more closely with actual user priorities and experiences.

## 5.2 Practical Contributions

Our research makes significant practical contributions to the field of DFA by providing actionable guidance for key stakeholders in the digital financial service ecosystem. First, digital financial service organizations and MPA developers could refine their strategies by determining specific DFA relational attributes and their effects on MPA use. Our findings can empower them to customize their products and services, ensuring they align with users' diverse preferences and needs across various cultural contexts. For instance, understanding which attributes resonate most strongly in specific regions can inform product design and marketing efforts, ultimately promoting higher usage.

Second, our research has implications for government agencies promoting comprehensive financial access. Our study unearths a spectrum of DFA relational attributes from consumers' perspectives within diverse cultural landscapes. These insights lay the foundation for targeted policy interventions. Governments could develop policies that incentivize the use of particular MPAs or encourage the development of digital financial services tailored to the identified attributes. This approach would enable governments to develop a more comprehensive digital financial ecosystem that aligns with local preferences and cultural norms, thereby fostering economic participation among underserved populations. Additionally, our research framework equips governments with insights to devise effective policies and regulations in the DFA domain, allowing them to balance innovation with consumer protection as the digital finance landscape evolves.

## 6 Conclusion and Limitations

This study provides essential insights into the affordances and constraints of innovations related to universal access to financial services, specifically MPA. It demonstrates how MPA online review data can be computationally mined and analyzed to obtain DFA relational attributes that inhibit or foster the use of formal financial services across different countries.

As with any data-driven and empirical study, the research has some limitations, which, however, present avenues for further research. First, the dependent variable used in this paper is a surrogate variable for DFA (i.e., MPA use), which relies on the number of downloads as an indicator. In the absence of granular transaction-level data, the number of downloads provides a visible precondition to use, which is particularly relevant when studying financial access in app-based contexts. Coding other qualitative and quantitative instruments could lead to more comprehensive findings. In addition, for the current research, time-lag data were unavailable for the number of downloads from the Google Play Store. Second, the theory is contextualized to MPA user-generated information. Although the data sample was sufficiently diverse and valuable for theorizing and explaining experiences regarding use, our findings are only generalizable to the online review data of a specific DFA innovation (i.e., MPA). The third limitation is our utilization of country-level cultural values. Although national cultural values may not accurately describe the cultural values of individuals within a nation, we view national cultural values as an indicator that differentiates one social system from another (Hofstede et al., 2010). Fourth, we acknowledge that platform-specific distinctions do exist. MPAs on Google Play (Android) and the App Store (iOS) may differ in terms of certain technological features due to variations in development frameworks, operating systems, and security protocols. However, despite these differences, the overall user experience can often be quite similar across platforms. For instance, key functionalities such as transaction processing and interface usability, and core features like account management are frequently standardized across both platforms to maintain a consistent user experience (Tiwana, 2015). As a result, many of the affordances and constraints identified through reviews from the Google Play Store may also apply to users in Apple's ecosystem, particularly for cross-platform apps designed to provide uniform experiences across both operating systems. $^{9}$ A detailed examination of Apple MPAs could be carried out in future research. Lastly, our dataset was limited to English-language content to reduce the risk of translation errors; although this choice restricted linguistic diversity, it provided a consistent and comparable basis for cross-regional analysis.

Future research could take multiple directions. First, different surrogate measures could be valuable. Our utilization of MPA use as one indicator of DFA represents a step toward gaining a deeper understanding of the financial well-being of underserved and underbanked populations, as recommended in multiple policy studies (e.g., BIS, 2015; WEF, 2018). Further research could consider using an average rating count over time as an alternative measurement or could aggregate the number of downloads over a longer period, such as monthly or quarterly, to account for the potential time-lag effect. This would provide deeper insights into the relationships between the DFA relational attributes and dependent variables in a temporal context. Finally, future studies could combine other approaches, such as interviews and experimental studies, with a customer-centric mindset, and extend the multilevel regression model to include different, previously unexplored DFA affordances and constraints.
