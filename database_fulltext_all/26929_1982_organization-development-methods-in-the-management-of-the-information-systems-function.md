# Organization Development Methods in the Management of the Information Systems Function

By: R.D. Loftin
J.M. Moosbruker

## Abstract

The work presented in this article relates directly to perhaps the most serious problem facing the Information Systems manager in a large, complex organization today, namely how to plan and manage in a rapidly changing, high-demand, resource-limited environment. The article describes an organizational change effort undertaken within a major data processing organization to seek improvements in four broad areas: data center production performance, responsiveness of the systems development activity, management control and decision making, and long range and operational planning processes.

Much of the change effort involved activities and tasks which were defined and implemented using Organization Development (OD) methods. OD involves the application of behavioral science knowledge in a collaborative and participative process in response to some perceived need within the organization. It is a planned and systematic way to alter patterns of organizational behavior.

The general change strategy utilized is described along with specific examples of particular OD techniques, those which worked well and those which did not.

Finally the article presents some observations as to why OD methods are important to Information Systems (IS) managers. OD methods are powerful skills and tools for working change of the most important kind, namely change in organizational and group behavior. As the primary agent for change in the corporation, IS managers can employ these methods to make the IS organization more responsive to the needs of the business. More importantly they can bring these same skills to bear on the larger problem of helping their corporation adjust to change, facilitating and leveraging that change in ways which mere technology cannot accomplish.

## Introduction

DPS employs over 640 people with an annual operating budget in excess of \$27 million. Its functions include corporate mail operations, voice communications, graphics and micrographics services, office automation and work management services, in addition to traditional systems, programming, and data center operations. ## DPS Background and Organizational Climate

Blue Cross of Massachusetts, Inc., and Blue Shield of Massachusetts, Inc., are separate non-profit corporations created by acts of the Massachusetts Legislature. In 1937, Blue Cross was established to act on behalf of members to pay for care furnished by institutional providers of health care services (hospitals). Blue Shield was similarly established in 1942 to pay professional providers (physicians). A joint agreement with Dental Service Corporation of Massachusetts, Inc., provides payment for dental care services. These corporations are among 140 Blue Cross and Blue Shield Plans nationwide, each independent and locally managed, and all members of the Blue Cross and Blue Shield Associations located in Chicago, Illinois.

In addition to their health system roles, both corporations function as financial institutions. They establish and collect payments from individuals and groups and pay claims, each in a variety of ways. At the end of 1981 the corporations provided these services to approximately 31,000 groups and a total of 3.5 million members. Approximately 6.2 percent of the premiums collected in 1981 went to operating expenses with increased upward pressure coming from energy costs and data processing costs. Premiums earned by the corporations were nearly \$1.5 billion in 1981 and assets rose to over \$716 million.

Over the last fifteen years several changes have occurred in the corporations that have impacted the level as well as the types of services provided. For example, the corporations are establishing a very strong consumer advocacy position within the health care system. They are among the nation's leaders in promoting alternative forms of health care delivery and financing, and they are beginning to provide medical care services directly through health maintenance organizations.

Competition in the industry has increased substantially over the last several years, and national cost growth in health care has received congressional attention. Consequently the corporations have assumed new roles as health care advisors, consultants, and analysts to their corporation clients and individuals alike, and these new roles require new types of information. Government business (Medicare, Federal Employees Program, etc.) is a major corporate activity with heavy operational cost-performance pressure, extraordinarily high entry threshold cost, and intense competition, especially from data processing specialty firms.

Massive changes occurred in the mid-1960s when the corporations began to serve as contractors for the new Medicare program. Claims increased dramatically and the total number of employees increased from approximately 900 to 2250 in less than eighteen months. The resulting operational demands placed a tremendous strain on DPS capabilities. The corporate climate was one of lack of confidence that DPS could perform the necessary functions adequately, exemplified by Blue Shield's contracting with an outside firm for the data processing of its Medicare B claims. This was the first time that any significant portion of data processing services was acquired from outside of the corporations, and management was sharply divided on the decision.

DPS performance was in fact poor, and continued to deteriorate. User relationships were also at an all time low. Finally in 1969 the corporations removed the DPS Vice President and retained a prominent consulting firm to recommend a solution. Ultimately their recommendations were rejected as a patchwork, and instead the early 1970s saw the start of a totally new database system to replace the old, heavily card-oriented applications.

The development process for the new system was one of total corporate involvement. All major corporate components were represented on a high-level task force commissioned for the purpose of writing the functional specifications. User managers were assigned responsibility for many nontechnical activities and overall project management rested with a steering committee chaired by the Executive Vice President.

The corporations' decision to limit the budget available for maintenance and enhancements for the old batch applications systems produced eventual user frustration when the development of the new system fell behind, and as technical problems forced a shift from IBM to Honeywell equipment. The transition from card-oriented technology to database-oriented technology was accompanied by production difficulties in both old and new systems, and maintaining a reliable production service became a continuing source of trouble.

During the mid-1970s terminal access to comprehensive, reasonably current databases often enabled user managers to reorganize work flows. Working sometimes with the manual methods staff, which was not part of DPS and which had little formal connection with the systems development activity, assumptions regarding terminal response or availability produced intense production dependencies which were intolerant of minor network outages. Honeywell timesharing was introduced to the organization in late 1974 but on the condition that no support services would be available. By 1976 numerous user organizations had become infiltrated with timesharing applications, and timesharing became an underground production environment.

The corporate steering committee became dysfunctional and was finally abandoned. In its place a three member committee of Vice Presidents was formed for the purpose of managing the development of the new system. Priority for the backlog of changes for the older batch applications was relegated to a second, lower-level committee, which became a graveyard for service requests. User personnel became generally accepted as project leaders, and routinely established DPS workload priorities. Since some DPS managers frequently accepted work assignments directly from counterpart user managers, it had also become extremely difficult to determine the total DPS workload commitment at any given moment.

By the late 1970s, the DPS development resource had become committed entirely to user-generated work. Projects which were initiated on behalf of DPS, for example, operational improvements, invariably fell prey to higher user priorities, often without an opportunity for DPS management to intervene.

The high visibility afforded data processing activities, begun with the good intentions of assuring user involvement and improving relationships, had a negative impact on the DPS climate which was still clearly evident in 1979. When operating problems occurred, detailed information was requested from very high up in the management chain. Technical people had to spend as much time explaining the problem as they did solving it with a resultant delay in recovery time.

Many DPS personnel had become “blame conscious,” feeling that the detailed questions they were being asked were an attempt on the part of management to blame them for the problems. One result was difficulty in obtaining information; communication was not open. Another result was very low risk taking behavior. People tried to avoid the spotlight rather than take initiatives which might improve productivity. The combination of intensive user scrutiny plus DPS’s inability to manage or even to measure its workload led to erratic tight control by upper DPS management; people were often told both what to do and how to do it, in an understandable attempt to control something.

The DPS climate was also generally lacking in the approval and rewards demonstrated by upward mobility. A total of eight senior managers reporting to the Vice President or Assistant Vice President had been recruited from outside the organization. Other senior managers had moved in from staff positions rather than coming directly up the line.

In late 1979 the DPS organization was in a state of transition. The structure was clearly in need of change and the key managers had not been identified. One senior manager had recently been removed, the performance of others was questionable, and an Assistant Vice President was being recruited from outside the organization.

The problems of the organization were seen as long-standing and the solutions appeared to involve behavior and attitude changes in large numbers of people, both within DPS and outside. In addition, there was a very high need for acceptance of the proposed solutions and task assignments by all levels of DPS management.

## Organization Development

Organization Development has its roots in Kurt Lewin's pioneering work in group dynamics, and in the survey-feedback techniques developed at the University of Michigan's Survey Research Center [1]. It is basically an educational process by which human resources are continuously identified, allocated, and expanded in ways which make these resources more available to the organization. It generally involves a planned and systematic attempt to change patterns of organizational behavior. Its goals are more effective organizational functioning and improvement of the quality of working life experienced by individuals within the organization. Typical objectives include:

\- creating an open, problem solving climate throughout the organization,

\- supplementing the authority associated with role or status with the authority of knowledge and competence,

\- locating decision making and problem solving responsibilities as close to the information sources as possible,

\- building trust among individuals and groups throughout the organization,

\- making competition more relevant to work goals and maximizing collaborative efforts,

\- developing a reward system which recognizes both the achievement of the organization's mission and the growth of people,

\- increasing the sense of “ownership” of organization objectives throughout the work force,

\- helping managers to manage according to relevant objectives rather than according to past practices or to objectives which do not make sense for their areas of responsibility, and

\- increasing self-control and self-direction for people within the organization [5].

OD's basic characteristic is the application of behavioral science knowledge in a collaborative and participative process in response to some problem or felt need within the organization. The assistance of a specialist to serve as a change agent or catalyst is often required to help the organization gain distance and perspective of itself, provide a theoretical base, and maintain the momentum of the change effort. The person is generally a consultant with knowledge of the theory and technology of applied behavioral science.

Usually the first step in an OD effort is a diagnosis of current organizational functioning. Data is collected by some combination of interviews, questionnaires, observations, and reading of selected organizational documents. The focus can include the entire organization, or some coherent system or part thereof. The data is analyzed by the consultant and fed back, in a fashion which protects the confidentiality of individual contributors, to a group of organization decision makers. Their task is to validate the data, prioritize the issues raised, and begin an ongoing process of organizational change in collaboration with the consultant.

Specific roles may vary and are determined by the group, as are the specific activities and techniques which may be utilized. The latter encompass a broad range of possibilities including team-building, process consultation, conflict management, and problem solving sessions with or between work groups. Development of individual managers is often one focus of an OD effort, and may be accomplished through one-on-one consulting (observation and feedback or behavioral coaching) and through a variety of training programs. An organizational intervention such as the development of career ladders may also facilitate management development. Sociotechnical interventions may be utilized, including job rotation, job enrichment, reorganizations, rearrangement of roles, functions and work, or flexible work schedules. Changes can also be made in the feedback system or the reward and punishment systems through revisions of the appraisal process.

The collaborative orientation of the effort means that the consultant works toward sharing skills and developing them in organization members. Over time, there is a continuous increase in the organization's capacity to understand and manage its own growth. Line managers should eventually be able to carry out the OD effort with little outside assistance. The timeframe for this occurrence is, of course, variable and is affected by dimensions such as the current state of development, turnover of key personnel, and level of support from upper management.

## Beginning the DPS Change Effort

The DPS organizational climate over the years had led, by late in 1979, to a situation of high employee turnover and low morale. These problems became the focus of discussion by a group of upper-level DPS managers which met periodically with the DPS Vice President. At first salaries and benefits were identified as the primary contributors to employee dissatisfaction, but the discussions soon broadened to include career mobility and work assignments. The problems thus defined and the solution determined, a consultant was sought to train DPS managers in conducting career development discussions with their employees.

After hearing some of the organization's history and current “symptoms,” the consultant suggested doing a broad-based organizational diagnosis to discover the causes of the motivation and morale problems. Subsequently all 32 DPS managers were interviewed to discover their perceptions of the organization's strengths and weaknesses. The questions were about goals, structure, job assignments, rewards, relationships, leadership, decision making, communication, climate, the appraisal process, planning, budgeting, and training [3]. The individual responses were held in confidence. (See Appendix I, Part A for a list of the questions.)

Following the survey-feedback model, the data was presented to a group of sixteen upper level DPS managers and staff at a two day offsite meeting. In retrospect our judgment tells us that three days would have been better. The group discussed and validated the data. While very little data was discounted, one or two individuals remained unconvinced of its accuracy. The group developed 33 statements of underlying problems suggested by the data. The statements were then arranged in order according to the urgency which the management group felt should be attached to each. The following six tasks were then defined, which, if accomplished, would solve the ten most important problems (Appendix I, Part B):

\- complete the development and implementation of the DPS planning function,

\- develop and implement ways to improve the utilization of DPS personnel resources,

\- develop and implement ways to make the employee appraisal system more effective,

• develop and implement an effective standards program,

• develop and implement consistent management practices, and

\- recommend ways to improve the effectiveness of the organization structure in the short term.

A task force was established for each task and given the authority to implement their solution. The chairperson was assigned by the Vice President. These results were reported to the entire DPS management group at a subsequent meeting where managers were invited to volunteer for membership on a task force. They did so almost without exception.

## The Task Forces and Their Results

OD is usually a top-down change effort, the need for involving top management having been demonstrated again and again by successful efforts which were curtailed because someone in higher authority did not understand or did not like them. Normally this management involvement would occur in accordance with the organization structure, employing existing organizational units and charters to implement the change tasks. That was not the model we followed. Instead, an across-the-organization approach using task forces was chosen. The reason for this divergence was that the Vice President did not believe that the leadership structure was in place yet. As mentioned earlier one senior manager had recently been removed, the performance of others was questionable, and the Assistant Vice President was being recruited from outside the organization.

Thus, at least for the six tasks described above, the existing organization structure was discarded in favor of task forces chaired and staffed by managers at all levels. The advantages were the ability to draw on managerial talent at lower levels of management, broadening the people's perspective of the organization by having them work on problems outside the scope of their usual sphere, and increasing managers' acquaintance and awareness of each other across the organization. Two beneficial results were increased communication and lower level solving of routine problems between organizational components.

However, some difficulties arose because the task forces were operating outside the usual management chain. The fact that their commissions were outside any existing organizational charter produced uncertainty and some passive resistance to the process by a few managers. Others, viewing the whole notion of “participative management” as a dilution of traditional areas of authority, felt threatened by this apparent “bypassing the chain of command.”

The task forces' effects were felt in two ways. The first is referred to as "Meta-change," that is they triggered an "unfreezing" [4] of the organization as a whole. (In Kurt Lewin's terminology, unfreezing is an alteration of the present stable equilibrium which supports the present behavior and attitudes.) This readied and enabled it to carry out the longer-range changes described in the sixth section. Second, the task forces themselves accomplished the specific organizational changes described below.

The task force which addressed itself to the need for a new organizational structure was composed of the Vice President, both Directors, three Division Managers, and the OD consultant (see Figure 1). It met nearly weekly from November 1979 to April 1980. The major conflict it encountered was a struggle to preserve the status quo in the face of pressure from the Vice President for a matrix approach. The process was of value in that the conflict was confronted openly, and the meetings were chaired by a division manager. It did result in the development of a new organizational structure which created new directors of Systems Engineering and User Services as shown below. The new structure was installed over a reasonable time utilizing a process which obtained feedback from all the managers, in contrast to former swift and secretive approaches to change.

The task force on consistency in management practices failed to implement any changes, but did involve nine managers in ongoing discussions of management issues, which all deemed helpful.

The standards task force managed in four months to define the problems and their solutions, implement the solutions and disband. The major problem was defined as an inadequate standards program due to ineffective administration and inappropriate mechanics. Inadequate enforcement, frequent non-compliance, unilateral decisions on development of standards, slow distribution, and poor maintenance of documentation were also cited.

This task force created and replaced itself with a Standards Board, composed of two managers and a senior technician from Production Operations and three managers and a senior technician from Systems Development. The Board's function is to authorize the development or modification of a standard (any manager can present justification), establish priorities, assign the task to a specific area, distribute drafts to all managers, collect reactions, reconcile differences, receive and review reports of non-compliance, hold approval reviews, and sign and distribute all approved standards. The task force also established a standards manual, a distribution process, and a central library for housing all current standards as well as those in process, and disapproved or antiquated standards.

The task force which concerned itself with the employee appraisal system conducted a questionnaire survey of all DPS employees. The 24 questions solicited both opinion and facts about the appraisal process as it was being conducted at that time. Managers were also surveyed to discover their current appraisal practices. Based on the results, a series of recommendations were prepared for various components of the corporations including Corporate Planning, Salary Administration, DPS Training Services, and DPS management. The task force on personnel utilization came the closest in its targeted area to the original motivation and morale concerns. They also took their injunction to implement solutions seriously. A ten percent sample of employees across DPS was interviewed to determine what they liked and disliked about their jobs, whether they in fact felt underutilized, knew what was expected of them, and had adequate work, responsibility, and training.

The data was summarized into problem areas for each role group within the organization (e.g., programmers, operators, secretaries). The task force then designed a consultation process whereby members could work with each department to facilitate communication about these problems by management and workers. The intervention included teaching each group how to use a problem solving model. This process was employed successfully in two DPS departments. On the basis of their experience it was decided to include formal facilitator training in the development program for systems analysts and others who have a need to interact with users in a problem definition and diagnosis setting.

The Planning Task Force represents a blending of the OD processes and what would have been usual organizational practices. An Office of Strategic Planning (OSP) was created, headed by a former Manager of Applications, currently staff to the Vice President.

The planning process had a long beginning, for a number of reasons. The charge to OSP was really to create a planning process which would involve the whole organization, but the structure was not in place. Long range planning is a complex, ambiguous task, fraught with psychological difficulties $[2]$ . The planning office became a convenient place to put bright people who did not quite fit the organizational mold. For all these reasons very little long range planning occurred until the new top management team designated itself the Planning Policy Committee and absorbed the task.

## The Second Stage Effort: DPS Changes and Corporate Impact

By June of 1980 the new organizational structure was in place, including the new Assistant Vice President. Once in place, the top management group (Assistant Vice President and Directors) found itself at odds with the task forces which were competing for time and energy from the line organization. Although not all of the change activity from the first phase was completed, the task forces were encouraged to come to closure.

At this point a second stage of the effort was entered, and the consultant's role changed fundamentally. Her role as facilitator of several of the task forces had given her wide access to all levels of DPS management. The new role made her a process consultant for the DPS staff group (comprising all positions in Figure 1) and she became accountable to that group for all work done in the organization. This was an important step in transferring “ownership” of the change effort from the task forces to the line organization. Until late 1981 the consultant continued as process observer to the DPS staff group. The process consultation included facilitating communication, decision making and conflict utilization among group members, helping them to do self-assessment, and conducting a two day facilitator training session for them.

When the DPS staff group felt that they were working quite effectively together, and could in fact be called a team, they terminated their contract with the OD consultant, although some group members continued with individual contracts for specific tasks. The “ownership” transfer was thus completed.

Following the task forces, and until the present time, other activities have opened communications within DPS and improved the overall climate. These include regularly scheduled rap sessions between managers and employees, the use of a newsletter for all DPS employees, and a more conscious effort by top DPS managers to deal with ongoing organizational problems so as to seek improvement in organizational processes rather than to assign blame for individual events.

Late in 1981 another OD consultant was retained by the DPS staff group to assist in creating a DPS management development program. This task included a questionnaire survey and interviews with all DPS managers regarding their development needs and expectations of the program. A majority of managers cited improved human interaction skills among their most important development needs. The first components of the program were conducted early in 1982.

As a result of the work begun in late 1979 — the organizational diagnosis, the task forces, the new organization structure, and the continuing use of OD methods — DPS has accomplished the unfreezing mentioned earlier, which allowed the organization to make additional changes toward increased organizational effectiveness. A better climate was created in which DPS managers are accepting more accountability for their work. The new climate also permits and promotes systematic, constructive self-examination. Communication has improved, internally and externally, and DPS generally works more smoothly than before. As DPS managers have become more involved in defining the needs of the organization, individual and group initiative has been stimulated, and they have become better able to respond to the needs of the business. The following paragraphs describe some of the corporate impact areas at the end of 1981, the first full year of operation under the new organization structure.

## Planning

During 1980 and 1981 the DPS staff group met several times off-site to discuss and develop the DPS planning process and the DPS strategic plan. A mission statement was developed along with a number of strategic objectives which are now reviewed semiannually. A detailed operating plan was prepared for 1981 and remained surprisingly intact throughout the year. The operating plans for 1981 and 1982 contained specific programs and objectives relating to DPS strategic issues, some of which are discussed below. Operating plans are reviewed quarterly.

In 1981 a group of corporate Vice Presidents was formed as the “Data Processing Strategic Planning Committee” (DP SPC) for the purpose of providing policy guidance to DPS on matters of strategic importance. The group meets every two months, all day, off-site. The DP SPC is specifically excluded from individual project reviews. Agenda items are provided by the members, and the DPS Vice President hosts the meetings. The Office of Strategic Planning assures continuity between the DP SPC agenda and internal DPS planning agendas. The DP SPC has discussed and validated a set of data policies and technology policies, and has also discussed and clarified a set of environmental, business, and technical assumptions which were considered critical to the System Architecture Plan.

In addition, a standard corporate life cycle methodology was reviewed and adopted, including an extensive education and training program conducted throughout the corporations by DPS. For 1982 the life cycle terminology was employed in user planning and corporate budget documentation. In addition, the DP SPC has ordered the development of benefit criteria to regulate project approvals at various life cycle steps.

The DP SPC also reviewed the 1982 DPS budget and workload, including major user priorities and DPS resource needs. In these reviews, as in many of its agenda items, the DP SPC has impacted broad corporate issues. In doing so it has made a specific contribution to the communications and planning processes in both corporations.

## Capacity Management

The Capacity Management Program was assigned to System Engineering in the new DPS organization. The overall goal of capacity management is to ensure that the computer configuration accommodates the workload, providing quality services, on time, and at a reasonable cost. The program includes both reporting and forecasting activities to assure that an adequate capacity margin exists. Configuration reviews are conducted quarterly and all hardware and software changes are synchronized with application development plans and production schedules.

Reporting is based on the concepts of software physics, a methodology which enables the reporting of all computer processing in units of "software work." This conceptual framework allows the theoretical maximum capacity of the computer to be compared with the workload currently being processed. A system to produce these reports was developed in 1981.

Forecasting employs analytic modeling software which enables an accurate appraisal of the impact of increased workload on the existing hardware capacity. It also provides a means for predicting the impact of various hardware configuration changes. Software to provide this forecasting ability was evaluated in 1981 and will be installed in 1982.

## Information Central

In 1980 the Information Central Department was established to reduce the turnaround time in satisfying ad hoc user requests for information, and to assist users in becoming more self-sufficient in obtaining responses to their data needs. Information Central employs a number of report generator and analysis packages with free access to all corporate data to satisfy specific requests. In addition a walk-in service center is operated, offering timesharing, word processing, and the use of personal computers. Free consultation and limited training services are also provided to walk-in users.

In 1981 Information Central services were used extensively in direct support of negotiations for a multi-billion dollar contract with the hospitals throughout the state of Massachusetts. In another case a complex series of special reports was prepared for two select groups of major corporate clients under a very difficult deadline.

In addition to their direct corporate benefit, the services provided by Information Central have satisfied an important set of user needs which might otherwise have been imposed upon the development or maintenance functions of DPS. With these interruptions removed the level of project performance was improved.

## Operations

The DPS data center recorded 94 percent on-time delivery for over 178,000 deliverables in 1981. Transaction volumes grew by 26.7 percent (to 46 million) while unit processing costs were reduced by 12.2 percent.

A word processing expansion program was undertaken in 1981 and 51 terminals were installed at year-end. User productivity gains were documented ranging up to 100 percent, and word processing is prominently featured in 1982 planning.

Micrographics also received major attention in 1981, and a conversion from roll film to fiche was begun. An inhouse COM facility was established and pilot projects were undertaken with users to replace paper reports with fiche. These were extremely successful and a corporate-wide expansion is underway in 1982.

## System development

A new dental claims processing system was developed and implemented early in 1982, almost exactly on the original schedule of one year. Utilizing online data entry and fully automatic adjudication, this new system provides clear operational and marketing advantages for the corporations. Although the project commitment was made to the board level, project management and control were exerted at the first (lowest) level of DPS and user management. This was in marked contrast to the high level scrutiny of prior years.

A new system was also installed to administer the activities of other Blue Cross and Blue Shield Plans who serve national accounts on behalf of the Massachusetts Plans. This system is utilized to collect and report claims activity in various client locations, and to provide important cost management information to the client headquarters. The system also enables the corporations to participate in new health benefit and financing arrangements which are offered by other Blue Cross and Blue Shield Plans.

## Observations and Comments

The stereotype of the computer person is someone fascinated with technology and systems. In recent years the Information Systems (IS) executive has succeeded in the eyes of corporate management by devaluing technical image in favor of one more oriented to the business of the company. Our experience suggests that the IS executive must also become more oriented to the nature of change itself if the IS executive is to preside over the introduction of technology to the business. This may require a further devaluing of the traditional technical image in favor of one more relationship oriented.

Technology and systems are being vigorously merchandised as instruments of change which inevitably lead to improved productivity and higher profits. As these technological products become easier to acquire, the customer increasingly finds that their effective utilization is stymied by change resistance factors such as poor organization structure, unclear decision making, or confusing areas of authority. These are not connected to, or solvable by, any technological application, and it is rare to find an IS executive who is skilled at identifying and resolving these nontechnical, "soft" problems. OD offers a means to identifying and resolving many of these underlying problems. The application of OD methods to the IS function is especially significant for the following reasons.

\- The IS executive is acknowledged to be a primary agent of change in the company. Experience with OD methods will enable the executive to gauge the extent to which the company and the IS organization are receptive to change and able to incorporate change.

\- By applying these methods within the organization the IS executive develops a cadre of managers who are better able to help their user counterparts adjust to change, and who are able to identify non-technical opportunities for improvement in users' organizations as well as their own.

\- The nature of IS tasks requires an extraordinary level of interaction among organizations within the IS function and with a diverse community of users. OD methods can help assure that these interactions are supportive and constructive.

\- OD methods focus attention on such things as decision making processes, information sharing and personal reward systems. These are powerful factors in employee motivation and morale and, consequently, productivity. This is unfamiliar ground for IS managers, who are more likely conditioned to view the human as a systems component.

\- The principles associated with OD call attention to the human dimension, much needed in IS organizations which tend to emphasize the mechanistic dimension. A great deal more time and energy is spent discussing what the machines can and will do than what the people can and want to do. The traditional emphasis is on code rather than on communication, the expectation often being that people, like machines, can be programmed.

\- OD promotes a process oriented view of the world, as opposed to a content oriented view. Consequently, the process of change is accommodated naturally, and change management can become institutionalized.

OD efforts in IS organizations are likely to require the use of a consultant to provide necessary expertise and an unbiased viewpoint. It is important that a clear contract be obtained with the OD consultant, covering objectives, roles, working relationships, and timeframes. Exercise care that the consultant not come to be viewed by managers as having implementation responsibilities and authorities.

An essential ingredient in the second stage effort was the notion of management accountability, a logical extension of the task forces' commission to implement. Accepting accountability was difficult for those DPS managers accustomed to an extreme dilution of individual accountability resulting from control by high level corporate committees. Some DPS managers failed to make the transition to the new organization structure and responsibilities. Some had succeeded as project managers or by being highly reactive, but could not adjust to becoming proactive managers who could accept broad mission accountabilities.

The IS executive who sponsors an OD effort in the organization must invite participation by subordinate managers, and must give up some measure of control to them in order to increase their ownership of the organization's problems and solutions. There is some risk that this behavior may be interpreted as weakness. The effective participative manager sets goals and structures processes in which work can occur, rather than directing solutions. Consistent demands and high expectations remain important.

The commitment of lower level managers to attack the problems within the IS organization must be matched by a corresponding commitment of the top IS executive to attack the broader corporate situations which complicate life for managers (for example, appraisal systems or corporate committees). This mutual commitment forms a basis for the early trust which is an essential factor in the success of the OD effort.
