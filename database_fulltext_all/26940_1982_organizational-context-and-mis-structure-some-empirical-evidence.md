# Organizational Context and MIS Structure: Some Empirical Evidence\*

By: Phillip Ein-Dor
Eli Segev

## Abstract

An empirical investigation of some relationships between organizational context and MIS structure is reported. The major findings are that MIS structure is significantly correlated with organizational structure which, in turn, is closely associated with organizational size. Additional relationships were found between organizational size and the rank of the MIS director, between implementor/user relationships and psychological climate toward MIS, and between the last two variables and degree of MIS integration.

Keywords: Centralization, implementor/user relationships, integration, MIS director, MIS structure, organizational size, organizational structure, propensity to pioneer, psychological climate, rank, time frame

ACM Categories: J.1, K.6.4

## Introduction

Some relationships between the structure of an MIS and its organizational context are investigated in this article. One of the pioneering empirical studies on computers and organizations was by Whisler [36] who conducted a field study of twenty-three companies within the life insurance industry. His work focused on the impact of computers on these organizations in terms of changes in organizational structure, centralization of control, decision making, and job content. Additional work by Mason and Mitroff [20], Lucas [18, 19], and the Conference on Implementing Operations Research/Management Science [31] pointed out that organizational context is one of the determinants of MIS structure and may have a significant effect on MIS success.

Ein-Dor and Segev's survey of the scientific, managerial, and trade journals yielded ten organizational context variables related to the success of MIS [8]. These variables are organizational maturity, organizational size, organizational structure, organizational time frame, the psychological climate in the organization toward MIS, the extra-organizational situation, organizational resources, rank and location of the responsible executive, and the steering committee. The literature survey also suggested propositions, relating the variables to the success of MIS. The large number of references to this issue reflects both the importance the literature attaches to the effect of environment on MIS success and the complexity of this relationship.

This study is the first in a series planned to investigate the relationships between the structure of MIS and its organizational contexts. Organizations included in the study were characterized by a successful MIS, as evidenced by its use by managers in the organization, and by a relatively high degree of maturity, expressed in formal organization, well understood and quantifiable processes, and availability of data.

## Conceptual Constructs

Relationships between two conceptual constructs, organizational context and MIS structure, are investigated. The two constructs are surveyed and discussed below.

## Organizational context

Of the ten organizational context variables mentioned in the introduction, four were included in this initial study: organizational size, organizational structure, organizational time frame, and psychological climate toward MIS. A fifth variable, organizational maturity, was controlled, as only mature organizations participated in the study.

Because of the nature of MIS as a reflection of managerial processes in organizations, organizational structure is hypothesized to have major effects on MIS. However, size is recognized as an important determinant of organizational structure, as well as of other organizational context variables, and is expected to affect MIS structure both directly and indirectly.

The dimension of organization structure studied in this article is the degree of centralization of the organization. The effects of this aspect have been widely referred to in the literature, [e.g., 5, 20, 25, 31]. There is a spectrum of degrees of centralization from complete centralization in which all important decisions are made at the top level, at one extreme, to decentralization of strategic decision making down to the lowest possible level, at the other extreme.

Organizational time frame is defined by the length of time available for reaching decisions $[5, 10, 12]$ . Among factors that affect the organizational time frame are the culture in which the organization is embedded, the industry in which the organization is engaged, and the size of the organization.

Every organization develops its own psychological climate with respect to MIS. This climate is set by the dominant view of MIS held by members of the organization. It finds its expression in the expectations [e.g., 4, 5, 11], preconceptions [e.g., 11, 26], and attitudes [e.g., 18, 31] prevalent in the organization.

## MIS structure

MIS structure is a multi-attribute variable since it comprises several dimensions, among which are the degree of centralization of MIS, degree of integration of MIS, deployment of hardware, and place within the organizational hierarchy. The degree of centralization of MIS refers to the centralization of the development and implementation efforts within the organization [16, 21].

There are five aspects to MIS integration discussed in the literature: 1) integration of data from different areas of the organization by means of a database [13, 24, 29, 32, 33], 2) integration of models when the output of one model becomes an input to another [33], 3) integration of models with a database, when models draw the majority of their inputs from a database and place their outputs back into it [33], 4) output integration — achieved by having compatibility between the outputs of various subsystems [15], and 5) facilitation of semantic integration of separate pieces of data, by individuals, into a single image [23]. In this article, the concept of MIS integration comprises the first two aspects mentioned above.

Deployment of hardware refers to the location of computer equipment within the organization. The continuum of possibilities ranges between a large centralized processing complex to dispersed mini or microcomputers $[30, 34]$ . The place within the organizational hierarchy is reflected in the rank of the MIS director $[11, 36]$ .

## Additional variables

Two additional variables were included, namely, the propensity to pioneer with MIS and implementor/user relationships.

During MIS development an organization may break ground in hardware, software, or management technologies. The readiness to undertake such innovations determines the organization's propensity to pioneer in MIS [1, 26]. Although clearly not an aspect of MIS structure, the propensity to pioneer impacts the process by which the MIS structure (including integration, centralization, and hardware deployment) is crystallized.

The quality of implementor/user relationships is determined by the degree of involvement of users in the MIS development process $[1, 3, 6, 18, 35]$ and by the level of conflict generated by interactions between users and implementors $[2, 6, 18, 36]$ .

## Hypotheses

The main hypothesis is related to the association between organizational structure and the dimensions of MIS structure.

Hypothesis 1. MIS structure is associated with organizational structure. The first hypothesis is the more centralized the organization (a) the more centralized its MIS structure, (b) the higher the level of integration of MIS, (c) the more centralized hardware tends to be, (d) the higher the rank of the MIS director, and (e) the higher the propensity to pioneer. Organizational context is a multidimensional construct; organizational size is posited as a dominant variable which is one of the dimensions of organizational context and affects other organizational context variables, especially organizational structure.

Hypothesis 2a. Organizational size is associated with other organizational context variables — organizational structure, time frame, and psychological climate toward MIS. It is posited that larger organizations tend to be associated with less centralized structures, favorable psychological climate toward MIS, and longer time frames.

Hypothesis 2b. Organizational size is directly associated with MIS structure. Since organizational size is hypothesized to be negatively associated with the degree of centralization of the organization, it is also hypothesized to be negatively associated with the dimensions of MIS structure (see Hypothesis 1).

Psychological climate is hypothesized to be directly associated with implementor/user relationships. It is generally assumed that better implementor/user relationships are associated with more favorable psychological climates.

Hypothesis 3a. Psychological climate toward MIS is associated with the quality of implementor/user relationships. It is also posited that there is a relationship between psychological climate and MIS structure.

Hypothesis 3b. Psychological climate is associated with MIS structure. Basically, the idea is that less centralized MIS are associated with more favorable psychological climates.

## Methodology

The data analyzed in this article were collected in a study of 53 organizations in a large U.S. metropolitan area during Fall 1978 and Spring 1979. Graduate students participating in a management systems course were assigned individual projects as one of the requirements. In these structured projects, the students were guided by a questionnaire which included twelve questions, seven of which provided the data for this study. Each of the questions related to a specific chapter in the textbook used in the course [7]. The terms used in the questions were presented in the chapters, and examples were given.

Based on interviews, organization data, personal experience, and published material, the students had to answer the twelve questions for the organization selected. The students were asked to choose a relatively mature organization and to justify their choice.

All 53 organizations are in one geographical area. They represent neither organizations elsewhere, nor a systematic sample of organizations in that area. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to assume that there was no one personal bias in choosing the organizations and any other population of mature organizations should produce similar results overall.

The evaluation of students was based on the understanding that they displayed of the concepts and their application to the specific organization. Each answer was supposed to be limited to half a typed page, although, in fact, some answers were longer. The students were encouraged to append photocopies of material which supported their reports.

When a report was completed, the students were required to present it to the MIS director in the organization for appraisal. The typical MIS manager commented on the project, often referring to specifics, endorsing the information or modifying it.

For processing purposes, the data were coded in a standard form by one of the researchers. The data are of three kinds:

1. Numerical data extracted directly from the project (e.g., sales, number of

employees).

2. Ordinal data directly referred to in the project (e.g., initial rank of MIS director).

3. Descriptive data. These data were contained in students' reports in descriptive form. The students were not requested to establish values for these kinds of data or to express their judgments. One of the researchers interpreted the reports and assigned values to the variables. This was done by a heuristic assessment of the operational criteria for each of these variables. The assessments were used to compare and rank the organizations on the two or three point scales employed for the specific variables.

Criteria for evaluation and ranges of variable values are exhibited in Table 1.

During evaluation, four low quality reports were excluded when it was evident that their authors did not grasp the concepts and could not apply them. When the same organization was studied by two or more students, only one project was selected, by the researcher, on the basis of the thoroughness of the reports, completeness of data, documentation of answers, and the evaluations provided by the MIS manager involved.

Of the 87 projects submitted, four were not included because of quality and thirty because of duplication. This article is based on data extracted from the remaining 53 projects. The number of observations of each variable is frequently less than 53 due to omissions in the students' reports or insufficient information for assigning values. Issues of validity and reliability arising from the data collection and evaluation methods employed are discussed in the penultimate section of the article.

Of the 53 organizations involved, sixteen were in the electronics, computer, or communications industries, seventeen were in natural resources, utilities, manufacturing, construction, or trucking, three were in warehousing or retailing, and nine were law firms, banks, or insurance companies. There were also one university, one hospital, and six federal, state, or local government agencies.

The hypotheses call for strong monotonic relationships between pairs of variables. Most of the variables were rank ordered, using either a three point scale, or a dichotomous scale. Thus, a relatively large number of ties was expected. The Kendall Rank Correlation Coefficient (Tau) is an appropriate non-parametric statistic which observes the ties on both variables $[14]^{1}$ . The sampling distribution of Tau has been extensively investigated and significance levels can be calculated.

## Findings

Hypothesis 1 states that organizational structure is associated with MIS structure. This hypothesis was tested by computing Kendall coefficients of correlation between degree of organizational centralization and structural aspects of MIS.

Table 2 provides clear support for the hypothesis. Organizational structure is significantly associated with most aspects of MIS structure. It is important to note that the correlation values, while generally significant, indicate moderate associations overall. Since the authors were dealing with a complex system, from which only a small subset of variables was selected for scrutiny, higher correlation coefficients were not to be expected.

As posited, the degree of MIS centralization is positively associated with the degree of centralization of the organization (line 1), and the centralization of hardware also follows this pattern (line 3). Lines 4 and 5 of Table 2 indicate that the current ranks of MIS directors are higher in more centralized organizations. This is consistent with the finding that management information systems also tend to be more centralized in centralized organizations. This is not true, however, of initial ranks. Although organizational structure is found to be associated with the degree of MIS integration, the association is negative, contrary to the authors' prior expectation (line 2). Thus the data indicate that the degree of MIS integration is higher in less centralized organizations. One way to explain this phenomenon is to refer to Lawrence and Lorsch's findings on differentiation and integration [17]. They found that high performance, differentiated organizations use various integrating devices, and that the level of differentiation is related to the level of integration. The authors' finding is consistent with Lawrence and Lorsch's findings: less centralized organizations need devices for integration. One of them is MIS; thus, less centralized organizations tend to have more integrated MIS.

Negative correlations also characterized the propensity to pioneer. Rows 6 through 8 of Table 2 deal with the relationship between organizational structure and the organizational propensity to pioneer with MIS. Here it is found that higher propensities to pioneer in general, and to pioneer in software specifically, are associated with decentralized organizations. This is not so with hardware pioneering. A probable explanation is that, in general, pioneering in the MIS field is perceived as producing and using new kinds of software, whereas the hardware technology is sufficiently mature so that little risk is seen as associated with it and cannot, therefore, induce pioneering. When pioneering relates to original software, then new, unorthodox ventures may characterize more decentralized organizations with divergent needs and local readiness to take risks, and incur expenses, while in more centralized organizations all risks, and expenses, are centrally assumed.

The propensity to pioneer was found to be clearly associated with hardware deployment. $^{2}$ This indicates that the propensity to pioneer is associated with at least one aspect of MIS structure: higher propensity to pioneer is associated with regionalized hardware. Thus, the inclusion of the propensity to pioneer in this study is slightly supported. However, relationships between the propensity to pioneer and other aspects of MIS structure are weak or nonexistent.

The second hypothesis focused on the association between organizational size and other organizational context variables, and on aspects of MIS structure. The overall concept of size was operationalized in this study by three measures: size relative to the relevant industry, number of employees, and total sales, replaced by revenue or budget in non-profit organizations or government agencies. A number of measures were adopted, for two reasons. The first reason is in order to capture the concept. The first three lines of Table 3 clearly indicate that the three measures are highly correlated among themselves, indicating that something of the essence of the concept has been isolated. Secondly, it was assumed that the different aspects of size may affect different facets of MIS. The findings may be interpreted to support this assumption, but because there are some inconsistencies and because some of the correlations are weak and insignificant, this interpretation is open to question.

Lines 4 to 6 of Table 3 deal with the relationships between organizational size and organizational context variables. The size, sign, and level of significance of the correlation coefficients in Line 4 indicate the expected association between organizational size and degree of centralization in the organization: larger organizations tend to be less centralized. Note that the correlation coefficients of the three measures of organizational size are of the same magnitude (-0.461 to -0.544).

The evidence provided on the association between organizational size and the two other organizational context variables is rather disappointing. The relative measure of organizational size proved to be positively correlated with organizational time frame (large organizations tend to have longer time frames), but correlations between organizational time frame and the two absolute measures of organizational size are very small and insignificant (see line 5). On the other hand there is some indication of negative association between the number of employees and the psychological climate in the organization, while the other two measures of organizational size indicate no relationship with psychological climate (line 6).

## Validity and Reliability

The problem of the validity and reliability of the data used in this study is apparent. Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret [22] raised this issue in their study on decision processes, the data for which were collected by teams of graduate students taking management policy courses. In their paper, they discussed reliability and power of the three methods available for studying strategic decision processes: observation, organizational records, and interviews or questionnaires. They concluded that “What the student teams captured really happened, but not all that happened was necessarily captured—” [22].

The data analyzed in the present paper were collected mainly by interviews as well as from personal experience and published material, and their collection was guided by structured questionnaires, coordinated with a textbook which defined each of the terms, and a class in which the concepts were discussed. The students were instructed to find answers to the specific questions, rather than to capture the nature of the process. They were aware that, later, the answers to the questions would be read and commented on by the MIS director in the organization. So it is postulated that, as Mintzberg, et al, suggest, the reports captured reality and are a reliable source for research data.

In preparing the analysis, data judged to be unreliable were not included. When the researcher found the data on a specific variable to be unclear, incomplete, or lacking, a “missing value” was assigned to the variable. The procedure resulted in relatively large amounts of missing data, but hopefully increased the reliability of the remainder. In this exploratory study, relative data in the students’ reports were interpreted either on a dichotomous scale, or when it was evident that a middle value existed, on a three point scale. Thus, if the data captured the nature of the variable, the categories used were wide enough so as not to introduce a bias. On the other hand, if a student misunderstood reality and made a gross mistake in the report, it was not smoothed by the researcher. For future studies, more differentiated scales should be used in order to capture finer nuances of the data.

## Discussion

In summarizing the findings of the study, one is impressed by the general consistency and significance of the associations of organizational structure, as measured by degree of organizational centralization, and many aspects of MIS structure. Specifically, organizational structure was found to be associated with the degree of MIS centralization, hardware centralization, degree of MIS integration, current rank of MIS director, and propensities to pioneer in software and in general. In addition, the degree of MIS centralization was found to be associated with the size of the organization, and psychological climate with the degree of MIS integration and the quality of user/implementor relationships.

Olson has also investigated relationships between organizational structure and MIS structure. In her thesis [27], Olson did not find “...any distinct pattern of relationships between organizational characteristics and centralization/decentralization of information services overall.” However, some organizational characteristics were found to influence the structure of information services: these are also reported in [28]. The findings in this article clearly do indicate the existence of such an association. This difference may be partly explained by the use of different constructs for describing organizational and MIS structures. However, even in those cases in which there is compatibility between the factors employed in both studies, the findings are inconsistent. For example, Olson and Chervany [28] report no relationships between the degree of centralization of physical hardware and any organization characteristics, while this study found a strong and highly significant relationship of hardware deployment and organizational structure. Olson and Chervany reported only the existence or non-existence of associations but did not specify their strength or significance levels so that no comparisons of these statistics are possible.

An important question, also raised by Olson and Chervany, concerns the directions of the associations found, or in other words, causes and effects. While the direction of association is a topic for further research, some speculation about causes and effects is possible. First, organizational size is an inherent organizational characteristic which is presumably determined before the decision about MIS structure; therefore it seems that MIS structure is at least partly determined by organizational size. To continue this line of thought, relationships were also found between the size and structure of organizations and between organizational size and MIS structure. This suggests that MIS structure is also affected by organizational structure. However, previous findings [36] indicate that introduction of MIS affects organization structure.

The indeterminancy of cause and effect creates great difficulty in drawing valid normative conclusions from MIS studies such as those discussed here. It would appear that one solution to this problem is the performance of dynamic studies, in which the sequencing of states throws some light on the nature of relationships between them. For example, the initial and current ranks of the MIS director were observed in this study. As reported in the Findings section, the initial rank is not correlated with organizational structure while current rank is. Assuming no changes in organizational structure, rank of the MIS director is affected by organizational structure.

In further analyzing inconsistencies in the findings reported in the two articles, the question of sample size inevitably arises. It is conceivable that the lack of consistency noted is a result of the samples in both studies being too small to be representative. This is exacerbated by use of different definitions and operationalizations in different studies, and by the restriction in this article to mature organizations with successful MIS. Standardization would permit more confident comparisons.

Although this study was restricted to mature organizations with successful MIS, different MIS structures were found, corresponding to different organizational structures and sizes. Thus the findings indicate that there is no one way to structure a successful MIS. In this context, the concept of the fit of an information system is particularly useful. Given a vector of values of variables that define the structure of an MIS, as in this article, success depends on the extent to which the particular values fit the organizational environment. This then explains why a system that is successful in one situation may be unacceptable in another.

The major implication of this study for organizations arises from the relative consistency in association between organizational size, organizational structure, and MIS structure. Since this consistency is found in organizations, all of which have successful MIS, it follows that different MIS structures naturally fit different organizational contexts. Organizations would be well advised to be aware of this conjunction of organizational and MIS modalities. Both organizations and implementors of MIS should know the risks they run if they attempt to challenge this relationship with organizationally inconsistent MIS structures.
