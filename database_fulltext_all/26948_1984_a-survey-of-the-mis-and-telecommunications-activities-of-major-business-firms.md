# A Survey of the MIS and Telecommunications Activities of Major Business Firms

By: Charles H. Kriebel
Diane M. Strong
Graduate School of
Industrial Administration
Carnegie-Mellon University
Schenley Park
Pittsburgh, PA 15213

## Introduction

Major changes in the field of telecommunications have been brought about by changes in the regulation of communications in the United States, and by the development of new communications technologies, such as satellite transmission, cable TV, and packet switching networks. These changes in the opportunities for corporate use of telecommunications led to an interest in assessing what corporations are actually doing with this technology and how it affects their MIS operations. With support from the local chamber of commerce and a major cable company, a telecommunications systems research project was initiated by the Graduate School of Industrial Administration at Carnegie-Mellon University. This project included an in-depth survey of major business firms in the region [5], the results of which are presented in this article.

A total of 80 firms were selected and contacted for personal interviews and completion of survey questionnaires; 48 firms (a response rate of 60%) agreed to participate in the study. A non-random sample of firms was chosen (with some stratification across industries), in order to survey those organizations most likely to be major users of telecommunications.

## Abstract

This paper presents results obtained from a survey of major business firms in a large metropolitan area done as part of a telecommunications systems research project. The goal of this study involved determining the telecommunication technologies and services currently used by business firms. The results show that firms are using intelligent terminals, satellites, microwave, local area networks, etc., and they are using these technologies for electronic mail, decision support system activities, and limited use of video teleconferencing. Information resource management in most of these firms is centralized. Hands-on use of terminals by managers and professionals does not appear as widespread as commonly publicized to date.

## Survey Results

The distribution of participating firms across industry sectors is shown in Table 1 (note that some firms participate in more than one industry). As might be expected, a majority of the firms are in the manufacturing sector. Table 2 provides data on company size measured by world-wide employees and 1981 corporate revenues.

Information on decision-making responsibilities for data processing and telecommunications tasks is provided in Table 3. Task responsibility was classified as primarily centralized (concentrated in one or a few departments), primarily decentralized, or some of both (decentralized with some centralized control). In general, decision-making appears to be centralized; for each task at least 50% of the companies are centralized. However, the management of telephone services and the selection and purchase of both word processing equipment and small business computers demonstrates some tendencies toward less centralization than the other tasks. In particular, responsibility for word processing equipment acquisition has a higher percentage of companies in both the decentralized and “some of both” categories than any other task. One explanation for more decentralization of the last three tasks is that they can be performed more easily by small groups without affecting the entire corporation. For example, each business location can manage the telephone system for its location.

All respondents have some data processing hardware. In the metropolitan area, at least two-thirds of the firms have word processing equipment; 52% have stand-alone personal or small business computers, 73% have minicomputers, 81% have mainframe computers, and 85% have “dumb” terminals.

Approximately 70% of the respondents use data communications within the metropolitan area.

Most of these firms use their data communications facilities for traditional activities such as timesharing, inquiry and retrieval, data entry, and batch and transaction processing. No firm used line speeds above 56K bps and only a few used 56K bps lines. Most companies (95%) have leased phone lines to carry their voice and data traffic, but some companies in the area are using satellite (18%), microwave (20%), or packet-switched networks (20%). Economies of scale for satellite and microwave communications generally limited the use of these technologies to corporations with a high volume of communications between two points. About one-half of the firms have or plan to obtain a local area network. Seven companies (15%) indicated that they have integrated their voice and data networks.

In the area of video communications, 18 of the 48 firms (38%) currently use or plan to use video transmission. Of these 18, three firms currently use and four firms plan to use video teleconferencing. While only one firm allocates a significant portion of its current telecommunications budget to video transmission, 16 firms (33%) indicate that video transmission will be a significant portion of their corporate budget by 1987.

On the question of video services that might be useful to employees in their city offices, 37 of the 48 firms (77%) indicated some interest. Educational materials (92%), professional seminars (89%), and financial news (51%) were most widely selected by the respondents. There was a moderate amount of interest in two-way communications with policy makers in Washington (43%), and businesses around the country (38%), as well as current business news and information (38%).

Several questions in the survey addressed uses of data communications and advanced applications development in the firm. About half of the firms consider themselves above average or leading edge with respect to linking MIS planning to corporate planning (Table 4). Approximately 80% of the firms access either internal or external databases from remote terminals. However, less than 30% of the firms indicate that their employees access the firm's computers from home terminals. Considering a less frequent application of data communications, 9 of the 48 firms (19%) use electronic mail. Only two firms said that electronic mail is a significant portion of their current telecommunications budget, but 25 firms (52%) expect electronic mail to be a significant portion of their 1987 budget.

Finally, tabulations for questions on MIS/EDP and telecommunications budgets indicate that the expected growth in telecommunications budgets is somewhat higher than the expected growth in MIS budgets. Both growth rates are concentrated in the 1 to 15% range for annual planned growth over the next five years.

## Validation

There were two questions on the survey that required more judgment on the part of the respondents. One asked about the sophistication of the firm relative to similar firms (Table 4), and the other asked about expected expenditures in 1987. There are several possible sources of bias in response to the question of sophistication. The response will depend on how much the respondent knows about what other firms are doing. Well-informed respondents may tend to rate the level of sophistication of their firms lower because they know about firms that are more sophisticated. Poorly-informed respondents may overrate their firm's level of sophistication because they are unaware of what can and has been done. Another source of bias in this question concerns which firms the respondents consider to be similar to their own. The question about expected expenditures involves judgment because it requires a forecast.

Generally, the summary tabulations are not surprising. Some of the results can be compared to the Diebold survey [2] of Fortune 500 firms. These comparisons are listed below.

1. The Diebold survey also reports that telecommunications spending is increasing at a slightly faster rate than MIS spending. Their average growth rates are a few percentage points higher than observed here, but this may be caused by the larger size of firms in their sample.

2. Voice and data networks are separate in 61% of their sample, but they are separate in 79% of this sample.

3. They report the use of higher speed data communication lines than observed here.

4. The uses of data communications, for those uses that were listed in both surveys, were similar. Diebold reported a higher use of timesharing, but a lower use of inquiry.

In summary, the data should provide for preliminary analysis, but conclusive results will probably require more testing and validation than was done with this survey. Also, any conclusions or hypotheses based on this data must consider the bias of the sample. The firms selected for the sample were those which were expected to report a significant use of MIS and telecommunications hardware.

## Observations and Interpretations

This section provides a discussion and interpretation of two interesting observations from the survey responses. These observations are interesting in the sense that they are somewhat different than expected (although not surprising) and they raise some questions which could provide a basis for further research.

## MIS planning

“Even ten years from now, however, we expect that only a few enterprises will coordinate their data processing planning with the corporate outlook and strategy” [3, p. 120].

This problem was recognized as early as 1968 by Kriebel [4] and a number of books and articles in the early and mid 1970's discussed it. For example, McLean and Soden [6] discuss the literature, the nature of the problem, and some solutions that have been used.

Given this background, it is interesting to note how data processing managers rate their company's linking of MIS planning to corporate planning. From Table 4 (item 3), we see that 32% rate their firms as leading edge, 49% rate at least above average, and 79% rate at least average. Since this is an opinion question, some interpretation of these results is required. A reasonable conclusion is that between 50% and 80% of the 48 firms link their MIS plans to corporate plans. Although this high response does not necessarily mean that the coordination problem has been resolved, it can be concluded that MIS and DP managers are aware that coordination is desirable and they are attempting to better inform one another. The data indicate that this process of linking MIS and coporate plans was initiated recently.

These ratings raise some additional questions about their accuracy and, if they are accurate, about the causes of change and the effect it will have. Since the question is an opinion question asked of data processing or MIS managers, the same question should be asked of top management and other general managers. The two sets of responses could then be compared to see if top management also feels that there is coordination between the plans.

Although the ratings given by the DP/MIS managers are probably exaggerated, some coordination is occurring. Why has this happened sooner than Dolotta et al. expected? Several answers seem plausible:

1. The current economic environment has caused firms to generally engage in more planning.

2. The top manager for MIS is at a higher level in the corporation than previously.

3. The top manager for MIS is now more likely to have a general management background than a technical background.

4. Top management regards timely and useful information as an important corporate resource and is taking steps to manage this resource.

Dolotta et al. recognized the trend of MIS managers to be at higher levels of the corporation but they did not expect this to solve the problem.

These authors believe the most likely causes are the last two. The following hypothesis summarizes this:

Firms with good coordination between MIS and corporate plans are more likely to have: (1) an MIS manager with a general management background rather than a technical data processing background, and (2) recognition by top management that information is a valuable corporate asset.

Datamation [1] reports a need for more generalists, less technical types, to become data processing managers since corporations are requiring more business planning and coordination from their data processing departments. However, it is not clear how much change has occurred in data processing management to meet these new demands.

Will this increased coordination solve the problem of slow response to corporate needs? Probably not, since quick response requires more than just coordinated planning. Adequate resources must be committed to the MIS function to support this response. A shortage of resources was mentioned as an additional problem in Dolotta et al. [3].

## Electronic mail and telecommuting

As mentioned previously, few firms are currently using electronic mail systems but more than half of the firms believe that electronic mail will be a significant part of their budget by 1987. More firms permit employees to access the corporate computers from home terminals (telecommuting) than use electronic mail (14 firms vs. 9 firms). There is a high correlation in the data between telecommuting and the use of electronic mail. This high correlation is not surprising since electronic mail facilitates communication between geographically dispersed colleagues and, conversely, may permit someone to work effectively without being at the office. For this set of data, if a firm does not use both home terminals and electronic mail, it is much more likely to only use home terminals than to only use electronic mail.

The following scenario suggests a relationship between these two items which could explain this data. Firms see telecommuting as beneficial since employees can work at home in addition to spending the usual hours at the office. A company can supply portable terminals for use while traveling as well as at home. $^{1}$ Although electronic mail may not be justifiable if everyone is in the same location, it will appear more useful when employees are accessing corporate computers from multiple locations.

This explanation can be formulated in the following hypotheses:

1. The primary justification for home terminals is to allow employees to work extra hours at home.

2. Portable terminals for traveling employees are provided primarily for computations or data entry while traveling, not for electronic mail.

3. Firms with decentralized operations will be more likely to use electronic mail than centralized firms.

4. If the use of electronic mail increases by 1987 as firms expect, the use of home terminals to access corporate computers will increase in a similar fashion.

The first two hypotheses cannot be tested with this survey data since the reasons for supplying or not supplying terminals to employees have not been explored. The last hypothesis cannot be tested since it is a forecast. The survey does have some questions concerning decentralization relevant to the third hypothesis. For the tasks of selecting word processing equipment and small business computers, companies who have decentralized these tasks are equally divided between use and non-use of electronic mail. When these tasks are centralized, most do not use electronic mail. Since few companies are decentralized, it is difficult to draw strong conclusions from this data.

## Conclusions

The survey results indicate that leading edge firms are moving toward a distributed environment, although not as quickly as some have predicted. Many firms have some network facilities and are accessing internal or external databases.

However, use of terminals by managers and professionals is limited, electronic mail systems are not common, and most decision-making is still centralized. There does seem to be a realization by the survey respondents that a more distributed environment is an appropriate goal. The fact that this goal has not been reached is probably due to the major centralized systems already in place which cannot be easily replaced. Therefore, new applications can be designed for a more distributed environment, but the older ones will continue to be centralized.

This situation provides opportunities for firms supplying software to telecommunications networks. Since MIS organizations often have a large backlog of development projects, and their staff may not be familiar with development for a distributed environment, firms may be more willing to purchase telecommunications services. One firm indicated that they are using an electronic mail system on a commercial network because there was no development delay and compatibility between locations was assured.

Suppliers of telecommunications facilities should offer some value-added services in addition to the hardware. Most companies interviewed seemed satisfied with the reliability and availability of the circuits provided by Bell Telephone, although cost is an important consideration. Those who were not using Bell, who employed their own means of communicating, showed little interest in a vendor offering merely connection services.

One example of such a value-added service would be an electronic mail system. Over 50% of the companies answering the survey expect electronic mail to comprise a significant portion of their telecommunications budget in the next five years. In order for such a system to work effectively, a means of transmitting as well as storing messages must be accessible and well maintained. Most firms were concerned about intercity connections. Another example of a value-added service concerns video transmission. Over 75% of the companies showed interest in receiving video services. The services most cited were educational materials, professional seminars, and financial news.

In general, we expect more competition and a greater variety of services from suppliers of telecommunications services. Packet-switched networks, satellite communications, microwave, and local-area networks are now in place. Cable companies are beginning to provide institutional networks for voice, data, and video transmission. Cable presents an exciting possibility for sales and market research if firms can access the home cable systems. This possibility is becoming more likely as institutional cable systems are installed and as home cable systems become two-way rather than “receive only.” For firms using these telecommunications services, more planning and coordination is required. More coordination between departments responsible for voice, data, and video communications should help reduce communications costs.
