## Abstract

Twenty information resource managers employed by medium to large organizations in diverse industries were interviewed to discover their difficulties in learning top management's objectives and their techniques for overcoming these difficulties. The thirteen difficulties can be viewed as strategy formulation or strategy communication problems. The sixteen techniques can likewise be viewed as facilitating strategy formulation or strategy communication. The research revealed that participants in strategy formulation have no problem understanding top management's objectives while information resource managers who do not participate in strategy formulation do experience that problem.

Keywords: Planning, information systems, information management

ACM Categories: K.6.0, K.6.4

The authors contributed equally to the design and execution of this research project and paper.

Corporate management is becoming increasingly aware of the potential contribution of information, both to the execution of the organization's strategies and to its bottom line. Information is not only a basis for operational decision making, but also a valuable strategic resource in its own right. Indeed, information systems are “no longer handmaidens to the back office (and) are fast becoming indispensable allies in marketing, customer service, product development, human resource management, strategic planning, and many other jobs” [6, p. 108]. For this integration to happen successfully, information systems managers must be aware of top management's objectives. This will enable these managers to ensure that the contribution of the information resource is maximized in the organization.

In 1984, Dickson, et al., [5] reported that improved information systems planning was the key issue facing information professionals. In a more recent investigation into the challenges of information resource planning, Lederer and Mendelow [10] found that a group of senior IS managers almost unanimously identified the ability to know top management's objectives as the greatest difficulty they faced in developing information systems plans. This article reports the results of a later phase of that research. The study was designed to identify the problems encountered by senior IS managers as they attempted to learn the objectives of their top management. The study also sought to identify the techniques used by senior IS managers to overcome the difficulties.

This article will review the growing importance of information in organizations and the need to integrate the objectives and strategy of the organization with those of the information function. The research methodology section will then discuss sample considerations and the structured interview process which was used to gather data. Following this, the results will be presented. The findings indicate that it is useful to view the difficulties as roadblocks to the formulation of the IS plan or to the communication of that plan. Several techniques for overcoming these difficulties will be outlined and the implications of these findings for research and practice will be discussed.

## Background

The ability to use information systems to “creatively collect, make sense of and distribute information is already spelling the difference between success and mediocrity” in diverse industries $[6, p. 109]$ . However, there is evidence that many organizations experience difficulty in realizing the full potential of the information resource $[3]$ . Reports abound of systems implemented late and over budget with the anticipated savings being unrealized. This state of affairs qualifies the deployment of information resources as a general management issue rather than a technical problem to be addressed exclusively by IS managers.

Information must be considered in the same manner as other resources of the organization and managed to fulfill the overall needs of the organization. To do so, there are three prerequisites. First, general managers must define the role of information in their organizations and ensure that this role is formally reflected in their strategies and plans. Second, IS professionals must develop a functional plan for the deployment of the information resource. And third, that plan must be integrated into the organization's plan.

The need to integrate and link IS plans with those of the organization as a whole has been recognized by several authors. McLean and Soden [13] clearly identified this connection in their model of information systems planning. King [9] explicitly noted that linkage by pointing out the need for the IS strategy to reflect alterations in the organization's strategy. More recent work on competitive advantage not only emphasizes the need for this linkage, but also suggests that the IS plan be part of the organization's strategic plan [3, 8, 15, 18]. However, this integration has not often been accomplished [2].

McFarlan [11] pointed to the overall objectives of the company as a useful starting point for achieving at least some of this integration. He found that the information systems plan was most useful when its developers, the IS professionals, were made explicitly aware of the overall objectives of the company. In order to stress this link, he suggested that the information systems plan include a statement of the consistency of IS goals with corporate goals.

In the context of the organization in general, Richards [16], proposed that the objectives for each business unit be developed to clarify how that unit will contribute to the organization's overall goals. Alexander [1] found strategic decisions were implemented more successfully when employees understood the goals behind them. Thus it would seem imperative that the goals of the organization be known by management throughout the organization.

During an investigation of planning difficulties confronting IS managers, Lederer and Mendelow [10] found lack of information on top management's objectives to be the most difficult issue confronting senior IS managers. In the ranking process of the Nominal Group Technique, eight out of nine managers identified this issue as the most important among the 26 possible choices which the group generated. The ninth manager identified it as the second most important problem. This almost unanimous vote instigated the current research. The investigation reported here sought to answer the research question: "Why is it difficult for IS managers to determine top management's objectives and what techniques are employed to alleviate this difficulty?"

## Research Methodology

The nature of the research question is such that the difficulties which IS managers experience in identifying top management's objectives could be related to both the IS function and to general management. Thus it was inappropriate to draw up a questionnaire based solely on general management literature related to identifying objectives. Furthermore, given the absence of prior empirical work on this research problem, a “direct” research approach involving a structured interview seemed the most appropriate precursor to a more conventional study relying on a larger sample and a survey methodology $[14]$ . The results of the interviews are reported in this paper.

## Sample selection

Since the focus of the research is primarily on the IS function, senior managers with the ultimate responsibility for planning and managing their organizations' information resources were sought for interviews. They were selected to include medium to large organizations in a wide variety of industries. Interview appointments were set up by telephone. All managers called agreed to participate, indicating a great interest in the problem. Table 1 contains profiles of the twenty participating organizations.

## Interview details

The interviews with the IS executives lasted about 3 hours. Subjects were promised anonymity. Although a written agenda guided the discussion of a wide variety of IS planning problems, techniques and philosophies, the following questions specifically related to this paper were asked:

\- Is determining top management's objectives a difficulty that you have experienced?
If so, why?
If not, why not?

If not, why not?

\- What do you do (or have you done) to overcome this difficulty?

All interviews were conducted by both co-authors of this paper. Although each interviewer specialized in one portion of the questionnaire, the other was free to interrupt, seek clarification, and probe further during the course of the discussion. Both interviewers took extensive notes of the entire interview and transcribed them as soon as possible (usually within two days). With the subjects' permission, all but the first interview were recorded on tape, and the tape was used to resolve any discrepancies during transcription.

In 19 of the 20 interviews, the subject was the firm's top IS manager. ## Results

The transcripts of the interviews were studied to identify the roadblocks facing IS managers as they attempted to learn top management's objectives. Several interviewees gave more than one response. The responses were collated into the thirteen difficulties shown in Table 2. The techniques which the interviewees advocated to overcome these problems are listed in Table 3. The low number of interviewees mentioning each difficulty or technique is attributable to the fact that the responses of the interviewees were not prompted with any suggestions. Because the aim of this research was to record managers' views, even reasons and techniques mentioned by only one manager are reported. Examination of interview transcripts suggested that being part of the planning process was a common thread of those who did not experience any problems. ## Discussion

Examination of Table 2 reveals that problems could be categorized under two broad themes — the formulation of the plan and the communication of the plan. These categories appear to account for the reasons that IS managers experience problems in knowing top management's objectives.

## Formulation difficulties

One of the most commonly recurring themes was that, at least in the eyes of the senior IS manager, the organization did not have a formal strategic plan (D1 from Table 2). One interviewee attributed this to poor management and to the fact that the business situation changed so rapidly that a plan would be outdated as soon as it was completed. Clearly, it would be impossible to know top management's objectives if top management themselves didn't know them. Steiner, after studying companies of various sizes, pointed out that some managers rejected formal planning because the organization had been successful without it and thus they saw no need to develop a formal plan [17]. In addition, he suggested that "top management (may) reject the formal planning mechanism by making intuitive decisions which conflict with the formal plans" [17, p. 297]. The lack of a formal strategic plan can, however, have a serious effect on the ability of the units in the organization to strive toward a mutual goal [16].

Five interviewees stated that business objectives change (D2) and that in the IS department they were unaware of the change. Another difficulty is the need to keep abreast of corporate developments. This does not always take place within IS because the senior IS manager is often not a part of the strategy formulation body (D3), due, in part, to the fact that he often reports to a functional head rather than to the president or CEO.

## Communication difficulties

The communication difficulties IS management encounter can be summed up, as one interviewee stated quite simply, “They don’t tell us” (D6). This supports Steiner’s [17] finding that top management frequently fails to review the organization’s plans with department heads. This may be related to the fact that top management simply does not have a plan. However, two other interviewees suggested that top management must maintain confidentiality (D9) to ensure that competitors do not learn of its plans. Another interviewee pointed out that the request for objectives is “instantly threatening to the person (D13). The human animal wants to keep options open and does not want to be pinned down.” Thus, flexibility of action is retained when objectives and plans are not disclosed. Top management may fear loss of control and yet may not achieve the potential return unless they give up some control.

Six interviewees indicated that top management lacked an understanding of the IS function (D4). However, several also pointed out that younger managers seem to have a greater understanding, perhaps due to increasing use of computers at seminars and in universities and widespread use of personal computers. Top management is, nevertheless, still intimidated by automation (D10). One of the interviewees pointed out that top managers are accustomed to a feeling of mastery of their immediate surroundings—after all, they were promoted to their position due to their ability to perform their jobs well. Now, top managers do not understand the new technology. They fear a loss of control and, thus, tend to avoid it.

Another communication difficulty arises because top management fails to communicate corporate objectives in a way to which IS personnel can relate (D5). For example, top management told one interviewee that the organization's major objective was to increase sales by a given percentage and that IS should provide systems to help do so. This provided little substantive direction as to what specific systems to develop.

Another interviewee mentioned that because IS experts are unable to relate to corporate objectives, they exhibit a general lack of receptivity to any communication from top management (D11). Small wonder then that two interviewees pointed out that top management and IS management tend to view the organization's objectives from two different perspectives (D7). In the light of all this, it is readily understandable why two other interviewees indicated that IR managers were viewed as "techies," people who have no feel for general business problems (D8).

One final communication difficulty was mentioned. An IS executive at the corporate level observed that the individual business units fail to communicate their plans and objectives to his corporate IS group (D12). This could have serious implications where information systems are managed as a corporate function, since policies must be set and resources allocated in a manner congruent with the business unit plans.

## Suggested improvements

Overcoming the difficulties in Table 2 is the responsibility of IS management. Indeed, several interviewees confirmed this view by stating that, as IS managers, they had failed to take the time to explain, educate and sell top management on the role of IS in their organizations. Some IS managers actively accept responsibility for the difficulties and have developed techniques by which they hope to overcome them.

During the interviews, participants identified techniques which they use to overcome these difficulties. Examination of Table 3 reveals that the techniques may be broadly classified into two themes. Generally, one deals with the formulation of the organization's strategic plan and the other theme deals with the communication of the plan after it has been formulated. Although the formulation techniques help overcome both the formulation and communication difficulties, the communication techniques address only the communication difficulties. The third column in Table 3 shows the difficulties listed in Table 2 which each technique ameliorates.

## Formulation Techniques

Six interviewees felt that active participation in the corporate planning process (T1 in Table 3) helps IS management to overcome the problem of understanding top management's objectives (D3). They would be aware of changing business objectives (D2). In addition, since IS management would have helped create corporate objectives, the confidentiality issue (D9) would have already been resolved. Top management would not need to formally tell IS management about its objectives (D6) and IS management would not need to make requests which appear threatening (D13). Different interpretations of top management's objectives (D7) would be ironed out during planning and the business units objectives (D12) would be considered. Also, such participation would reduce the view of IS personnel as "techies" (D8) and increase IS receptivity to top management's objectives (D11).

According to six interviewees, reporting to the president (T2) was a useful technique to overcome the problem of learning top management's objectives. This proximity to top management would increase the likelihood that IS management might participate in strategy formulation. It could also result in better formal and informal communication, making IS executives more aware of changing business objectives (D2) because it would be easier for top management to tell IS managers about objectives (D6). In addition, top management would feel less inclined to maintain confidentiality (D9) because the IS manager would be seen as part of the top management team, and thus automatically be apprised of business unit objectives (D12). IS management would not need to make threatening requests of top management for its objectives (D13). Finally, reporting directly to the president would help ensure the accurate interpretation of corporate objectives (D7) by both IS and top management.

Two interviewees indicated that IS managers should be prepared to propose that top management develop a formal strategic plan (T3) should one not exist (D1). One interviewee from a smaller company suggested that IS managers lack the confidence to confront top management and firmly state that unless a strategic plan for the organization exists, the IS department cannot be of any help in achieving the organization's strategic goals. The same interviewee stated that he was primarily responsible for convincing his top management to undertake the formal development of their company's strategy. This development was occurring at the time of our interview.

## Communication Techniques

Five interviewees mentioned the technique of listening and reviewing corporate plans while passing on appropriate reports to the IS area (T4) as a useful way to learn top management's objectives. If IS managers were not actively participating in the formulation of the organization's objectives, this technique would at least help keep them informed of changing business objectives (D2) and of business unit objectives (D12). It also circumvents the need to make requests which might appear threatening (D13). Finally, it helps compensate for top management's failure to directly tell IS managers about the organization's objectives (D6).

Formal education (T5; e.g., seminars, presentations and courses) was suggested by five interviewees. Five different interviewees suggested that informal education (T6; i.e., person-to-person communication) would be useful. In general, both formal and informal education would improve top management's IS understanding (D4) and would help top management communicate its objectives in IS terms (D5). In addition, top management might become less intimidated by automation (D10) and develop a greater appreciation of IS management issues. This could evoke top management sympathy for the problems of IS professionals and thus reduce their “techie” image (D8).

Having an IS plan (T7), proposed by two interviewees, might promote the image of IS managers as professional business managers. As such, it helps overcome general management's view of IS personnel as "techies" (D8). It also reduces the intimidation of automation (D10) by expressing the critical elements of the IS plan in business terms. The IS plan reveals IS objectives to top management and thus highlights different interpretations of the organization's objectives (D7) before the implementation of plans to achieve the objectives.

Hiring a consultant (T8), also proposed by two interviewees, directly responds to the failure of top management to identify its objectives (D6) and to the failure of business units to inform IS management about their objectives (D12). An outsider is a catalyst whose questions may appear less threatening (D13) than those from IS managers.

Although steering committees are important and popular, surprisingly only one interviewee suggested that a corporate management committee to approve IS projects (T9)

might be a useful technique. It compensates for top management's lack of IS understanding (D4) and lack of communication in IS terms (D5). This committees' decisions on resource allocation would reflect top management's objectives and would help to reduce the problems caused by changing business objectives (D2), confidentiality (D9), lack of business unit objectives (D12), and threatening requests for objectives (D13).

Setting up informal links with top management (T10) and staying strategically tuned (T11) compensate somewhat for the lack of a formal business plan (D1) and the absence of IS management from the strategy formulation body (D3). The informal links encourage both top management and business unit management to tell IS management directly about their objectives (D6 and D12) or about changes in their objectives (D2), even though these might be confidential (D9). Moreover, the informality reduces the threat of direct questions about objectives (D13).

Delivering systems on time and within budget (T13) and refraining from using technical jargon (T14) help overcome the “techie” image (D8) and promote an image of responsible business planners.

The final techniques, automate constant functions (T15) and roll with the punches (T15), avoid the issue. The automation of constant functions supports ongoing and unchanging business processes which do not require knowledge of top management objectives.

## No problems for some executives

It was interesting that of the twenty IS executives interviewed, four stated outright that they did not have any problem in understanding top management's objectives. This prompted a search of the interview transcripts for a pattern identifying the techniques, which if practiced, would explain why those interviewees had no problem understanding top management's objectives while others did.

For example, the researchers investigated whether the use of steering committees (T9) distinguished organizations who had the problem of not knowing top management's objectives from those who did not have the problem. However, all companies had steering committees. Hence it did not differentiate. Likewise, reporting to the president (T2) was not a predictor.

The analysis revealed that all four of the interviewees without the problem were direct and active participants in the formulation of their firms' strategies, while none of the sixteen with the problem were so involved. In addition, two interviewees suggested this active participation as a technique but were unable to implement it and continued to experience problems in knowing top management's objectives. This hypothesis was rejected at a statistically significant level using a Chi square test with Yates correction (see Figure 1). This suggests that making the IS manager a part of the corporate strategy formulation process is one cure of the problem. It confirms a widely held view that IS managers should be a part of the corporate strategy formulation process [12, 13]. A closer link between strategic and MIS planning is held to result in increased effectiveness [4]. Conversely, the other techniques may merely be palliatives.

It is likewise interesting to note that there are several information systems planning methodologies aimed at linking the IS and the corporate strategic plan, of which IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP) is probably the best known [7]. However, these methodologies merely attempt to incorporate top management's objectives into the IS plan once the objectives are established for the organization as a whole. This strongly contrasts with the situation presented by the four interviewees actually involved in the corporate strategy formulation process. These four interviewees are also, then, responsible for planning the utilization of information resources.

## Conclusions

Information affects the organization's ability to execute its strategy. A positive contribution arises when the optimum use of information gives the organization a competitive edge. Information can help reduce costs, open new markets, or even create new products, thus increasing profits and strengthening the organization's long-term viability.

Negative impacts occur when IS projects are not consistent with the strategy of the organization. In this case, scarce organizational resources are diverted from activities which may make a favorable contribution to the firm.

In order to enhance the positive contribution and reduce the negative impact of IS, senior IS managers must know the objectives of top management. This enables them to apply their expertise to suggest ideas for the strategic deployment of information within the organization. The current study reveals thirteen difficulties which hamper IS managers' efforts to obtain this knowledge. IS managers might peruse this list of difficulties to evaluate the extent to which they are experiencing each difficulty. The techniques discussed in this article may serve as a plan of action to address the most pressing difficulties. They can pursue this plan with greater confidence because they know that their difficulties are not unique.

An examination of the techniques indicates that, for the most part, their implementation requires an enormous marketing effort to convince top management of the value of the information resource to the organization and of the necessity for IS management to be aware of top management's objectives. Moreover, this research highlights the need for IS managers to take the responsibility for solving their problems by applying the various techniques. For example, it might require a considerable marketing effort to convince a company president to restructure reporting relationships so that the IS executive reports directly to him/her. Still, these techniques are only palliatives—necessary conditions, but generally insufficient.

One technique—making the senior IS manager a member of the strategy formulation body—directly addresses the problem. This technique was distinguished from all other techniques by its ability to predict organizations not experiencing problems.

Still, whether palliative or cure, all techniques appear to require continuous efforts on the part of IS management. Moreover, they seem to require IS managers to shed their “technical guru” image and become “company-smart” in order to become candidates for the senior management team. This will maximize the positive contribution of information to company profits and minimize its negative impact of diverting resources from more critical areas.

For academics, this project has opened the way for additional research to discover more about the difficulties which IS managers experience as they attempt to learn top management's objectives. The findings reported here should be validated using conventional methodologies, including a larger sample size and more explicit forced-choice questionnaires. From this data, it might be possible to identify the variables associated with different difficulties of understanding top management's objectives and their magnitude in order to answer such questions as:

1. Do larger organizations have different difficulties with different magnitudes than smaller organizations?

2. Do larger IS departments have different difficulties with different magnitudes than smaller IS departments?

3. Do difficulties differ across industries?

4. Do the personal and technical skills of the IS manager result in different difficulties with different magnitudes than other skills?

5. Do IS managers with functional area experience have different difficulties with different magnitudes than IS managers with exclusively IS experience?

Answers to these and similar questions could provide an understanding of the circumstances under which various problems occur in order to develop a theory of IS planning.

In addition, similar questions about techniques used to overcome the difficulties might be asked. A contingency approach might result. It would eventually allow practitioners to select the appropriate technique for a given organizational setting and difficulty. Using this knowledge, it might be possible to reduce or eliminate some of the difficulties more efficiently.

Finally, it might also be revealing to obtain the views of CEOs as to whether and why the IS managers in their organizations are experiencing problems learning top management's objectives. Contrasting the views of CEOs and IS managers within the same organization might reveal new insights.
