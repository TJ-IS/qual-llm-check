## Abstract

While businesses are investing enormous resources in information technology (IT), there is little evidence linking IT investment to organizational performance. The purpose of this article, therefore, is to increase understanding of the basis for IT investment in firms. Six mini case studies of companies in five different industries address questions of how they define IT for the purpose of determining the level of investment, how they track IT investments, and what other factors influence IT investment decisions.

Each organization uses a different definition of IT, but there appears to be an overall trend to broaden the definition. Although companies track IT investment with varying degrees of rigor, they appear to be generally moving toward centralized tracking of all IT investment. Political considerations are important and significantly impact investment decisions. In all cases, the effectiveness with which IT investment is converted to useful output is acknowledged to be affected by the implementation process, the culture of the organization, and the skill of management.

Three major implications for practitioners responsible for IT investment are: the need to adopt a broad definition of IT and track it over time against a convenient base; the need to separate different types of investment and match them to appropriate organizational performance measures; and the need to take into account factors such as management commitment and previous experience with IT. The latter impacts the effectiveness with which the firm converts it investment into useful outputs.

Keywords: Information technology investment, organizational performance, cost-benefit analysis, information technology strategy

## Introduction

PRESIDENT: I have been struggling with how heavily this company should invest in IT.

CONSULTANT: Clearly it all depends on your strategy and associated niche, competitors, environmental turbulence and industry structure, technological profile of your firm, level of differentiation and vertical integration, and, of course, how you feel about it.

PRESIDENT: Really?

Businesses are investing enormous resources in information technology (IT). A recent study (PIMS Program, 1984; Strassmann, 1985) finds that the average information technology expenditure in 1983 was 2 percent of revenues. Another survey (Diebold Group, 1982; 1984) finds that centralized management information systems (MIS) expenditures on average accounted for 1.44 percent of revenues in 1984. For a Fortune 500 firm with average revenues of \$5 billion, this expenditure amounts to \$100 million per year. Even more dramatic, two studies demonstrate that high-performing companies allocate a significantly greater proportion of revenues to IT expenditures than companies with lower performance (PIMS Program, 1984; Harris and Katz, 1988).

While it is tempting to conclude that so much IT investment increases organizational performance, there is little evidence that this is so. Frequently, the only spending guidelines managers have for IT investment are the spending levels of competing firms in their industry (based on percentage of revenues), or a flat increment on last year's IT budget. Unfortunately, therefore, much of this investment is based on blind faith that real returns will occur. Conversations such as the one reported above illustrate the quandary many managers face. Information technology is essential to their businesses, but inadequate guidelines exist for determining the appropriate level of investment.

The purpose of this article is to increase understanding of the basis for IT investment. We have performed six mini case studies of large companies in five different industries, addressing the questions of what firms define as IT and how they manage their investment in IT. Our goal was to gain insight into the management of IT investment and the relationship between IT investment and organizational performance. The cases led to a series of implications and recommendations for practitioners responsible for investment decisions.

## The Problem

The general problem is: What is the impact of the level of IT investment on firm performance? This is a complex issue, as IT investment is only one of the many factors that affect firm performance (Lucas, 1975; Turner, 1985). The crux of the problem is whether investment in IT really makes a difference. This problem raises the following related questions:

1. Is there a significant, unique effect of investment in IT on firm performance?

2. What is the nature of the relationship between the level of IT investment and firm performance? Does increased investment lead to increased performance? Is there a level of investment that produces an optimum level of firm performance, after which further investment has a negative effect (Bender, 1986)? Alternatively, is there a diminishing return, where increased IT investment produces decreasing but still positive effects on firm performance?

3. Is the relationship between levels of IT investment and firm performance dependent on the industry in which the firm operates?

4. Is there a threshold investment that firms must make to survive?

5. Does the strategic reason for investing in IT make a difference? What effect does the objective for investment have on firm performance?

6. Do other factors that affect firm performance overwhelm the effects of IT investment levels?

7. Is it possible to predict the effect of a particular IT investment level on performance for a given firm?

While we do not claim to have answers to all these difficult questions, they are the basis for another major research study we currently are conducting. The purpose of this article, however, is to examine how practitioners responsible for IT strategic planning approach the investment decision. We report the results of six mini case studies, during which we attempted to answer the following specific questions:

1. How do firms define and conceptualize IT?

2. How do firms measure and track or otherwise manage their investments in IT?

3. How do firms infer or calculate return on IT investment?

4. Do firms in different industries approach IT investment differently?

5. What is the impetus for IT investment? At what level in the organization does the impetus originate? Is it top-down (driven by corporate strategy at the highest level), or is it bottom-up (driven by operational requirements)?

## What is IT?

Each study uses a different definition of IT, making comparison of results difficult. Some studies only include management information systems and related personnel, consulting, external services, and personal computing expenditures within the centralized MIS budget (Datamation, 1985; 1986; Cahners Publishing, 1987; Diebold Group, 1982; 1984; Strassmann, 1985; Turner, 1985). One study broadens the definition to include expenditures for communications

With the recent noticeable trend toward decentralization of expenditure in IT to functional departments, considering IT investment as equivalent to the centralized MIS budget is probably inadequate. However, since these decentralized expenditures are generally embedded within the functional department's operating budget, they are more difficult for surveys to identify. As a consequence, most surveys underestimate firm-wide IT investment.

## Measures of IT investment

Studies have used a variety of measures of the levels of investment in IT.

In its biennial surveys of major corporations over the last ten years, The Diebold Group (1982, 1984) consistently uses three measures:

\- MIS budget as a percentage of revenues

\- MIS staff as a percentage of total staff

\- Ratio of hardware expenditures to personnel costs

Datamation regularly conducts a survey of the magazine's circulation base and the Fortune 1000 and 400 firms to assess centralized MIS investment (Datamation, 1985; 1986; Cahners Publishing, 1987). The measure used is MIS budget as a percentage of revenues. In a similar approach, Bender (1986) uses the ratio of IT investment to premium income in the insurance industry. Strassmann (1985) refines the measure to be the fraction of management costs devoted to IT. He believes that the base of management costs is a more appropriate measure than revenues, since it is more directly influenced by management decisions.

Several studies rely on substitute measures rather than absolute investment dollars for comparison of firms within an industry. Cron and Sobol (1983) use three substitute measures:

\- Computer ownership: owned/leased vs. timeshare

\- Number of standard application areas computerized

\- Types of application areas computerized (accounting, management control, or environmental links)

In a study of the banking industry, Turner (1985) uses a number of measures, including “relative DP expenditures” (ratio of DP expenditures to total assets) and the number of standard functional areas having computerized applications.

All of these approaches have advantages and disadvantages. It is doubtful that any of them captures all of a given firm's investment in IT due to the increasing decentralization of IT investment and increasing ad hoc expenditures on IT (e.g., personal computers) from non-IT operational budgets.

In the 1986 Datamation survey, the MIS budget as a percentage of revenues for all industry groups combined was 0.57 percent (Datamation, 1985; 1986; Cahners Publishing, 1987). This figure is statistically biased (due to the large number of small sites) and underestimates MIS expenditures in large organizations. The actual figure for large MIS centers is more likely around .9 percent to 1.0 percent. $^{1}$ The Diebold (1982; 1984) survey showed average IT expenditures to be 1.44 percent of revenues and to have grown steadily over the six years represented by its surveys. Strassmann (1984) found that the average IT expenditure in 1983 was 2 percent of revenues. In the government sector, IT is a significant expense, which in 1982 was 1.2 percent of the federal budget and increased steadily to 1.6 percent in 1986 (U.S. Office of Management and Budget, 1985). In 1986, half this expenditure was on outside commercial IT expenses, such as computer service bureaus. Panko (1982) finds that, on average, firm IT investment was 3,000 per year per information worker.

The disparity in the results reflects the differences in defining and measuring IT investment. Comparison of these broadly defined investment levels among companies (particularly across different industries) is useful only as a rough guide. The macro investment percentages are taken out of context of firm strategy. In addition, the ability of a particular management team to effectively convert the investments into useful outputs (i.e., improved organizational performance) is not considered.

## Measures of performance

The determination of performance is highly problematic in this type of work. A variety of social and economic measures can be adopted (Zammuto, 1982). Many combinations have been used, often with more regard to convenience than appropriateness.

One view is to establish a single global measure of performance. In a study of the insurance industry, Bender (1986) uses the single measure of the ratio of expenses over premium income. Another view is that one measure of performance will not capture all factors that contribute to high performance, and, consequently, the use of any single measure is relatively arbitrary (Turner, 1985).

In a study of the impact of IT investment on financial performance for a group of wholesalers, Cron and Sobol (1986) use four measures:

\- Pretax profits

\- Return on assets

\- Return on net worth

\- Five-year growth rates

Other performance measures include market share (PIMS Program, 1984) and the ratio of net operating income to total asset base (Turner, 1985). Given the complexity of organizational performance, it is clear that no single criterion is sufficient and that a number of measures is needed (Zammuto, 1982).

## Relationships between IT investment and performance

A comparison of the results from this research demonstrates some of the difficulties of assessing IT investment and performance.

In his study of 58 savings banks, Turner (1985) concludes that “unexpectedly no relationship is found between organizational performance and the relative proportion of resources allocated to data processing” (p. 1).

Bender (1986) divides IT investment into components and finds only the expenditures on people, hardware, and environment to be significantly related to performance. In his study, investment in software is not significant. He concludes that in the insurance industry there is an optimum level of investment in information processing. Firms that invested below this optimum had lower performance, and firms that invested above this optimum added to costs unnecessarily. However, Bender assumed that all firms convert the investment in IT into measurable performance with the same degree of effectiveness. In reality, some firms are more likely to be effective at this conversion that others; thus the assumption of an industry-wide optimum level of investment is questionable.

Cron and Sobol (1983) find that firms in the warehousing industry that made extensive use of computers were either very strong or very weak financial performers. This finding appears to support the strategic planning literature that stresses the importance of strategic position and concurs with findings from PIMS (1984). The PIMS study finds that of the firms that increased IT investments annually, those with superior strategic position increased management productivity three times more than firms with inferior strategic position (PIMS, 1984; p. 35). The study concludes that if the firm has a strong strategic position, investment in IT will enhance performance. If the firm has a weak strategic position, significant investment in IT will be less effective.

A four-year study of 40 insurance companies (Harris and Katz, 1988) reveals that the most profitable firms are more likely to spend a significantly higher proportion of their non-interest operating expenses on IT. Any statement of causality in the study was not possible, leaving the question open of whether higher investment causes higher performance.

The findings reported above are sparse and somewhat contradictory. Where a relationship between IT investment and performance is found, the results cannot be generalized beyond the particular industry studied. The methods of measuring investment and performance and the units of analysis differ, limiting possible comparison across studies. The studies measuring both IT investment and performance ignore the time lag between the two.

A further weakness of these studies is that, in each, no distinction is made among different types of IT investment. Investments in operational IT (i.e., transaction processing and operational control systems) are intended to impact different aspects of firm performance than investments applied to systems for strategic competitive advantage (i.e., new products or services, executive information systems, etc.).

These conflicting findings are of little joy to practitioners faced with daily IT investment decisions. In the next section, we describe a series of six case studies to derive a series of recommendations for managers concerned with IT investment and organizational performance.

## Case Study Results

We conducted six mini case studies to help us understand how firms define and manage their IT investments. Five of the companies are large, profit-making firms operating in four different industries. The sixth is a large private university. We conducted one lengthy, semi-structured interview with the appropriate representative of each organization. All interviewees are senior managers in corporate planning or information systems. All the interviewees have general knowledge of the IT investment of the entire organization. The interviewees were frank and open; we have made every attempt to conceal the identity of the organizations and any sensitive information that was discussed.

The primary questions were the following:

\- How do you define IT for the purposes of determining the level of investment?

\- How do you manage and track investments in IT?

\- Where in the firm is the impetus for IT investment (i.e., top-down or bottom-up)?

\- What other factors influence IT investment decisions (e.g., political considerations, competitors' strategies)?

The results of the cases are presented in two stages. This section concentrates on a brief presentation of the demographics and interesting findings in each company. In the next section, we present a brief aggregate analysis of all cases, organized by the key factors emerging from the interviews.

## Organization 1 — insurance

Organization 1 is a large insurance company with three major businesses: individual insurance, group insurance, and investment. The interviewee is manager of corporate technology planning. The company performs an inventory of all IT investment each year. The inventory includes all DP center expenditures, communications (excluding voice-based technology), personal computers, software, education, and consultants. The definition does not include consumables (such as paper, diskettes, etc.) and full-time computer operators. The company is decentralizing rapidly, and the management of IT investment is also becoming more decentralized. A steering committee oversees major investments. Return on investment analysis is used to justify investment where ROI is considered appropriate; however, there have been some cases where investment was driven by a need to stay with the competition or by “gut feeling.” There is a general perception that, in the industry, which is of course highly information intensive, a certain threshold investment is required to be competitive.

Historically, IT investment decisions at the firm were “less political, as there were fewer experts,” but the interviewee noted a definite increase in the political nature of the process. Approximately 14 percent of the total budget is devoted to IT investment. The impetus to invest is both “bottom-up and top-down,” and the intention is that corporate strategy should drive investment in IT. However, the competition also drives IT investment, and IT also impacts corporate strategy; thus there is a complex circular relationship between IT investment and corporate strategy.

The interviewee believes that the MIS function is becoming more complex and sophisticated, but he had not observed any attempt to increase management control of this function at a higher management level. He perceives the issue of converting IT investment into useful output as "just another example of the familiar problem of resource management" and observes that some organizations are more skilled at this task than others.

## Organization 2 — banking

Organization 2 is a large, geographically dispersed commercial bank. The interviewee is director of corporate systems. The bank includes in its working definition of IT “everything related to IT.” According to the interviewee, the bank is “getting carried away” with its investment in IT. Individual projects are assessed using ROI techniques, but there is no notion of an overall return on IT investment. The bank tracks IT investment at both the project and corporate levels; multiple measures are used because no simple measure is considered “comfortable” in terms of accuracy. Some divisions are considerably more dependent on intensive information technology than others, but the bank attempts to estimate company-wide IT investment.

Historically, the impetus to invest was bottom-up. According to the interviewee, the bank views information as the product of the organization; it makes a distinction between administrative IT (e.g., MIS and office automation) and IT for productive capacity (e.g., transaction processing and general ledger).

The interviewee had not observed an increase in the political nature of IT investment; however, there have been attempts to change organizational power structures by shifting responsibility for the management of the IT resource.

## Organization 3 — insurance

Organizaton 3 is a large, diversified insurance group. The interviewee is the manager of capacity planning in corporate administration. The company defines corporate investment in IT as only those expenditures in the corporate administration information systems budget and includes all data centers and communications. In 1987, this expenditure was approximately \$270 million. This does not include divisional expenditures for information systems, including personal computers, programmers, and some minicomputers. The latter expenditure was estimated to be at least as large as the corporate budget expenditure. The company has begun to decentralize its IT management and budgets, in line with perceived trends of its competition.

The interviewee was unaware of any formal tracking of the IT resource; he understood that there was a rough estimate of investment by the president's staff for the entire organization. The company justifies IT projects by traditional ROI measures but emphasizes cost containment. There is some “seed money” available for experimenting with the use of new IT, but it is very limited. The interviewee does not believe there is a common perception of a threshold investment in IT in the industry.

The nature of IT investment decisions in the company is highly political; according to the interviewee, the “technological aspects are secondary.” The decision is usually a function of the “clout of the division based on revenue generation.” Divisions with poor performance have a harder time obtaining resources. This could be due to political power of a division or to a “standard” guideline of percent of last year’s revenues to determine this year’s divisional IT budgets. The impetus for investment is purely bottom-up.

There is a long-term IT strategy, but it is not well integrated into overall corporate planning. There are separate departments for IT planning and corporate planning. There is an organization-wide attempt to “keep IT investment flat, as it is difficult to understand the return on investment phenomenon.”

## Organization 4 — consumer products

Organization 4 is a large, multi-national conglomerate in the consumer products industry. The interviewee is manager of corporate information resources planning.

According to the interviewee, the company makes no attempt to aggregate or manage IT investment at the corporate level; this process is the responsibility of the individual divisions. Within the context of planning, the definition of IT includes MIS-related expenditures as well as all communications expenditures excluding voice, facsimile and telex. IT also includes expenditures on personal computers and external consultants. All IT investment directly related to manufacturing capacity is excluded.

The interviewee believes there is an optimal level of IT investment that will maximize return but says the company makes no attempt to estimate it. He sees the impetus for investment to be primarily from “middle-down” rather than top-down. Bottom-up push for investment is rare but does occur. Investment decisions are often highly political in nature, and the hot issues change over time. The company considers itself “lean and mean”; its current strategy to extract short-term profitability from products reduces the opportunity for significant speculative IT investment, since all investment is charged against product revenues.

## Organization 5 — education

Organization 5 is the business school in a large private university in New York City; the interviewee is the associate dean for administration.

According to the interviewee, investment in IT is measured and tracked in an aggregate form. He estimates IT to account for half the management and administration budget, and thus 10 percent of the school's total annual budget. IT is defined to include computer centers, department tal equipment budgets, telecommunications (except voice-based), and IT expenditures in administrative departments, such as the bursar's office. Word processors and maintenance are significant expenditures. More capital is invested in non-DP areas on personal computers than the school's total remaining DP investment.

IT is tracked and managed at the school level, although there is no attempt to include the “relatively small expenses.” Some departmental expenditures on personal computers are not captured in this tracking. No measure of ROI for IT investment is used, although each department is assessed on an input-versus-output basis. Inputs include expenditures on people, space, and equipment; output is defined as research output, student course ratings, and estimated contribution to revenues via tuition.

The interviewee feels that a threshold investment in IT is “required to survive” and remain competitive among business schools. Strategic IT investment is linked to the overall “strategy of quality to maintain and add competitive advantage.” One performance measure of quality is GPA and SAT scores of incoming students; this is a relative measure commonly used to rank business schools. As a result of this strategy, the impetus for IT investment is top-down, to either cut costs or improve quality. Some cuts in administrative staff have been achieved with IT investment.

## Organization 6 — telecommunications

Organization 6 is a very large communications company that is currently restructuring its operations. The interviewee is manager of communications.

According to the interviewee, the definition of IT is “moving to include everything.” This change has not yet been achieved, and many managers “would not know their departmental IT investment.”

He says that, in the opinion of top management, there is no evidence of a return from IT investment encompassing the “thousands of systems” currently installed. The organization, he says, spends more on IT than its size would suggest.

The company has recently created the new position of director of information management, whose responsibilities are primarily the “stewardship and quality of data.” The management of

IT resources is a very political issue, and some of the recent changes have been “politically unpalatable.”

A major initiative is under way for overall planning for information systems. The scope of the plan includes hardware, software, development projects, databases, telecommunications staff, and organizational design. This plan is driven by the organizational strategy, and two of the primary objectives are “to cope with rapidly changing technology and to understand data as a corporate resource.”

## Analysis of Key Factors

This section presents an analysis of the key factors that emerged from the six cases. Tables 2 to 4 show the findings in summary form.

## The definition and tracking of IT

Table 2 shows the industry of each organization, the level of the interviewee in the organization, and its definition of IT. Each organization uses a different definition of IT investment. All include hardware, software, related personnel, overhead, and some communications expenditure. The majority does not include voice, telex, facsimile, or postal services. IT dedicated to productive capacity (e.g., computers used for process control in manufacturing or general ledger in banking) is or is not defined and treated separately for different industries. There appears to be an overall trend to broaden the definition of IT to include many more aspects of the technology.

## IT investment management

Table 3 summarizes the methods of tracking and calculating return on IT investment. The companies track IT technology expenditure with varying degrees of rigor. One difficulty is that IT investment by departments or divisions in their own discretionary budgets is generally not captured in corporate IT tracking. As a consequence, corporate (centralized) IT investment in MIS or DP centers is adequately tracked (in all but one organization) but decentralized expenditure is not. The general trend toward decentralization of responsibility for IT investment compounds this problem. All respondents say that with the large investments in IT, senior managers had initiated (or were likely to initiate) methods of tracking the organization-wide investments in all types of IT. Unfortunately, this type of information is not collected in the usual costing and accounting databases and is rarely a line item shown on the consolidated budget.

The interviewees agree that integrated coordination of IT investment is necessary. The organizations use different methods for calculating returns, but none is completely comfortable with its approach. Major investments are often subjected to traditional ROI methods on a project basis. However, a threshold investment is often necessary to compete in the industry and is often not subject to ROI or any other type of return calculation. Most interviewees believe there to be an optimal level of IT investment, but none feels it is possible to achieve it or even be sure of what it is.

As indicated in the table, the organizations are generally moving to corporate-wide tracking of all IT investment (i.e., total centralized and decentralized investment).

## Political and organizational influences

Political considerations, important in most of the organizations, significantly impact investment decisions. These considerations sometimes eclipse the technical and economic considerations and are generally perceived as becoming more and more important. (See Table 4.)

The interviewees note a trend to decentralized responsibility for the management of the IT resource. This is consistent with the increased decentralized IT expenditure.

The impetus for investment comes from different levels within organizations. In some cases where lower-level management was unable to complete the required transactions with the available resources (e.g., process the required number of invoices), the impetus for investment was bottom-up. Top-down impetus for IT investment occurred where the business strategy included the objective to incorporate more IT into the organization. These initiatives usually originated at the CEO level. In one organization, the impetus for change originated middle-down and not from top management. This company was divisionalized, and IT investment decisions were left to the discretion of each division.

The link between IT and corporate strategy varies. In two organizations, there is no explicit link. In one organization, the link is purely for cost containment; i.e., IT plays a role in the explicit strategy of cost reduction. In the university, IT is included in the strategy of improving the quality of the program. In the communications company, there is an explicit link from corporate strategy to IT support for it. In one of the insurance companies, a circular link is identified where IT is input to strategy formulation, and then the IT required to implement that strategy is identified.

## Conversion effectiveness of IT investment

All interviewees agree that the effectiveness with which IT investment is converted to useful output was important and could vary from one organization to another. We have called this phenomenon conversion effectiveness. The majority of interviewees believe that this conversion is just another resource management task and that organizations that are good at general resource management would be effective converters of IT investment. They consider the implementation process, culture of the organization, and skill of the managers to be major determinants of conversion effectiveness. This result appears to reinforce the findings of Harris and Katz (1988) and the PIMS Program (1984) that high-performing companies invest more heavily in IT. It appears they are more efficient at converting resources, including IT, to organizational performance than lower-performing companies.

## Implications of the Findings

Three major implications emerge from these findings for practitioners responsible for IT investment. The three areas are listed here and recommendations to deal with these three areas follow in the next section.

## Recommendations

The three implication areas are of general concern to most firms as they grapple with the problems of managing IT investment. The following are our recommendations for beginning to deal with these problems.

## Define and track IT investment

The first step in managing IT investment is to know exactly what that investment is. The measurement and tracking of the investment is difficult, and the following suggestions are offered:

1. Adopt a broad definition of IT, including all hardware, software, people, communications (phone, telex, facsimile, satellite etc.), overhead, training, and maintenance.

2. Measure and track this expenditure over time against a convenient base. The base could be revenues, total expenses, or management-controlled costs (Strassmann, 1985). It is important to capture all decentralized expenditure by functional departments.

In many cases the general definition of IT investment is too broad, as different investments in IT are often made with very different objectives. Our recommended approach is to track separately the amount of IT investment of each type and treat these as a portfolio of IT investments with different aims and associated levels of risk.

The objective for which IT investment is embedded in the business strategy can be categorized into three general types: strategic, informational, and transactional (Turner and Lucas, 1985). Strategic IT is defined as IT that “changes a firm’s product or the way a firm competes in the industry” (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; p. 1193) and can be identified as IT investment made to increase sales. An example of strategic IT is the establishment of a communications network to deliver information-based products to customers. Strategic IT is generally associated with long-term considerations, including a long lead time between investment and return. Strategic IT is temporal; i.e., what is strategic today will not be strategic in ten years.

Informational IT provides the information and communication infrastructure of the organization. Included is a range of investments from telephones and facsimile machines to computing resources for accounting and production planning purposes. The commodity of this infrastructure is information for management of the firm. Informational IT is usually concerned with a medium-term time horizon.

Transactional IT supports operational management, with short time horizons and significant repetition, and is associated with short-term ROI. Transactional IT usually involves the processing of repetitive transactions, such as accounts payable and order entry. Transactional IT can be precisely defined as IT investment made with the objective of cutting costs.

Although the resulting systems objectives are quite different for these three types of IT investment, it is possible that they are housed in the same large information system.

A fourth type of IT investment emerged in this study: a threshold IT investment required to compete in a certain industry. Returns on threshold investments are likely to be smaller and of a different nature than other IT investments, since this threshold investment is mandatory to compete in the industry and may not be directly associated with real, short-term returns. Thus, threshold investment can be considered a sunk cost required to participate in the industry and often may not be amenable to ROI approaches for justification.

We recommend that a firm's IT investment be categorized into these four types and that investment be tracked over time. This approach highlights the need to link IT investment with the strategy of the firm.

A few organizations have a centralized IT planning function that coordinates firm-wide IT expenditure and ensures consistency between the organization's strategy and IT investments. The tracking of IT investment would be a logical extension of this function.

Finally, there are differences in IT investment needs, as well as profiles among different industries, making comparisons across industries difficult and potentially misleading. This is due primarily to the different cost structures and stage of technical adoption, in addition to the nature of investment in productive capacity (i.e., in manufacturing versus an information-intensive organization, such as insurance). As a consequence, we suggest great care in interpreting the broad investment-level statistics often found in the popular trade press. The fact that the average level of IT investment in all manufacturing is 2 percent of sales is of little help to the small chocolate manufacturer trying to determine the appropriate level of IT investment in his or her firm.

## IT investment and return

The justification of IT investment is a major stumbling block for many firms. ROI calculations and other capital budgeting techniques do not apply to many IT investments. Decisions to invest in other types of technology (e.g., an NC machine tool) are often made on a project basis using ROI methods. Investment in IT does not lend itself to ROI approaches for two reasons:

1. It is very difficult to determine the income stream, particularly for IT investment made for strategic purposes. Thus, the calculation of a meaningful ROI is not possible.

2. Investment in IT tends to be pervasive throughout the organization, and the consequences of investment are likely to be the result of interactions among a number of different project investments in IT (e.g., database, data entry, hardware, etc.).

A firm-wide approach to determining the relationship between IT investment and organizational performance is needed.

We have the following recommendations:

1. Recognize that ROI-type calculations are not relevant for many IT investments. The exception is transactional IT investment, where a common objective is cost displacement by the substitution of capital for labor.

2. Recognize that if threshold investment is required to compete in an industry, meeting that threshold may actually have negative ROI but be essential to the firm's survival.

3. Many IT investments, particularly of the strategic type, are made on "gut feelings." For these types of investments, companies should measure and track the investment and attempt to determine its effect on organizational performance.

Many contemporary business organizations contain a number of distinct businesses. To analyze IT investment at the organizational level will not provide a boundary that logically connects IT investment to performance. We choose to analyze each business as a separate unit; to achieve this we adopt the definition of Strategic Business Unit (SBU). $^{2}$ SBU has a distinct set of products or services and serves a distinct group of customers. The SBU also has a well-defined set of competitors. Analysis at this level removes many of the aggregation problems of assessing the firm level.

The performance measures used in the assessment of the impact of IT investment must be chosen to reflect the business objectives of the IT investment.

A large number of financial and social measures of firm (SBU) performance are available (Zammuto, 1982). We suggest a combination of measures in order to capture the diverse impacts of IT investment on the different aspects of performance. The aim is to choose measures that can be logically linked to the performance objective of each type of IT investment. We suggest the following matchings, inspired by the strategic intent for investing in various types of IT:

Performance measures for each type of IT investment match the objective of each type of IT investment. We based our choice of measure on discussions with IT managers, $^{4}$ as explained below.

\- Strategic IT investment is generally made with long-term goals relating to competitive advantage. To capture this goal, we suggest using the sales growth rate of the firm.

\- Informational IT investment is generally made with medium-term goals of improving management decision making; we suggest using the financial measure ROA to match it to performance.

\- Transactional IT investment is generally made with goals of reducing the costs of doing business by substituting capital for labor. To capture this performance, we suggest assessing the change in non-production labor (adjusted for sales).

These measures are suggested as a guide and should be tailored to the industry and then the specific organization's needs. It is absolutely vital to perform any assessment of the effect of IT investment on performance over time. Cross-sectional assessment will lead to misleading pictures of the effects, as any lag between investment and performance effects will be ignored. This is one of the reasons that many of the studies reviewed earlier produced conflicting findings.

The objective is to track both IT investment (by type) and the appropriate measures of firm performance over time and look for trends and patterns. This process will assist linking IT investment to business strategy and give general indications of IT investment and performance associations. The technique would be particularly useful when applied to several SBUs in a single firm.

We do not suggest using a performance measure for threshold IT investment. This investment is required for the firm to enter, compete or remain in a marketplace where a threshold IT exists.

## Organizational issues

One issue that emerged during this study is the importance of conversion effectiveness. Investment in IT is likely to be converted to productive outputs with different effectiveness, depending on the firm. If it is true (as suggested by the respondents) that conversion effectiveness is just another resource allocation task, then good organizational performers will have superior conversion effectiveness. We suggest that firms with good conversion effectiveness will have a stronger association between IT investment and performance. Conversion effectiveness is likely to have many components, four of which were identified by the respondents and are presented without justification below.

\- Top management commitment to IT

● Previous firm experience with IT

\- User satisfaction with systems

\- The turbulence of the political environment of the organization

We recommend that attention be given to these four components when any significant IT investment is made. All four are intuitively appealing and consistent with implementation literature. Furthermore, an aggregation of these four components was found to significantly moderate the relationship between IT investment and firm performance in 33 manufacturing firms (Weill, 1988).

## Conclusion

Previous studies of IT investment and its relationship to organizational performance have had conflicting findings. In the case studies reported here, designed to gain insight into the way firms define and track IT investment and its relationship to performance, we found a wide variety of definitions and methods of tracking. We also found that while firms acknowledge the importance of IT investment to organizational performance, they do not have a satisfactory way of assessing the relationship. We believe that some of the more interesting observations — the importance of political considerations, the concept of an industry-based threshold investment, and the notion of conversion effectiveness with which investment affects performance — are useful to managers who are responsible for managing and tracking IT investment.

In future research, we are seeking to refine and operationalize the appropriate definition of organizational performance, depending on the type of IT investment. We are also refining the concepts of threshold investment, investment types, and conversion effectiveness in a larger, cross-sectional survey of 33 manufacturing firms. Future research also needs to take into account the problems of comparing investment levels across industries and, as expenditures and authority for those expenditures become more and more decentralized, of measuring IT investment firm-wide.
