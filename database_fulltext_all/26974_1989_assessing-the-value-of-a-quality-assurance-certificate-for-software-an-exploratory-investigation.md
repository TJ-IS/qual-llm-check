## Abstract

An analysis of the opinions of decision makers who select software for business use indicated that a software quality assurance certificate would add value to and confidence in a software product. Marketing research techniques were used to obtain and analyze data from business users of software. Their problems, anxieties, influences on software choice, the value of a certificate in the choice process, and the importance of auditing the various aspects of the software development process were assessed. The findings indicate that such a certificate would benefit users and demonstrate how marketing research techniques can be applied to assess market acceptability. This study used three market segments and proposed two services to cater to their needs.

Keywords: Quality assurance, marketing research, software development

ACM Categories: D.2, D.2.9, K.6.3

## Introduction

This article reports the results of a study that assessed the feasibility of introducing a software quality assurance certificate. The research was conducted by staff members at the University of Bradford Management Centre in collaboration with the British Standard Institute (BSI) $^{2}$ and was funded by the Alvey Programme $^{3}$ of advanced information technology research. This collaboration was intended to provide a good research balance between academic and commercial quality assurance interests.

At the time of the project (1986/87) no accepted definition of what value users would or could place on a software quality assurance certificate had emerged. It was considered vital to establish such a definition before a range of meanings was determined as a result of custom and practice. The intention was to launch a third-party software quality assurance certificate issued by a competent and nationally accredited agency independent of supplier interest. The certificate would be issued only if suppliers met standards of performance for criteria relevant to the software development process. In order to obtain the certificate, a software supplier would be checked by an independent, nationally accredited auditor to ensure that the supplier had followed well-defined procedures in the development of the software. The certificate would be issued on a commercial basis, i.e., the supplier would pay the agency for the auditing service and the certificate. It should be noted that cost implications to software suppliers were not specifically addressed in this research. It might, however, be reasonable to suggest that a supplier who implements quality assurance procedures in order to meet the criteria would find total production costs reduced through the elimination of much rework and rectification (Oakland and Hinch, 1986). This would likely be an important argument with software suppliers in discussions that would take place before a certification scheme could be introduced.

The certificate would have the properties of a new product (because no comparable product existed). It was believed that marketing research procedures and practices could be applied to assess the probability of success and to determine the key characteristics (e.g., what criteria the certification should cover) and price of the product. Green and Tull (1978) show many examples of the application of marketing research to these kinds of issues. Although Milne and Weber (1983) formulated a theoretical market model showing the interactions between the producers of generalized software and users, practical applications of marketing research techniques are seldom reported in the information technology literature. One exception is the study of Frank and Green (1968), which used cluster analysis to characterize interbrand competition between computer hardware suppliers. Our project, therefore, afforded an opportunity to apply a marketing-oriented approach to the development of a new product concept within the IT industry.

## Research Objectives

A marketing approach to new product development places the customer at its focus: for a product to be successful it must satisfy some need or want. A major part of this study focused on the assessment of customer requirements regarding software products for business applications. In particular, the project sought to identify:

1. The problems encountered by users of software

2. The degree to which a software certificate could reduce those perceived problems.

3. The users' perception of the marginal value a certificate would add to a software product

4. The influences on software choice and the degree to which the certificate would influence the decision

5. The form of certificate, i.e., which aspects of the software development process should be audited

A further objective of the study was to establish a methodology for researching product users, which could also be applied in researching many other types of computer-related products and services.

## The Study

The project was divided into two stages: qualitative and quantitative. The role of the qualitative research as a precursor of the quantitative stage has been well-documented (Crimp, 1985; Green and Tull, 1978; Moser and Kalton, 1976). Our study used it to explore the problems encountered by software users, the role and value of a certificate, and the extent to which business users would be prepared to pay a price premium for software with a certificate. The qualitative interviews also provided insight on the factors influencing software choice and sources of software endorsement. The quantitative stage was designed to assign specific numbers to several of the following parameters: How much extra would users be prepared to pay? How influential would a certificate be in software choice? What criteria should be audited? Do market segments that differ according to their purchasing characteristics exist? This research focused on users of software. (We intend to address suppliers' opinions in a future study.) Additionally, the scope of the study was limited to software for use on mini and micro-computers. Thus, the results cannot be generalized to software for mainframe computers.

## Qualitative Research

## Methodology

Nineteen semi-structured interviews were conducted with business users of software. Companies were chosen on a quota basis to include manufacturing, service, local authority, and educational establishments. Both large and small organizations were included in the sample. Another quota control ensured that four classes of software were investigated: systems software, process control software, office systems software, and small systems applications.

Participation in the study was solicited by contacting industrial colleagues, describing the broad objectives of the study to them, and asking them to suggest the most appropriate person to respond to a questionnaire in an interview setting. These people were then telephoned to arrange the interview. They were told that the interview concerned their use and purchase of software for business applications. Almost all contacts agreed to participate in the study, and the actual respondents were highly cooperative and involved.

The focus of the interviews was on software for mini or micro-computers, depending on ownership. The interviews typically lasted one to two hours. A list of topic areas was defined on the questionnaire, but a degree of flexibility was built into the interview situation to allow respondents freedom to talk about issues that were salient to them. Respondents were usually very forthcoming and interested in the subject of enquiry.

## Qualitative findings

## Problems

Problems had been experienced with all aspects of the software investigated in this study. One problem concerned software (in particular, custom-designed software) that did not perform as the sales literature, documentation, and/or salespeople claimed it would. Another problem (again, especially associated with custom-designed software) stemmed from backup support, which was criticized because some vendors were negligent or unskilled in answering queries and correcting faults. The situation was exacerbated by staff turnover at supplying companies, and it tended to worsen as the product aged. For instance, backup to second and third generation products was sometimes worse than backup to first generation products.

Much criticism was also directed toward manuals, which were often unclear, inaccurate, and difficult to use (e.g., poor indexing). In one case, a manual was not provided until one year after the delivery of the software. In other cases, manuals for current software were useless with a new release unless the new release was accompanied by a modified manual. The overall impression was that many manuals were poorly written, with little attempt made to write them from the user perspective.

Most problems were experienced with custom-designed packages produced by smaller software houses. In contrast, a high level of satisfaction was found with the operation of established and standardized software packages for micro-computers. In general, users were satisfied that such packages (e.g., Lotus 1-2-3) performed as specified and were free from operational problems. Users felt confident about purchasing such software because of its proven record in the marketplace. It was hypothesized that it was this kind of software for which a certificate would have low marginal value in terms of quality assurance.

Conversely, it was hypothesized that a certificate would be of most value to users when they evaluated smaller software houses selling new products, particularly the custom-designed variety. Users perceived that a certificate would be useful if it reduced the problems associated with this kind of product.

## Value of a Certificate

The reaction of respondents to paying a price premium for certificated software varied according to their past experiences. Some purchasers of standardize packages felt that the certificate would not produce much “added value” and therefore were reluctant to pay a price premium. Others who had experienced problems thought that a certificate would justify a higher price for software if it meant that these problems would not recur. Another group who felt a price premium would be justified consisted of inexperienced users who perceived the certificate as providing a quality standard that could aid them in their choice.

The way in which software decisions are usually made revealed another benefit of the certificate to suppliers. Users invariably constructed a “short list” of products to evaluate before purchase. Almost all respondents stated that certified software stood a better chance of being put on this short list. Although the certificate might not justify a higher price for some user groups, it was hypothesized that a higher sales volume was likely to occur as a consequence of being on more short listings.

A final significant finding was that the inclusion of the British Standards Institute (BSI) name on the certificate gave added assurance to users that the certificate was meaningful and of value.

## Conclusions from Qualitative Research

The wide range of problems revealed at this stage indicated that a potential market exists for a certificate that could aid users in their selection of software. These qualitative results suggested that further quantitative research should be conducted to provide more evidence regarding the nature of the certificate and its value to business users.

## Quantitative Research

## Methodology

Thirty-two structured interviews were conducted at this stage. Samples in excess of 30 are considered adequate for most industrial marketing research (Hodey and West, 1984). Qualitative findings revealed that a major discriminating factor regarding problems experienced with software was whether it was standardized or custom-designed. It was therefore decided to base one of the quota controls for the quantitative study on these categories. Twenty-two interviews were based on standardized software and 10 on custom-designed software. Sample proportions were biased toward the standardized type since it represented the larger market size. The approximate 2:1 breakdown was believed to be a reasonable compromise between the realities of the marketplace and the need to include enough custom-designed software to be able to make meaningful analyses of the data.

The other quota control was based on whether computer ownership was either a micro or a mini-computer. These considerations resulted in 20 interviews based on micro-computer ownership and 12 on mini-computer ownership. Although not subject to tight quotas, care was taken to include organizations from the same groups used at the qualitative stage. Once again industrial contacts were used to gain access to companies. Each was asked to provide the name of a person in his or her company who was a key decision maker or influencer in choosing software. A telephone call was made to this person to secure an interview. As in the earlier stage, a very high degree of cooperation (greater than 90%) was obtained by employing this method of approach. Except in two instances, interviews were with different respondents (and organizations) than those in the qualitative stage. No indication of the precise objectives of the study was given to respondents — only that the interview concerned the use and purchase of software for business applications.

The questionnaire gathered information on the type of hardware and software the company used, the stages passed through when choosing each type of software, the factors that were influential when deciding which software to buy, the value placed by users on a certificate, and the importance placed on various aspects of the software development process. Classificatory data were also obtained. When the certificate was discussed, respondents were told that the interviewers had no vested interest in its successful launch since they were simply acting as marketing research consultants. This was thought necessary to reduce the possibility of respondents giving answers they thought might please the interviewer.

The influences on the decision-making process were measured by a number of statements derived from the qualitative interviews, to which respondents recorded their opinions on a “Not Important at All” to “Extremely Important” five-point scale. Examples of such statements were “the software manufacturer is good at answering queries,” “the software is the cheapest on the market,” and “there are well-written and easily understood installation instructions.” Another question concerning choice influence asked respondents to score a series of possible endorsements out of 100 (with 100 meaning 100% confidence). Examples of endorsements included “recommended by a hardware supplier,” “supplied by a reputable software house,” and “held a BSI quality assurance certificate.”

The importance placed on various aspects of the software development process was assessed through a conjoint analysis experiment (Green and Srinivasan, 1978). First the stages in the development process of a software product were identified from a senior information technology executive in the Quality Assurance Services division of the BSI. From this description, six elements of the process were considered to have potential for quality assurance testing. These were:

1. The specification process used by the supplier company

2. The use of system documentation within the supplier company

3. The approach to the programming adopted (structured or unstructured)

4. The independent (third party) testing of the finished software

5. The installation procedures and instructions supplied to the customer.

6. The user manuals (both hard copy and online) made available to customers

For different purchasers, however, it was believed that different elements of the auditing process were likely to be of different importance. For example, we might expect that the purchaser of a tailor-made piece of software would attach more importance to how closely the specification process matched user requirements. Purchasers of standardized software, however, might attach less importance to system documentation but more importance to the quality of the user manuals.

The objective of the conjoint analysis experiment was to gauge the relative importance of auditing each of the six aspects of software development to each respondent. Aggregations were then made to total sample and subgroup levels for the purposes of comparison.

Conjoint analysis addresses the problem of measurement of psychological judgements of rank-order quality (Green and Srinivasan, 1978). It decomposes a set of overall responses to factorially designed stimuli so that the importance of each stimulus component can be inferred from the overall evaluations of the stimuli. In our experiment, the approach used was to construct descriptions of eight hypothetical software products based on the development process used to create them. Each description explained whether each of the six aspects of the development process had been verified by third-party quality auditing. As a result, there are $2^{6} = 64$ total combinations or descriptions possible. Clearly, to evaluate such a large set of descriptions was beyond the scope of any respondent. A fractional factorial main effects design (Addelman, 1962) was therefore employed to reduce the number of descriptions to a more manageable eight.

Each respondent was presented with eight cards that described the hypothetical software products. They were then asked to rank them from "best quality" product to "poorest quality" product. The rankings of the overall product descriptions were then used to estimate the importance attached to quality auditing of each of the six aspects of the development process using LINMAP IV (Srinivasan and Shocker, 1981).

A factor analysis with varimax rotation was then performed on the endorsement influences and the five-point importance scales. The results of this analysis are given in Table 2. Eight orthogonal factors were identified as influencing choice. The first factor gathered endorsements from several sources such as the computer press, a software house, or colleague, while the second gathered endorsements specifically from a BSI certificate, independent testing, and an easy manual. This is an important result. It means that, although the BSI certificate is on a par with good endorsements from other sources (as listed in Table 1), it contributes to a second factor that provides a different source of confidence. It is a separate dimension that adds value, irrespective of endorsements from elsewhere: in isolation it provides an endorsement to a product; in conjunction with other endorsements it still adds value through increased confidence. Therefore, the BSI certificate has the potential to benefit both established and new software vendors.

## Value of the Certificate

With all other things equal, 75% of the respondents said they would choose software with a BSI certificate in preference to software without certification. Moreover, 79% of the 75% (59% overall) said they would be willing to pay more for software with a BSI certificate. The amounts they were willing to pay varied widely, ranging from 7% to 100% extra; most were between 20% and 25%. There were no systematic variations in the amount respondents were willing to pay across original price, mini/micro applications, or segments of customers. This indicates an opportunity to provide a service that adds value for the buyer and seller of software.

Cluster Two (13 respondents): Named “uncertains,” these respondents felt they lacked the knowledge to define their user needs, and this consideration dominated their idea of the process that leads to a good quality product.

The second cluster placed the identification of user requirements first; all other features, particularly structured programming, were less important (Figure 2b). This cluster was almost an inverse of the “professionals” of Cluster One. The group appeared to be less knowledgeable about computing than the “professionals” and therefore needed initial assurance that the software would do the job as desired. However, they seemed unaware that well-specified software can fail in operation.

Typically, these “uncertains” worked in medium-sized companies, but bought expensive systems for either minis or micros (Table 5). Like the “professionals,” they viewed a BSI certificate as the strongest source of confidence in software (Table 4).

Cluster Three (seven respondents): This cluster was named “isolates” because it placed major importance on independent testing — all other features were minor (Figure 2c). Whereas Cluster Two’s “uncertains” were dominated by their concern for specification risk, “isolates” needed reassurance that the software would work.

“Isolates” generally came from small companies that purchased relatively inexpensive software (Table 5). They were particularly concerned that software would operate “as claimed” and have a good “system documentation” (Table 3).

They had the greatest confidence in products from known software houses but had lower-than-average confidence in other endorsements. However, they viewed a BSI certificate as a better-than-average source of confidence, and their overpowering desire for independent testing made them a target for a BSI service.

Cluster Four (one respondent): This cluster was named “odd ball” because there was only one respondent who placed total importance on identification of user needs; no other factors were of any consequence to him (see Figure 2d).

## Discussion and Conclusions

The results of the marketing research surveys clearly revealed the high potential for introducing a software quality assurance certificate. The analysis of influences on choice showed that a BSI certificate would be a major source of confidence in a software product and would provide a dimension of endorsement that was unique. Furthermore, the certificate could be used by suppliers as a source of differential advantage (Day, 1984; Doyle, 1987; Porter, 1985): if users perceived two software products as being equal on other dimensions, most purchasers would choose the product that had the BSI certificate. Moreover, a majority of those would be willing to pay a price premium (modal range of 20-25% more).

The results of the conjoint experiment demonstrated that auditing all six elements of the software development process would be important to users. One product option would be to provide a full quality auditing service for these six elements. A certificate would be awarded to those suppliers who met the standards required for each criteria (e.g., documentation, structured programming).

We recognize that generalizations have been drawn from a small sample in this exploratory study. Nevertheless, we believe we have identified at least three clusters or market segments within the total market. A larger sample may reveal further segments and differing relative sizes. The internal consistency of the cluster member responses, together with their intuitive reasonableness and appeal, however, suggests that these three clusters may be the key segments. These segments are: the professionals, the uncertainties, and the isolates. In our view, the three segments would be covered by two services:

1. The first service is aimed at the "uncertain" segment, providing a consulting service that ensures that buyers are able to specify their requirements. This service would start with the examination of user needs and end with a specification being given to a supplier who has the ability to do the job properly. The findings from this project were well-received by the assessor for the Alvey Programme, and further research is proposed to test the concept of a BSI quality assurance certificate with suppliers of software. The results of this user study can be used to present a product/service concept(s) in terms of benefits and costs to a supplier. Established marketing research techniques for concept testing (Moore, 1982; Wind, 1982) can be applied to gauge the likely acceptance of a certificate by those potential customers.

This article has presented the results of an exploratory study of user reaction to a new product concept. The study has demonstrated that marketing research techniques and procedures can successfully be applied to aid decision makers in the go-no-go launch decision and on marketing decisions such as product/service form. Clear indications of the value of the proposed product to user groups were obtained and alternative product/service forms were identified by aggregate and market segment analysis. We hope that the success of this study provides the impetus for others to apply a marketing-oriented approach to new product development within the information technology domain.
