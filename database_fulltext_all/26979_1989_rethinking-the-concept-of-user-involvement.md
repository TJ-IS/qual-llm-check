# Rethinking the Concept of User Involvement

By: Henri Barki
Jon Hartwick

Faculty of Management

McGill University

1001 Sherbrooke Street West

Montreal, Quebec, Canada H3A 1G5

## Abstract

Within the field of information systems (IS), user involvement generally refers to participation in the systems development process by potential users or their representatives and is measured as a set of behaviors or activities that such individuals perform. This article argues for a separation of the constructs of user participation (a set of behaviors or activities performed by users in the system development process) and user involvement (a subjective psychological state reflecting the importance and personal relevance of a system to the user). Such a distinction is not only more consistent with conceptualizations of involvement found in other disciplines, but it also leads to a number of new and interesting hypotheses. These hypotheses promise a richer theoretical network that describes the role and importance of participation and involvement in the implementation process.

Keywords: User involvement, user participation, implementation

ACM Categories: D.2.9, H.1.2, K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

For more than two decades, the concept of user involvement has been proclaimed a critical ingredient in the success of systems development efforts. Unfortunately, as Ives and Olson (1984) have recently pointed out, severe theoretical, methodological, and measurement problems associated with involvement research prevent definitive conclusions regarding the benefits of user involvement from being reached. Moreover, as Robey and Farrow (1982) have noted, very little is known about the specific mechanisms through which involvement is apt to have its effects. Thus, in spite of the considerable effort given to studying user involvement, understanding of the involvement construct in the information systems (IS) field remains mostly descriptive and somewhat shallow.

In information systems, user involvement generally refers to participation in the system development process by potential users or their representatives. Recently, it has been defined and measured as a set of operations or activities that such individuals have or have not performed (Baroudi, et al., 1986; Franz and Robey, 1986; Olson and Ives, 1980). The purpose of this article is to propose an alternative definition of the user involvement construct. Instead of viewing involvement as a set of operations or activities performed by the user, this article proposes to define involvement as a subjective psychological state. A user is involved when he or she considers a system to be both important and personally relevant. The distinction between user participation and user involvement necessitates a re-alignment of current thinking concerning the involvement construct in IS. The term generally referred to as “user involvement” and the various measures developed for this construct (e.g., Baroudi, et al., 1986; Franz and Robey, 1986) need to be re-labelled “user participation.” Moreover, the research that has been conducted thus far should be considered investigations of the antecedents of user participation, not user involvement.

The proposed definition of user involvement is more consistent with current conceptualizations of involvement in other disciplines. As an intervening variable between user participation and indicants of system success it also provides an explanation for the relatively weak research results obtained to date. In addition, it leads to new and interesting hypotheses within the IS field. To develop these arguments, the article first examines the use of the involvement construct in IS research. Then, the conceptualization of this construct in the fields of psychology, marketing, and organizational behavior is presented. Subsequently, drawing on the research results obtained in these fields, recommendations for IS research are made and directions for future research are suggested.

## The Involvement Construct in IS Research

Conceptualization and measurement

More than a decade ago, Swanson (1974) made the following assertion: "That management should be 'involved' in MIS development is a popular wisdom. . . . Unfortunately, what is meant by involvement is rarely clear, and nothing has been done to my knowledge to provide a rigorous foundation for its measurement" (pp. 178-179). Swanson claims that the characterization of a manager as "involved" with a management information system must be stated in terms of the activities of the manager. According to Swanson, this involvement can either be in the manager's role as a user of the system or in association with the activities necessary to make this use possible (e.g., in the design, implementation, and operation of the system). The former is labelled "inquiry involvement," and the latter, "a priori involvement." Managers are characterized as "inquiry involved" when they make frequent queries utilizing the system. They are characterized as "a priori involved" when they, for example, frequently initiate changes either to the computer programs or to the content and format of various files, or when they initiate written communications or call meetings for the purpose of educating users.

Since Swanson's initial statement, a number of studies have been conducted that investigate user involvement and its relationship to system success. In 1984, Ives and Olson (1984) conducted a critical review of Swanson's work. In their review, they define user involvement as "participation in the system development process by representatives of the target user group" (p. 587). Swanson's view of user involvement as a set of activities performed by users has therefore remained over the years. Two facets of user involvement are delineated by Ives and Olson — type of participation and degree of participation. Type of participation varies from indirect (where user representatives participate in the system development process) to direct (where the users themselves fully participate in the development process). Each type of participation permits some expression of the user's ideas, potentially allowing for his or her influence in the design of the system. A second facet of involvement, degree of participation, refers to the influence that users actually have over the final product. According to Ives and Olson, this can range from having no influence to having strong control over the design of the system.

In their review, Ives and Olson note the need to develop and validate a standard measure of user involvement. Two recent studies have done just that. Baroudi, et al. (1986) identify 30 different user activities rated by a number of academics and professionals as effective mechanisms for ensuring user involvement in the system development process. User involvement was measured using questions that required respondents to indicate how frequently they had performed each activity. The behavioral conceptualization of involvement thus remains.

Franz and Robey (1986) have also developed a measure of user involvement. They conceptualize involvement as user influence during system development, where involved users perform activities that are influential in leading and managing particular aspects of the system development process. Four core areas of such influence are detailed — explaining and clarifying information needs, detailing input and output requirements, stating system needs and objectives, and asking questions and providing answers. Franz and Robey note that this influence can occur at different stages of the system development process. Two key stages are differentiated — the planning and design stage and the implementation stage. Based on these distinctions, questions were developed to tap user involvement. Each question described a behavior or activity that the user could perform. Two scales of involvement were then developed — one to represent user involvement during the planning and design stage (based on six questions concerning user activities performed during this stage) and one to represent user involvement during the implementation stage (based on seven questions concerning user activities performed during this stage). Once again, involvement is considered to be a set of behaviors or activities performed by potential users as they participate in different stages of the system development process.

## Empirical findings

Swanson (1974) also proposes and provides empirical support for a theoretical model describing the involvement process. In this model, a priori involvement “coproduces” IS appreciation, and IS appreciation “coproduces” and “is coproduced by” inquiry involvement. Thus, managers who frequently engage in system development activities will become more appreciative of the system. This increase in appreciation translates into an increase in the use of the system to make inquiries. Finally, this increased use will result in an even greater appreciation of the system. In recent years, the terminology has changed somewhat. A priori involvement is now generally referred to as user involvement, inquiry involvement is more typically referred to as system usage, and IS appreciation is generally labelled as user attitude or user satisfaction. Yet, the basic theoretical notions of Swanson have remained intact.

In the years since Swanson's initial work, research has attempted to demonstrate a relationship between user involvement and a number of indicants of IS success. In their review of this research, Ives and Olson (1984) found the evidence to be inconclusive. Only eight out of 13 studies investigating user involvement and user satisfaction found a significant relation. Four out of nine studies investigating user involvement and system usage reported a significant relation. Just two out of six studies investigating user involvement and system quality found a significant positive relation. These results prompted Ives and Olson to conclude that the “. . . benefits of user involvement have not been strongly demonstrated. . . . Having reviewed these studies, however, the authors must conclude that the consistent lack of rigor in research to date seriously limits understanding of the nature of user involvement in CBIS development. . . . Future research requires rigorous attention to measurement and methodology” (p. 600).

Two recent studies have followed this dictum. In the first, conducted by Baroudi, et al. (1986)

"Care was also taken to address many of the methodological problems existing in prior research. Generalizability across organizations and systems was enhanced by including over 200 users in the same function from many different organizations. User involvement and system usage were measured with rigorously developed instruments, while information satisfaction was measured by an instrument with demonstrated reliability and validity. . ." (p. 237). Significant relationships were found between the measure of user involvement and measures of both information satisfaction and system usage. However, given the methodological sophistication and rigor of this study, the relationships must be considered weak. The correlation between involvement and system usage is .28; the correlation between involvement and information satisfaction is only .18.

In the second of these studies, Franz and Robey (1986) obtained significant correlations between their measures of user involvement and a measure of perceived system usefulness. However, while significant, the relationships are relatively small in magnitude. User involvement in the design stage correlates .33 with perceived system usefulness; user involvement in the implementation stage correlates .24 with perceived usefulness.

## Summary

User involvement, as it has been defined and measured in the IS field, refers to a set of behaviors or activities performed by potential users during the system development process. Empirical work has generally been concerned with a determination of whether involvement relates to various indicants of system success — user attitudes, system quality, system use, etc. Such studies have provided mixed or weakly positive results. As Ives and Olson (1984) point out, the early mixed results can be attributed in large part to methodological inadequacies of the research. However, even with the better measures and more rigorous research designs of more recent work, empirical results, while significantly positive, have been weak. Such results suggest the need for research that attempts to delineate the intervening mechanisms that exist between user involvement and various indicants of system success.

# The Involvement Construct in Psychology

## Conceptualization and measurement

In the field of psychology, the concept of involvement has long been used in theories and investigations of attitudes and their change. This work claims that individuals are more or less involved with various social issues. Individuals are said to be involved with an issue when it has “intrinsic importance” (Sherif and Hovland, 1961), “personal or psychological significance for the individual” (Sherif, et al., 1965), when it is “important, salient and relevant to the individual” (Sherif, et al., 1965), or when they “expect it to have significant consequences for their own lives” (Apsler and Sears, 1968). Involvement is therefore considered a psychological state triggered by two key aspects of an issue — its importance or significance and its personal relevance.

## Empirical findings

Within psychology, empirical research has concentrated on the relationship of involvement to attitudes and the effects of involvement on both attitude change and the translation of attitudes into subsequent behavior. The concept of involvement is deemed conceptually distinct from the concept of attitude. The two are, however, said to be related. Indeed, two separate relationships between involvement and attitude have been uncovered (Sherif, et al., 1965). A person's attitude about some issue reflects his or her evaluative feelings concerning the issue. A small, positive relationship generally exists between involvement and attitude. Individuals who find an issue to be both important and personally relevant tend (weakly) to have more positive feelings concerning the issue. However, a much stronger relationship between involvement and attitude has been uncovered for highly involved individuals. These individuals were found to have more extreme or polarized attitudes — very positive or very negative feelings about the issue.

Much of the research in psychology has focused on the relationship of involvement to attitude change. Early research suggested that highly involved individuals were more resistant to attitude change or persuasion attempts (Apsler and Sears, 1968; Sherif, et al., 1973; Sherif and Hovland, 1961). However, recent work suggests that the relationship between involvement and attitude change is more complex. For example, highly involved individuals are more resistant to change attempts using weak persuasive arguments, but are more likely to change their attitudes when presented with strong persuasive arguments (Leippe and Elkin, 1987; Petty and Cacioppo, 1979, 1984; Petty, et al., 1981a, 1981b). Such results are explained in the next paragraph.

High involvement is said to increase an individual's motivation to process issue-relevant information. Consistent with this argument, Leippe and Elkin (1987) found that highly involved individuals have superior recall for such information. When this information consists of strong persuasive arguments, involved individuals are therefore more likely to be influenced by the arguments. However, when the information consists of weak arguments, highly involved individuals are more apt to detect and reject the weak arguments. In such cases, involved individuals are less influenced. Additionally, other researchers such as Chaiken (1980) and Petty, et al. (1981a) have found that peripheral characteristics (for example, the expertise or status of the endorser) have a greater influence on those individuals who are low in involvement. Such individuals may be less likely to consider the relevance of such characteristics and therefore are more susceptible to influence by them. Thus, while the highly involved individuals are concentrating on the meaningful arguments contained within a persuasive message, those lower in involvement seem to be affected by the peripheral cues present in the situation.

Psychologists have also found greater attitude-behavior consistency for highly involved individuals. When they have positive attitudes, highly involved individuals are more likely to form intentions and act in accordance with their attitudes. On the other hand, when their attitudes are negative, highly involved individuals are less likely to form such intentions or perform such behaviors (Leippe and Elkin, 1987; Sherif, et al., 1973; Sivacek and Crano, 1982). There are two likely explanations for this increased attitude-behavior consistency. As noted previously, highly involved individuals have more extreme attitudes. Extremely positive or negative attitudes are more clearly and readily translated into appropriate action. Thus, there will be greater attitude-behavior correspondence for highly involved individuals. A second reason is also suggested by the attitude change studies. In such studies, less involved individuals were found to be more influenced by interpersonal cues present in the situation. Thus, when deciding whether to perform certain behaviors, less involved individuals may be more affected by interpersonal concerns, conforming to the wishes and desires of others who are important to them (i.e., family, friends, bosses, etc.) but often paying less attention to their own personal wishes and desires. As a consequence, less involved individuals show less attitude-behavior consistency than highly involved individuals.

## The Involvement Construct in Marketing

Conceptualization and measurement

In the field of marketing, consumers are described as being more or less involved with different advertisements and advertising media (Krugman, 1967; Wright, 1973), with different products and product domains (Howard and Sheth, 1969; Hupfer and Gardner, 1971), and with different purchase decisions (Clarke and Belke, 1978). Although the field has no single precise definition of involvement, there is general consensus that involvement is an individual-level, internal-state variable that refers to the importance and personal relevance of these objects or events (Greenweld and Leavitt, 1984; Krugman, 1967; Mitchell, 1979; Zaichowsky, 1985; 1986). This, of course, is consistent with the definition found in psychology.

There have been two recent attempts to develop measures of consumer involvement. In one, Laurent and Kapferer (1985) developed a multifaceted measure of product involvement. One facet of their instrument, labelled "importance," corresponds to traditional measures of involvement and incorporates notions of personal relevance and importance. Another facet, labelled "pleasure," reflects the affect or value associated with a given product, and thus corresponds to traditional measures of attitude. Even though these two subscales are significantly correlated, they load on distinctly different factors in Laurent and Kapferer's (1980) validation study. This result supports the view that attitude and involvement are distinct, a position consistent with work in psychology and organizational behavior.

Zaichowsky (1985) provides strong evidence of reliability and validity for a 20-item scale of involvement using a semantic differential format. Thus, any object or event can be rated with the scale — advertisements, products, purchase decisions, etc. Twenty bi-polar items, reflecting the two aspects of involvement noted above — importance (e.g., “unimportant-important,” “insignificant-significant” and “trivial-fundamental”) and personal relevance (e.g., “means nothing to me-means a lot to me,” “irrelevant-relevant” and “doesn’t matter-matters to me”) — are presented to individual respondents. For each item, the individual checks a response along a seven-point continuum that reflects his or her first impression or immediate feeling concerning the object or event being rated. Each item is scored from 1 (representing low involvement) to 7 (representing high involvement), resulting in an overall involvement score ranging from 20 to 140. This instrument is promising as a context-free measure of involvement.

## Empirical findings

Marketing researchers have uncovered a number of consequences of high involvement. Zaichowsky (1985) found that high involvement leads to greater interest in the gathering of information about products, and Laurent and Kapferer (1985) found that it leads to extensive processing of such information when it is presented to individuals. Elaborating on this latter finding, highly involved individuals generate more personally relevant thoughts (Krugman, 1967), produce more supportive arguments and counter arguments (Gardner, et al., 1985; Wright, 1973), and have better memory for the information presented to them in persuasive advertisements (Gardner, et al., 1985). Such processes are likely to have a number of consequences. Highly involved individuals are likely to have more extreme and firmly held attitudes. Change attempts using weak persuasive arguments are apt to have little effect on such individuals. However, change attempts using strong arguments are very likely to be effective (Petty, et al., 1983). These findings parallel those of psychology. Also consistent with the findings of psychology is that there is greater attitude-behavior consistency for highly involved individuals (Petty, et al., 1983).

# The Involvement Construct in Organizational Behavior

Conceptualization and

measurement

The topic of job and work involvement has received considerable attention within the field of organizational behavior. In this work, involvement has been defined in three distinctly different ways. First, involvement has been defined as the degree to which one is allowed or encouraged to participate on the job (Allport, 1945; Bass, 1965; Wickert, 1951). As an example of this approach, Bass (1965) considered participative job behaviors such as making important job decisions, setting one's own work pace and contributing to the organization's goals to be important indices of job involvement. This definition corresponds to the definition of involvement currently found in IS. Involvement has also been defined as the degree to which individuals identify psychologically with their job and work (Dubin, 1956; Lawler and Hall, 1970; Lodahl and Kejner, 1965). For example, Lawler and Hall (1970) suggest that job involvement reflects the degree to which the job situation is central to the person and his or her identity. Dubin (1956) refers to a job-involved person as one for whom work is a very important part of life, or in his words, "a central life interest." This second definition would seem to parallel definitions found in the fields of psychology and marketing. Involvement has also been defined as the degree to which a person's work performance affects his or her self-esteem (Gurin, et al., 1960; Lodahl and Kejner, 1965; Vroom and Yetton, 1973).

Kanungo (1979; 1982) decried this situation, suggesting that the lack of definitional consensus had led to a state of confusion. In spite of a vast empirical and theoretical literature investigating job and work involvement, Kanungo notes that work had not progressed beyond a superficial descriptive level. He details four major sources of confusion. Because of their relevance to the present situation in IS, these concerns will be presented in some detail.

According to Kanungo (1979, 1982), the concept of involvement has been defined and measured in two very different ways. At times, involvement is considered as the presence or absence of various objective job and social conditions. At other times, involvement is considered a subjective psychological state of the individual. Unfortunately, there is no assurance that a state of involvement measured through the identification of objective conditions will parallel a subjective measure of the concept.

Related to this is another problem. According to Kanungo (1979, 1982), there has been a failure to maintain a conceptual distinction between involvement, its antecedent conditions, and its subsequent effects. He suggests that this distinction needs to be made, both for the sake of conceptual clarity and so that empirical tests of the causes and effects of involvement can be carried out. As an example, it would allow for a separation of involving conditions (such as the degree of participation allowed or encouraged on the job) from a psychological state of involvement. This would enable determination of whether involving conditions leads to individual feelings of involvement and if so, the degree of involvement.

According to Kanungo (1979, 1982), there has also been a failure to maintain a distinction between involvement and a variety of other positive emotional states (e.g., various positive job attitudes and feelings of job satisfaction). Highly involved employees may at certain times feel a high degree of satisfaction with their work and at other times or under other conditions feel deep dissatisfaction. Consistent with such an argument, both Lawler and Hall (1970) and Kanungo (1982) have shown job involvement and job satisfaction to be conceptually distinct, though theoretically and empirically related.

Finally, Kanungo (1979, 1982) claims there is a similar failure to distinguish job involvement from intrinsic motivation on the job. Lawler and Hall (1970) have also suggested that involvement and intrinsic motivation are conceptually distinct. Moreover, they argue that the third definitional distinction given previously is actually closer to a definition of intrinsic motivation and provide empirical evidence to support this contention.

In suggesting a resolution to these problems, Kanungo (1979, 1982) argues that job and work involvement must be defined in accordance with the second definitional distinction presented above; that is, as a psychological state of identification with one's job or work. Involvement could then be measured with Likert items such as "I live, eat, and breathe my job" or semantic differential items such as "unimportant-important" and "attached-detached." Consistent with the work in psychology and marketing, Kanungo recommends that job and work involvement be defined as a subjective psychological state reflecting the personal significance of one's job and work. In contrast, he suggests that participative job behaviors be considered as possible antecedents or causes of involvement. Finally, while noting the strong possibility of theoretical and empirical relationships with job attitudes and satisfaction, Kanungo argues that these also are distinct concepts.

## Empirical findings

The field of organizational behavior identifies a number of antecedents and consequences of job and work involvement. Job involvement is viewed as a result of both the early socialization of the individual and of current organizational conditions (Kanungo, 1982; Lawler and Hall, 1970; Lodahl and Kejner, 1965; Rabinowitz and Hall, 1977). For example, Lawler and Hall (1970) suggest that involvement reflects a value orientation learned early in the individual's socialization process. This suggests the possibility of wide personal differences in levels of involvement, regardless of one's current work situation. In contrast, participative management theorists such as Argyris (1964), Likert (1961), and McGregor (1960), stress that involvement results from organizational conditions. Supporting such contentions, Bass (1965) found six conditions that lead to heightened involvement — an opportunity to make important job decisions, making an important contribution to one's organization, self-determination, the freedom to set one's own work pace, achievement, and success. Similarly, Patchen (1970) found job interest to be increased by high difficulty, high control over the means of work, and high feedback. Together, these studies suggest that meaningful participation is an important antecedent of involvement.

Job and work involvement are also related to a number of other variables. Of key importance, the relationship to job satisfaction is positive, though moderate in magnitude (see Kanungo (1982) for a review of this work). As Guion (1958) notes, job-involved workers are not necessarily happy with their jobs. Very angry people are often very involved with their jobs. This suggests a relationship between involvement and the extremity of job satisfaction, similar to the relationship found by Sherif, et al. (1965) in their attitude research. Finally, job and work involvement have been studied to determine their impact on individual motivation and performance. As Kanungo (1982) notes in his review of this work, weakly positive results have generally been obtained.

## A Comparison and Integration

The IS involvement construct is very different from the construct currently used in other fields. In IS, involvement generally refers to a series of activities or behaviors performed by potential users or their representatives in the systems development process. In other disciplines, involvement is used to describe a subjective psychological state, reflecting the importance and personal relevance of an issue (e.g., Sherif, et al., 1965), of a product or advertisement (e.g., Krugman, 1967), or of one's job or work (e.g., Lawler and Hall, 1970). It is not without considerable thought and empirical effort that such disciplines have converged to such a position. Other fields, most notably the field of organizational behavior, have gone through situations like the one that currently exists regarding user involvement in IS. To take advantage of their experience and align the work in IS with these other disciplines, we recommend the following four changes.

1. The term "user participation" should be used instead of "user involvement" when referring to the behaviors and activities that the target users or their representatives perform in the systems development process. Since most of the items in measures developed by Baroudi, et al. (1986) and Franz and Robey (1986) assess individual responsibilities and influence in systems development, such measures should be considered measures of user participation. The first recommendation is consistent with the recommendation made by Kanungo (1979; 1982) as it relates to the field of organizational behavior. He argued that participative job behaviors are distinct from a state of job or work involvement. Instead, he suggested that such behaviors are best considered as one of many possible antecedents or causes of such a state.

2. Consistent with work in other disciplines, the term "user involvement" should be used to refer to a subjective psychological state of the individual and defined as the importance and personal relevance that users attach either to a particular system or to IS in general, depending on the users' focus. (It is also possible for users to attach personal importance and relevance to the activities of systems development, thereby becoming involved not with a system but with a process. Thus, one could alternatively define users' involvement with the development process.) A measure of user involvement reflecting this definition must be developed and validated. The context-free measure developed by Zaichowsky (1985) in the field of marketing would seem to be a good starting point for such a measure. Using the twenty-item scale, users or potential users could be asked to indicate their initial impressions or feelings concerning a system (alternatively, concerning IS in general, or the process of system development).

3. Once the concepts of user participation and user involvement have been differentiated, there will be a need to empirically determine their relationship. A relationship between participation and involvement has been implicitly assumed in past discussions of the involvement construct in IS. Consistent with this view, research will likely demonstrate that user participation is one of the more important antecedents or causes of user involvement. Individuals who have actively participated in the system development process are apt to develop beliefs that the system is both important and personally relevant. In part, such beliefs will result from their feeling of having had an influence on the resulting system. However, a number of factors are likely to influence the strength of the participation-involvement relationship, including the reasons for participating (e.g., whether participation is non-voluntary or voluntary), the kind of participation (e.g., whether participation is indirect or direct) and degree of participation (e.g., whether one has little or great influence in the process). User participation is also likely to have other concrete effects, influencing, for example, system quality and the user's knowledge of the system. In addition, other possible determinants of user involvement include the user's personality, present and future needs and goals, and past experiences with other systems. It is through the separation of user participation and user involvement that such hypotheses are generated. Ultimately, a richer theo retical network describing the role and importance of participation and involvement should be attained.

4. Finally, there is a need to ground the concepts of user participation and user involvement in a theoretical network that explains when and how these factors influence key criterion variables such as system usage or user satisfaction. There are two theoretical models that could be used to provide a general framework for this work. The first, initially proposed by Swanson (1974), suggests that user involvement leads to positive attitudes concerning the system. These attitudes in turn lead to positive attitudes concerning the system. These attitudes in turn lead to greater use of the system. A second model, presented more recently by Ives and Olson (1984), suggests that a number of cognitive and motivational factors mediate the relation between involvement and outcomes such as a perceived system quality and system usage. Whatever model is used, embedding the concepts of user participation and user involvement in a highly developed and well supported theory would seem to be an important step forward.

At the present time, we would argue for the use of the attitudinal approach suggested by Swanson. Much of the research conducted in other disciplines has focused on attitudes — the relationship of involvement to individual attitudes, the influence of involvement on attempts to change attitudes, and the influence of involvement in the translation of attitudes into subsequent behavior. By employing an attitudinal framework, IS researchers will be able to make use of this work in the formulation of their own research hypotheses.

Work in other disciplines has found involvement to have a weak positive relationship with attitude. This finding parallels that of work in IS. However, a much stronger relationship between involvement and attitude has been uncovered in other fields. There, research finds a strong relationship between involvement and extremity of attitude. In an IS context, this would suggest that highly involved users are likely to develop very positive or very negative attitudes toward a system. Not only is this latter possibility new, it obviously has important implications for the implementation process and deserves immediate attention from researchers. Questions concerning the factors that lead some individuals to form very positive attitudes while others form very negative attitudes are also suggested. One important variable in this regard is system quality. When a system is perceived to be of high quality, involved individuals form very positive attitudes concerning the system. On the other hand, when the system is perceived to be of lower quality, involved individuals form very negative attitudes. Of additional interest, no amount of participation is going to change the attitudes of these latter individuals. Indeed, it may even intensify the negative feelings.

Another area for research is suggested by the involvement research in other disciplines. In this work, highly involved individuals were found to be influenced by strong persuasive appeals emphasizing factual information and logical arguments. Such individuals were also found to behave in accordance with their attitudes. Given a high quality system, highly involved individuals are then likely to develop positive attitudes and use the system. They just need to be given the facts concerning the system. On the other hand, individuals who are low in involvement were found to be more susceptible to normative influence attempts. Factual appeals may then be less effective in changing their attitudes and behavior. Instead, less involved individuals are more likely to change attitudes and engage in various behaviors because others urge them to do so, or are doing so themselves. This enhances the importance of top management support and peer attitudes and behavior in the development of positive user attitudes and behavior in IS implementation.

## Conclusions

IS researchers have frequently critiqued the field for not grounding its research in the established theories of other disciplines and for not building a cumulative knowledge base. The user involvement research is a case in point. Ives and Olson (1984) suggest that the major weakness of user involvement research is a lack of methodological rigor. However, empirical results remain weak, in spite of a number of recently conducted, methodologically sound studies. Our own conclusion to this state of affairs is that the IS user involvement research suffers from the lack of a solid conceptual foundation.

Interestingly, our review of the involvement literatures in psychology, marketing, and organizational behavior revealed that researchers in such disciplines have also gone through a period of struggle before achieving conceptual clarity. The review has also revealed that, after widely varied starts, researchers in these disciplines now show strikingly similar patterns of thought, having converged in their conceptualizations of the involvement construct. Involvement is consistently defined as a subjective psychological state, reflecting the importance and personal relevance of an object or event. IS researchers can benefit from the accumulated knowledge of these areas, learning from the mistakes, adapting the achievements, and thereby avoiding a lot of unnecessary effort. The concepts of participation and involvement need to be distinguished and embedded in a sound theoretical framework. Empirical research may then be employed to further the distinction and foster understanding of both the importance and role of participation and involvement in the system development process.

## About the Authors

Henri Barki is assistant professor of information systems at McGill University. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the School of Business Administration, University of Western Ontario. His papers have been published in the MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, and the Canadian Journal of Administrative Sciences. His current research interests are in behavioral and cross-cultural aspects of systems development and implementation.

Jon Hartwick is associate professor of organizational behavior at McGill University. He is a member of the Academy of Management and the American Psychological Association and has been published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, the Journal of Management, and the MIS Quarterly. His current research interests include attitudes and behavioral prediction, motivation, job satisfaction and performance, and user participation and involvement.
