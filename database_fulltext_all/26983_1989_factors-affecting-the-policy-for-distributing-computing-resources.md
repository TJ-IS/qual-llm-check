## Introduction

Until the late 1970s the question of computing resource distribution had been mainly an academic issue. Information technology (IT) at that time was imposing centralized solutions regardless of the nature and the managerial philosophy of the organization. The advent of small-size and low-cost computers as well as the rapid advancement of communications technology have enabled management to fit IT to the organizational needs. Today it is possible for an organization to devise a distribution policy that is adequate for the specific managerial needs of the organization without being constrained by technological feasibility. Thus, the primary policy questions at present concern the organizational characteristics that should affect the distribution policy.

This article discusses the findings of an empirical study whose major purpose was to analyze the relationship between various organizational attributes and the deployment of hardware resources. The study used a sample of 303 organizations. The article begins by reviewing some fundamentals of information systems (IS) distribution policy. After providing the basic research hypothesis and discussing the data collection process, it then describes the sample and the distribution of the data and presents the statistical analysis. The last section discusses the findings and provides some concluding remarks.

## Abstract

This article discusses the findings of an empirical study conducted on 303 organizations. The major purpose of the study was to analyze the relationship between various organizational attributes and the deployment of hardware resources. The salient finding was that the most influential variable is the distribution of decision-making processes in the organization. The more decision making is distributed, the more hardware is distributed. No significant relationships were detected between hardware distribution and any of the following variables: organizational structure, economic sectorial association, and the size of the organization.

Keywords: Centralization/decentralization, distributed systems, systems management

ACM Categories: C.2.4, K.6.4

## Information System Distribution Policy

Distribution policy has been a major issue in IS research for many years (King, 1983). Two basic approaches to distribution policy can be identified: the technical approach and the organizational approach. The technical approach centers on the end product, i.e., the distribution policy, and attempts to analyze various modes of distribution. The organizational approach attempts to relate distribution to organizational traits. These two approaches are briefly reviewed in the next two sections.

## The technical approach

The main purpose of the technical approach is to depict various distribution alternatives and to discuss how each alternative should be evaluated and monitored. Buchanan and Linowes (1980a; 1980b) are notable proponents of this approach. They divide the IS realm into three categories: development, operation, and control. Each category is further subdivided into a number of activities, such as system analysis, training, database administration, computer operation, program maintenance, security, budgeting, and the like (16 activities in all). They suggest that each activity can be performed in a certain degree of distribution depending on the needs of the organization.

Ahituv and Sadan (1985) and Ahituv and Neumann (1986, Ch. 10) expand this approach and distinguish between distribution mode of individual applications and distribution policy for groups of applications. They suggest criteria for selecting a distribution mode and delineate the process by which an organization should devise a distribution policy.

Theoretically, the number of possible distribution modes is very large (assuming, for instance, 16 activities, each having three to five distinct distribution degrees and allowing for any combination among the various levels in each activity). In practice, however, the number of distribution alternatives could be reduced to a manageable size — manageable in the sense that management will be able to determine the rules and the degree of authority delegated to each mode (see Ahituv and Sadan, 1985). Since this study, as explained later, centers on hardware distribution, the discussion is confined to only three major degrees of distribution:

1. Centralized system: A system that is based on processors located in one site (except for microcomputers and remote workstations).

2. Distributed system: A number of processors deployed in different locations and linked in a common communications network.

3. Decentralized system: A number of processors deployed in different locations, not connected to a common network and functioning autonomously.

## The organizational approach

The technical approach outlined above provides a systematic tool for distribution analysis and design. However, it does not relate the distribution policy to organizational factors. This facet is covered by the organizational approach, which emphasizes the relationship between organizational characteristics and the distribution policy (Cash, et al., 1988; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982; King, 1983; Olson and Chervany, 1980). The organizational characteristics that have been identified as affecting the distribution policy are:

1. Organizational structure: Several studies state that the formal structure of the organization (e.g., functional structure, product structure, matrix structure) is a cornerstone in determining the distribution policy (e.g., Buchanan and Linowes, 1980a; Cash, et al., 1988; Lorin, 1981; Scherr, 1978; Shirey, 1980).

2. Size: Scherr (1978) suggests that the size of the organization is a factor affecting the degree of distribution.

3. Managerial philosophy and style as revealed by the distribution of decision-making processes: This factor is mentioned in Ahituv and Neumann (1986); Buchanan and Linowes (1980a); Cash, et al. (1988); King (1983); and Lorin (1981) although none of them suggests a method to measure the distribution of decision-making processes.

Note that the two approaches to distribution policy are not contradictory but rather, complementary. The organizational approach should be the first step in devising the policy by deriving the overall guidelines from organizational variables. The technical approach can then serve to refine the policy and articulate the detailed rules imposed on the organizational units (e.g., Ahituv and Neumann, 1986; Cash, et al., 1988; Davis and Olson, 1985; Siegel, 1956; Sprague and McNurlin, 1986; Tom, 1987).

This study draws from the two approaches. The idea to relate organizational characteristics to IS distribution was taken from the organizational approach. The characteristics examined were size, structure, and decision-making process distribution. The concept of distribution degrees was drawn from the technical approach. It was confined to the distribution of hardware because many studies suggest that hardware distribution is a basic prerequisite to the distribution of other resources, such as software development and operation (see Laskey, 1982; Martin, 1981; Scherr, 1987; Tenenbaum and Van Renesse, 1985; Williams, 1982; Withington, 1987; and Zachman, 1987).

## Hypothesis and Research Methodology

The basic premise of this research is that there is a relationship between organizational characteristics and the hardware distribution policy of an organization. Moreover, since the policy should derive from organizational traits and should support organizational strategy, the relationship is one-directional, i.e., from the characteristics to the policy. Support for this view can be found in Ahituv and Neumann (1986); Cash, et al. (1988); Davis and Olson (1985); McLean and Soden (1977); Sprague and McNurlin (1986); and Wiseman (1985).

The operationalization of the variables was based on organizational behavior research (e.g., Daft, 1983; Mintzberg, 1979), and on IS research (e.g., Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; 1982; Olson and Chervany, 1980). The operationalized definition of the variables is as follows:

1. Size is measured by the number of employees. This is then divided into three categories: small (1-250), medium (251-1000), and large (1001 and above).

2. Structure is a nominal variable of four categories: functional, product, matrix, and integrated (namely, a combination of several categories).

3. Decision-making-process-distribution is an ordinal variable of three categories: centralized, integrated, and decentralized (the terms are explained later).

A fourth organizational variable addressed in this study is sector classification. Data were collected from five different economic sectors: manufacturing, commerce, service, non-profit service, and horizontally integrated conglomerate. Thus, although no previous evidence to a relationship between economic sector (nominal variable) and hardware distribution was found in the IS literature, it was examined through the empirical data.

The dependent variable, the distribution of hardware, is (as previously mentioned) an ordinal variable of three categories: centralized, distributed, and decentralized.

The data for this study were collected by means of a questionnaire. The parts of the questionnaire pertaining to this study are presented in Appendix A. (The questionnaire was also used for other studies.) Questionnaires were mailed to a random sample of 1360 organizations in Israel; 303 were returned. This is a return rate of 22.28%, which is considered to be relatively low. However, the sample size of 303 organizations was large enough for the statistical analysis. The questionnaires were completed by the highest ranked IS manager, by the organization's CIO, or by a senior executive to whom the CIO reports. The next section summarizes the collected data.

## Statistical Analysis

As pointed out earlier, the basic premise of this research is to show that there is a relationship between organizational characteristics and the policy on hardware distribution in an organization. This study examines four organizational variables — two nominal (structure and sector) and two ordinal (size and decision-making distribution). The hardware distribution variable is ordinal. The purpose of the analysis is to identify relationships between any of the organizational variables and the hardware distribution variable. Three non-parametric statistical tests were employed to measure these relationships:

1. C Measurement (contingency coefficient): This measurement is based on the chi-square test; it examines the relationship between two variables by analyzing the difference between the table of observed data and the table of expected data.

2. Lambda Measurement: This measurement examines the relationship between two variables by analyzing whether the information on one of the variables may assist in predicting the values of the other variable.

3. Kendall's Tau B (Kendall's rank correlation coefficient): This measurement tests the relationship between two ordinal variables. The measurement determines the direction of the relationship (positive, negative, or no relationship), as well as its intensity (Blalock, 1972; Nie, et al., 1975; Siegel, 1956; Upton, 1978).

A mandatory condition for applying the chi-square test and the C measurement is that no more than 20 percent of the cells of the expected frequency table can contain figures that are less than 5, and no one cell can be less than 1. Since this condition is somewhat limiting, in this study we used the lambda measurement whenever C was not applicable. (For more information on these tests, see Blalock (1972), Nie, et al. (1975), Plackart (1974), Siegel (1956), Upton (1978).) The following subsections analyze the relationships between each organizational variable and hardware distribution.

## Organizational structure and hardware distribution

The results of the statistical analysis of this relationship are provided in Appendix B. The cross-tabulation matrix clearly shows that C could not be applied in this case (four out of 12 cells contained frequencies lower than 5; one cell was 0.69, which is less than 1). The result for lambda was 0.05217. This value was considered very low (lambda ranges from zero to one where zero implies no relationship) and indicated only a very weak relationship among the two variables.

In order to recheck this relationship, further analyses, focussing on omitting or aggregating certain categories, were performed. None of those trials provided any indication of a solid relationship between the two variables (see Zviran, 1988). Hence, we concluded that information on organizational structure cannot assist in predicting the degree of hardware distribution.

## Economic sector and hardware distribution

The results of the statistical analysis of this relationship are provided in Appendix C. The cross-tabulation matrix clearly shows that C could not be applied in this case either. The result for lambda was 0.10435. Similar to the previous case, this value was considered very low and indicated only a very weak relationship among the two variables.

In order to recheck this relationship, further analyses, focussing on omitting or aggregating certain categories, were performed. None of those trials provided any indication for a solid relationship between the two variables (see Zviran, 1988). Hence, we concluded that information on sectorial association cannot assist in predicting the level of hardware distribution.

## Organization size and hardware distribution

The size of the organization and the hardware distribution are two ordinal variables whose mutual relationship was tested by using Kendall's tau B (see Appendix D). The value equaled 0.27048, with a high level of significance (p<.0000). Since tau B ranges from zero to one, this value indicated a low level of relationship. Hence, we concluded that information on organization size cannot assist in predicting the level of hardware distribution.

## Decision-making processes and hardware distribution

Similar to the previous case, the relationship examined in this instance was between two ordinal variables (see Appendix E); Kendall's tau B equaled 0.78947, and the result was significant $(p < .0000)$ . This value was considered very high, thus indicating a firm relationship among the variables.

The cross-tabulation shown in Appendix E indicates that as the distribution level of decisions increases, so does the distribution of hardware resources. In other words, organizations whose decision-making processes are centralized will tend to centralize their hardware; organizations whose decision-making processes are more decentralized will tend to decentralize the deployment of hardware. Since the independent variable (i.e., decision-making processes) was determined without any connection to the information systems in the organization, we concluded that this variable can assist in predicting the level of hardware distribution in an organization.

## Combination of organizational factors

In order to test the data for any additional relationships, further analyses of the data were performed. These included the testing of various combinations of any two organizational variables (size, structure, sector classification, and decision-making processes) and hardware distribution (see Zviran, 1988). A basic problem in all these analyses was a low number of observations in many cells. Nevertheless, none of those trials provided any indication for a solid relationship between any combination of two organizational variables and hardware distribution (lambda values ranged from 0.0532 to 0.1147). Hence, we concluded that the only relationship found in our sample was between the organization's decision-making processes and hardware distribution.

As a concluding remark to this section, the reader should note that some of the cross-tabulation matrix cells have low numbers, which might impact the quality of the results.

## Discussion and Conclusions

Previous studies in this area have reported similar findings. Garrity (1963) examined 211 organizations and found a relationship between the distribution level of the organization and IS distributions. Blau, et al. (1976) reported that a higher level of IS distribution was detected in organizations where there were more layers in management hierarchy and larger geographical dispersion of organizational units. Ein-Dor and Segev (1982) found a positive relationship between distribution of organizational units and distribution of hardware; the intensity of the relationship, though, was weaker than the one reported here.

There are three major traits that distinguish this study from previous ones. First, the direct relationship between decision processes and hardware distribution is unique to this study. Second, the level of significance $p=0.05$ and the intensity are higher. Third, the sample size (303 organizations) is larger than in any of the previous studies.

Another salient observation of this study is the absence of a significant relationship between hardware distribution and any of the three other organizational variables: size, structure, and economic sector. This result is somewhat counterintuitive, at least with regard to size and structure. Previous related studies do not report consistent results. For instance, Hickson, et al. (1969) suggest that there is a relationship between organizational structure and IS structure (where IS structure includes, among other variables, hardware distribution). In contrast, Olson and Chervany (1980) report that they could not identify a relationship between hardware distribution and organization size or structure. Ein-Dor and Segev (1982) report a possible weak relationship between hardware distribution and size.

It should be noted that the survey was conducted in Israel and the sample consists of Israeli firms. Israeli economy is very similar to most Western economies in terms of information technology (Couger, et al., 1987; 1989), managerial style, production and services technology, and the like. Although we believe that the findings of this research may also apply for other countries, there is still a possibility that there are cultural, political, and other factors that could have influenced the study results. Thus, a comparative analysis of various countries might be a promising subject for future research.

In summary, the results of this study suggest that out of four organizational characteristics, only the distribution of decision-making processes is reflected in the degree of hardware distribution in an organization.
