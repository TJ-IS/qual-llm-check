## Abstract

Problems can arise in decentralized organizations when usage-based chargeback systems are implemented to control computing resources. A case study of a company that is changing from negotiated pricing to full cost pricing illustrates the fact that concerns about fairness can arise not only with respect to specific characteristics of the chargeback system (e.g., understandability, controllability), but also as a result of central management policies governing internal transactions between the users and MIS. In particular, this examination identifies three factors that affect the fairness perceptions of autonomous divisional managers: (1) a high degree of interdependence between user divisions and MIS such that the behavior of MIS could have a significant impact on divisional performance; (2) restrictions on divisional managers' freedom to purchase computer services from external sources; and (3) the tendency for full cost to exceed market prices early in the life of a data center. A dual pricing approach may be an interim solution to reduce conflict and improve user perceptions of fairness.

Keywords: Chargeback systems, chargeout systems, cost allocation, resource allocation, transfer pricing

ACM Categories: K.6.2

## Introduction

A report issued by the Management Accounting Practices Committee of the National Association of Accountants (1987) indicates that, due to the “substantial and growing nature of the costs” associated with information systems, the allocation of computer costs has become a serious concern for most operating executives. Supporting this assertion is the fact that more and more companies have begun to install usage-based chargeback systems in an effort to manage and control the allocation of costly computer resources (Bergeron, 1986a; 1986b).

Although the stated objectives of these systems vary, most chargeback systems are designed to directly or indirectly affect organizational decision making where the use and/or management of computing facilities is concerned (Turney, 1977). A growing body of literature evidences academic concerns regarding the effects of chargeback systems on economic decision making and the demand for computer services (Bernard, et al., 1977; Cushing, 1976; Dearden and Nolan, 1973; McKell, et al., 1979; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987), on the evaluation of MIS performance (Bernard, et al., 1977; Cushing, 1976; Schaller, 1974; Sollenberger, 1977), and on user involvement in MIS activities (Drury, 1980; McFarlan and McKenney, 1983; Nielsen, 1970; Olson and Ives, 1983).

Surveys conducted in both chargeback environments (Drury, 1980) and non-chargeback environments (Choudhury, et al., 1986) provide evidence that MIS managers generally favor chargeback to control the utilization of scarce information resources and to increase both user and management involvement in MIS activities. In a more broadly focused study of information system controls (Mautz, et al., 1983), MIS managers were in agreement that chargeback systems provide these benefits. They noted, however, that these systems are difficult to operate on an equitable basis. Their concerns about the fairness of chargeback systems were shared by line managers who indicated that, regardless of the type of chargeback system used, “they believe they are charged extravagantly for serv ices provided and that they have little ability to control the costs" (Mautz, et al., 1983, p. 35).

Although issues of equity and fairness have received relatively little attention in the chargeback literature, managers' perceptions of the fairness of these systems may be a crucial determinant of chargeback system success. Most researchers would agree with Anthony's (1957) statement that a control system cannot be effective unless the people affected by it believe the standards to be both reasonable and equitable. A charging scheme that is regarded as unfairly influencing managers' financial performance may defeat the system's purpose by lessening managers' motivation to make sound economic decisions (Shillinglaw, 1977). Control systems that are perceived to be inequitable may also trigger a variety of unanticipated, dysfunctional behaviors as managers experience diminished autonomy and attempt to circumvent the system (Thomas, 1980).

The intent of this article is to offer a systematic analysis of the problem of perceived fairness in chargeback environments, focusing specifically on the policy related issues that arise when usage-based chargeback schemes are employed in decentralized organizations. Of primary concern is the conflict that occurs when corporate management establishes restrictive control policies that impinge on managers' decision-making autonomy. Since most previous chargeback research has been concerned with the design of the chargeback system itself, the policy questions identified here have received relatively little attention in the MIS literature. To facilitate understanding, a brief case study is presented as a vehicle for discussion.

## Previous Research on Chargeback Systems

Although most theoreticians agree that chargeback systems can have a potentially significant effect on users' motivations and decisions with respect to computer utilization, relatively little behavioral research has been done in this area. A number of surveys have focused on describing current chargeback practices (Drury, 1980; Solomon and Tsay, 1985). Others have looked at MIS managers' attitudes toward chargeback systems (Choudhury, et al., 1986; Drury, 1980) and selected organizational characteristics that are associated with chargeback vs. non-chargeback environments (Drury, 1982). However, only three theoretically based empirical studies on chargeback have been published to date in mainstream MIS journals. Each of these three studies has attempted to link specific characteristics of chargeback systems with selected measures of effectiveness.

## Three empirical studies

## Nolan

Nolan (1977) was the first to explore the organizational consequences of these control systems in a study on the effect of chargeback systems on manager attitudes toward information systems in general. His research model was based on the observation that, for a chargeback system to be effective with respect to its control objectives, it “must be perceived to be a fair system for performance evaluation” (Nolan, 1977, p.180). Equating “fairness” with “maturity,” Nolan selected four aspects of the chargeback system and the administrative process surrounding it as measures of “maturity”:

1. Understandability — the extent to which managers can associate costs that are charged to them with the specific activities necessary to carry out their functional tasks.

2. Controllability — the extent to which managers can control their computer-related costs.

3. Cost/Benefit Incidence — whether the manager responsible for task accomplishment also receives the bill for related computer services.

4. Accountability — the way in which computer-related costs are included in a manager's performance evaluation.

Chargeback systems were categorized as “mature” to the extent that managers gave them high ratings on these four dimensions.

Based on the responses of managers in 13 companies, Nolan classified their chargeback systems into five maturity groups corresponding with his previously published Stage Hypothesis (Nolan, 1973). A regression model was then used to test the relationship between maturity group and user attitudes toward information systems.

Although Nolan's results suggest that manager attitudes toward information systems may depend in part on the “maturity” of the chargeback system as he defined it, only 4% of the variability between companies with respect to user attitudes was explained by the maturity variables. In addition, a number of methodological problems affect the interpretation of his results. For example, assignment to maturity groups corresponding to his Stage Model was of questionable value, given both the small sample size (n=13) and the lack of empirical support for this model (Benbasat, et al., 1984). Furthermore, the variables selected to assess “maturity” relate to both the extent to which chargeback information is integrated into the performance evaluation system of the sample organizations and the extent to which managers can control their individual outcomes. Thus, it is unclear whether the reported differences in user attitudes were the result of deficiencies in the information provided by the chargeback system (e.g., lack of understandability) or failure to make appropriate use of chargeback information in the performance evaluation process (e.g., no accountability).

## Olson and Ives

A second study of the effects of chargeback systems on user attitudes was conducted by Olson and Ives (1982), who hypothesized a relationship between the “quality of the user interface” and user attitudes toward information systems. Despite the change in terminology, this investigation was largely a replication of Nolan’s study, unencumbered by the limiting framework of his Stage Hypothesis. The dimensions used to measure “quality” were the same four Nolan used to measure “maturity,” and the same attitude measurement instrument was used as well. Olson and Ives extended Nolan’s study by classifying the chargeback schemes based on their approach (overhead allocation, expense allocation, or resource rate-based charging) and including a second dependent variable, user involvement, in the development and use of computer resources. Statistical testing was again done using an aggregate measure of “quality” or “maturity”; however, the chargeback systems were not pre-classified into groups as in Nolan’s study.

Contrary to Nolan's (1977) findings, Olson and Ives' results showed no significant relationship regarding the effect of “interface quality” on user attitudes or user involvement in the development and use of computer resources. They did observe, however, a decrease in mean information satisfaction scores as the allocation methods became more sophisticated. Where resource rate-based allocation schemes were used, users were less satisfied with their information systems than were those who were operating in an expense allocation environment.

Olson and Ives' study suffers from the same lack of rigor in operationalizing the independent variable that characterized Nolan's study. As a result, it is not clear whether their seemingly contradictory findings were a result of problems with the charging mechanism itself or the way in which chargeback information was used by the firms studied.

## Bergeron

More recently, Bergeron (1986a) speculated that the conflicting results produced by these earlier studies might be the result of “an incomplete characterization of the chargeback system itself and of its environment” (p. 226). To remedy this oversight, he proposed “chargeback information use,” defined as the extent to which “users analyze their data-processing charges with the intent to control the spending of data-processing resources” (Bergeron, 1986b, p. 188) as an intervening variable between chargeback system characteristics and user attitudes. Bergeron’s model, which shows the relationship between his research and the work of Nolan (1977) and Olson and Ives (1982), is shown in Figure 1. The chargeback system characteristics tested by Bergeron include:

1. Accountability

2. Authority over data processing activities

3. User involvement in MIS budget preparation

4. Cost variability (overhead vs. resource rate-based allocation)

5. Quality of billing information (meaningfulness, accuracy, timeliness)

Bergeron's analysis suggests that each of the five independent variables is significantly correlated with chargeback information use in the 53 firms studied, but only cost variability, accountability and involvement were good predictors of use. These results are not surprising for several reasons. First, they suggest that billing information is more likely to be used by managers who are charged in proportion to their use of computing resources than by managers who receive a simple overhead allocation. Since overhead allocation schemes are largely based on fixed formulas unrelated to usage, managers are not likely to receive much in the way of specific billing information from them, nor is there much they can do to affect their allocations in any meaningful way. Second, it is not surprising that managers who are more involved in data processing budgeting activities and are held accountable for any variances that result from their usage decisions make greater use of chargeback system reports — their performance evaluation and rewards ultimately depend on their efforts to control their own usage. Third, as Bergeron himself points out, the lack of correlation between “quality of billing information provided” and “chargeback report usage” more than likely stems from the fact that when managers are held accountable for their charges, they may feel compelled to use whatever information is available to them for control purposes, regardless of the quality of that information. And finally, although it is difficult to imagine that a chargeback system can effectively achieve its objectives if managers do not use the control reports generated by it, report usage alone does not ensure effectiveness. As Prakash and Rappaport (1977) point out, the very fact that information is used for evaluative purposes may lead managers to manipulate it to reflect favorably on their own behavior.

## Limitations of previous research

While the three empirical studies reviewed here represent a start toward developing a comprehensive theory of chargeback system effectiveness, the scope of their efforts is so broad that the results are difficult to interpret and apply. Each of these research projects sought to identify the characteristics of effective chargeback schemes by examining the cost allocation mechanisms used in a structurally diverse group of organizations. But different allocation approaches have different control objectives and therefore must be evaluated using different effectiveness criteria (for an excellent discussion of these differences, see Biddle and Steinberg, 1984). Furthermore, as contingency theorists in management accounting point out, there may not be a single control system that is universally appropriate in all organizations under all circumstances (Otley, 1980). If this is so, any attempt to assess the effectiveness of a given control system must take into account both the characteristics and objectives of the organization in which it is embedded.

Accountants who have studied the resource allocation problem point out that the nature of this problem depends to a large extent on the way in which decision-making authority is distributed across an organization (Thomas, 1980). Thus, the allocation problems that arise in centralized organizations are different from those that occur in decentralized organizations (Bruns and Waterhouse, 1975; King, 1983). In an effort to define the scope of the chargeback problem more narrowly, this article is specifically concerned with the behavioral issues that arise in conjunction with resource rate-based chargeback systems that are implemented in decentralized organizations. In the next section, the nature of this particular resource allocation dilemma is briefly outlined.

## The Transfer Pricing Dilemma

Chargeback systems represent a specific case of the more general problem of transfer pricing, which has concerned accountants since the advent of the decentralized firm. All cost allocation procedures (including chargeback schemes)

involve the setting of prices for products or services that are traded between organizational units. However, the term “transfer pricing” is generally used to describe internal pricing schemes employed in decentralized firms where at least one of the organizational units involved is an autonomous divisional unit whose manager is evaluated based on divisional profits (Horngren, 1977).

Organizations are considered decentralized when top management delegates both the authority and responsibility for making important decisions to lower-level managers (Simon, 1954). Decentralization is generally believed to provide a number of benefits, including increased efficiency in information processing and decision making, and managerial motivation (Arrow, 1959; Demski, 1976). If decisions are made at the divisional level, where managers have firsthand knowledge of both the production process and the marketplace, less information exchange is required. Furthermore, their nearness to the marketplace allows divisional managers to make decisions in a more timely fashion (Ronen and McKinney, 1970). Greater efficiency and motivation may also result if divisional managers have the sense that they are running their own businesses (Solomons, 1965).

Decentralizing would be a relatively simple method of dealing with organizational problems such as firm size and diversity if there were no need for interactions among divisions in their day-to-day business dealings. Typically, however, interdependencies do exist among operating units, because pools of corporate resources must be shared among profit centers (Vancil, 1979). When organizational units are highly interdependent, the presence of autonomous unit managers can result in control problems. For example, some managers may be tempted to overconsume scarce resources to improve their own outcomes at the expense of others if corporate management has no controls for preventing such behavior. Coordination problems can also result if the decisions made by one manager can adversely affect the outcome of another (Zimmerman, 1979). To ensure joint profit maximization, some mechanism for coordinating the allocation of scarce resources among competing divisions is required (Hass, 1968).

In economic theory, prices are the primary mechanism for allocating resources in the marketplace. Economists and accountants believe that, if such market-clearing mechanisms can be simulated in the internal resource allocation process, an optimal allocation can be achieved to ensure that overall organizational profits are maximized (Hass, 1968; Singer, et al., 1968). Transfer prices are intended to act as such a coordinating device; they are, in effect, proxies for the market prices that are missing when divisions trade with one another. The justification for using transfer prices to control and allocate resources is based on the assumption that “users can and will alter their pattern of use over time in response to changes in the (transfer) price” (Kriebel, et al., 1977, p. 539). Thus, the effectiveness of a given transfer price depends on is ability to cause divisional managers to act as if they were separate units competing for scarce resources in a market environment.

From the viewpoint of central management, responsibility for corporate performance can only be effectively assessed if managerial goals are congruent with those of the enterprise (Thomas, 1980). When each divisional manager is evaluated and rewarded on the basis of his own unit's performance, however, the goals of the divisions and those of central management do not always coincide. Solomons (1965) identifies two primary purposes for internal pricing schemes, which, together, suggest how goal congruence can be achieved through transfer pricing:

1. Transfer prices are designed to motivate profit center managers to make “sound” economic decisions — to use the available resources efficiently and effectively and thus to act in a manner consistent with the best interests of the corporation as a whole.

2. Transfer prices provide a basis for evaluating the performance of individual responsibility centers in terms of their separate contributions to corporate goals.

Ronen and McKinney (1970) were in agreement with Solomons with respect to these two objectives but added an important constraint: The transfer pricing system must motivate managers to increase their operating efficiency, without a loss in the autonomy of divisions as profit centers.

But achieving these objectives while preserving the autonomy of divisional managers can be a difficult problem (Grabski, 1985). How can a division be truly autonomous when it is subject to the constraints of a control system that, by design, limits divisional managers' choices to those that result in central management optimal results? Can a control system that ensures optimal results for central management provide a fair basis for evaluating divisional performance? Answers to these questions are important, as the effectiveness of the transfer pricing scheme in motivating economically sound behavior ultimately depends on managers' perceptions of the control system as a fair basis for performance evaluation. If managers believe they are not fairly rewarded for their contributions to the company, they may behave in ways that negatively affect corporate performance (Eccles, 1985).

Like transfer pricing systems in general, chargeback systems are unlikely to motivate sound, economic decision making if divisional managers perceive them to be unfair. Therefore, in order to design effective chargeback systems for decentralized organizations, MIS researchers must first determine what factors affect managers' perceptions of allocative fairness. In the next section, a case study is presented as a vehicle for beginning this identification process.

## Specialty Publishing, Inc.: A Company in Transition

The data for this case study were collected through semi-structured interviews with managers of a decentralized organization that was revising the cost allocation procedures for all of its centralized service departments. Because of the announced change in allocation procedures, this company provided a rather unique opportunity to investigate the chargeback situation at a time when managers throughout the organization were actively engaged in evaluating the impact of increased computer costs on their budgets. The interviews were held during the budget preparation cycle, shortly after the allocation changes were announced. As a result, the problem of allocative fairness had been widely discussed and debated, and the managers interviewed had developed strong opinions on the matter. Interviews focusing on the computer billing system were conducted with the executive who instituted the new cost allocation procedures, the MIS manager, and several divisional managers who use computing services. The divisional managers selected to be interviewed included the two users with the largest budgets for computing services (“heavy users”), as well as several managers whose divisions were not using a significant amount of computer time (“light users”). (These light users were selected solely on the basis of availability.) Each interview was taped in its entirety and transcribed to ensure accurate reporting of the participants’ perspectives; an attempt has been made in this case narrative to incorporate the viewpoints of all those interviewed.

## Background

Specialty Publishing, Inc. (SPI) $^{2}$ is a communications company that provides specialized information and related services to health care professionals. The majority of SPI's publications are periodicals distributed free of charge, deriving their revenues largely from advertising. In addition to its traditional magazine offerings, SPI also provides up-to-date pharmaceutical information in machine-readable form to some segments of the market.

At the time of these interviews, SPI was in the early stages of budget preparation for the coming year. Prior to the start of the annual budgeting cycle, the organization's new controller announced a significant change in the system of allocating service department costs. Under the previous controller, a variety of allocations schemes had been used, including negotiation between the service department manager and each divisional manager. The policy announced by the new controller stated that all divisions would pay full cost for centrally provided services, such as information systems, text processing, editorial services, art, and research. Changes in the allocation scheme had also been announced for the internal charges generated for rent and occupancy, telephone, and copier expenses.

## The data processing environment

At the time of the interviews, SPI's data center had a staff of 32 people and a budget of nearly \$3 million per year. An ATEX text processing system provided support for magazine production work. An IBM 4300 series mainframe was used for general purpose computing, which supported the inquiry, analysis, and transaction processing activities of a broad base of users throughout the organization. SPI operated its computing facility 2½ shifts a day, five days a week. Although overall utilization of the IBM system ran about 40% of capacity, prime time use was predominantly interactive, and frequent bottlenecks occurred. The ATEX system was running at close to 90% of capacity and had a limited number of communications ports available for new connections.

Under the old allocation scheme, a standard job accounting package was run monthly to calculate resource utilization by job on the IBM system. For most routine applications, charges were then calculated using a fixed rate schedule. The rates were established somewhat arbitrarily by MIS and were in most cases substantially lower than those charged by external service bureaus. Time of day differentials were not used. At the discretion of the MIS manager, discounted prices were sometimes offered as an inducement to new users to encourage the implementation of a new application, but these reduced rates were generally in effect for a limited time period only.

Mailing list maintenance activities, which comprise the bulk of special processing services at SPI, were handled on the IBM machine and charged at a prespecified rate per item processed or per operation performed (select, merge, etc.). The rates varied from user to user, however, because they were negotiated in advance based on the rates charged by service bureaus that specialize in mailing list processing.

The lowest rates were typically charged to users who were both persistent and skillful negotiators. Since the chargeback system had historically been used to manage the flow of work through data processing, the level of demand for the system at any given time was also an important factor in pricing negotiations. Excess capacity on the IBM system and the absence of a full cost chargeback policy tended to keep processing rates low relative to external market rates. As a result, less than half the costs associated with the IBM system were charged to users. Text processing rates were generally higher than the IBM rates, but they were not distributed in any proportionate manner. Furthermore, despite heavy utilization, the total charges to all ATEX users did not cover the costs of operating the text processing system.

## Corporate management's perspective

SPI's new controller decided to take a strong stand on full-cost allocation of all service department costs, based on the belief that these services would be misused if the divisions were not made to bear the full cost. His stated objective was to better monitor the profitability of the publishing divisions (profit centers) as well as the performance of the MIS department. He was particularly concerned about the fact that SPI had been growing rapidly, adding staff, and increasing the amount and types of services provided to the divisions by corporate service departments. He believed SPI would soon be facing some major investment decisions — not only was the company approaching full capacity in its two office buildings, but the text processing system was nearing its limit. Thus, the controller viewed full-cost pricing as a way to reduce the demand for both physical space and processing time. It was his contention that full-cost pricing would better approximate the true costs to SPI and would also force divisional managers to evaluate their needs more carefully and eliminate non-essential activities.

With the support of the executive committee, the new cost allocation scheme was developed under the controller's direction. Cost analysts met with each service department manager and studied their operations to determine the most equitable way of allocating the costs. Since the revised policy mandated full cost chargeback, anticipated expenditures for the coming year were fully documented in the process. The allocation changes expected to have the biggest impact on the organization were data processing, and rent and occupancy.

While capacity considerations were foremost in the controller's mind, the fact that the IBM system was under-utilized also led to an edict prohibiting the profit centers from purchasing computer services from outside vendors if MIS could meet their needs internally. This decision, coupled with the imminent “price increases” arising from the changes in the allocation scheme, led to heated discussions between the controller and some profit center managers who objected to the new policy. The controller believed, however, that the additional charges could be justified because of the added control users would have over internal data processing activities.

## The MIS manager's perspective

The vice president of MIS at SPI was less than enthusiastic about the proposed change in cost allocation procedures. He viewed the chargeback system as a tool for regulating demand and balancing the workload across shifts, not as a motivational device for cost reduction. While the old variable pricing scheme sometimes led to conflict (e.g., when profit center managers compared notes on the cost of various services), he expected the new system to have a negative impact on experimentation and creativity and to precipitate additional conflict within the organization.

The MIS manager raised several issues relating to the fairness of the new policy. Because the IBM system was under-utilized and not charged back at full cost, users had become accustomed to artificially low prices. Under the new policy, rates would be substantially higher for most users, given the forecast level of demand at that time. If the rate increase caused some users to consider alternatives other than SPI's MIS department (e.g., microcomputers), overall utilization of the IBM system might be further reduced. Since data center costs are essentially fixed over the short term prices would most certainly rise again before the next budgeting cycle if any applications were removed and not replaced by new ones.

Concurrent with the new cost allocation announcements, the MIS manager had been assigned the added responsibility of maintaining a reasonably high level of utilization on the IBM system by selling computer services to other outside organizations. In order to achieve this goal, he felt he must provide some guaranteed level of service in terms of both response time and turnaround time; this could lead to a reduction in the level of service provided to his current users within SPI. Thus, he argued, SPI's internal users might eventually find themselves paying higher rates for poorer service.

## The users' perspective

Users' reactions to the new pricing policy were mixed. Light system users were generally ambivalent about the change in data processing charges. They were more concerned about the effects of the newly imposed rent and occupancy charges because these charges would have a greater effect on their profitability. Heavy system users were greatly disturbed by the new policy, because they believed it would have a negative impact on their overall performance and affect their bonuses the following year. Since the mandate on internal sourcing effectively removed their freedom of choice, they argued that they should not be held responsible for their computing services charges.

The strongest negative reactions came from those departments that incurred significant processing charges for mailing list maintenance activities on the IBM system. These users pointed out that external market rates for such services were much lower than SPI's new price schedule and that they would have more “clout” with outside service bureaus when problems arose. They also believed that mandated internal sourcing would create an excessive level of dependence on the MIS department and weaken the users' bargaining position.

When asked what they proposed to do about their computer-related charges under the new system, light system users indicated that they would simply learn to live with them. However, several heavy system users indicated that they had already had some heated discussions with the controller over the new allocation procedures and that they planned to continue fighting the change. One heavy user's solution was to look for more ways to do his unit's work on personal computers. Although such a strategy would require an additional investment in hardware and software, it would eliminate some portion of the seemingly uncontrollable monthly costs associated with centralized computing services. Another manager voiced his intention to continue pushing for the option to use an outside service bureau. Curiously, none of the managers interviewed indicated that they planned to review their current processing activities to identify jobs that could be run less frequently or discontinued altogether.

## Perceived Fairness: The Key to Chargeback Systems Effectiveness

The SPI case provides a good illustration of how the chargeback policies established by corporate management can serve to emphasize the sometimes conflicting goals of managers in decentralized organizations. Although all of the managers interviewed were supportive of the controller's efforts to reduce costs, most seemed to feel that the methods he had chosen were unlikely to achieve this goal without precipitating other problems. Furthermore, despite philosophical differences between the controller and both the divisional managers and the MIS manager, there was little evidence that any of these individuals had a personal “ax to grind.” For SPI's heavy users, the proposed new chargeback policy was an emotionally laden issue as well as the source of some tension and conflict between departments. For the divisional executives, the controversy centered around the question of fairness as they struggled to reconcile the discrepancy between their responsibilities as relatively autonomous profit center managers and a chargeback scheme that severely limited their freedom of choice. In addition, these managers questioned the fairness of the proposed full-cost pricing scheme, which they believed would negatively affect their future performance outcomes.

## Definitional problems with fairness

The fact that fairness was a serious issue for SPI's divisional managers is not too surprising, because fairness is generally believed to be an important concept in most accounting cost allocation models (Ayres, 1985). However, the terms "fairness" and "equity," which are used synonymously, have been defined in a variety of ways. For example, some have argued that there be no "free riders" — all users must be charged something (Moriarty, 1975). Others have suggested that units using equal amounts of a service be charged the same amount and that the charge for one user not be affected by the level of usage of another (Jensen, 1977). When constraints such as these are combined, the resulting concepts of equity can become quite complex and unwieldy. (For examples and discussion, see Thomas 1980.)

Seemingly rational definitions like these imply that there exists an optimal way of allocating resources that would be viewed as fair by SPI's managers and, indeed, by most people. However, evidence suggests that people are more likely to agree on the basic rule of distributive justice — that rewards be proportional to contributions — than they are to agree on its concrete applications (Zajac, 1985). Fairness is, in fact, a difficult term to operationalize because its meaning is largely context-dependent.

Given that allocative equity is largely a subjective quality, it is likely that perceptions of fairness are more important in practice than rational definitions of fairness. For this reason, this discussion is concerned with understanding what people perceive as fair rather than fairness in some absolute sense.

## Interdependence and perceived fairness

Nolan (1977) contends that, to be effective with respect to its objectives, a chargeback system “must be perceived to be a fair system for performance evaluation” (p. 180). There is agreement in the literature that for performance evaluation to be regarded as fair in decentralized firms like SPI, divisional managers must have the necessary authority to affect those outcomes or performances on which they will ultimately be judged (Dopuch, et al., 1982; Eccles, 1985; Goonan, 1987). This “criterion of controllability” can be interpreted to require that a manager’s performance evaluation not be influenced by the actions of other managers (Demski, 1976; Thompson, 1967). Nevertheless, the activities of one group often affect the outcomes of another when divisions trade with one another.

For example, managers who were heavy users of SPI's computing facilities were quick to point out that they could not possibly meet their publishing deadlines (and thus achieve their operating goals) without information-processing support. They also recognized that both the prices and priorities for using information resources were determined by the MIS manager. Thus, their outcomes could be substantially influenced by the behavior of MIS. In companies like SPI, where a high degree of interdependence between units affects the control that managers believe they have over crucial resources, the system used to price internal transfers can clearly affect their perceptions of fairness.

## Sourcing restrictions and perceived fairness

Under the old rules, SPI's administrative groups and publishing divisions were treated as independent entities, each having the authority to choose whether to buy from or sell to another unit within the organization. Because MIS was forced to compete for the business of SPI's publishing divisions in order to remain viable, the MIS manager had to make it attractive for the divisions to have their processing performed internally. The two obvious ways to do this were through better service and lower prices. Having a limited target market (internal users) and no mandate to operate at a profit, MIS frequently used bargain prices to stimulate demand for its services.

A transfer pricing policy such as this that allows internal buyers and sellers to choose their own trading partners and negotiate mutually agreeable prices is referred to as a policy of “exchange autonomy” (Eccles, 1985). Since SPI’s profit center managers were not required to trade internally, they retained full authority over their business operations and thus could expect to be held fully accountable for their results. In situations where “exchange autonomy” governs internal transfers, divisional managers have both the responsibility and authority to operate as independent entities, and questions of fairness with respect to the internal pricing policy seldom arise (Eccles, 1985).

Under the new rules, the relationship between the MIS department and the publishing divisions will change dramatically as the divisional managers lose their authority to choose their own suppliers and, as a result, become increasingly dependent on MIS for the achievement of their performance goals. Although relations between MIS and the divisions have traditionally been amicable, the divisional managers are nonetheless concerned by their apparent loss of both bargaining power and control over their own destinies.

Mandated internal sourcing policies like SPI's are most commonly implemented in companies following a strategy of vertical integration, for without such a mandate, it is almost impossible to implement such a strategy (Eccles, 1985). Not surprisingly, most large firms are vertically integrated with respect to their administrative functions, including MIS. Economies of internal control and coordination alone make it beneficial to perform these services internally rather than contract them out (Porter, 1980). In addition, economies of scale make it advantageous for many firms to centralize the provision of information services rather than distribute this function across divisions.

To ensure that the anticipated benefits of this substantial investment in computer equipment are realized, it is common practice for companies like SPI to implement policies that preclude external purchase of information services or severely restrict users' freedom of choice if their needs can be met internally. In fact, only 14.7% of the 101 firms studied by Drury (1980) allowed users to spend their data processing dollars outside the organization at their own discretion. However, despite the ubiquity of such policies, restricted sourcing creates an asymmetrical exchange relationship wherein the organizational unit that controls the resource can use its power to influence or in some way constrain the behavior of those who have a need for it (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978). This leads to feelings of unfairness that may be further intensified if the dependent unit is dissatisfied with the level of service provided or the prices charged but has nowhere else to turn (Zajac, 1985).

## Market referents and perceived fairness

The SPI case suggests that managers' perceptions of fairness can be affected not only by increased interdependence between organizational units and sourcing restrictions that limit divisional autonomy but also by the relative prices that are charged for internal transfers. The divisional managers at SPI had become accustomed to prices that were, for the most part, artificially low, because they reflected neither the market nor the cost structure of the data center. Under the new policy of full cost pricing, these managers anticipated having to pay substantially more for computing services in the future. Those who had previously used external service bureaus or had gathered cost data from external sources were particularly sensitive to the new pricing structure, because they perceived the prices to be excessive relative to the alternatives. The discrepancy between internal and market prices was extremely controversial, especially when coupled with the newly announced sourcing restrictions.

As Kahneman and Tversky (1979) point out, participants in an exchange transaction define their outcomes relative to some reference state rather than in absolute and objective terms. This is especially true in situations like this one, where managers lack a clear basis for valuing system benefits in dollar terms. In the absence of clearly defined standards of what constitutes a “fair”

price, buyers tend to evaluate the fairness of their charges relative to market prices, posted prices, or prior transactions with the seller (Kahneman, et al., 1986). Reference transactions such as these provide a neutral point against which other alternatives can be evaluated and judgments of fairness can be made.

From the standpoint of fairness, few would dispute that the prevailing market price is the ideal transfer price in perfectly competitive markets (Benke and Edwards, 1980). Given the peculiarities of its supply and demand functions, however, the computer services market is an imperfect one. On the supply side, increasing returns to scale result in marginal costs that are lower than average costs for any given level of output. On the demand side, the difficulties associated with calculating the expected value of information make it impossible to empirically calculate the demand function of a single user. Furthermore, the activities of heavy users can have a significant impact on the prices charged to all users (Borovits and Neumann, 1978).

Both specialized and general purpose service bureaus are plentiful and willing to provide price lists or bids to technically unsophisticated users who may view their services as comparable and their prices as “fair market” rates. However, as a result of market imperfections, comparisons to external prices often produce information that is misleading for management decisions, especially when the objective is to maximize organizational profits (McFarlan and McKenney, 1983). Early in a data center’s life cycle, the full cost of running an application is likely to exceed the external market price for equivalent processing due to the fact that sizable fixed costs are spread over a relatively small number of internal users. Even later in the life cycle, market prices may be less than internal costs, since a service bureau may be able to achieve economies by supporting a limited number of widely used applications (e.g., ADP’s payroll processing services).

Because mailing list maintenance is a relatively standardized application that is available from a number of specialized service bureaus, SPI's managers found that the new full-cost prices exceeded both of their reference transactions — last year's internal prices and external market prices. Not only did the divisional managers perceived this to be unfair, but they also believed that they would have an increasingly difficult time maintaining market share in a competitive arena like publishing, since the competition was not similarly constrained.

## Summary

It is evident from the foregoing discussion that the controller's edict mandating full-cost pricing may solve some of SPI's existing problems, but only at the expense of creating others. For example, full-cost pricing may be an effective way to reduce demand for the ATEX system, but it is likely to have a similar effect on demand for the IBM system, which is under-utilized. The new policy may also have an adverse effect on the motivation of divisional managers, resulting in a very short-term strategic orientation or other types of dysfunctional behavior that are detrimental to the organization as a whole. For example, should these managers opt to transfer existing applications to microcomputers, the corporation would experience both an increase in the prices charged to the remaining users as well as a net loss overall in terms of its ability to coordinate and control its information resources.

The problems SPI is experiencing seem to stem from a fundamental flaw in the controller's approach to allocative equity. The standard of fairness he is employing says that if we treat all centralized service operations the same and if we also treat all profit centers the same, everyone will perceive his or her treatment to be fair and equitable. Obviously, the divisional managers who are heavy users of computing services do not perceive this “sameness,” nor do they subscribe to the same notion of allocative equity. They are concerned that the new allocation procedures will not only increase their dependence on MIS but will also reduce their autonomy (and perhaps set a precedent for further reductions in the future). Furthermore, as profit center managers, they are disturbed at the prospect of paying prices in excess of market, given that their financial rewards are based on the bottom line. Thus, their concerns center around inconsistencies in the performance evaluation and reward system and the policy on internal transfers whereby they are responsible for, but not in full control of, their financial results in a highly competitive marketplace.

## Dual Pricing: A Transitional Solution

Surveys of transfer pricing practices used by U.S. companies indicate that the prices charged for internal exchanges are generally either cost-or market-based (Benke and Edwards, 1980; Tang, 1979; Vancil, 1979). Furthermore, there is some evidence that cost-based prices are preferred when the selling unit is a corporate cost center, while market-based prices predominate when both the buying and selling units are profit centers (Vancil, 1979). However, in an in-depth study of transfer pricing practices used in 13 companies, Eccles (1985) identified three firms that employed a combination approach when faced with a situation like SPI's, where neither cost-nor market-based pricing alone seemed wholly appropriate. Although the MIS literature has paid little attention to this method of pricing internal transfers, dual or hybrid pricing could prove to be an equitable solution at certain times in the life cycle of a data center when full cost prices exceed going market rates. $^{3}$

Normally, the dual pricing approach consists of charging buyers full cost for products/services purchased internally while crediting sellers with market price on the sale. Market price may be established based on competitors' prices, published list prices, or the most recent bid price received. But regardless of the method chosen, market price generally exceeds full cost. This arrangement allows the selling unit to operate as a profit center with respect to internal sales. The double counting of profits that results is reconciled periodically on the corporate books.

As Eccles (1985) points out, when unrestricted sourcing policies are implemented in conjunction with dual pricing, the lower transfer price is regarded as an incentive to the buyer to source more internally. On the other hand, when internal sourcing is mandated, the favorable pricing policy can be viewed as a way of compensating the buying profit center for the loss in decision-making autonomy. Thus, dual pricing helps to ensure that both the supplying and buying units feel they are being treated equitably (Lococo, 1983).

In the case of SPI's IBM center, where market imperfections coupled with low utilization have resulted in costs that exceed known market prices for some types of processing, the dual pricing approach would necessarily be reversed. Those divisions that could demonstrate a significant disparity between market prices and full cost would be allowed to purchase internal computer services at market rates, while the MIS department would receive credit at full cost. Again, the discrepancy between the two prices would be reconciled at a corporate level. Since the entire corporation should accrue some benefit as a result of the tighter controls and coordination provided by a central data processing facility, the addition to overhead expenses would not be difficult to justify.

A number of potential benefits to such a pricing strategy may make it an attractive alternative for firms like SPI. First, as mentioned above, dual pricing is a way of compensating divisional managers for the loss of autonomy resulting from a policy of mandated internal sourcing; user managers should have few objections to purchasing internally if quality service is provided at a competitive price. Second, because the divisions are able to buy computing services at competitive rates, divisional concerns about fairness should diminish, freeing managers to concentrate their energies on making sound economic judgments about their use of computing resources. Third, given that SPI is planning to seek external customers to fill its excess capacity and recover some portion of its investment in computing facilities, the only way it is likely to be successful in this effort is by pricing its external sales competitively. A strategy of dual pricing on internal sales will avoid the inevitable conflict that would occur when the divisions found that external customers were charged less. Fourth, dual pricing should, in effect, phase itself out over time if there is sufficient demand (both internal and external) for the available machine capacity. As utilization of the system increases over time, the cost per unit of service should fall steadily until it eventually approaches the market rate. When this occurs, market-based pricing can be easily implemented with a minimum of controversy.

While dual pricing could provide an effective solution to the problems currently experienced by SPI, it is not without some drawbacks that should be considered prior to implementation. First, the presumption that dual pricing will phase itself out in time is only valid if there is sufficient demand for the system. If internal demand for the system does not increase and the MIS manager is unable to attract external customers to fill the excess capacity, the corporation could end up subsidizing an inefficient MIS operation. Second, dual pricing is not recommended as a long-term solution because of the associated administrative burden. Not only is there a need to forecast and reconcile differences at the corporate level on a regular basis, but there is also a need to monitor market prices for changes due to market fluctuations (Madison, 1979). Although the companies in Eccles' (1985) study were generally satisfied with the results of dual pricing because it resolved a short-term problem, all of them had phased it out after three to five years of use because of the administrative requirements. Finally, because a dual system provides each of the trading units with a favorable price, it leads to the creation of a large amount of organizational slack, eliminating the competitive benefits of inter-unit relationships, as well as reducing both interdependence and conflict (Eccles, 1985). As a result, top management may lose a valuable source of information about subunit performance, since the buying and selling divisions are left with little or no motivation to monitor one another's activities. In the absence of such monitoring, top management must remain vigilant to the possibility of resource mismanagement by both MIS and the user divisions.

## Summary and Conclusion

The intent of this article has been to examine the problems that arise in decentralized organizations when usage-based chargeback systems are implemented to control computing resources. Clearly, as Nolan (1977) points out, managers' perceptions of fairness are important considerations in the development of any control system. As the case of Specialty Publishing, Inc. illustrates, however, concerns about fairness can arise not only with respect to specific characteristics of the chargeback system (i.e., understandability, controllability, etc.), but also as a result of central management policies governing internal transactions between the users and MIS. In particular, this examination identifies three factors that affect the fairness perceptions of autonomous divisional managers: (1) a high degree of interdependence between user divisions and MIS such that the behavior of MIS could have a significant impact on divisional performance (and vice versa); (2) restrictions on divisional managers' freedom to purchase computer services from external sources; and (3) the tendency for full cost to exceed market prices early in the life of a data center. Under these circumstances, a dual pricing approach is suggested as an interim solution, with the divisions paying market price for computer services and MIS receiving credit for the full cost of providing those services. Dual pricing provides a way of compensating divisional managers for the loss of decision-making autonomy. It may also help to avoid charges of unfairness that would likely result if excess capacity were sold to external customers at competitive market rates.

In addition to the specific recommendations made with respect to minimizing chargeback-related conflict in situations like SPI's, this analysis also suggests that previous conceptualizations of the chargeback problem have been inadequate in several respects. From a design standpoint, they have been overly ambitious in attempting to understand in a single study the chargeback problems of a heterogeneous mixture of organizational types using a variety of different allocation mechanisms. As the SPI case illustrates, because of the presumed autonomy of divisional managers, the problems of decentralized organizations are significantly more complex than those experienced by centralized organizations. From a definitional standpoint, previous studies have perhaps been too narrow in limiting their concerns to the chargeback mechanisms themselves, focusing on issues such as understandability and controllability, while ignoring the larger policy-related issues that can affect the willingness of divisional managers to try to understand or control their charges. It is hoped that this investigation will precipitate more directed research into other behavioral issues related to the use of chargeback systems to control resource allocation.
