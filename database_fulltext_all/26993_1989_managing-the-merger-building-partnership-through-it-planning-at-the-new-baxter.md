## Abstract

In mid-1987, Baxter Healthcare Corporation undertook a major IS strategic planning study on the heels of the company's merger with American Hospital Supply. Central to this planning effort was the building of a new level of partnership between business and IS managers in the new corporation. This article describes Baxter's seven-month planning effort and the role of this effort in building the partnership. Two specific planning behaviors are noted for their contribution: dual purpose executive interviews, designed to both elicit information and provide a basis for executive understanding of planning options; and “micro” and “macro” analysis templates, which served to focus and clarify planning activities and options at both local and global levels within the firm. Several key components in effective partnership — shared knowledge, shared commitment, and shared decision making — were therefore encouraged and supported by this approach.

Keywords: Strategic IS planning, partnership, IT organization, IT planning methodologies

ACM Categories: H.1.0, K.6.4

## Introduction

In early 1985, American Hospital Supply's (AHS) chairman, Karl Bays, attempted to merge the \$3.4 billion hospital supply firm with the then \$4.2 billion Hospital Corporation of America (HCA). HCA, the country's largest hospital management firm, operated 420 for-profit hospitals at the time. Shareholders of both companies supported the move, but hospitals that competed with HCA, many of them large AHS customers, objected strongly. Some threatened to take their business elsewhere, and ultimately the merger collapsed.

Soon thereafter, AHS was again in the financial news, this time, however, as a buyout target. Baxter Travenol Laboratories, a \$1.8 billion health care products manufacturer in 1985, made an unfriendly and ultimately successful bid for American. A major asset attracting Baxter was American's industry-known automated order entry system, Analytic Systems Automatic Purchasing (ASAP), which directly connected purchasing agents at hospitals with AHS's extensive product distribution network.

Baxter's chairman, Vernon Loucks Jr., treated the buyout as a merger between the two firms. Michael Heschel, formerly AHS's senior executive in charge of MIS, was named CIO of the new corporation. For Heschel, the merger meant consolidating, at an operational level, American's 400 MIS employees and \$55 million budget with Baxter's 450 MIS employees and \$45 million budget during a tumultuous period of organizational change. Strategically, it meant planning a new IS organization, a new management planning and control system, and a new IS architecture to support the company's future goals. Not the least of Heschel's concerns was the two companies' distinct, well-known and widely different business cultures: Baxter was known more as a conservative and analytical company, which reflected in part the firm's product technology, product development, and manufacturing expertise. American's executives were known more for their aggressive, field-based sales, marketing and distribution culture. It was a highly decentralized, customer-oriented, so-called "streetsmart" organization. Immediately following the merger, Heschel's new IS organization, composed of former American and Baxter personnel, reflected these two distinctly different business cultures.

To move the company as rapidly as possible from a pre-merger to post-merger business mind set and operational environment, top management quickly initiated several high-level business and information systems strategy initiatives. Broadly based, these initiatives focused on core business strategies, internal and external organizational synergies, internal operational effectiveness, and change management. One such initiative, completed by an outside consulting firm, recommended that Heschel radically decentralize corporate IS, thereby reducing staffing levels by over 80%. In effect, the consulting firm's recommendation followed a general interest on the part of many firms to move to more of a “minimalist” corporate management environment — a decentralist philosophy not unlike that of pre-merger AHS. The question, however, was whether the “minimalist” approach would work in the IS function at Baxter. Deciding that the consultancy’s report had posed the question but not made the case, business and IS management decided to put together a more lengthy, in-depth study of the issue.

This article summarizes the resulting seven-month corporate IS planning effort. It focuses specifically on the study's three key planning phases, as illustrated in Figure 1:

\- Defining the Business Context: contrasting Baxter's pre- and post-merger business environments

\- Executing the Plan: defining and internally selling the study's key objectives, scope, and planning methodology

\- Results: presenting recommendations to top management, followed by implementation

Before moving on to discuss the study's business context phase, a brief overview of key results is provided.

The overall impact of the study was best summarized by one of Baxter's senior group-level vice-presidents: "My time on this project was very well spent . . . For the first time I really understand the mission of the systems group." ## Defining the Business Context

As one Baxter executive succinctly put it, “It was extremely difficult to bring together two such different business cultures . . . the more centralized and conservative management style at Baxter with the more decentralized management philosophy at American.” One approach, a renewed emphasis on strategic business and IS planning, is part of a classical repertoire of activities usually brought to the fore after major shifts in business viewpoint or organization. From a practitioner’s perspective, however, the question is not so much whether strategic planning efforts are undertaken (most firms do them) but rather how well the plans are done and the effectiveness of the implementation.

How well these are accomplished is in part a function of the scope and complexity of the planning effort. The complexity of Baxter's post-merger business organization is well-illustrated by Figure 2. As shown, the merger resulted in 35 business divisions, 18 of which were from the old Baxter Travenol, with the remaining 17 from American Hospital Supply. While nearly all 35 businesses existed before the merger, the new Baxter grouped divisions along customer and product-oriented lines, with the intent of improving customer service through improved cross-functional, business process integration. In short, a more process-oriented view, focused on the customer, was taken.

A quick look at Figure 2 might tempt the reader to conclude that the integration of business processes along customer and product-oriented lines would strongly imply centralization of systems to achieve the well-known economies of scale and service levels associated with that approach (e.g., common systems, common data, common standards and architecture). In fact, the question at Baxter, as in most firms, was not so much how to define what was common across the corporation (and therefore likely to be supported by a more centralized, architectural approach) but rather what business processes, functions, and work activities needed to remain locally specific while being coordinated across functions, product lines, and the like. The goal was to achieve the required degree of customer responsiveness and service quality at local levels in the firm (Rockart and Short, 1989).

Consider, for example, the differences between Baxter's Hospital and Alternate Site health care markets. In the Hospital market, three sales and marketing divisions manage their product portfolios by leveraging the production resources of 10 Baxter manufacturing divisions and a large number of outside vendors. The major systems issue is how to facilitate integrated selling approaches to a single customer (the hospital). In contrast, there is no corporate manufacturing presence in the Alternate Site market. Rather, 12 sales and marketing divisions buy vendor-made products to sell to a large and diverse customer base (for example, homes, doctors, health maintenance organizations, corporate employees). In other words, for Baxter's IS study to be credible to both line and corporate management, the study had to deal concretely with this level of business complexity. No line manager responsible for 12 sales divisions and many different customers and customer groups was going to sit still for a corporate-directed systems study that cursorily recommended a corporate IS solution.

## Defining the Planning Process

## The IT planning literature: insights and directions

IT planning has been a topic of key importance for both academicians and practitioners alike since the mid-1960s. Blumenthal's (1969) MIS — A Framework for Planning and Development was one of the first books on IS planning to give the subject a comprehensive and realistic treatment. As Henderson and Venkatraman (1989) note, strategic IT planning has evolved over the last two decades in a pattern quite similar to that of general business planning. As in the business planning approaches of two decades ago, IT planning first focused on the effective allocation of the firm's resources to the function itself (e.g., to the information technology function). IT planning (ITP) in this era served “the project manager and project sponsor by carefully delineating system requirements, by establishing goals and responsibilities for those involved in the system project, and by providing a means of integrating project controls with more general organizational financial controls” (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1989, p. 1). Most ITP approaches viewed the IT plan as a response to business strategy and sought through differing means to identify and accomplish the objectives in a classic top-down planning and bottom-up implementation approach (Mason and Mitroff, 1981; Zani,

1970; Zmud, et al., 1986). Examples of planning methods include IBM's Business Systems Planning (BSP) and its spinoffs (IBM, 1981) and Rockart's Critical Success Factors (CSFs) (Rockart, 1979).

As firms began to find ways to apply information technologies as a means of gaining competitive advantage in the marketplace, ITP evolved to include (within the planning process itself) a much higher level of interaction between business strategy and IT strategy (Benjamin, et al., 1984; Pyburn, 1983). As Boynton and Zmud (1987), Cash and Konsynski (1985), Henderson, et al. (1987), Henderson and Venkatraman (1989), King (1978), and McFarlan (1984) have pointed out, researchers and strategic IT planners alike have identified the need for an integrated business and information technology strategy that explicitly recognizes the potential for IT to shape or enable critical business strategies. IT planning agendas and methodologies in this second era of ITP reflect this more externally oriented, competitive perspective, which directly incorporates techniques such as Porter and Millar's (1985) value-chain analysis.

In their review of much of the literature base in information systems planning over the last three decades, Boynton and Zmud (1987) identify nine planning agendas and 11 planning behaviors critical for effective planning efforts. Planning agendas are defined as planning issues that require attention; planning behaviors are behaviors promoting the interactions among organizational actors necessary to raise and resolve planning issues. (See Figure 3.) Boynton and Zmud note that most work has concentrated on identifying and explicating planning agendas — in short, the issues requiring attention in the plan itself. Considering the attention given planning agendas, it would seem appropriate for future research to emphasize planning behaviors. ## Defining objectives and scope

The study team's mission and key objectives included to:

\- Align IS capability with the operating objectives and strategic growth of the new company, effecting, if necessary, a major restructuring of the IS function

\- Improve the efficiency and effectiveness of current and anticipated IS capabilities

\- Document strategic systems needs and new ideas identified during the planning process

\- Plan for and provide a workable implementation plan for the study's recommendations

The scope of the planning effort encompassed all functional areas in all 35 domestic business divisions at Baxter. From an IS perspective, this amounted to gathering data, analyzing data, and making recommendations on over 300 major systems applications across the business.

The project began in June 1987 and concluded with recommendations and an implementation plan delivered to top management in January 1988. The project team was composed of the senior IS management group, the study's project manager (director of business technology planning, Corporate Information Resources), and several Baxter-retained corporate strategy consultants. The Center for Information Systems Research from MIT's Sloan School of Management participated in the study. CISR's role was to help in reviewing the planning framework and provide a research perspective that included observations from other companies.

## Building the planning framework

Figure 4 illustrates the project team work plan. Note that the statement of objectives, key study elements, time frame and project deliverables were set in June 1987. This work plan was used to introduce the study during executive interviews and at interim project update meetings.

Figure 5 illustrates the planning framework. Note that there are three central components: line of business or micro analysis (part I); the cross divisional or macro analysis (part II); and directional statements (part III). Internally, the process of completing each level of analysis was referred to as the “micro loop,” the “macro loop,” and “new directions.”

The micro loop is a division / function-specific approach. The objective was to ensure that unique divisional requirements were maintained and reflected in the team's final recommendations. Key components of the micro loop are: (1) documenting the current IS configuration; (2) understanding division and function-specific business strategies; (3) determining the impact of these strategies on the current systems configuration; (4) developing management options to address gaps between business strategy needs and current systems capabilities; and (5) developing evaluation templates, discussed later, which helped both business and IS management evaluate and select options.

The macro loop is a cross-divisional approach. In this case, the objective was to determine the appropriate level of IS resource sharing across divisions without compromising unique divisional and functional requirements. The key components of the macro analysis are: (1) identifying cross-functional (business) synergies across divisional, group, and corporate business units; (2) identifying cross-divisional and cross-functional technology synergies in areas such as hardware platforms, database management systems, and languages; (3) determining needs for information views, or across company information accessibility, referred to as horizontal (across business unit) and vertical (down the management hierarchy) information views; (4) assessing and analyzing similarities in customers' product and service requirements; (5) identifying leading-edge case examples from other industries in cross-divisional and cross-functional business/IS integration; and (6) identifying opportunities for divisional or functional transformation to achieve the appropriate level of IS resource sharing across divisions and functions.

## Executing the Plan

## Selling the process to management

As in any planning activity, effectively selling the planning scope, objectives, and methodology to senior management is essential. To accomplish this, the team planned and facilitated more than a dozen key executive working sessions. Each session presented the planning objectives, solicited executive input into the process, and created ownership in the study and planning methodology at that level. These sessions were deliberately kept small, focused, and tailored to each executive or executive group.

## Executive interviews

Line-of-business executive interviews provided first-hand understanding of the key contrasts between “pre” and “post” merger Baxter business strategies. Not surprisingly, post-merger strategies were fundamentally different from pre-merger directions. Moreover, strategies in many areas of the business were being re-examined and refined during the study process. If the project team had based their recommendations on a simple extension of pre-merger directions, recommendations for organizational revisions, if implemented, would have actually impeded Baxter from attaining its post-merger business goals. (See Figure 6.)

In designing the interview process, the team focused on four objectives: (1) selecting the “right” executives; (2) designing (tailoring) the interview for each executive; (3) preparing (tailored) interview materials; and (4) analyzing results.

\- Selecting executives — The team used two criteria to select executives: (1) scope of their responsibilities relative to the project scope; and (2) criticality of the corresponding business unit to the study outcome. In addition, other executives were interviewed because of their strong interest in information technology and in the IT planning process itself. Thirty-five executives were interviewed, with positions in the company ranging from chief executive officer to division president.

\- Designing the interview — Four objectives were key to interview design: (1) communicating the scope and goals of the project; (2) achieving consensus on business strategies; (3) understanding and discussing the systems requirements and implications of these business strategies; and (4) building executive commitment to the study. It was decided that the interviews would average 90 minutes. The first half was to convey the purpose of the study and review the relevant business unit strategies. The second half was to focus on the IS role in enabling these strategies. After the interviews were completed, the team was to send follow-up letters to the executives summarizing the agreed-upon strategies and IS implications of those strategies.

\- Preparing interview materials — Interview preparation included reviewing business unit strategic plans and developing strategy summary documents for use in the interviews. As illustrated in the top half of Figure 7 (Baxter's Hospital Group business unit is illustrated as an example), these preparation documents outlined the divisions, key products and markets, sales force, manufacturing and distribution channels, and financials (sales, market share, etc.) for each major business unit. On the same document, illustrated in the bottom half of the figure, key business strategies were sorted by functional area. For example, in sales and marketing, developing a corporate partnership-selling program was one key goal of the Hospital Group. Additionally, business unit strategies with apparent systems implications were identified to aid in discussing the role of IS in supporting these strategies. As illustrated in Figure 8, again for Baxter's Hospital Group, strategies such as developing value-added relationships or partnership-selling programs implied systems support in areas including telemarketing systems, customer service support systems, and improving automated customer order-entry systems. These strategies and systems implications were discussed with each executive.

\- Analyzing results — From these interviews, the project team assembled a comprehensive database of business unit strategies and corresponding systems implications. More than 200 strategy statement and system implication pairs were indexed by business unit and function. This indexing allowed the team to analyze strategies from both a business unit and functional perspective.

Beyond establishing a strategy database for internal planning purposes, the executive interviews provided an important opportunity for Baxter's IS management to demonstrate an in-depth understanding of business unit strategy and the creative use of technology in enabling business goals. One divisional president characterized this best by saying: "It's great to have someone talking with me about systems in plain English . . . it makes it a whole lot easier for me to understand your role."

## Documenting the current IS infrastructure

Maximizing the IS function's contribution in accomplishing Baxter's business unit and cross-divisional goals required a detailed understanding of the existing IS infrastructure. The project team assembled key information on Baxter's 300 + applications, including description of functionality, age, technology base, cost, and headcount.

These applications and corresponding information on each application were cross-indexed by two criteria (see Figure 9): (1) on the horizontal axis, by major business group (for example, by Hospital Group); and (2) on the vertical axis, by the use of one of two dimensions: functional area (such as distribution, manufacturing, or sales and marketing) or “Logical Application Group” (LAG) (Rockart, et al., 1977; 1979). A logical application group is a complete application system, such as order entry, which may span one or several functional areas. The significant factor underlying the LAG concept is that each LAG is identified with a logically separate business process performed by the organization. Examples included order entry, customer systems, raw materials purchasing and management, product engineering, and decision support systems. The project team referred to this cross-indexed database (containing information on applications, business groups, and business functions or LAGs) as the project’s IS infrastructure “fact base.”

## Identifying gaps

With the business strategy database and IS infrastructure fact base complete, the project team put the two together and analyzed the current

IS organization's capability to support the company's future business goals. The result of the strategy analysis was a listing of capabilities (by division and function) not met by the current systems infrastructure. Some examples included the capability to generate product line P/L statements, customer P/L statements across divisions, and common Baxter invoices across all 35 divisions. This list of capability short falls was used as one of the primary inputs to generating recommendation options. One benefit to having strategy statements and IS infrastructure data stored electronically was that the project team was able to handle more complexity at a lower level of business unit granularity than would have been possible otherwise.

## Developing options

At this point, the team was ready to begin analyzing the projected IT organization itself. The approach taken was to generate for management review several IS organizational options ranging from complete decentralization of the function to complete centralization. Options were developed for all 35 business divisions and applicable functional areas and logical application groups. Note that while the lowest-level building block was the business division/business function-LAG combination, analysis was completed at this level only when line of business/function-LAG (LOB/F) specificity required it. In most cases, the project team aggregated data to the highest level appropriate.

## Evaluating the options

The option evaluation phase of the project analyzed and selected IS organization support options for LOB/F pairings using “templates” developed for use in both micro and macro analysis “loops” (discussed earlier). Critical to this phase were evaluation criteria that facilitated data reduction but retained and highlighted critical information.

Several different evaluation templates were used over the course of the study. The two most significant were a template developed in the micro analysis loop to help evaluate the organizational effectiveness of proposed configuration options and a template in the macro analysis loop to help evaluate technological, functional and customer-related synergies across the company. ## Micro Analysis Template

For each LOB/F pairing, the objective of the micro analysis loop was to select one of three configuration options: centralized, hybrid (some IS management functions centralized and some decentralized), and decentralized. Figure 11 illustrates an example micro organizational effectiveness template for the Hospital Group/Order Entry business unit/business function. Note that this template's key criteria included three categories: organizational critical mass, effectiveness (largely a subjective measure), and resource control. Under organizational critical mass, for example, the team selected the best configuration maximizing IS management talent, experience sharing, and career paths. For this business group/business function pairing, the project team recommended centralized IS support, as indicated by the filled dots in Figure 11. The result of the micro loop was a single IS support recommendation option for each LOB/F pairing.

## Macro Analysis Template

In the macro analysis loop, the team analyzed each LOB/F pairing for organizational synergies across business groups, business divisions, and business functions. The challenge was to develop recommendations maximizing cross-divisional and cross-functional IS synergies while preserving the required business-specific flexibility.

To accomplish this, the macro analysis loop applied three principal criteria: technological intensity (a measure of critical mass required for systems development, operations, and maintenance), functional similarity (similarity of functional business process across divisions — for example, Accounts Payable, General Ledger), and common customer (IS support targeted to the same customer account or customer group). For each LOB/F pairing, the team evaluated whether IS support should be shared across divisions or whether it should stand alone.

Figure 12 illustrates the Baxter-wide results of the macro analysis at the business group level. Note that the recommended information systems support options are a mix of centralized (shared across the groups) and decentralized (group-specific) IS organizational configurations.

## Building commitment to selected options

With option evaluation complete, the team focused on building senior executive commitment to the preliminary recommendations by: (1) reviewing recommendations with the senior IS management team; (2) conducting follow-up discussions with selected business executives; and (3) reviewing recommendations with the executive committee.

\- Senior IS Review — The project team conducted a two-day off-site meeting with Baxter's CIO and staff. In this meeting, the team received input on both preliminary recommendations and supporting documentation.

\- Executive Follow-ups — Following the IS review, both the project team and IS executives initiated individual review meetings with key business executives. These meetings were frank discussions of likely impacts on each executive's business function and responsibilities. Discussions solicited input, sold recommendations, and built commitment to implementation.

\- Executive Review — Baxter's CIO then presented final recommendations to the executive committee and gained strong support from the group. Careful pre-planning and attention to the overall integrity of the planning process ensured this outcome.

Following endorsement by the executive committee, Baxter's CIO presented final recommendations to the CEO and president, who approved them for immediate implementation.

## Summary and Results

The IS planning effort provided a structured approach for Baxter's business and information systems management to align the company's IS organization to meet the needs of the new corporation. While largely a subjective measure, there is no question that the scope and intensity of this planning effort and the effort subsequently directed at implementation have built a new level of partnership between business and IS managers in the company. While there is no generally accepted way to empirically assess the strength of partnership and then assess the benefit stream resulting from partnership to the company as a whole (see Henderson, 1989, on this point), subjective measures around characteristics of partnership (such as shared knowledge, shared commitment, mutual benefits, and organizational linkage) have all improved.

Other key outcomes include:

\- The IS planning methodology was incorporated into Baxter's general business planning process for the first time in company history.

## Lessons in this Approach

The IS planning methodology developed over the course of the seven-month effort at Baxter combines features of both “first era” IS strategic planning methodologies, such as Critical Success Factors and BSP, and “second era” methodologies that reflect a more externally oriented, competitive view. Techniques incorporated include value chain analysis and improved planning behaviors designed to better integrate business with IS strategy.

Unique in this approach, and as such we believe a contribution to the current IS planning literature, are two techniques designed to improve planning behaviors (e.g., “behaviors . . . [promoting] the interactions among organizational actors necessary to raise and resolve the planning agenda” (Boyton and Zmud, 1987, p. 62)):

\- Dual Purpose Interviews. The executive interviews were designed to both elicit information and provide a basis for executive understanding of planning options. This allowed each executive to participate more effectively in the selection of solutions. This resulted in improved communication with IS management during implementation.

\- The Micro and Macro Analysis and Templates. Presented to business executives via templates, this method separated the analysis into micro and macro components. There were several benefits to this approach. First, by separating business-unit specific information into a micro analysis loop and micro templates, executives were more comfortable that the planning process itself dealt adequately with their level of business complexity and concerns. This contributed to a greater sense of shared knowledge and mutual commitment. Second, the macro analysis loop and macro templates communicated to each line executive the corporate IS planning issues and their tradeoffs. The macro view also revealed the complexities and imperatives regarding investments in corporate systems and strategic technologies as viewed from an external, competitive point of view. In short, the templates served as forums for effective communication between business and IS management. They were effective in part because of the complexity and level of information that could be quickly and efficiently presented, and also because the templates themselves served as starting points for discussion and debate throughout the interviews. In short, managers understood that they were not only being interviewed about their specific IS needs but were engaged in helping the company determine its future information technology and organizational capability. A key element in effective partnership, shared decision making, was therefore encouraged and supported by this approach.
