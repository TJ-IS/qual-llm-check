## Abstract

A variety of forces have been serving to alter the traditional triangular shaped IS organization. This article describes an approach to dealing with those forces that maximizes career path opportunities and helps to develop and retain a staff of highly capable professionals. The result is an evolution from a triangular to a pentagonal-shaped organization.

Keywords: Human factors, staffing, systems development

ACM Categories: K.6, K.6.1

## Introduction

Combustion Engineering, Inc. (C-E) is a diversified company serving electric utilities and general industry worldwide. The corporation employs 40,000 people and had a sales volume of \$3.57 billion in 1982. The Power Systems Group (PSG) is the unit within C-E that is referred to here. PSG is recognized as a leading designer, manufacturer, constructor, and services suppliers of fossil and nuclear fueled steam-generating equipment and air pollution control systems for electric utilities and other industrial applications.

Within C-E, data processing operations are centralized in corporate facilities located in Windsor, Connecticut and Houston, Texas; information systems development is decentralized at the group level. PSG is the largest of the groups and has an information systems development staff consisting of 79 professionals. They provide systems development services to the administrative headquarters personnel and a variety of divisions and manufacturing plants in the United States and Canada.

Throughout the 1970s, there was constant growth in the size of the PSG Information Systems Development (ISD) staff. During that decade, it grew from a staff of 23 professionals to its present size. The first online system was installed in 1972 and since then the group has effectively kept up with the state-of-the-art. The most significant staff growth occurred in 1978 when a large online order entry system was undertaken. By 1980, the IS department in PSG had installed several advanced, integrated systems, using database technology which fit R. Nolan's [1] definition of Stage IV applications. During this period, the increase in the size of the staff ensured ample promotional opportunities.

ISD management in PSG recognized that after this peak build-up of personnel in 1978, the staff size would begin to stabilize. They wanted to be able to continue to provide growth opportunities and effective career path planning. This would be essential in order to retain the excellent staff that had been assembled to develop the sophisticated order entry system installed in January 1980.

The staffing philosophy within ISD was to promote from within and hire high-potential people at the entry level. Another policy was to provide the

The pattern of work within the unit was that each analyst or programmer worked on multiple jobs. Individuals were loaded with as much work as they could handle; this gave them more opportunity to develop. To keep up with this growth, there were two types of promotional opportunities: (1) supervisory openings that were created due to increases in staff size, and (2) openings created as a result of functions being added, e.g., database administration, office automation.

## Reducing the Base of the Triangle

Toward the end of the 1970s, there were several forces at work that resulted in reducing the number of lower level programmers and analysts who formed the base of the ISD organizational triangle.

\- Increased use of application packages led to less demand for “junior” programmers and more demand for senior level project leaders and analysts whose function was to customize the packages, train the users, and complete the implementation.

\- Programmer productivity aids (e.g., online screen design facilities) tended to mechanize the more routine programming functions which were done by the lower level programmers.

\- Off-loading tasks to users, such as report generation functions and simple file maintenance activities, also led to a reduction in the workload of lower level systems analysts and programmers.

\- Personal computer applications that are pursued by users with user friendly software also displaced some of the less skilled systems and programming tasks.

Because of the above changes, the base of the ISD organizational triangle was reduced. The shape of the new structure was as shown at the right side of Figure 1.

In addition to this effect, the generally declining business situation in the early 1980s led to reduced hiring. This meant that there were fewer entry level people coming in at the base of the triangle and that the average experience level of the group went up. With the size of the staff remaining constant, there were fewer promotional opportunities materializing. Unless something was done to deal with this situation, it could be expected that promotional opportunities for senior level people would be very limited.

## Other Organizational Changes

At the same time, there was a need developing to change the organization to accommodate a significant number of new skills that were coming to be required. These skills were needed to effectively manage a more sophisticated applications portfolio and to provide leadership for ISD activities that were pushing into new areas. The critical need for expertise in these areas is described by Nolan and Norton [2], who identify “managing the diversified technologies” as a key issue for mature IS organizations. Some of the new positions that were required were:

\- Online Specialists — These people have become experts in teleprocessing monitors (INTERCOMM and CICS) that were used by PSG.

\- Database Management Specialists — Several systems are used by PSG (IDMS, TOTAL, IMS, and INQUIRE).

\- Analysts for Application Packages — These are constantly being evaluated, acquired, modified, and installed.

\- Database Administrators — These individuals are needed to effectively manage the company's information resources.

\- Internal Business Consultants — These positions require very experienced systems personnel who know the company's business and are highly skilled in one or more application areas.

## Parallel Promotional Paths

Because of the changes that were taking place, it seemed evident that in addition to the normal promotional path up the supervisory ladder, there should also be a parallel path that would provide career opportunities for the increasing number of specialists and super-technicians, and for the most effective individual performers in systems development, who are often unable or unwilling to make the transition to management.

Under a parallel promotional path arrangement, the best individual performers can be promoted without being forced into the management ranks. Many times, one highly skilled person can do the work of two or more less skilled people. (We have not tried to prove this quantitatively, but it seems to be axiomatic within the industry). It is widely recognized that the seasoned EDP professional who knows the company's applications and hardware/software environment is the best buy per dollar.

This situation was discussed with the PSG Industrial Relations Department and they agreed that an organization that provided for parallel promotional paths was appropriate to the situation. A schematic representation of the parallel positions is shown in Figure 2.

The effect of the parallel promotional paths was to provide for more specialists and “super-star” individual performers. The resulting structure is pentagonal in shape as indicated in Figure 3.

In some companies, the ISD organization is not allowed to evolve naturally and is constrained to conform to a triangular organization structure. The constraint comes from failure to adapt personnel practices to the changing mix of skills required. In this situation, there is usually more turnover among senior people and the IS department is less effective because there is a much bigger learning curve effect when new people are constantly being brought in. Also, if lower level people are hired as replacements, the overall skill level of the department is reduced.

The organization that provides parallel promotional paths and allows evolution to a pentagonal structure will maintain a higher average skill level and may be generally more effective.

## User Organizations Will Also Become Pentagonal in Shape

In highly mechanized user departments with sophisticated information systems, it is likely that the shape of the organizations will change in much the same manner as has the IS organization. The trend in user departments may be slower in developing than in the IS organization, but many of the same phenomena are likely to appear. For example, as many routine clerical functions are mechanized, the broad base of the triangle in user organizations will diminish. The users will also require higher skilled personnel, such as EDP coordinators, report generator specialists, and expert technicians who can work with modeling and simulation systems.

Those user departments that take full advantage of the computer and harness its power to manage better will also magnify the capabilities of their professional people. This will lead to an increase in highly skilled people who can conceptualize and operate decision support systems. They will also need to acquire new expertise in order to fully utilize the company's information resources and thereby be able to differentiate its products and services to yield a competitive advantage. Users of this type are a prerequisite to the strategic use of IS, to “produce unique, differentiated products,” as described by McFarlan, McKenney, and Pyburn [3]. In this environment, the process of reshaping the information systems hierarchy will be closely followed or paralleled by a reshaping of the major user organizations that they service.

## Conclusion

As ISD moves into more advanced EDP applications, there should be a natural evolution toward a pentagonal shaped organization. The same concept should apply to the user departments involved. Those companies that recognize this trend and remove the artificial constraints of the traditional triangular shaped structure will have a significant advantage. They will provide for the increasing number of specialists required, thereby enabling better career path planning, less turnover, and a more effective organization.
