# Developing a Corporate Private Network

By: Herbert N. McCauley

## Abstract

The integration of telecommunication resources has been fundamental to Harris' information management systems strategy since the inception of a corporate-wide MIS program in 1977. From 1977 to 1979, communication requirements expanded rapidly. Traffic volume was evaluated periodically to determine the appropriate time to integrate voice, data, facsimile, text and, ultimately, video transmission for teleconferencing. In 1979, corporate MIS started a major program to optimize transmission of digitized voice and data over a private, satellite/microwave network, with switch/access networks at the three satellite earth station nodes to concentrate local traffic for wide area satellite transmission. The primary objective was improved cost-effectiveness by minimizing distance-tariff-usage sensitivity.

The network has been in operation since December, 1980 and is achieving all cost and performance goals. Due to the high visibility of the network, Harris divisions are more sensitive to the cost and management of communications. The network positively impacted the overall MIS program, particularly the consolidation of telecommunications/office/data processing systems management, and its success influenced Harris' plans for future markets.

## Introduction

The integration of telecommunication resources has been fundamental to Harris' information management systems strategy since the inception of a corporate-wide MIS program in 1977. From 1977 to 1979, communication requirements expanded rapidly. Traffic volume was evaluated periodically to determine the appropriate time to integrate voice, data, facsimile, text and ultimately, video transmission for teleconferencing.

The business problem identified was one of growing voice and data traffic, particularly the latter, with no sign of relief from rate increases from the tariff-sensitive public networks. Harris was enjoying technological improvements which substantially reduced unit costs and provided improved services for private computing facilities. Although comparable technological improvements were occurring in communications, there were no resulting unit cost reductions on the tariffed public networks.

In 1979, corporate MIS started a major program to optimize transmission of digitized voice and data over a private, satellite/microwave network, with switch/access networks at the three satellite earth station nodes to concentrate local traffic for wide area satellite transmission. The primary objective was improved cost-effectiveness by minimizing distance-tariff-usage sensitivity [4].

Other companies have reached the conclusions Harris made in 1979 and continuing tariff escalations are driving more companies to private networks. The New York Times on March 24, 1983, in an article by Robert Bennett, stated [1]:

April 1, 1984, will mark Citicorp's way of challenging the communications companies, which the bank says have been raising its domestic communications by 20 percent a year. It will be the first time a financial company will have used its own satellite communications system to connect its offices across the country, bypassing such common carriers as the American Telephone and Telegraph Company and Western Union.

Citicorp says it paid \$80M last year to transmit and receive information in the United States, according to Stephen M.

Piraino, vice president who specializes in satellite communications. If these costs were to keep rising at 20 percent a year, they would double in five years and again in ten years, to \$320M by 1990, more than Citicorp's \$293M in net operating earnings from its business in North America in 1982.

Citicorp is determined not to let that happen and has spent tens of millions of dollars to control its communications destiny.

Private networking has been in operation at Harris Corporation and is now an accepted business practice, similar to the private management of data processing facilities. Economies are understood and quality is accepted as comparable or better than traditional AT&T voice and data services.

Harris is a producer of advanced communication and information processing systems, equipment, and components for the information technology market. Thus, a characteristic of the company was top management's familiarity with the technologies to be employed, the economies to be realized, and a desire to showcase Harris technologies in a private networking application. However, there was an unwillingness to force a captive service upon an autonomous division organizational structure without grass-roots acceptance by all divisions, including those not familiar with the technologies to be employed.

Stakes were high if users did not accept the results. Failure of the private communications service was not an operational risk, as fall-back to the AT&T network was inherent in the new network's design. The risk was in the cost of failure to corporate MIS and the company.

This article presents the approach to gaining approval for the network project, project implementation in terms of time and resources, operational characteristics of the network, cost/benefits planned and realized, network expansion, future directions, the network's impact on the company's overall information systems strategy and on future business opportunities for Harris.

## Background

The Corporate Network development addresses the Harris implementation of private facilities for a switch and access network in the Melbourne, Florida area and for a three node satellite, wide area network. For background and definition purposes, terms used in this article refer to networks in three principal categories [2]:

Local Area Networks — These facilitate office-worker access to computer power, word processing, document transfer, and teleconferencing. Additionally, through a PBX, the user has telephone communications, voice messaging, and limited terminal capabilities. About sixty to eighty percent of all communications traffic takes place within a building or a local complex, which emphasizes the requirement for local area networks.

Switch and Access Networks — Local area networks are not the entire solution. Networks utilizing private applications of digital microwave equipment, lightwave equipment, and new digital PBXs offer major opportunities for network cost savings. A typical switch and access network can concentrate data and voice traffic from several local area networks in a 50-75 square-mile area for integrated, wide-area transmission via the public switch network and/or a user-owned network.

Wide Area Networks — These networks are the tariffed public switched networks, predominately the AT&T core network, and private or user-owned networks not requiring tariffing. In the case of the Harris network, these networks are operated together. User-owned networks can capitalize on certain routes which are uneconomic using the AT&T network and link to the public switched network where transmission volumes do not warrant investment in private facilities.

## Gaining Approval

From periodic traffic reviews conducted by the Telecommunications Department (part of the Corporate MIS organization), it was clear that although data communication requirements were growing rapidly, the primary near-term justification for private networking would be voice traffic. Therefore, analysis efforts were, for the most part, based on PBX tapes of traffic at major locations. In 1979, the Telecommunications Department, with the assistance of Harris' Satellite Communications Division, developed the recommendation for an initial three-node private satellite network. The network proposal was presented initially to the president of the company and the senior vice presidents of the Communications and Information Systems Sectors. Their concerns, and MIS responses follow.

Reliability/Quality — Citing prior experience in satellite communications and the technologies to be used, the proposal convinced management of reliability and quality equivalent to, or better than, the AT&T network. The quality issues raised were echo and the half-second delay inherent in satellite transmissions. The plan proposed the insertion of echo cancellers on voice channels. Echo cancellers are the latest technology for elimination of echo during the half-second (44,800 mile round-trip) transmission delay. Until recently, echo suppressors were utilized on satellite systems but cancellers provide better performance.

Insofar as delay, the proposal included the use of satellite-delay compensation units for data transmission. The half-second delay for voice communication was considered a negligible deterrent as the public network's use of satellite communications was increasing, thus the delay factor was becoming a way of life. However, it was pointed out to management that the “user perception” of delay might be exaggerated by several factors. The highly visible, eleven-meter antennas installed at three sites of major employee concentrations, and the attendant publicity that would accompany the inhouse network, would make Harris people considerably more conscious of the fact that they were talking over satellite. Regardless of whether satellite delay, or any other problem, was caused by Harris or AT&T facilities, it was likely to be perceived by users as being due to the “new private network.” This, in fact, occurred to some degree and the method of addressing the situation is discussed later.

Cost Benefits — A return on investment of thirty percent and a three-year payback period were projected, based on traffic analysis for data and voice. It was proposed that, during the payback period, users be charged a rate equivalent or less than the best external service. Subsequent to the payback period, the objective was to achieve a ten to twenty percent reduction versus external alternatives. With long-distance data and voice billings from AT&T projected at \$9 million by 1984, this represented annual savings in the \$1 million range.

Competition — One question raised by management was the ability to maintain competitive pricing internally versus future offerings by AT&T and other providers. Although Bells' Advanced Communication Service (ACS) was announced about that time, corporate MIS management felt it would be at least five years before AT&T or other carriers could possibly be cost-competitive with the proposed network. By then, even if competitive threats were to materialize, the network would have more than paid for itself. To date, MIS management has been proven correct and is confident that this will continue to be the case in the foreseeable future.

Although the project had the agreement of Harris' president and two senior vice presidents, corporate MIS did not request final approval at that point. Recognizing senior management's concern for preserving the autonomy of divisions, the MIS objective was to sell the users on the concept before requesting final approval. To accomplish this, the corporate vice president of MIS, then the director of MIS, together with representatives of the Telecommunications Department and the Marketing Department of Satellite Communications Division, embarked on a “sales tour” of all involved divisions.

The network proposal was presented to the division general manager, controller, MIS director, and other members of management that the divisions chose to have in attendance. The proposal evoked the same general questions asked by corporate management, plus a variety of others, depending upon the degree of division familiarity with the technology. Some questioned the operational characteristics of how a person would actually place a voice call compared with current practice. Other questions were related to the technology to be employed. In all cases, cost and reliability were prime concerns. Acceptance was gained from all divisions with the caveat that the corporate Telecommunications Department be able to demonstrate that the cost of communications on the network would in fact be equivalent to, or less than, services provided by the public networks.

Upon gaining approval of division management, corporate MIS developed the formal recommendation to proceed and reviewed it with the Operations Policy Committee (all senior vice presidents, the president, and the Chairman of the Board). Shortly after the project was authorized, Harris announced its merger with Farinon Corporation, which added two product lines integral to future network plans — voice tandem switches and microwave radio. In view of this, the scope of the initial installation was expanded to include a switch and access network in the Melbourne, Florida area utilizing microwave links and tandem switches. This increased the total program authorization from \$2.0 million to \$2.5 million, and enhanced achievement of the network's cost-benefit objectives. The Corporate Telecommunications Director reviewed the expanded operating scenario and new equipment to be used with division general managers in order to obtain their approval of the increased costs. The approval procedure with division general managers is a continuing process each time the network is expanded.

It is important to note that throughout the approval process, the primary emphasis to management was cost-effectiveness based on current and projected traditional voice and data transmission. Electronic mail and video conferencing were identified as future potential benefits of more cost-effective communications, but they were not the basis for network justification. Program plans for these new applications were not fully developed at that time. However, from a corporate MIS perspective, the network project was a major step forward in emphasizing the importance of communications in integrating information technologies. Harris' MIS plans recognize networks as key elements in the integration of information management systems. These plans include the interconnection of three principal types of networks — local area networks, switch and access networks, and wide area networks to achieve total integration of telecommunication resources.

The Harris private network provided the visibility required to accelerate implementation of integrated networks throughout the company. This was a fundamental requirement to support Harris' overall Information Management Program. The goal of this program is to increase productivity of managers, professionals, and other “knowledge workers” by providing terminal access to multiple locations for word processing, data processing, engineering/scientific computing, and to internal and external databases. Users also have access to a comprehensive array of system utilities, and application software products, including high-level language decision support programs, to aid end users and professional programmers in applications development.

## Project Responsibilities and Resources

Corporate network justification, planning, implementation, and operation is the responsibility of the corporate MIS Telecommunications function. The program manager of the network project was the corporate MIS Telecommunications Director who had assistance from two members of his staff. The major vendors were Harris Satellite Communications, Farinon, and Digital Telephone Systems divisions, all of which provided equipment and installation support. The Satellite Communications Division also provided systems engineering support. Overall, the project management took about three man-years; two man-years from Corporate MIS, and one man-year from the Satellite Communications Division.

Elapsed time from authorization to implementation was one year — December 1979 to December 1980. The project was completed on schedule and \$50,000 under planned cost. When project reviews were held, members of senior corporate management were invited as well as senior members of division management, whether or not they were participating as initial users of the network. Such participation was occasional since most people were satisfied to receive minutes of the meetings. The project was a significant systems integration task involving many Harris products and some non-Harris equipment. The technological integration went smoothly. The major business management challenges were in billing and administration issues, which are discussed later.

## Private Satellite/Microwave Network Operation/Equipment

The initial project called for three, eleven-meter satellite earth stations located at Westerly, Rhode Island, Dallas, Texas, and at corporate headquarters in Melbourne, Florida. Although the network is described in terms of its initial implementation (see Figure 1), additional data and voice links have been established on an on-going basis. The satellite network has a total of six wideband channels spread over three links — Melbourne/Dallas, Melbourne/Westerly, and Dallas/Westerly. Each link has two carriers, e.g., one from Melbourne to Dallas and one from Dallas to Melbourne [3].

Satellite — The network configuration has space on the Western Union WESTAR III satellite on transponder 4. A transponder has 36 megahertz of bandwidth (60 megabit) and the Harris satellite system initially used five percent of a transponder.

Traffic Analysis — Data was gathered from billing tapes at the sites involved. Analysis utilized software packages licensed from Telco Research Corporation, Nashville, Tennessee, providing nodal and network optimization capabilities.

Traffic analysis played a key role in establishing existing costs for telecommunications, namely the target for equivalent or improved costs. This analysis resulted several times in immediate recommendations by the corporate telecommunications staff to implement division cost-reduction programs prior to initial network implementation. These recommendations to divisions somewhat eroded the basis upon which the original proposal for savings was developed, but not to a sufficient degree to undermine achievement of benefit objectives.

## Melbourne microwave switch and access system

In today's private networks, the cost of tie-line access to connect remote locations to the network switching nodes is a significant part of the total network cost. Melbourne, Florida (10,000 Harris people and multiple PBX locations) was an obvious candidate for private facilities for tariff insulation, reduced operating costs, and greater network control.

Due to the large number of circuits required for access and the relatively short distances involved, private microwave was the selected implementation. In this approach (see Figure 2), a user of any Melbourne-area PBX dials an access code and is automatically connected via microwave radio to the tandem switch. The call is then completed via the appropriate route.

Voice Operation — At each earth station a Harris PBX functions as a tandem switch for voice call routing. The tandem switch automatically selects the least expensive route for each out-going call — the choice being satellite links, Bell fixed facilities (tie or foreign exchange lines), or usage-sensitive facilities (WATS or DDD). The messages that go out via satellite are then switched by another tandem at the receiving earth station into the local network and, again, the tandem ensures least-cost routing for the call. All incoming messages arriving in Melbourne pass through the tandem switch into the Harris-owned microwave distribution system. Figure 3 illustrates voice operation, e.g., a call from the Government Systems Sector PBX is connected via microwave to the tandem switch, routed via the satellite link to the Rhode Island earth station, where the tandem switch then selects the least cost route (FX line) to Boston.

Data Operation — Data transmission is accomplished by two general methods, switched data and private-line data. Switched data uses the voice-switching facilities (tandem and PBX) of the network to establish the data connection, and communication facilities (satellite, microwave, terrestrial tie lines, FX and WATS lines) to transport the data. Terminal-to-terminal connection is possible through a dialed switched-data connection since end-to-end circuit switching is accomplished through the tandems and PBXs. The network is designed to support data connections of this type up to 4800 bits per second (bps). Figure 4 demonstrates how data is transmitted to and from a distributed data processor (DDP).

The Dallas DDP location is connected via the satellite link to the earthstation electronics in Melbourne, then via microwave to a 3705 controller and CPUs at the corporate Data Center. Another Harris division, in Grapevine, Texas, uses a distributed data processor connected to a 9600 bps leased line to the Dallas earth station to effect data transmission to and from Melbourne. Key elements of data transmission are modems and the satellite-delay compensation units. Digital data, in HASP protocol, is transmitted by the distributed processor and the satellite-delay compensation unit converts it to the bit-oriented protocol necessary to minimize the effect of the half-second satellite delay. The data circuits are point-to-point circuits, therefore no switching (as in voice operation) takes place.

In Melbourne, microwave connects the earth station with the data communications equipment. No modems are required as it is a digital system, but satellite-delay compensation units are needed. The data channels operate at 9600 bps but data rates, from 2400 bps to 1.5 megabit, are possible.

Protocols — Via direct interconnection, the network accommodates any synchronous data-link protocol, such as Binary·Synchronous (BSC), HASP Multileaving, SDLC and HDLC, on the private-line data service. The switched-data service can handle any synchronous-link protocol, as long as it functions in a half-duplex mode, and any asynchronous-link protocol in common use today.

Data Communications Devices — The network is transparent to the type of data communication devices applied, since only a communications channel is being provided by the network. As long as the devices operate within network data-channel-speed limits, and can support a common-link protocol that will perform satisfactorily with satellite communication delays, the network can be used as the communications facility.

Network Control Center Functions — The Network Control Center (NCC) responsibilities encompass both the operation and administrative functions of the network. The overall operation of the network is essentially unattended. Once it has been turned on, each element of the system functions without the need of manual intervention. The operational requirements consist of monitoring, performing diagnostics, and effecting repairs. Actual manning of the network takes place at the Network Control Center located in Melbourne. The Center supports the Dallas and Westerly sites via remote telemetry of system status, and services faults and user-service requests emanating from tributary PBX locations. Earth station electronics at the nodes are redundant for high availability. Redundancy-switching control, based on centralized monitoring, is effected throughout the network from the Network Control Center at the Melbourne node [3].

## Billing and Administration

Corporate MIS goals are to completely recover costs in the same budget year that billable services are provided, with a few exceptions for major startups. The network was such an exception, and was planned on an underrecovery basis until enough voice and data circuits could be added to fully recover the capital expenditures and the expense of the telecommunications staff required to support the network installation and operation.

The Network is equipped with storage devices which accumulate all network activity data. This information is processed offline by Telephone Accounting and Management System (TAMS) software for billing purposes. The same data is used for traffic analysis of expansion opportunities using Telco Research software.

Data Billing — Billing for private network data lines is equal to, or in most cases, twenty percent less than common-carrier charges for leased lines, including the line, modems, and circuit conditioning.

Voice Billing — Voice billing on the network system is ten percent or less than alternative costs for the same service, and no more than 65 percent of Bell DDD rates. ## Organizational Impacts

Senior Management — Primary impact was on the increased attention to the cost and value of communications. This resulted in greater management support for upgrading division telecommunications staff, and new applications of teleconferencing and electronic mail. Audio conferencing was established as a prelude to slow-scan teleconferencing and full-motion video. Decision support software at the Melbourne service center was readily available via the combined private and public network facilities. Two major Harris locations developed the required telecommunications expertise to implement local area networks. Overall, the network visibility created an environment that accelerated several advanced MIS programs by one to two years.

Division/Users — The corporate network was designed for minimal operating impact on users. Access-code procedures are the same as before; however, only a single access code is used rather than multiple codes for different facilities. In the event of a failure, alternate transmission paths are available. This is accomplished through manual-dial backup for data, and automatic voice rerouting to the telephone network either via the tandem switch or by dialing "9" at the PBX in the event of microwave or tandem-switch failure.

Division MIS Directors — Involvement in the program increased the communications awareness of all division MIS directors and gave impetus to electronic mail applications that feature local PBX-oriented directories with companywide routing of intercity text messages based on established voice-routing structures.

Division MIS Organization — For several years before implementation of the company network, corporate MIS had company-wide responsibility for strategic direction, products and architecture for MIS, telecommunications, and office automation. However, in most cases MIS functions at the division level had only the responsibility for traditional data processing activity. The importance of communications, the benefits of cost control, and the advantages of improved communications capability were all made highly visible by the corporate network. This accelerated the program of consolidation of MIS, telecommunications, and office systems in the divisions by two to three years.

In most cases, existing MIS personnel were assigned the top-level MIS role over all the functions. In one case, the consolidation of functions resulted in the appointment to sector director — MIS, a person with a strong engineering and communications background. This underscores the importance of increased understanding of telecommunication technologies and capabilities critical to MIS objectives of achieving business-system goals. Fortune 500 companies, with comprehensive data and voice networks and increasing emphasis on video, facsimile, and text transmission, will not be satisfied to leave the management of their telecommunications to AT&T. Although these companies would not consider having IBM run and control their private data processing centers, many have not yet established comparable telecommunications capability in their organizations.

Corporate Telecommunications Staffing — Most surprising to visitors is the small staff required to operate the network. Initially, this comprised three people, and with substantial network expansion, it now totals five. Visitors leave with the impression that managing one's own network is not in the “too hard” category.

## Cost Benefits

Attainment of cost-benefit objectives is proceeding better than planned. Current projections confirm payback in slightly less than the planned three-year period, with an increased rate of return (now estimated at 35 percent). Although the original plan was to bill customers at the equivalent rate of external services for three years, charges have been ten percent less than competitive alternatives for long distance voice calls and twenty percent less for data transmission. Competitive rate comparisons have been tested, and internal benefits verified, via a network analysis model of Harris traffic.

The volume of transmission is expected to grow significantly with minimal incremental costs to the network. This will result in achieving more than the target annual savings to users of ten percent by 1984. By 1985-1986, savings are expected to move toward twenty percent (approximately \$2 million annually) as new applications are added and AT&T continues to increase local rates. It is anticipated that when AT&T initiates local usage-sensitive rates in the Melbourne area, the routing of local calls via the established microwave system will yield \$500,000 savings annually for an additional one-time investment of \$300,000. Further microwave opportunities are also developing at other Harris locations.

A greater than expected benefit was the degree of communications cost awareness. Because of the publicity of the network, and the many discussions with divisions regarding telecommunication cost comparisons, divisional senior management became very aware of the tools available for managing the cost of communications. By distributing listings of communication costs to their departments, and directly billing costs to those departments, users became more conscious of the cost of unnecessary and unnecessarily long calls.

In May 1981, shortly after the network was initiated, there was a major tariff increase affecting voice costs. Harris divisions in the Melbourne area responded with a seventeen percent decrease in long distance calls being processed through the tandem switch in Melbourne. Through cost control alone, in the period July 1981 to July 1982, Harris effected a savings of \$1 million on an annualized basis. Cost control results are easily measured via the traffic analysis data from the tandem switches. This type of analysis is possible without a private network, but the communications awareness would not have occurred as rapidly or as extensively without the network and the increased involvement of division general managers.

The leading-edge network was a motivator beyond anyone's expectations. Of the entire MIS program, the corporate network has been the most exciting program for Harris users. The attraction and interest in the satellite/microwave technology gained strong user participation in the understanding of telecommunications capabilities, costs, and cost-control techniques.

## Service

Network availability has exceeded the 99 percent availability goal every month since inception. Only one, early, major outage was experienced and the transparent fallback avoided user disruption. In one case of severe disruption to Melbourne AT&T facilities lasting 24 hours, the private network was the only access for long distance calls from and to Harris' Melbourne facilities.

The user-perception problem regarding delay and echo, which was anticipated at project inception, did in fact occur. This was addressed via publicity citing analysis statistics tracing reported problems to AT&T facilities more frequently than to the private network. The analysis showed that two out of three problems were associated with AT&T. To continue demonstrating network quality, special two-week analyses are conducted periodically. During these periods, a sample of users at all levels is requested to report daily on problem calls and overall satisfaction levels. The user is unaware of how calls are being routed and calls are deliberately forced via Harris and non-Harris network facilities on alternate days.

At the end of the tests, users receive summaries of the problems reported and the facilities that were in use during the problem calls. The results of these surveys, together with day-to-day experience, have been effective in demonstrating to users that Harris' private network facilities are comparable to, or better than, public services. The surveys did identify that a minority of users resist satellite delay, regardless of the facilities being used. Another minority perceived the delays as enhancing conversation, encouraging fewer interruptions and better listening. The majority of users function equally well in a terrestrial or satellite mode.

## Applicability of Harris Experience to Others

As a general guideline in the U.S., a satellite link is worth consideration by those companies having net sales exceeding \$500 million, two or more facilities located over 500 miles apart, and a volume of traffic between those locations of 100,000 call-minutes per month or the telephone data equivalent. However, it is not just a question of cost comparison. It also has to do with the quality of service available on the public network. A minicomputer, for example, may put out data at 19,200 bits per second (bps), but in order for that computer to send data over existing public service telephone lines, transmission rates have to be slowed to 9600 bps. Private systems offer more flexibility and higher transmission speeds.

Microwave and fiber-optic links are vital parts of a network. They provide all-digital local-distribution systems economically, and allow extra channels to be added quickly and inexpensively. The links used in the Harris network are eighteen GHz (gigahertz) and two GHz digital microwaves. These digital systems can handle anything from just a few T1 lines to as many as 28 T1 lines. Each T1 line can either handle 24 or 48 voice channels or multiple 56 kilobit or less channels, or a combination of both. For example, 672 to 1344 voice channels can be handled on one microwave (T3 equivalent) link. For data transmission, the network has the flexibility to handle data rates from 2400 bps to 1.5 Mbps at significantly reduced cost versus terrestrial facilities and, most importantly, it avoids further tariff increases for leased lines.

## Network Expansion

Network expansion began shortly after initial implementation and is an ongoing effort. Expansion consists of concentrating additional voice and data traffic at each of the earth-station nodes. This concentration is effected through public networks (AT&T, MCI, etc.) facilities and through statistical multiplexers required in certain locations for further data concentration. Satellite transponder use has increased from five to eight percent; however, capacity efficiency has increased significantly through more effective utilization of bandwidth. In March 1983, the satellite/microwave tandem private facilities handled 317 voice circuits and 841,123 on-net voice minutes. Thirty-two data circuits (from 2.4 kbs to 56 kbs) are supporting twelve divisions connected to the Melbourne computer processing facilities.

AT&T divestiture will affect expansion of the network in several ways. The AT&T strategy appears to be competitive where competition exists, namely in wide-area communication. However, tariffs are being filed for significant increases in the non-competitive local/regional areas for private voice and data lines. This is causing some erosion of near-term planned savings, based on existing costs, via use of AT&T private lines. On the other hand, these major tariff increases enhance justification for additional private microwave switch and access networks comparable to the one described in the Melbourne area.

## Future Directions

Additional Tandem Switches — PBXs implemented in New York and Chicago are easily upgradable to tandem features, and this will happen as the network evolves. A tandem switch on the West Coast is being investigated.

Interactive Terminal Support — Several approaches are available for additional interactive terminal support to remote locations. One approach is to utilize additional IBM 37XX communication controllers at the nodes. They can serve as SNA extensions of the Information Processing Center in Melbourne for remote polling of local communication controllers. The communications protocol will be SDLC, a bit-oriented protocol which is currently satellite-efficient up to 19.2 kbs, or higher with extended modulo, eliminating the need for satellite delay compensation units. Another approach is to use nodal processors to provide remote-polling selection at the remote earth station node. The nodal processors can also be utilized to provide protocol conversions, switching of electronic mail, and other desired capabilities.

Computer Aided Engineering — An extensive library of computer aided design/computer aided engineering software in the Melbourne area may be accessed by remote design centers requiring substantial bandwidth. These requirements were not part of the original specification but may be a major part of the network in the future.

TDMA Satellite System — TDMA (Time Division Multiple Access) satellite systems are expected to be the future direction and will allow more effective utilization of bandwidth. The Harris network (currently time division multiplex) is expected to be upgraded with TDMA technology.

## Business Opportunities

Recognizing the increasing business potential of private networks, supported by the success of its in-house network as well as other major installations for Martin Marietta and the Atlantic Richfield Company, Harris formed a new business unit — the Telecommunications Network Division. This division provides network analysis, consulting, and turnkey systems support for private networks.

## MIS Image

Although the MIS activity has been well accepted in Harris since its inception, its greatest boost in the company came from the network project. It was, by far, the most significant contribution to advancing the larger concept of integrating telecommunications, office systems, and data processing. The network demonstrated to Harris people, as it does to other companies visiting the Harris network facilities, that managing a private network is no more difficult than managing private data processing or engineering/scientific computing utilities.

Shortly before the network became operational, the senior MIS position was elevated to company officer status, and as noted by Harris' President, Jack Hartley, "the corporate MIS telecommunications program was one of several major factors warranting that recognition." Harris' senior management's testimony regarding success of the network is further evidenced by the formation of the Telecommunications Network Division. The new business venture required the approval of the top management who have been first-hand observers as network users and close observers of the monthly cost-recovery analysis to ascertain the success in achieving cost-benefit objectives.

In the keynote address to the Communications Networks Conference in Atlanta, Georgia in January 1982, Dr. J.A. Boyd, Harris Chairman, made several references to the Harris network in his remarks on developing trends in integrated communications networks. Among other things, he noted, “The traditional public system does not provide a very wide bandwidth capacity to most locations. You can get a telephone, but when you start trying to get conditioned lines for broadband data, video or whatever, it becomes quite a complex problem in most parts of the country today. Through our own network (the Harris Private Network), we anticipate continuing better service and further reductions in costs over the long run” [2].
