# Influences on the IS Manager's Perceptions of Key Issues: Information Scanning and the Relationship With the CEO

By: Richard T. Watson
Department of Management
University of Georgia
Athens, Georgia 30602

## Abstract

This study investigates some factors that may influence the information systems (IS) manager's perceptions of key issues. A conceptual model relates key issues to the IS manager's scanning behavior and relationship with the CEO. The study, based on data collected from 43 Australian IS managers, describes the information scanning behavior of IS executives. The investigation reports two findings: (1) IS managers who have two-way communication with the CEO rate IS strategic planning as less critical; and (2) IS managers tend to scan sources that are close to the industry, and they are most influenced by their peers. The findings suggest that IS managers need to be more aware of the necessity to match the ambiguity of a message with its method of transmission, and they should consider broadening their scanning if they wish to find new opportunities for exploiting information systems technology.

Keywords: IS management, scanning, organizational communication, key issues
ACM Categories: K.6.0, K.7.0

## Introduction

There have been several studies to determine the key concerns of information systems (IS) managers in North America (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Dickson, et al., 1984), Europe (Davenport and Buday, 1988; Moynihan, 1990) and Singapore (Rao, et al., 1987). None of these studies attempts to discern why some issues are ranked more highly than others. Understanding the process by which some IS issues are perceived as more important than others may shed some light on the factors that influence the IS executive's judgment. This article reports the results of a study to discover why some issues are seen as more critical than others.

No prior work on the general origin of key issues was found in the MIS literature. However, there is some examination of why IS planning, a top key issue in several studies, is considered important. Lederer and Mendelow (1987) look specifically at why IS planning is often ranked as a number one concern. Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1989) explore the relationship between the rank of the IS manager and IS planning. Other research suggests that the behavior and opinion of executives is influenced by their information scanning behavior and relationship to the chief executive officer (CEO), and this research is used to develop the broad conceptual model that drives this study. The first section of the article outlines the conceptual model supporting the study. Next is a discussion of the methodology used, followed by a report of the findings and a discussion of the results and conclusions.

## The Conceptual Model

This study is founded on the premise that an IS manager's perception of key IS issues is largely determined by the signals he or she receives from within and without the organization. IS managers receive many signals, most of which they ignore because they lack the capacity to process all the information they receive. IS managers “tune” themselves to notice, and indeed seek, those signals that they deem meaningful. A manager's assessment of what is meaningful is presumed to be based on the context in which the IS manager operates his or her personal traits. The model shown in Figure 1 suggests that three classes of contextual variables (environmental, organizational, and IS departmental) and the characteristics of the IS manager influence scanning behavior and, in turn, the perception of key issues. For example, an IS manager in a manufacturing company with plans to automate production is likely to scan for information on robotics. To some degree, irrespective of what signals are received, the IS manager may judge that robotics is a key issue due to the firm's decision to automate production.

Aspects of the environment that may be important include the industry's competitiveness and information intensity, and characteristics of customers, markets, and competitors. For instance, IS managers in firms operating in highly dynamic, competitive industries are likely to spend more time scanning because they need to be prepared to handle environmental contingencies. At the organizational level, important factors can be the relationship between the IS manager and the CEO, the firm's culture, the perceived strategic value of IS, and the power and status of the IS manager. For example, in an enterprise that accords IS a position in the strategic quadrant of McFarlan, et al.'s (1983) classification of IS environments, the IS manager would be expected to scan more vigorously and rate IS issues with a possible strategic impact as more important. IS departmental factors that could be influential are departmental resources, local problems, and the quality of IS personnel. Managers with scarce human resources may have to spend a great deal of time on administrative matters and have little time to scan. In addition to contextual variables, there are individual variables associated with the IS manager that could influence both scanning and the perception of key issues. Managers with flexible personalities tend to focus on short-term issues, but managers with a high need for achievement are oriented to long-term concerns (Miller and Toulouse, 1986). Some IS managers may have particular skills, experiences, and cognitive abilities that bias them toward selectively scanning certain sectors of the environment.

The model depicted in Figure 1 is very broad in scope and is beyond the scope of a single piece of research. As there has been no prior work in this area, this first attempt to understand the origin of key issues focuses on a small but important segment of the full model.

An IS manager receives signals from without and within the organization. Sources of external information include industry journals, newspapers, consultants, vendors, and conferences. Within the organization, the main source of signals is the CEO, and an IS manager's perceptions of the CEO's goals will be influenced by the “closeness” of the IS manager to the CEO. This study tests parts of the model shown in Figure 1 by examining the relationships shown in Figure 2, which assert that an IS manager's perception of key issues is influenced by his or her scanning behavior and closeness to the CEO.

## Information scanning

Decision makers' interpretations of environmental stimuli are a major force in promoting organizational change (Kanter, 1983), with scanning being the first stage of a response mechanism that leads to an organization adapting to its environment (Hambrick, 1981b). Because many organizational activities in a post-industrial society are information-intensive, IS executives who can accurately perceive environmental signals and determine a relevant response can play a significant role in maintaining the competitive stance of a firm. As the importance of information technology (IT) to organizational survival increases, organizations may need to co-opt the IS manager into the dominant coalition (March, 1962) if they want to take advantage of the IS manager's skills in deciphering IT changes and linking them to the organization's future.

Technological innovation requires managers who can comprehend the importance of external information and transfer this knowledge to appropriate personnel in the organization (Utterback, 1971). An IS manager, as a link between IT and an organization's managers, should gather information about new information technology and advise fellow managers on its effective deployment. In order to perform this boundary-spanning role (Tushman, 1977), an IS manager must scan the external environment for IT developments and scrutinize the internal environment for opportunities to marry external developments with internal opportunities. Under this scenario, an IS manager's scanning behavior is motivated by organizational goals.

The model just described is highly rational in that it portrays the IS manager as consciously scanning with the intent of linking internal problems and external solutions. A counter view is put forward by Cohen, et al. (1972) in their “garbage can” model of organizational choice. They maintain that when goals and technology are hazy and participation by organizational members varies greatly, a choice opportunity can be viewed as a garbage can into which problems and solutions are dumped as they are identified. Decisions are based upon the current mix in the garbage can and demands on the decision maker's time. Decision making is a happenstance mix of problems and solutions. Scanning occurs, but the motivation is different. An IS manager contributes to the garbage can because the more solutions that are advanced, the greater the probability that one of these will collide with a particular problem in the organizational mix of issues and thus lead to an increase in resources allocated to IS. Under the garbage can model, scanning is a political activity aimed at increasing the IS department's resources.

Environmental scanning, the process of learning about events and trends in the organization's environment (Hambrick, 1981b), is a form of “coping by information” (Hickson, et al., 1971). All middle- and upper-level managers conduct some, primarily informal, environmental scanning (Aguilar, 1967; Kefalas and Schoderbek, 1973). Hambrick (1981a) maintains that the environment can be decomposed into four elements: output, throughput, administrative, and regulatory. Of the four elements, the throughput domain should be the major concern to IS executives because it is concerned with “external developments bearing on the processing or delivery of products and services” (Hambrick, 1981a, p. 257). Hence, IS executives could be expected to search mainly for opportunities in the throughput domain.

The ability to cope with uncertainty is a source of power (Hickson, et al., 1971), and two forms of coping behavior are defined by Hambrick (1981a). The first is based on the functional area specialization of the executive. For instance, the skills and orientation of an executive in marketing, compared to the IS executive, are more suited to handling an output contingency. However, with acceptance of the notion of using IT to create competitive advantage (Gerstein and Reisman, 1982; McFarlan, 1984), IS managers could now be expected to also handle output contingencies. Thus, IS executives have two potential sources of power—throughput and output contingencies—for coping with uncertainty. Scanning can be viewed as a political activity because it increases an IS manager's capacity to handle contingencies and gain power within the organization.

Coping with the environment by scanning is a second and separate basis for power. Executives who scan critical information in other functional areas have additional power (Hambrick, 1981b). One would therefore expect a relationship between the amount of scanning and power of an IS manager. High-scanning IS managers should have an increased probability of recognizing IT opportunities and be well-equipped to cope with contingencies when they arise.

Scanning also has a social component. An IS manager detects environmental signals by reading industry newspapers, attending seminars, speaking to colleagues, and so forth. The signals that a manager receives are usually generated by opinion leaders because these are the people who gain access to the media, speak at seminars, and are consulted for advice. If opinion leaders are advocating greater attention to end-user computing, then it is likely that IS managers, collectively, will rate end-user computing as a more important concern than an issue that has received less importance. In this case, an IS manager is seen as a social person who can be influenced by the opinions of high-status individuals.

Three models have been put forward to suggest why IS managers scan. A rational perspective suggests that IS managers scan to find opportunities to support the attainment of organizational goals. The garbage can model indicates that IS managers scan so they can add to the garbage can. The power model premises that IS managers scan in order to increase their ability to handle contingencies. All models suggest that it is important for an IS manager to scan. Furthermore, it is suggested that an IS manager's interpretation of environmental signals, irrespective of the rationale for scanning, is influenced by the social status of the sender. Generally, it seems appropriate to view scanning as a mix of rational, political, and social behavior.

While the three models provide different rationales for scanning, very little is known about the actual scanning behavior of IS managers. This study reports on IS managers' scanning behavior and examines the relationship between scanning behavior and the perception of key issues. It proposes that an IS manager's perception of the key issues is influenced by scanning behavior. Managers who scan broadly by examining many sources of information may develop a different set of perceptions from those who scan narrowly. Managers who spend more time scanning may also have a different set of views on key concerns. This leads to the first proposition:

Proposition 1: The scanning behavior of an IS manager will influence his or her judgment of key issues.

## Relationship with the CEO

The CEO's task is to set an organization's basic goals, policies, and strategies in the face of a high degree of uncertainty (Kotter, 1982). CEOs typically know more about the enterprise than any other person in an organization and spend 40 percent of their time on contact activities related to the exchange of information (Mintzberg, 1973). As the dominant source of information within the organization, the CEO should have a major influence on an IS manager's assessment of key issues. However, an IS manager's judgment will be strongly influenced by how directly and clearly information disseminated by the CEO is received. Mintzberg (1973) observed that the cursory manner in which CEOs deal with written material places remote subordinates at an information disadvantage compared to those who work more closely and communicate verbally with the CEO. The information disadvantage of the remote subordinate is compounded for messages of high equivocality, which require rich media, such as face-to-face interaction, to enable an exchange of information that can lead to a common perspective and resolution of disagreement (Daft, et al., 1987). In addition, an ability to maintain communication links with top management is a critical success factor for an IS manager (Rockart, 1982). The IS manager who is close to the CEO is likely to have a more accurate perception of what the chief executive expects.

In dealing with the CEO, the IS manager's choice of medium is dictated by three variables: (1) message ambiguity; (2) symbolic cues; and (3) situational determinants (Trevino, et al., 1987). When message ambiguity is high, in the ideal situation where medium choice is unhindered by organizational reporting levels and norms of behavior, the IS manager should use the richness of face-to-face discussion because of its potential for immediate feedback and multiple cues (voice inflection, pitch, speed, and tone). In unambiguous situations, the IS manager can use a lean medium such as a memo. The choice of a particular medium provides its own symbolic cues. For example, the IS manager who uses a memo to convey an important message to the CEO is sending mixed signals because a memo has a sense of non-urgency and routineness, and the CEO could well perfunctorily judge the message as being unimportant because of the inherent symbolic cues. High-performing managers are skilled at matching the ambiguity of the message with the appropriate medium (Daft, et al., 1987).

Two situational determinant variables influencing medium choice are geographic dispersion and job pressure (Steinfeld and Fulk, 1986). An IS manager physically remote from the CEO, such as in another building or city, will obviously have less opportunity to interact directly with the CEO and will be forced to use leaner media. Furthermore, the sheer competition for access to the CEO's scarce time can make it very difficult for a one-on-one discussion, and an IS manager may have to use a less pertinent medium. A fundamental situational determinant is the organization's reporting hierarchy, the purpose of which is to regulate the flow of communication to the CEO. IS managers who report directly to the CEO have greater flexibility in medium choice than their counterparts reporting at lower levels. A consideration of the factors affecting medium choice leads to the conclusion that IS managers who are close to the CEO should have a greater understanding of organizational goals; this relationship should impact their assessment of key issues.

In the specific case of IS planning, Lederer and Mendelow (1987) report that IS planning is a problem for those IS executives not actively participating in the formulation of the organization's strategy. They proffer some 20 tactics that an IS manager can use to increase involvement in strategic policy formulation. Many of these techniques are directed at getting the IS executive closer to top management. Lederer and Mendelow recognize that in order to undertake IS strategic planning, the IS manager must know the objectives of top management. Undoubtedly, the IS manager who is close to the CEO should have a more precise understanding of the objectives of top management than an IS manager remote from the CEO. Therefore, the second proposition is:

Proposition 2: The relationship of an IS manager to the CEO will influence the IS manager's judgment of key issues.

In summary, this research examines the notion that an IS manager's perception of key concerns is, among other things, likely to be influenced by external signals and internal messages received from the CEO. Information scanning by the IS manager is a key form of receiving external signals, and the relationship of the IS manager to the CEO is likely to determine the nature of their communication.

## Methodology

A self-report questionnaire for measuring scanning behavior and the relationship to the CEO was completed by 43 IS managers of the top 200 Australian organizations (a response rate of 21.5%). This instrument was administered during the third and final round of a Delphi study of the key issues of Australian IS managers (Watson, 1989).

The first part of the questionnaire, which is designed to discover scanning behavior, is modeled on an instrument developed by Schoderbek, et al. (1985). The purpose of the instrument is to find out what sources of information IS managers normally use to keep up-to-date on information systems management and management issues. Respondents were supplied with a list of 39 possible sources (21 for information systems and 18 for management) and were asked to check those that they used and how much influence each source had on forming their opinions. The questionnaire did not provide guidelines to assist repondents to distinguish between IS management issues and management issues. It was left to their judgment. Influence was measured using a seven-point scale anchored on “little” and “great.” Respondents were also asked to estimate how many hours per week they spent on keeping up-to-date on both information systems management and management issues.

The second part of the questionnaire assessed the IS manager's relationship with the CEO. The relationship with the CEO was operationalized in three ways by requesting information on: (1) the number of reporting levels to the CEO; (2) the physical location of the IS manager with respect to the CEO; and (3) the mode of communication between the IS manager and the CEO for an important problem.

Following analysis of the questionnaire data, some respondents were contacted by telephone and interviewed. The purpose of the interview was to discover respondents' rationales in selecting their most important issue, and this information was used to cross-check the findings suggested by the data analysis.

## Findings

The findings are presented in two parts. The first part reports the descriptive results of the study and provides details of information scanning behavior and the relationship between the IS manager and the CEO. The second part discusses the study's hypotheses.

## Descriptive analysis

## Information Scanning

The reported sources of information used by information systems managers for remaining up-to-date on IS management issues are shown in Table 1. The most widely used sources of information (cited by 91 percent of respondents) are hardware vendors' reports and industry seminars. These are closely followed by discussions with other professionals (86 percent), industry newspapers such as Computerworld (84 percent), software vendors' reports (84 percent), and visiting other organizations (84 percent). Discussions with other professionals are the most influential source of information (mean score of 5.02 on a scale of 1 to 7). IS executives are also influenced by internally prepared staff reports (4.82), industry conferences (4.77), industry seminars (4.74), and visits to other organizations (4.69). There is no significant association (r = 0.31 and p = 0.18) between the percentage of managers using a particular source of information and its influence. This lack of association between the degree of usage and the importance of a source is also reported by Kefalas and Schoderbek (1973). For instance, nearly all managers scan hardware vendors' reports but are not heavily influenced by their content. It is noticeable that academics, through either courses or journal publications, and consultants are not widely used or influential. Broadly, it appears that IS executives mainly seek information of immediate value, hence the focus on sources very close to the industry, and are most influenced by other IS practitioners.

The reported sources of information used by information systems managers for remaining up-to-date on management issues are shown in Table 2. The two most widely used sources are the Australian Financial Review (a national financial paper similar to the Wall Street Journal) and discussions with colleagues in other professions (both reported by 74 percent of respondents). Some 60 percent of respondents cited The Australian (a national morning newspaper), industry seminars, and management conferences. The most influential sources of information are management conferences (mean score of 4.77 on a 1 to 7 scale), discussions with colleagues in other professions (4.69), internal training sessions (4.68), industry seminars (4.62), and visiting other organizations (4.46). There is an association (r = 0.46 and p = 0.06) between the use of an information source and its perceived influence. In the case of management issues, IS executives tend to use those sources they find most influential. Again, it is observed that academics and consultants are not influential. The overall impression is that IS executives stay abreast of current management issues by reading a national newspaper and interacting with their peers.

## Hypothesis testing

The conceptual model tested in this study relates key issues to information scanning behavior and closeness to the CEO. Given that this is the first study to investigate the source of key issues, a significance level of 10 percent is applied. Discussion commences with the method used for examining the relationship between information scanning behavior and key concerns.

The data for this study were collected in the third and final round of a Delphi study of IS managers' key concerns. Each IS manager's rating of the importance of an issue was measured on a 10-point scale. In addition, the same questionnaire collected data on scanning behavior and closeness to the CEO. Thus, for any issue, Pearson correlation coefficients can be calculated for respondents' rating of that issue and scanning behavior and closeness to the CEO.

Hypotheses were tested using correlation analysis. When all entries in a correlation matrix are examined for significance, a Bonferroni type correction (Neter, et al., 1985) to the significance level should be made. For a family significance level of 10%, the significance level for any entry in an n × m correlation matrix becomes 0.10/(n × m). This significance level becomes very small when a large correlation matrix is examined; our attention is therefore confined to only the top five issues identified in the final round of the Delphi study.

## Information Scanning

Four measures of scanning behavior were used. The reported number of hours for keeping up-to-date on IS management and management issues, as supplied by IS managers, provides a measure of the extent of scanning. Similarly, the reported number of sources for keeping up-to-date on IS management and management issues provides a measure of the breadth of scanning. Pearson correlation coefficients for the top five key issues with measures of scanning behavior are shown in Table 3. Each cell shows the correlation coefficient and, in parentheses, the significance of that correlation. As the resulting correlation matrix is $5 \times 4$ , the adjusted significance level is 0.005. Inspection of the correlation matrix indicates that there is no association between key issues and scanning behavior. Therefore, proposition H1 is rejected, and it is concluded that there is no relationship between key issues and scanning behavior.

## Relationship With the CEO

The second aspect of the conceptual model investigated is the relationship between key issues and closeness to the CEO. Because of the small number of observations in some of the original categories, the variable measuring communication with the CEO was recoded prior to correlation analysis into categories of two-way (face-to-face or telephone) and one-way communication (memo or via boss). The correlation matrix is $5 \times 3$ , giving an adjusted significance level of 0.007. Examination of the correlation matrix in Table 4 suggests that there is a relationship between IS strategic planning and communication with the CEO (shown bolded). The mode of communication between the IS manager and the CEO explains 19% of the variation in IS strategic planning ratings. Analysis of the mean planning score for each classification shows that managers who have two-way communication with the CEO (mean rating = 7.00 on a scale of 1 to 10) are less concerned with planning issues than those managers who have only one-way communication (mean rating = 8.73).

The interpretation of these findings is fairly straightforward. Managers who have two-way conversations with the CEO have a better understanding of the goals, objectives, and direction of the organization and consequently find that planning is a less onerous task. In an expanded follow-up telephone interview, a manager who gave IS strategic planning a low rating (i.e., it was not a major problem) indicated that he was a member of the group of four to five people who set the direction for the organization and had constant communication with the CEO. In contrast, an IS executive who rated IS strategic planning as a major problem cited the great difficulty he had in speaking to the CEO. In fact, the last two occasions on which he had raised IS matters directly with the CEO, outside of a formal meeting, had been in the street (when he serendipitously bumped into the CEO) and at his organization's Christmas party. Those managers who are remote from the CEO and interact through memos or via their bosses are less informed about the organization's overall direction and have much greater difficulty in planning because they simply cannot read the CEO's mind. These managers are not part of the dominant coalition (March, 1962) and are generally excluded from the strategic decision-making process.

The nature of this relationship is clearly apparent in Table 5, and the statistical significance is confirmed by Kendall's tau-b statistic (Freeman, 1987), which has a value of 0.558 (p < 0.0001). The higher an IS manager is in the organizational hierarchy, the more likely he or she has two-way communication with the CEO. This is not surprising because a major purpose of a reporting hierarchy is to structure the vertical flow of communication between a manager and subordinates.

The two findings can be linked to create a causal model (see Figure 3), which suggests that the reporting level of the IS manager influences the mode of communication between the IS manager and the CEO, which in turn affects the IS manager's assessment of planning as a key issue. This finding supports the prior discussion suggesting that reporting level is a situational variable that influences the IS manager's choice of medium when dealing with an ambiguous message.

## Discussion

Australian IS managers tend to be somewhat inward-looking in their use of sources of information to remain abreast of IS management issues. They focus on sources very close to the industry and are most influenced by other practitioners. IS managers are primarily influenced by interpersonal channels (e.g., other professionals, staff reports, and organizational visits); mass media channels (e.g., industry newspapers) are less influential. This is not surprising because only the few early adopters of new technology tend to be swayed by mass media channels (Rogers, 1983). The greater number of late adopters rely on interpersonal channels. An IS manager who wishes to get ahead of competitors might be advised to take more notice of mass media reports and seek information outside the immediate IS arena. IS

For example, marketing trade journals may be a good source of ideas for developing information systems to gain competitive advantage. IS managers who are seeking to innovate should recognize that they are more likely to find new ideas if they adopt a scanning strategy different from their peers.

Regarding management issues, IS managers appear to take a much broader perspective in their scanning. However, it is worth noting that IS managers spend about twice as much time (roughly five hours versus two and one-half hours) scanning IS management issues as they do scanning management issues. Given that so much of the IS management literature (e.g., McFarlan, et al., 1983) proposes that IS executives should be more actively involved in the management of the organization, it may be pertinent for IS managers to spend more time scanning management issues.

No relationship was found between scanning behavior and key issues. A possible explanation for this finding is that though the level of scanning varies among IS managers, they all receive very similar signals because the various sources of information tend to repeat the important signals. In effect, key issues are like the top 10 pop tunes in that they get a lot of air play and anyone tuned in will soon get the message. Hambrick (1982) suggests a similar explanation for his finding of a lack of a relationship between scanning and organizational strategy. An alternative explanation is that the model tested by this research does not take account of all the variables that can influence perceived key issues. When some of the variables discussed in relation to the full model of Figure 1 are controlled, then a relationship between scanning behavior and key issues may emerge. Nevertheless, Hambrick's rationalization is very powerful, and it might be useful for future research to investigate his explanation. One approach would be to content analyze the most influential sources of information and count the number of messages for each key issue. This data could be used to test the premise that a message about a key issue appears repeatedly in influential sources of information.

A significant relationship was found between the mode of communication with the CEO and the rating of IS strategic planning. While this study is based upon Australian data, IS strategic planning is also the number one concern of IS managers in Europe and the United States (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Davenport and Buday, 1988). A complex problem such as IS strategic planning is characterized by equivocality. As Daft, et al. (1987) point out, equivocality occurs when there are multiple and conflicting interpretations about an organizational situation, when there is no established common viewpoint, and when the manager has to interpret vague cues. The IS manager who is confused about organizational goals and who does not, or cannot, use rich methods of communication with the CEO will certainly find IS strategic planning a major problem.

The essence of this problem is nicely captured by Nietzsche (1886): “A matter that becomes clear ceases to concern us” (p. 80).

The simple solution to the IS manager's medium choice problem is to suggest that, when highly equivocal issues must be resolved, the IS manager goes directly to the CEO. The reality of life in many corporations is that such behavior is often acceptable only when the IS manager reports directly to the CEO and may not be a feasible option for many IS managers. The IS manager, like most other managers, is confined by the hierarchical reporting structure. Indeed, a key purpose of a hierarchical reporting structure is to restrict direct access to the CEO to those managers who perform functions assessed as critical to the success of the organization. The IS manager has two courses of action.

First, the IS manager should examine the importance of the IS function to the success of the organization by using a tool such as McFarlan, et al.'s (1983) strategic grid. If IS is key to the success of the organization, then the IS manager must prepare a case for elevating the reporting status of IS. Of course, this puts the IS manager on the spot because this message should be delivered directly to the CEO, and it will be necessary to wait for an opportunity to personally deliver the message or rely on an intermediary, such as an executive or consultant with direct access to the CEO.

The second line of attack is for the IS manager to accept the existing reporting structure and work on ensuring that it accurately communicates messages to and from the CEO. This means that the IS manager will have to carefully brief his or her immediate supervisor and request regular policy debriefing sessions. The goal is to increase the quality of communication so that the IS manager's immediate superior accurately transmits and receives messages.

Essentially, the IS manager can try to change the organization's structure, which is typically a difficult task because it perturbs the existing power structure, or can make more effective use of the organizational communication system, which is possibly an easier path to take. In practical terms, there may be little that many IS managers can do to change the medium choice for many messages. Thus, the issue is not so much medium choice but making the immediate supervisor a more effective transmitter and receiver of messages. Consequently, the IS manager may need to spend more time educating his or her immediate superior about the potential organizational role of IS and become more skilled in questioning the immediate superior about organizational goals and their impact on the IS function.

IS planning may remain a problem for the IS manager remote from the CEO. The fact that the IS manager is distant from the CEO is an indication that the organization does not place a high value on IS and IS planning. In this situation, the IS manager must realistically adjust to the organization's perceived importance of IS.

IS managers should be sensitive to the medium choice decision. The IS manager must learn to recognize those messages that call for face-to-face discussion with either superiors or subordinates. The IS manager could consider providing subordinates with guidelines for medium choice so they learn to make the appropriate choice when dealing with the IS manager and, more importantly, users. Some system development activities (e.g., information requirements determination) can contain a high degree of ambiguity, and a reliance on written documentation as the major means of analyst-user communication does not recognize the importance of matching the medium to the equivocality of the message. Matching the message and the medium is important for all communication, whether it is up, down, or across the hierarchy.

The findings of this research are limited in several ways. First, only two independent variables have been studied, and, as Figure 1 clearly demonstrates, there are many other variables that need to be considered. Second, the results are based upon the reported behavior of a sample of IS managers from Australia's top 200 organizations. The results may not generalize to other cultures, nations, or to organizations that differ greatly from the top 200 Australian enterprises.

This study is an initial attempt to discover why certain IS management issues are rated more highly than others. Future research could extend the model used in this study by including some of the variables from the categories identified in Figure 1. Future research could use a different research method to investigate the information scanning behavior of IS managers. For example, observation of the scanning behavior of IS managers should provide some insights into what managers scan and what they seek. Because this study found that medium choice can explain the prominence of IS strategic planning, it may be appropriate to investigate how practicing IS managers who do not report directly to the CEO handle the medium choice problem for equivocal messages. In particular, because high-performing managers are skillful at medium choice (Daft, et al., 1987), the communication strategies of high-performing IS managers could be compared with those of less-successful managers.

A major message of this study is that it shows that IS managers may have the wherewithal to reduce the importance of their collective number one concern, IS strategic planning. IS managers who can select the correct mode of communication with the CEO or can improve the quality of their communication with the CEO through their immediate superior should be able to reduce the ambiguity that makes IS strategic planning a major problem. While this may be superficially obvious, it is clear that some IS managers make the wrong medium choice or do not effectively communicate with their superiors.

This study also found that IS managers tend to be narrow in their scanning because they focus on sources close to the industry and are most influenced by other practitioners. Ambitious IS managers who wish to be innovative should ensure that they scan more widely than their colleagues so that they are among the first to recognize new opportunities for IT.

## Appendix B

## The Top Five Key Issues and Their Rationale

1. Issue: Improving IS strategic planning.

Rationale: It is increasingly critical to an organization's success that it align its long-range plan with its strategic business plan. Rapidly changing business environments, increased involvement of end users, and accelerated technological change underscore the need to continue improving strategic planning skills.

2. Issue: Specifying, recruiting, and developing human resources of IS.

Rationale: Current and future shortages of qualified information systems personnel threaten the IS department's ability to keep up with the information needs of its parent organization. Computer personnel need to be recognized as people who can assist the organization to grow. They have to develop the ability to identify with the employer.

3. Issue: Developing an information architecture.

Rationale: A corporate/global information architecture is needed to identify the major information categories used within an enterprise and their relationships to business processes. It is necessary to guide applications development and facilitate the sharing of data.

4. Issue: Aligning the IS organization with that of the enterprise.

Rationale: The effectiveness with which IS can support the enterprise's information needs is dependent on the IS department's position within the enterprise. Too often IS is not located appropriately within the organization.

5. Issue: Improving the effectiveness of software development.

Rationale: The application development backlog remains at unacceptably high levels. Users are getting impatient. Add to this the increasing costs of human resources, and the need for improved effectiveness in systems development becomes clear.
