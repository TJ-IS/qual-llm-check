# After the Sale: Leveraging Maintenance with Information Technology

By: Blake Ives

## Abstract

Post-sale maintenance is already an important part of the competitive strategy of some firms and will become increasingly important to many others in the future. Information technology can be a significant factor in leveraging investments in maintenance and in directing a firm's overall approach to the maintenance issue. This paper presents a conceptual framework for understanding, along with a series of examples that illustrate, the role information technology can play in the maintenance process. The framework and examples are intended to serve as a catalyst for identifying applications of information technology to post-sale maintenance.

Keywords: Competitive uses of information technology, service, product maintenance

ACM Categories: H.4, J.1

## Introduction

Americans spent approximately \$50 billion on car repairs in 1986, and another \$19 billion to repair televisions, lawnmowers, air conditioners, and refrigerators (Tractenberg, 1986). The \$13 billion that U.S. companies spent on computer hardware maintenance in 1986 could expand to \$36 billion by 1992 (Taylor, 1987). The repair of housing, industrial machinery, farm equipment, and other vehicles also represents a sizeable part of our gross national product. Such statistics do not include the billions of dollars spent to replace small appliances, watches, toys, etc., that break and are just thrown away — often with a resolution “never to buy anything from XYZ again.”

On the other hand, some firms historically have relied on maintenance to feed new product sales. Sears Roebuck and Company recognized long ago that the person who repairs major appliances is also in a position to sell new ones. Sears has therefore made substantial investments in maintenance and after-sale service. Sears' maintenance contracts remain in force if the product is resold, thus providing the firm with a lead to a potential new customer. In addition, again to spur new sales opportunities, Sears' service representatives are qualified to repair air conditioners and furnaces produced by other manufacturers as well as those sold by Sears.

The opportunity to use maintenance as an element of company strategy is not unique to hard goods. Airline tickets get lost or need to be amended; encyclopedias, dictionaries, and maps become outdated; insurance policies should change as families form, children are born and grow older, and property is acquired. The need for after-sale maintenance is as common in the service sector as it is in manufacturing.

Lele (1986) notes that in a “number of industries . . .service support is emerging as the major area in which firms will soon be striving for competitive advantage.” Maintenance, which often seems to fall between the cracks in business education, is often also neglected by management. For instance, until recently American automakers consistently ignored the deteriorating service provided by their dealer organizations (Day, 1984), overlooking an opportunity to use these maintenance operations as feeders to new product sales. Similarly, mainframe computer vendors’ inattention to customers with high reliability needs paved the way for Stratus Computer's successful line of redundant systems targeted at that market niche.

Porter (1985) identifies “service” as one of five general primary activities that make up a firm’s value chain, which is a framework for understanding its costs and the existing and potential sources of differentiation. Yet maintenance, a part of service, has received scant attention from the academic community. No previous article in the information systems literature has addressed the potential for post-sale service improvement. Competitive repositioning, made possible by the application of information technology (IT) to the maintenance process, has also not been addressed. Textbooks are equally silent. Still, IT can play a large role in many maintenance operations. Sometimes, as we illustrate below, this role can be strategic. For many firms, the potential to leverage maintenance with IT is very high.

## Maintenance and Information Technology: A Strategic Opportunity

Competitive advantage may be especially hard to achieve and sustain where the advantage sought is based on product design. Lele (1986) notes, for example, that “rapid diffusion of technological change makes it increasingly difficult to maintain a competitive advantage based on product features or design.” After-sale support provides a more promising opportunity; Lele goes on to note that “both customers and manufacturers are [therefore] focusing on service as the key differentiator.” If Lele’s conjecture is correct, it may be fruitful to search for opportunities to use IT as part of the maintenance process. In fact, as the following examples illustrate, some firms already have achieved advantage by applying IT to the maintenance problem.

Ship Engines. One manufacturer of engines for large, ocean-going freighters has turned its customers' maintenance requirements into a competitive advantage. A damaged engine results in lost income for the ship owner due to additional time at sea and time spent in a maintenance facility; the cost of repair may be insignificant by comparison. A system developed by the engine's manufacturer permits the captain of a ship with a damaged engine to contact the manufacturer directly via a satellite link. Data about the damaged engine as well as the ship's position and destination is transmitted to the engine manufacturer's headquarters. The system identifies the nearest repair facility, dispatches a replacement engine or appropriate parts, and provides the ship's crew with directions for preparing the engine for subsequent repair.

Elevators. Otis Elevator Inc. traditionally handled requests for elevator repair by having customers call a local branch office; answering services took calls after hours and on weekends. Service was uneven, and corporate management was occasionally unaware of particular problems until the problems had reached crisis proportions. A new system now routes all service calls to a centralized corporate switchboard where operators dispatch service representatives from the local branch offices. Dispatch operators have access to a database containing maintenance records for all elevators. The system has dramatically improved effectiveness of the dispatch function, while providing headquarters management with the means to monitor and control the service side of their business.

Credit Cards. The American Express “Buyer’s Assurance” program doubles the period of the manufacturer’s warranty for most products purchased with an American Express Card. Buyers register their purchases by mailing in photocopies of the American Express charge record, store receipt, and manufacturer’s warranty. The program, which provided the basis for an extensive advertising campaign, increased both the number of cardholders and the use of the cards for large-ticket items. Additionally, information about ownership of each item provides American Express with valuable market data.

As these examples illustrate, IT can turn the maintenance “problem” into a strategic opportunity. The ship’s engine manufacturer differentiates its product by reducing the variable costs associated with engine failure. Otis uses IT to provide higher quality customer service and better management control over a critical element of the business. American Express turns the customer’s fear of “lemons” into “lemonade” through a system built around a database of product purchase information. Each of these applications represents a strategic use of IT, but, as examples below will illustrate, IT can also provide tactical support for the maintenance function. Following a discussion on maintenance approaches, a framework for recognizing and exploiting both strategic and tactical opportunities to use IT as an integral element of a firm's approach to maintenance is presented.

## Three maintenance approaches

Lele (1986) describes three approaches to meeting customer maintenance requirements. These approaches, detailed in Figure 1, include building more reliable products, providing support systems to respond more quickly to maintenance requests, and reducing customer risk through warranties or customer service agreements. IT can play a role in implementing any of these strategies.

## Product Design

The first opportunity to address maintenance concerns occurs during product design. Care can be taken to ensure that the design is fault free at the beginning. This is evident, for example, in the elaborate procedures taken to ensure that newly designed airframes will remain structurally sound during their lifetime. Alternatively, modular construction can ensure that the product is quickly repairable, as with an airplane's navigation gear and radio. Finally, component redundancy can ensure that failure causes no downtime, or only graceful degradation for critical systems, e.g., an airplane equipped with multiple engines and dual cockpit controls.

Information technology can play an important role in addressing the maintenance issue during the design stage. Bell Laboratories has developed a computer system that combines known failure rates for individual product components with knowledge of a potential product's design. The system estimates the likely time of the first breakdown and then predicts the average repair time (Port, 1985). The idea is to recognize at the outset the likely maintenance characteristics of a given design. The inclusion of electronic monitoring devices in automobiles, aircraft, railroad tank cars, and industrial equipment can provide the owner with better product performance and the manufacturer with valuable data for use in the design of future products.

## Reducing Risk

It is rarely economically feasible or even possible to design failure-free systems, so an alternative strategy focuses on lowering customer risk through warranties and service contracts that insulate the customer from the expense associated with product failure. Such warranties usually pertain only to the direct costs of product repair, but may extend to indirect costs as well. Volvo, for example, recently announced that it will pay the cost of road service and lodging incurred if a Volvo breaks down while the customer is away from home. Product warranties, common for automobiles, appliances, and computers, are becoming increasingly common for other products as well. Long-term service contracts, another way to reduce customer risk, also provide many firms with substantial revenues.

Warranties and service contracts are well-known examples of the use of information to augment a product. By enhancing the effectiveness of such agreements, IT can also help reduce customer risk. Sears uses an extensive information system to track product warranties. The system permits Sears to transfer warranties as products are resold by their original owners, to offer discounts to customers who purchase multiple appliances, and to send postcard reminders when it is time for the customer to renew the contract. Federal Express feels so confident about its automated package tracking system that it guarantees that a package can be located within 30 minutes or the delivery fee is returned (Federal Express Corporation, 1985).

The American Express “Buyer’s Assurance” plan, discussed previously, uses the buyer’s need for risk reduction as a means to collect some valuable marketing information. Similarly, a U.S. textile manufacturer guarantees the fabric from which its rugs are made, thereby providing a direct information connection to the final customer — a trail that is usually difficult to reconstruct for sales made through independent distributors.

## Service Support

For many firms the greatest untapped potential can come from using IT to provide support after product failure has occurred. Product failure usually triggers a complex, information-intensive process. For an expensive piece of equipment the process might include the following stages: the user, recognizing that something is wrong, discontinues operations, locates repair personnel, communicates the problem, and makes arrangements for maintenance. Repair personnel then conduct a diagnosis, hypothesize a cause and a cure, estimate the cost, contact the customer for approval, obtain necessary parts and tools, make the repair, test the result, calculate a bill, and notify the customer where and when the product will again be available for service. The repairer may conduct a follow-up analysis on the damaged components, the efficiency of the repair personnel, and/or the overall operating effectiveness of the product design. Once the repair is complete, the customer must reobtain the product, confirm the repair, and then resume operations.

Meanwhile, the owner of the broken product may be incurring additional expenses. Previously ordered raw materials may be stacking up, expensive labor may be standing idly by, and customers may be seeking out new sources of supply. The direct costs of product repair may, as in the ship's engine example, constitute an insignificant percentage of the total costs incurred.

The nature of product failures in the service sector is different from that of durable goods, but the maintenance process is equally information-intensive. For example, misplaced overnight packages, lost airline tickets, stolen travelers checks, or expired insurance policies can be difficult and expensive burdens to the customer.

And, as with durable goods, these problems can present promising opportunities to use information technologies to differentiate a product through enhanced customer service. The elevator manufacturer described previously significantly increased the service provided to the customer by building an enhanced service-support system. The next section describes another company's “continuous processing” computers, and how they combine the service-support and design-related approaches.

## The Case of Stratus Computers

Stratus Computers Corporation is a rich example of how a company uses information technology to support customer service. Stratus recognized in 1980 the increasing need of some computer owners to run applications in fault-tolerant operating environments. The start-up firm targeted this market niche with a strategy based on reliable design, redundant components, user serviceability, and a strong service-support system (Stratus Computer Corporation, 1986).

Designing for reliability is an encompassing objective for Stratus' engineers. Circuits are duplicated on each board and the boards, in turn, are duplicated. In the typical Stratus multiprocessor installation, each processor constantly monitors the other processors as well as its own redundant components. Reliable design and redundancy provide interruption-free operation and, when combined with a service-support system, ensure that failed components are quickly diagnosed and replaced. Here, again, a design-related strategy plays a critical role; most Stratus components are user replaceable, and approximately 60% of replacements are handled by customer personnel.

The service-support system that makes self-service possible starts with Stratus' database of customer configurations. Stratus' service-support system periodically polls customer computers to determine their current configuration and status. When an error is detected in a processor, the suspect component is taken offline and diagnostic routines are automatically triggered. If a persistent error is detected, automatic notification is sent to the Stratus service-support facility. Stratus support personnel, using remote diagnostic tools, complete the diagnosis and issue an order for a replacement part, usually within an hour. An overnight courier service delivers the part and replacement instructions to the customer, often before the customer is even aware of the problem.

This “telemaintenance” system permits Stratus to operate with a relatively small support staff and to leverage their best experts. Using the remote diagnostic tools, a disk specialist located in Marlboro, Massachusetts can diagnose a disk problem in, for example, London in the morning and work on another in Honolulu in the afternoon. Small investments in service-support technology replace large recurring expenses in labor and transportation.

## Searching for Applications

The examples of Stratus and others demonstrate that information technology can be used to enhance product maintenance, and thereby differentiate a product, or develop maintenance into an integral part of corporate strategy. But can these opportunities be unearthed via a formalized discovery process? The literature is generally optimistic but offers limited guidance on the means. McFarlan (1984) suggests five broad ways in which information systems might be employed to enhance a firm's competitive position vis-à-vis Michael Porter's five competitive forces (Porter, 1985).

Porter and Millar (1985) recommend five different steps that senior executives can follow “to take advantage of opportunities that the information revolution has created.” These steps include both internal analysis of products and processes, and external analysis of existing industry structure and the opportunity to spawn new business. Ives and Learmonth (1984) suggest using the Customer Resource Life Cycle (CRLC) to look at the product or service from the perspective of the customer. This entails focusing on ways in which information technology might be used to assist the customer in specifying, acquiring, owning, or disposing of the product or other resources used in conjunction with the product. Various other authors have provided prescriptive guidance for managers (Keen, 1986; Klein, 1986; Vitale, et al., 1986; Wiseman, 1985).

Ives, et al. (1986) describe the use of the traditional input-process-output (I-P-O) analysis framework (shown in Figure 2) for further refining the stages of the CRLC. They suggest that the I-P-O model can be used with any of the thirteen stages of the CRLC to provide a further level of precision to facilitate the search process. The I-P-O model can be enhanced by the addition of the “trigger” concept — looking at the specific events that trigger the stage of the life cycle under consideration.

In the following section the trigger-input-process output (T-I-P-O) model is used to analyze the maintenance process, both to illustrate its applicability to identifying applications and to provide a series of “real world” examples. The examples serve as a catalyst and as an aid to management seeking a vision of how information technology can be used within the maintenance process. The objective is to use the examples as analogies that may trigger related ideas.

## Application of the T-I-P-O Model

The process shown in Figure 3 is product or service “maintenance,” though terms like “repair” or “after-sales service” may also be employed. This process in turn consists of subprocesses such as “problem detection,” “diagnosis,” “solution generation,” “repair,” and “testing.” For the sake of simplicity we treat the maintenance activity as a single black box comprising all these subprocesses. Figure 3 provides a candidate list of triggers, inputs, and outputs that might apply to the maintenance of a physical good.

The process can be considered from numerous perspectives: the person who is going to fix the product; the person who discovers the problem; the person who dispatches the repair personnel; the management of the repair facility; and so on. (In an idea-generation session assigning roles to various participants is important to ensure that each perspective is adequately considered.)

The historical focus of service-support systems has been an internal one that considers the needs of the maintenance operation while neglecting the needs of the customer, other than for product repair. But competitive applications of IT frequently arise from careful delineation of the customer's role. Focusing attention on the customer opens up a new set of opportunities to differentiate service, and a fresh look at repairer concerns can lead to efficiencies or a better service record.

The following sections consider how information systems can be used to augment the triggers, inputs, or outputs of the maintenance process. Numerous examples illustrate these opportunities.

## Triggers

Maintenance operations can be broken into three categories: prevention, repair, and enhancement. Each is triggered by some event, and the events vary in severity. For product repair, in the worst case the product ceases operation or no longer serves the purpose for which it was intended. In a slightly less severe case the operator believes that the product is not functioning properly but is not sure. In a third scenario, the product informs the user that it is broken, as when an indicator light begins to flash. In the most desirable situation, the product informs the customer that it is broken and also provides repair information, or perhaps repairs itself. There are similar levels of refinement among the triggers for preventive maintenance. Most problematic for the customer is the need to remember that it is time to take the product in for a checkover or a cleaning — if forgotten, the warranty could become invalid. At the other extreme is a product that informs the customer of the specific steps necessary for preventive maintenance. A similar set of triggers can be formulated for product enhancements. In the worst case scenario, the customer must independently learn of the available upgrade and seek it out. At the other extreme, the enhancement occurs automatically. The three trigger categories of “repair,” “prevention,” and “enhancement” are shown at the top of Figure 4. Some ways that IT can enhance all three categories are described in the following sections.

## Repair Trigger

IT can be used to detect a product breakdown and request repair personnel. Otis Elevator, for instance, has begun equipping elevators with self-diagnostic control systems that automatically notify Otis when maintenance is required. The customer suffers fewer out-of-service elevators, while Otis retains contracts formerly lost to third-party service firms who are now locked out of the Otis network (HBS, 1986). A similar opportunity may eventually be offered to home appliance owners through a consortium of 30 companies that are attempting to establish standards for wiring the so-called “smart house.” These wiring standards will integrate audio, video and electrical power outlets, computing capacity, and phone lines. One manufacturer has begun work on a smart washing machine that would call a repair shop when it breaks down (Gray and Miller, 1986).

Breakdowns can also be detected by comparing the product's current condition with past performance or operating specifications. For instance, Palomar Technology International Inc. has developed a portable computer that can analyze certain types of industrial equipment. A maintenance engineer enters temperature and pressure readings from gauges associated with the equipment and vibration information gathered with a magnetic probe. This data is compared with historical data stored in the computer and the system then identifies required repairs (Port, 1986).

## Prevention Trigger

IT can trigger a preventive maintenance process in several ways, of which elapsed time is the simplest. Like dentists, automobile dealers use personal computers to notify customers when service visits are due. The reminders, coupled with the extensive maintenance information retained by the dealers, encourage customers to return (PC Week, 1984). Elapsed time is also the basis for a reminder system that General Computer Corporation is marketing to pharmacies. Med-Minder, a “computerized calling system,” uses information from the druggist’s patient file and automatically calls patients whose prescriptions are about to run out (Tyndall, 1987).

Actual product usage can serve as a more subtle trigger. An auto manufacturer builds a microcomputer system into each car's engine to encourage owners to have their cars serviced on time. Using data about operating conditions, such as the number of cold starts, the system calculates when to bring the car in for maintenance. The computer activates an indicator light that can be reset only by an authorized mechanic.

A light aircraft manufacturer has begun using IT to help change its customer relationships, partly by changing the maintenance process. The firm will sell airframes but will retain ownership of the engines, which will be leased to the customers. As a result, the customer pays a lower initial price and is freed from the cost and the government-mandated record-keeping associated with engine maintenance. In order to gather engine-use data, the company will install a microcomputer and a modem in each plane. At regular intervals the plane owner will taxi to a long telephone cord and connect the aircraft to the manufacturer's network. When usage conditions warrant, the owner will be instructed to bring the plane in for maintenance.

## Enhancement Triggers

The need for product enhancements or upgrades can often go undetected by customers unless they are contacted by the supplier. Suppliers are sometimes required to notify the customer of upgrades — automobile recalls are an example. General Motors' Computer Recall Identification System permits dealers to identify automobiles that have outstanding recall notices. A visit to the dealership for a normal check-up may trigger a free repair for a known defect (Chaudier, 1986).

Product enhancements may also provide an opportunity for the supplier. One of the authors, for instance, was recently surprised to learn that his auto insurance policy limits were at the same levels they had been when he first obtained insurance from the same firm some fifteen years earlier. The policy limits were dangerously low, presenting a product enhancement opportunity of value to the customer and to the carrier. Similar opportunities are presented as children reach driving age, policy holders near retirement, or the market value of a home rises.

If a product is information based, upgrades can often be triggered electronically. For instance, several computer vendors are now notifying their customers via electronic mail when new versions of software become available. In some instances, the software is automatically downloaded to customer computers. Maintaining the most recent version of software packages is a particular problem for personal computer owners. The wide variety of software suppliers and the number of customers who forget to return warranty cards presents a value-added opportunity for software distributors. At the simplest level, by keeping track of purchases, a software distributor could send postcard reminders to affected customers as new releases of software became available. At the other extreme, the system might notify a customer electronically and provide the customer with the ability to try out, order, pay for, and acquire the software through a communications link.

## Inputs

Product diagnosis and repair normally follow the triggering event. The inputs to diagnosis and repair will vary depending on the nature of the product and the symptoms, but will likely include information on repair personnel, the broken product, tools and instruments, spare parts, and diagnostic information. Some uses of information systems to enhance these inputs are described in the following sections.

## Personnel Inputs

Information systems can help to match a broken product with repair personnel, to facilitate manpower scheduling and allotment of such personnel, or do away with them altogether.

For some products, maintenance personnel remain in one place and work comes to them. In these cases, systems may be useful in allocating the work among a number of technicians. For instance, the General Motors Service Research Center, an experimental service facility, uses a computer system to assign technicians to repair a particular car depending on the type of work the car requires and load balancing considerations (Krebs, 1986). In other cases there may be a wide assortment of repair locations from which the customer must select. Travel clubs, rental car firms, and large fleet management firms use toll-free telephone numbers and computer databases to help on-the-road customers locate repair facilities.

If the equipment is large or immobile, or if the customer is willing to pay a premium, service personnel will travel to customer locations. Keeping track of the service staff can be demanding, but also an opportunity for substantial savings. At IBM, sophisticated service-support systems provide powerful tools for communicating with the field service organization. Field engineers carry small hand-held computers that communicate with branch offices via digitized radio transmission. Field engineers are dispatched to customer sites by means of the systems and, in turn, use them to report the status of service calls. Service dispatchers use a centralized service-support computer system to schedule the field engineer's time based on workload and customer location.

Perhaps the most dramatic improvement to the maintenance process comes when the repair personnel can operate remotely. Such applications permit a firm to leverage talented personnel or to locate maintenance facilities where labor costs are low. As previously noted, Stratus Computer's user-serviceable components, combined with their service-support system, permit product experts to accurately diagnose problems on the other side of the world (Stratus, 1986). Kodak has installed a similar capability, called The Telassistance Network, to support its large Ekataprint duplicators. Customer staff, assisted by Kodak service personnel connected via phone line to the broken machine, can repair a high percentage of machine malfunctions (Kodak, 1986).

## Product

The broken product is usually the most significant input to the maintenance process. Locating that product can sometimes be an issue. One computer vendor's configuration database provides complete location information for each piece of equipment in the field. For products that move, the process may be more complex. The ship's engine manufacturer, referred to previously, uses ship location and weather data provided via satellite communications links to determine the best port for carrying out necessary repairs. Sophisticated use of satellites for locating missing or broken down vehicles is likely in the future. Satellite transceivers will relay the vehicle's location to a ground station and provide two-way transmission of digitally coded messages between the driver and headquarters (Zygmont, 1986).

## Symptoms

Diagnostic information is often a helpful input to the repair process. This may be the operator's description of the problem — “It vibrates above 50 m.p.h.” — or data from sophisticated sensors. The computer command control center built into GM cars, for instance, is capable of capturing automobile operating data, including the driving habits of individual drivers (Bowman, 1985). Such data is of value to repair personnel and might be valuable to the customer as well — the owner of a vehicle frequently driven by a teenager might be interested to know when and how often the car was driven over the speed limit or was rapidly accelerated.

## Parts

Getting the right part to the right problem can be a challenging logistics problem, particularly if the product line is complex and the company is old. General Electric's appliance division, for instance, maintains a catalog of 50,000 items, including parts for appliances GE has not made in '25 years (Cunningham, 1985). Identifying the correct parts and obtaining them in a timely fashion and at a reasonable price are all potential objectives of a service-support system, and all offer opportunities to use information systems for competitive advantage.

Identifying the required part is the first step. Several third-party suppliers are helping parts buyers interface with potential parts suppliers. For example, Inventory Locator Service provides air carriers with the ability to locate parts needed for aircraft repair. The system identifies identical parts from different manufacturers and drafts “request for quotation” letters to potential suppliers (Computerworld, 1983). VideoLog Communications Inc. matches owners of broken electronic equipment with suppliers of spare parts. An electronic mail service permits engineers to communicate directly with any of 20 distributors of electronic equipment (Powers, 1986). Similarly, Coordinated Services' CoordNet, an online network, permits computer service firms to list parts for which they are oversupplied and to search for parts they require (Myers, 1986). A number of auto parts manufacturers have established similar systems for online parts ordering (Davis, 1985).

Parts, having been procured from suppliers, still need to get into the hands of repair personnel. The Xerox customer support system, which Benjamin, et al. (1984) claim “is strategically important to Xerox,” tells maintenance people which parts will most likely be needed when they make customer calls, thus reducing the number of return visits. The Xerox system sends the parts out to the customer site with repair personnel. Stratus uses overnight mail services to deliver self-installable products to customer locations. Parts delivery is still a problem when the work is done at a repair location. GM’s Service Research Center plans to provide car mechanics with touch-sensitive screens showing the automotive system on which the mechanic is currently working. By touching a part on the screen, the mechanic automatically transfers a part order to the parts department (Krebs, 1986).

## Tools

Tools represent another interesting opportunity for using IT competitively to help manage existing tools, to transform tools, or to build entirely new tools.

In some types of manufacturing, the high cost of tools represents a unique management challenge. The Todd Shipyard Corporation, for example, employs over 3,000 skilled workers who each withdraw dozens of tools every day from any of 22 satellite tool rooms. Lost or stolen tools were a frequent problem which was in turn exacerbated by tool hoarding among frustrated workers. A system that employs bar-coded tools and scannable worker identification badges reduced tool check-in/check-out time by ten minutes per person per day, saving 120,000 man-hours of production work annually. NASA's Kennedy Space Center uses a similar system to track more than 2,000 pieces of testing equipment (Aberth and Bohl, 1986).

Tools are products in their own right and can be transformed by technology in many ways. For example, a power screwdriver can be given intelligence to avoid overtightening screws or bolts. Manuals, another type of tool, are now being produced with electronic documentation production systems that ensure that product modifications are immediately reflected in repair documentation. If a component is replaced with another halfway through the product's life, maintenance personnel will immediately be apprised of the new component's characteristics as well as the specific products that contain the new and the old versions (Winslow, 1985). Such manuals are also becoming available online or are being integrated into simulations that visually lead personnel through a repair operation.

Finally, IT provides the opportunity to develop completely new diagnostic and repair tools. Robots and expert systems are both examples. Honeywell Inc. has developed Mentor, a portable computer-based expert system, to help field technicians diagnose air conditioners' problems. The expert system asks the field engineer for data on the outside temperature and humidity as well as symptoms of the unit. The program detects most maintenance problems before failure occurs. Honeywell hopes to use Mentor to "capture a leading share of the national market" for centralized air conditioning systems (The Air Conditioning, Heating and Refrigeration News, 1986). Ford Motor Company, recognizing that machine tool repair people may "forget their training before a machine breaks," are now requesting their machine tool suppliers to provide expert systems support tools along with the machine tools (Bulkely, 1986).

## Output

The usual outcomes from the maintenance process are product repair, preventive maintenance, and/or product enhancement. The most obvious maintenance output is a fixed, refurbished, or upgraded product. Other outputs include bills, satisfied or dissatisfied customers, and advice on how to avoid the problem in the future. Other information is required by the firm's accounting function. In addition, the maintenance process provides an opportunity to capture information of considerable value for product design, manufacturing, sales, purchasing, as well as for suppliers. In this section we will focus not on the specific output, but on the recipients of those outputs and the linkages emanating from the maintenance process. Porter (1985), noting the importance of such linkages, adds: “Given the difficulty of recognizing and managing linkages, the ability to do so often yields a sustainable source of competitive advantage” (p. 50). Some of the potential linkages are shown in Figure 5.

## Customer Linkages

Once the product has been repaired, the customer must be notified. For example, a Boston optician offers same-day repair service and provides customers with beepers; when the glasses have been repaired the customer, who is perhaps shopping or working nearby, is paged and returns to pick up the glasses. IT might also be used to help the customer obtain the repaired product. An automobile manufacturer provides dealers with automated phone systems that call a customer when a car is ready to be picked up or inform a customer about the current status of the car.

Other customer communications that present opportunities to use IT competitively include the customer bill, service level reports, and customer satisfaction surveys. The service-support system provides the basis for answering the question, "How are we doing?" At one computer company, for example, the service-support system accumulates statistics on the priority and status of all open maintenance requests. Customers may ask to be notified automatically when the status of a particular maintenance request changes. Another customer service system selects at random 15% of the daily service customers for participation in a phone survey. Customers who in the past reported dissatisfaction with service considerations are automatically added to the sample.

Service-support systems can provide the basis for demonstrating superior service to the customer. An air freight service provides large customers with monthly reports detailing their ontime delivery performance for package delivery. The firm thus demonstrates to the customer the reliability of its service. If quality service is a distinctive competence of the firm, such service-support systems can make it possible to write and monitor “service-level” contracts.

## Production and Design Linkages

The service-support system is a potential source of valuable data for engineering and production. Stratus Computer's service-support system, for instance, accumulates data on component reliability, which is accessible to product engineers and purchasing managers. Electronic mail provides customers with ready linkages to software developers for reporting bugs and suggesting improvements. A well-designed service tracking system can provide statistical data for analyzing the effectiveness of new materials, new production processes, employee training programs, or even alternative distribution outlets.

Firms providing maintenance activities for competitors often have an unusually advantageous opportunity. An airline based in a remote part of the world handles many maintenance activities for competitors' planes which land at the firms' home airport. From information such as the number of meals ordered the carrier gathers information on competitors' route volumes.

## Supplier Linkages

The service-support system often provides a means for purchasing agents to evaluate suppliers of the materials, components, parts, or tools that are used to build the product and for subsequent repair. Such linkage opportunities may extend to the suppliers themselves. The requirements associated with just-in-time inventory systems have opened up new information flows with suppliers during the product design phase (Bertrand, 1986). Some firms may find it advantageous to provide component reliability data directly to suppliers, perhaps accompanied by comparisons with other suppliers, past performance, or contractual provisions.

## Sales Linkages

A maintenance activity is often a sales opportunity. Sears repair personnel are equipped with portable microfiche readers. The readers assist service personnel in their repair work, but they also provide an opportunity to show customers new appliances. Sears' success aptly illustrates the potential benefits of linking the sales and maintenance operations. In fact, information technology provides an opportunity to bridge the maintenance-sales gap that is often missed. For example, various commentators have criticized American automobile manufacturers for failing to recognize and exploit the sales leads available through their dealers' maintenance operations.

The service-sales links may be subtle ones. A change of address or of marital status may signal an opportunity for an insurer to sell a broader line of coverage or to increase a customer's policy limits. These “pre-qualified” customers are often better leads than other prospects. Moreover, involving policy maintenance personnel in sales activities tends to make them more friendly and courteous with policy holders. The instant shoe repair facilities that have recently sprung up in trendy Los Angeles shopping centers could provide an interesting sales opportunity. As the shoeless customer awaits the repair, a video kiosk, such as those recently introduced by Florsheim (Forbes, 1986), might be available for perusing hundreds of new shoe styles readily available for home delivery.

International Trucks has taken the maintenance-sales link a step further by using information technology to help customers improve their own maintenance operations. International Trucks began gathering vehicle maintenance data from some of their large fleet customers in the 1970s. Initially, International used the data in their own product design process, but they soon recognized an opportunity to use the accumulated database to assist their customers to better manage their fleets. International's system, which runs on a portable computer, helps customers estimate the number of maintenance personnel, the investment in spare parts inventory, and the size of the maintenance facility necessary to maintain a particular truck or bus fleet. International Trucks has used the system to develop an ongoing relationship with large fleet owners who can now benefit from a more tightly run maintenance operation. Since the system is based on data that International Trucks has been gathering for a long period of time, it is difficult for competitors to duplicate (Navistar, 1986).

Again, firms who provide maintenance support for their competitors' products may have interesting opportunities. For example, a manufacturer of hearing aids uses its sophisticated service-support system to help identify potential sales opportunities. The product is sold through independent, professionally educated dealers who normally carry the lines of only one or two manufacturers. Customers return broken products to the dealer, who forwards them to a repair center (not necessarily that of the original manufacturer). The firm uses the service-support system to detect increased demand for repairs to other suppliers' products, an increase which often signals a breakdown in the quality of the original supplier's product or service. The firm then targets sales visits to affected dealers.

## Management and Maintenance Personnel Linkages

The utility of a service-support system as a management control tool is well known. Productivity statistics can be generated on a variety of categories, including individual employees, departments, and customer accounts. Such reports can serve as yardsticks and motivators. Exception reports can flag strong employees for special considerations and weak employees for counseling, retraining or reassignment. Providing individual workers with statistics that compare them to group averages can be a strong production incentive, particularly to workers who are underperforming.

A service-support system can also provide information useful to senior management. At Otis Elevator the implementation of a centralized service dispatching center provides Otis executives with direct control over a previously decentralized function. Service requests left open for more than a few days automatically receive the attention of an Otis vice president, and particularly difficult situations are brought to the attention of the president (Harvard Business School, 1986).

## Conclusion

The search for competitive applications of information technology will bear the most fruit in an environment characterized by information-intensive processes or products (Porter and Millar, 1986). For many organizations, maintenance, or after-sale service, is an information-intensive process that presents a rich opportunity. Moreover, as the Sears, International Truck, Otis, and Stratus examples illustrate, the maintenance operation can be effectively leveraged by investments in information technology. The search for either tactical or strategic opportunities may require an interdisciplinary team. Such a team might include individuals familiar with the existing maintenance operation and with customer need, distribution channels, information technology, product development, procurement, and sales. Analyzing the T-I-P-O model while maintaining a customer perspective will help the group delineate existing problems and new opportunities. Maintenance examples, like those described here, provide analogies that will generate innovative ways to apply information systems technology to the maintenance process.

If the firm's objective is to identify strategic applications of IT, whether for maintenance or other purposes, active involvement from functional and general management will be necessary. There must be a desire to use information technology for competitive advantage and a vision of how this will be done. In many organizations the desire is strong but the vision is weak. Delegating to the information systems organization the responsibility to “tell us what information systems can do for our business” will rarely achieve effective results. We have presented here a non-technical vision of the opportunities that information technology can bring to the maintenance process. Careful selection of examples from this and other works (Beath and Ives, 1986; Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Keen, 1986; Learmonth and Ives, 1987; Runge, 1985; Wiseman, 1985) when combined with consideration of the firm’s current strategy and distinctive competencies, can provide a starting point for shared discussions between general managers and information systems professionals concerning the potential role of information systems in their business.
