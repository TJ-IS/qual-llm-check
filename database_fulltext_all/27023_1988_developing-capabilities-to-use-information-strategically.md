## Abstract

Efforts to deploy information technology for competitive advantage in eleven industries are evaluated through a field study. Findings are based on analysis of interviews with senior executives of a major competitor in each industry.

Findings indicate that competitive advantage depends on the interaction between industry conditions and internal capability to identify and exploit opportunities. Internal capabilities and conditions and the processes that created these capabilities are defined and described. A brief review of the value added chain model from economics is included based on the authors' conclusion that this model is useful both for understanding the experience of companies in the study and as a guide in searching for opportunities.

Keywords: Competitive advantage, information value added, organization and personnel development, strategic systems, inter-organizational systems, line/IS integration, IS capability

ACM Categories: J.1, K.4.3, K.6.1, K.6.4

Can virtually any corporation with a strong information systems function use the capabilities represented within that function as leverage to achieve improved competitive position in their industry? A recent study of efforts at large companies suggests that the answer, at least for the present, is “no.” This study reveals that successful deployment depends on the presence of a set of external environmental factors and on developing a set of internal capabilities that extends beyond the IS function.

## Background and Purpose

Recent years have seen a rapid increase in recognition of information technology (IT) and the more integrated role it has begun to play in the strategy and tactics of a wide range of businesses. Articles on the subject of strategic use of IT have appeared in the business press (McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985; Business Week, 1985). Conferences sponsored by organizations such as the Society for Information Management have dealt extensively with use of IT as a competitive tool. Attendance at conferences, discussions with information systems managers, and observable growth in the number of new systems intended to produce advantage for their sponsors reveal that numerous companies are attempting to find and exploit IT-based opportunities. Some of these efforts result in systems that yield advantages for their sponsors and other users. Some efforts are abandoned prior to completion or fail to yield the expected advantages. The successes become the content of new articles extolling the power of IT to enhance competitive position. Efforts that do not produce success are not often publicized. Knowledge about them is exchanged privately in candid conversations between associates engaged in systems work.

The study reported here is intended to improve the success rate by providing guidance to line and IS managers in

— Assessing the extent to which their company's competitive environment might contain opportunities to gain advantages from more proactive use of information technology.

\- Evaluating their company's readiness to undertake a successful campaign to gain advantage from information technology.

— Leading the development of internal capabilities that are necessary to take advantage of existing or potential opportunities.

## Methodology

Attempts by large corporations to utilize IT for competitive advantage are the focus of the study. The participating companies represent eleven industries (see Table 1 for industries represented). Companies were invited to participate based on an evaluation of the strength of their IT efforts relative to other competitors in their industries. However, the evaluations relied heavily on publicly available information rather than inside knowledge of current IT initiatives and progress. Each company chosen is a major contender in its respective industry. The smallest company in the study has annual sales of approximately \$2 billion, and most are considerably larger. Some have achieved public acclaim for successful strategic uses of information technology. All participants have reputations for strong IS functions and indicated in initial discussions that they were either searching for opportunities or implementing systems. All were willing to discuss their efforts if confidentiality about specific initiatives was maintained.

The study was sponsored by AT&T and conducted by a team of five consultants experienced in strategy and information technology work. The intent was to gather information about what leading firms with strong IS cultures were doing to utilize IT competitively. The expectation was that patterns of factors associated with successful efforts would be found and that analysis of the interviews would reveal the logic of the associations. AT&T subsequently utilized the study's finding to improve the ability of its field organization to support customer IT initiatives.

Data gathering consisted primarily of interviews lasting one to four hours with company executives. Interviewee selection was based on the attempt to attain both coverage of important initiatives and balance between IS, strategy, and line perspectives. Table 2 provides a breakdown of the executives interviewed plus the project team's evaluation of the influence that each interviewee had over strategic deployment of IT within the company. Of the ninety interviewees, just less than half were IS managers. The remainder were managers of strategic planning or senior line managers — either general managers or marketing executives. The focus of each interview was on exploring past and current IT initiatives of a strategic nature by particularly probing for the executive's perception of the reasons for undertaking each initiative and the factors which either facilitated or impeded success of each individual initiative and the company's overall efforts to utilize IT for advantage.

Iterative content analysis of interview notes was conducted by members of the study team. These reviews identified patterns of attitudes, practices, business issues, and numerous other factors that appeared to differ between more successful and less successful efforts. Once a pattern was tentatively identified by a team member, it was treated as a hypothesis by other members who reviewed interview notes for data that either confirmed or contradicted it. This process began after interviews had been conducted at three companies and continued after completion of the interview phase of the study. Although the approach lacked elegance, a thorough analysis of the voluminous interview notes resulted and insights emerged that might have been overlooked had the study been premised too heavily on the conventional wisdom.

## Findings

The major findings of the study are:

— Industry environmental factors influence the direction and pace of strategic deployment of information technology.

— Companies vary substantially in the extent to which information technology has integrated with the primary strategies of the corporation or key business units.

## Competitive environment factors influence strategic IT development

Differences among industries in competitive use of IT are determined by several factors. These factors spring from the nature of an industry's primary products and services and the structure of the processes that are used to produce value. In addition, the competitive environment of the industry influences the pace and direction of strategic IT efforts. The three factors discussed below appear to account for most of the variation among industries found in our study. While these three factors are “environmental,” they are not immutable. All three factors may be altered by forces within the industry (i.e., a competitor's initiative) or without (i.e., deregulation or collapse of industry boundaries).

## Significant Information Content in Key Relationships

Businesses that have a high volume of transactions within and between organizations in their value added chain usually lead in the development of competitive uses of IT. (Please see the Appendix for a discussion of the value added chain (VAC) and its significance in the search for competitive advantage.) No reader of this article will be surprised to find that high transaction volume leads to IT usage. However, opportunities for gain are not always collocated with transactions between companies. For example, consider the back office systems provided to travel agents along with reservation systems. Also, consider the systems used for tracking drug interactions that are supplied to pharmacies by some distributors. In neither case was the system supplier involved in the transaction originally. Each case illustrates the use of initial systems links (order entry) as a means to understand customers' businesses and then to supply additional systems that

— provide value to the user,

— earn a return for the sponsor, and

— strengthen ongoing relationships between organizations in the VAC.

Among industries in the study, the food products company provides an instructive example of missed opportunities to utilize IT for advantage.

This company has an excellent reputation as a progressive “IT shop.” Several of its programs have appeared in the business press as examples of aggressive, forward-thinking programs to broaden information capabilities throughout the company. Management takes pride in being “a fast second.” They wait for competitors to initiate new practices and observe how each works.

The IS vice president stated, “If a new practice is promising, we throw enough resources into our effort to overwhelm their lead.” Later, he noted that major food chains had become more difficult to deal with due to better market information based on point-of-sale (POS) devices. The observation reveals that by focusing on direct competitors rather than the VAC, food processors had missed the opportunity to leverage their superior technical capabilities to utilize POS information. In a classic case of increased buyer power, the retail chains have seized the power afforded by adoption of POS technology.

Opportunities for competitive leverage also exist based on the value or significance of information to a member of the VAC, even though the volume of transactions may be small. The consumer packaged goods company found an opportunity to provide useful information on product usage under varying conditions of use to an important subset of its customers. The system they set up differentiates their product, raises switching costs, and also provides valuable feedback on product performance under varying conditions. The key to capturing this opportunity is the realization that occasional requests for information are indicative of the larger group of customers who need to know how to use the product under varying conditions but do not know how to ask.

## Products or Services Have a Limited Life

Companies with large inventories of products or services that have high market values prior to some moment or period of time and little or no value afterwards are candidates for IT applications that increase the probability of sale prior to the loss of value. Airlines are obvious examples. Some airlines have highly developed yield management programs to offer their seats at an array of prices that yield the most income for their flights. Reservation systems, the complex web of multiple fares and frequent flyer programs, are all aimed at optimizing the sale of seats. At successful airlines, these sales systems are complemented by even more sophisticated tactical planning systems. These systems ensure both proper seat availability and effective operational control so that seats once sold are not lost through flight delays or cancellations.

Hotel chains have similar problems with “room nights” that lose value if not occupied by a paying customer. Not surprisingly, hotels have also turned to IT based reservation systems and frequent traveler programs to achieve competitive advantage. As one hotel executive stated, “Our worst sin is to turn away requests in error” (decline a request for a room that eventually goes unoccupied). To prevent such errors, the leading firms have begun to develop sophisticated systems that track room availabilities on an accurate real-time basis and reliably predict no-shows. Efforts are also underway to define customer segments and improve the fit of service offered to the needs of key segments.

The need to manage inventories of valuable and perishable products creates an opportunity to utilize information at all stages of the VAC to minimize inventory loss. Similar opportunities exist in other industries. The spread of “just in time” inventory systems requires inventory throughout the VAC to be managed as though it were perishable, since material arriving late has less value.

## Increased Competitive Pressure within the Industry

A number of companies in the study turned to strategic use of IT as a means of responding to increased competitive pressure in their industry. The increased competition arises from such diverse sources as:

— Deregulation (airlines, financial institutions)

— Increased foreign competition (packaged goods, industrial products)

— Entry of strong competitors from another industry (financial services)

— Changes in technology, industry structure, or other critical factors — particularly if the changes shift potential to add value to a new VAC level (pharmaceutical distribution and petroleum/petrochemicals)

— Successful introduction of an IT initiative by a competitor

Competitive pressures increase rapidly when associated with an event such as deregulation or announced entry of a strong new competitor. Alternatively, they increase gradually in pharmaceutical distribution as margins erode due to the rise of drug chains and the ability of manufacturers to establish new linkages to bypass the distributor.

The industry factors act as catalysts that set off serious attempts to find ways to gain advantage from IT — particularly in the airline, financial service, and distribution companies in the study. Executives in the oil and office equipment companies do not perceive as much information content in key relationships, have products whose value is not as strictly time dependent, and do not perceive competitive pressures that dictate an all-out effort to build IT advantages. However, executives in industries whose external conditions are not driving IT innovation need to recognize that those conditions can change suddenly as they have in other industries. The sections that follow describe other factors that distinguish the successful efforts. The first factor, integration of IT and strategy, obviously correlates highly with successful strategic use of IT. Moreover, it is a direct outcome of management actions and practices that are explored in later sections.

## Integration of IT and strategy

The sample of companies in the study was intentionally biased in favor of participants who had either already achieved publicized advantages from IT or who were believed to have strong IS functions and to be actively engaged in the pursuit of advantages. Despite this selection bias, we found significant variation in the extent to which IT was integrated into strategy. These differences are reflected in the following typology (illustrated in Figure 1):

Type 1 or Traditional: IT supports operations but is not strategy related.

Type 2 or Evolving: IT supports strategy.

Type 3 or Integrated: IT is integral to strategy.

The Type 1 companies in the sample focus IT efforts primarily on improving administrative and managerial information systems such as accounting and decision support. They also focus heavily on linkages between functions and between corporate and operating group levels. Strong interest is expressed by IS professionals in finding opportunities to create advantage, but again these efforts are primarily focused internally and largely driven by IS.

Type 2 companies define and develop strategies at the corporate and business unit level without explicitly considering the competitive potential of IT. However, once strategies are defined, IS groups actively seek out opportunities to use IT to support the strategies. Rockart's Critical Success Factor (CSF) methodology was used by several Evolving companies as a model for defining information systems to support strategy (Bullen and Rockart, 1981).

Type 3 or Integrated companies display a more proactive orientation toward IT. In one such company we were told that the CEO persistently challenges both his line executive cadre and IS people to “find ways to change the rules in our business so that we can use our IS resources to win.” Integrated companies use IT to create new products and services, to alter linkages with suppliers and customers, and ultimately to establish new standards of performance in their industries. In changing the competitive patterns in their industries, they also alter the structure, processes, and scope of their own organizations in ways that facilitate continued leadership in competitive uses of IT.

Type 3 line and IS managers alike focus more attention on external relationships than do managers in other organizations. They use a forward-looking variant of the CSF methodology. The content of their interviews indicates concerns reflected in such questions as:

— What value is added by our distributors and suppliers and how do they go about it? How can we improve their ability to add value? As a strategy executive at the pharmaceutical distributor said, "Once we found how to make our customers more successful, they rewarded us with a lot more volume per customer.

— How could we capture and use information that exists in our organization or in our customers, distributors, or suppliers to improve either the coordination of VAC relationships or the internal operations of our company or another member of our VAC?

— How could we export work to suppliers, distributors or customers while building in switching costs?

## Interaction between opportunity and IT integration with strategy

The industry factors enumerated earlier serve to create opportunities or threats, driving companies to seek ways to use IT competitively. The extent of these opportunities or threats differed among the firms, as did their success in integrating IT with strategies. Figure 1 depicts the relationship between the presence of competitive opportunity or threat and integration of IT and strategy by the sample companies. Some companies had assessed opportunities and achieved a productive integration. The hotel, oil, and office equipment companies represent situations where inherent opportunities exceed the strategic IT readiness of the participating companies. These companies are vulnerable to a sudden increase in competition or attack by aggressive competitors who find ways to create value from information and develop systems and internal capabilities to exploit them. On the other hand, the supermarket chain and consumer products company are better prepared to respond effectively to such an attack or to launch competitive initiatives of their own.

As Figure 1 shows, all three Type 3 companies are in industries that involve significant information content in dealings with other VAC entities, limited life goods or services, and increased competitiveness. The tight integration between strategy and IT is cited as a key to successes achieved by each of these companies. There is also consistency among executives of all three companies in enumerating the internal capabilities which enable them to exploit IT opportunities. However, since the research was not longitudinal over the period in which developments took place, we cannot say with absolute certainty the extent to which internal conditions cause or are the result of the successes. Even so, the importance attached to these capabilities by participating executives makes an exploration of them worthwhile.

## Internal conditions supporting strategic utilization of IT

Executives at companies that had successfully introduced IT initiatives attribute past and present success to many factors. However, our analysis shows general agreement on the significance of five internal factors. Furthermore, success among the total sample closely parallels what we were told about the presence of absence of these five factors in the individual companies. The five internal factors or conditions are:

Leadership — is both the vision to see the strategic opportunities for IT and the personal force and persistence to overcome barriers to effective implementation. One IS vice president observed ruefully, “Our CEO understands the (IT) opportunity but he doesn’t keep the pressure on our operating units to work at it with us. That’s just not his style.” The experience records of the firms in this study — both the successful and less successful — consistently demonstrated that senior line management involvement in both the discovery and development stages was critical to the results achieved. Interestingly, there were no examples among these companies of major IT initiatives being successfully led by the head of the IS function. Also worthy of note is that since completion of data gathering, this finding has been discussed with several small groups of IS executives. None of these executives liked this finding. But so far none have provided an example of a successful initiative without solid and active support (not just assent) from senior line management. On the contrary, most had examples of promising initiatives that died from lack of line involvement.

Integration of IT and strategy functions in the corporation— this functional level integration is most effectively achieved by transfers of senior people between the two functions, by joint task forces evaluating strategic potentials of IT, and by the actions of executives with the specific charge to expand the capability to mount a strategic effort.

Direct contact between IS function and line divisions — is more highly developed when those responsible for IT report directly to line management rather than to another staff function (i.e., finance). Line/staff contact at multiple levels favors the generation of useful ideas and the channels for ideas to filter upward in the organization.

Capability in IS function — is greatly enhanced when some IS people have strong business acumen based on quality experience in the key line functions or strategic business units of the company. Moreover, we found that Type 3 companies had strong administrative and decision support systems in place before shifting resources to strategic applications. They had compensation and reward systems that induced IS managers to take risks — particularly risks intended to produce extraordinary business results. The IS vice president at a Type 3 company observed “Our whole department knows that they are valued for the contribution we make to this company’s success and they also know that some individuals in IS make a bundle — and they’re worth it.” On the other hand, one of the Type 1 companies with strong technical capabilities had not yet completed implementation of needed administrative and decision support systems, and therefore was restricted by these unmet needs from allocating resources to strategic issues.

Mechanisms for line influence on IT — in addition to reporting relationships, companies deriving significant benefits from strategic use of IT had mechanisms in place that permitted key line managers to exercise controls over budgeting, priority setting, and resource planning for the IS function. These devices, used properly, ensure that IT efforts are relevant to achieving significant business results. Perhaps the most important outcome of the line control, however, was the commitment and support of the key managers for IT efforts.

The impact of the CEO in creating a conducive environment and in beginning the process of defining and implementing initiatives is evident. In each Type 3 company, interviewees described how the CEO had persistently urged other senior executives to find and pursue IT initiatives and had provided specific support at times when efforts might have died without their support. There were also stories about how their general management perspective had helped to avoid tendencies toward “technical myopia” (Wyman, 1985) present in their companies.

As mentioned earlier, our methodology imposed the need for care in accepting the precision of retrospective accounts of past successes. Attribution of cause/effect relationships based on such accounts is particularly risky. For this reason and because the research was intended to provide information on current efforts, most of the inquiry focused on current programs and the factors which supported or detracted from these efforts. The same internal factors that were key to past successes were cited as instrumental in progress on current efforts. This concurrence led us to conclude that the conditions described above were indeed related to both past and present success.

In the course of discussing present programs that help gain advantage from IT, we also gained further understanding of the knowledge base and process skills involved in defining and executing these efforts. The next section will present these further insights.

## Leading the Organization Toward Competitive Advantage from IT

Finding and exploiting opportunities to gain advantage from IT has become more complex. The primary reason for the greater complexity is not technical, but arises from trying to meet the basic objective stated by the CEO quoted earlier: "Find ways to change the rules of the game . . . so that we can use our IS resources to win." Changing the rules requires more than a clever manipulation of information. It also requires securing the agreement of other people — both in the sponsoring company and in other VAC organizations — to cooperate with the changes. For people to agree, they must both understand conceptually how they can "win" and be in command of the knowledge and skills that the new rules require.

The experiences of all the companies in the study contributed to understanding what works and what does not work in achieving advantage. In addition, we have had the opportunity to test our initial conclusions from the study sample in discussions and collaborations with executives of other large corporations engaged in the effort to create advantage for their companies. Analysis of this experience leads to formulation of three sets of related actions that seem to be necessary to the successful exploitation of IT based initiatives. Efforts that have not been as successful as intended can often trace their shortcomings to the lack of one or more of these three requirements, and those that have succeeded have generally met the requirements (although not always in “textbook fashion”).

Based on detailed knowledge of the experiences of eighteen corporations, we conclude that success requires coordinated action in three areas:

— building a knowledge base

— conducting the search for opportunities

— developing and managing the initiatives as a change process

## Building a knowledge base

The process of building a knowledge base should begin prior to opportunity search. It should initially include senior managers with strategic decision roles and other managers whose business units or functions will likely be involved in the search for opportunities or in later implementation steps. It should also involve managers and other professionals who are likely to directly participate in efforts to identify opportunities. The experience of companies in the study and the efforts of other companies to deploy IT competitively consistently indicate need for broad line involvement at an earlier stage than indicated in other planning processes (Rackoff, et al., 1985). The objectives of the sessions should include:

— familiarizing participants with the experience of other companies (both the successes and shortfalls and the reasons for the outcomes)

— introducing and applying the VAC model as a tool for considering where opportunities might exist and how initiatives might impact business results at each VAC level

— developing agreement about what new knowledge (concerning both IT issues and how the VAC operates) will be needed, where initial search efforts should focus, and how search activity will be staffed and led

— establishing a plan for expanding the knowledge base to update the initial group and including new people in the process as needed

At some companies, search efforts have been initiated (usually driven by IS people) after only superficial efforts to build a knowledge base in those areas of the company that would potentially be affected by changes have been tried. A common result has been line resistance to search steps and/or hesitation to support investment of resources for implementation. It is easy to understand the hesitation where the accountable manager is being asked to support initiatives that could produce impacts that he or she is unable to anticipate or evaluate.

There were some notable exceptions where initiatives succeeded despite a narrow knowledge base. In these cases, implementation proceeded slowly — small steps were taken (usually at one locale), allowing sufficient time for each step to be consolidated and evaluated prior to proceeding. Obviously, if rapid implementation is necessary as a barrier to emulation or leapfrogging by a competitor, the slow implementation approach carries some risks. Therefore, attention to building the knowledge base is important both to implementation success and to the overall competitive impact of the initiative.

Previous sections of this article related findings about search opportunities. A number of articles cited earlier described specific examples of advantages achieved. The next section attempts to blend these examples with the findings from the study into useful perspectives for conducting the search.

## Conducting the search

The overall perspective employed by the successful initiatives was a search for performance improvement. Most organizations have analysts experienced and skilled at finding these opportunities and their skills are valuable in the search for competitive advantage. However, there are significant differences between intracompany efforts and efforts to improve VAC performance.

First, the search spans organizations that are accustomed to protecting privacy of information from other companies in their VAC. Indeed, their profitability often depends on using information that others lack to execute transactions before others recognize the opportunity. For example, channel intermediaries depend on knowledge of current market conditions as a major profit contributor and are reluctant to disclose either information or sources. Their reluctance is often made even more intractable because their profit measures do not distinguish between market knowledge and other profit sources (e.g., transaction processing, material handling, and location advantages). In these situations, sample companies often found it necessary to employ a third party to explore possibilities while assuring confidentiality of proprietary information.

Second, success of most strategic systems depends on voluntary and, if possible, universal use of the system. An order entry system used by a small portion of a market or for only portions of a customer's orders generates less information value than universally used systems. In many cases, value rises exponentially with usage volume. Therefore, the search must find ways to create value for all participants and ideally the value attained by each should be proportional to use. Order entry systems offering price discounts proportional to total purchase volume exhibit this property. Several examples were found of costly systems that were developed and delivered only to die of neglect because analysts failed to recognize that participation was based on incentive — not persuasion.

Since most strategic systems involve at least two levels of the VAC, search for opportunities should be based on analysis of the entire VAC. Opportunities for competitive leverage will often be concentrated in the relationships between two or three levels. However, there are lessons to be learned from the most successful competitive systems. The system sponsors in this study (i.e., McKesson, AHS, American Airlines) all began with a link between two VAC levels. In each case, the sponsor built on the foundation of the initial success by using three types of extensions:

— becoming sufficiently knowledgeable about the other level to find ways to create additional value from information that would impact the relationships between that level and yet another level (e.g., a pharmacy and its customers)

— using information from the transaction processing system to create value for entities not directly involved in the transaction (e.g., selling POS information gained from order entry to product managers at packaged goods producers or providing information derived from reservation systems data to airframe and engine manufacturers)

— developing additional applications to improve performance of organizations at the level of the original relationship (e.g., back office applications)

## Developing and managing initiatives

Corporations searching for opportunities should regard first initiatives as simply a step in leading performance improvement throughout their VAC. Their aim should be to develop a sequence of innovations that continues to build value from information. The experience of McKesson and others demonstrates the power of using first mover advantages to build a leadership position based on the ability to make their entire VAC (or a key segment) more productive.

Companies whose competitors have introduced successful strategic systems cannot expect to overcome first mover advantages by providing similar systems with more user-friendly screens or other system “bells and whistles.” Instead, they must leapfrog by introducing a system that at least matches their competitor’s features and, in addition, exceeds it in one of the three directions mentioned above. The second offering can succeed only by providing greater incentive to users than the first offering. That will generally require either a direct subsidy or a system that creates greater total value than the first mover’s system.

These last observations about the differences in the challenge facing first and second movers lead to a final set of observations about the experience of the sample companies. In the Type 3 firms, initiatives to improve VAC performance, underway at the time of the study, were clearly owned by line organizations and were carried out by teams from IS and other functions. Ownership in Type 1 and 2 companies appeared to belong to IS. The ownership difference reflected subtle differences in how management regarded efforts to gain advantage from IT. Type 3 executives were in effect saying, “This is how we run our business.” Type 2 attitudes seemed to be, “This is how IS helps us run our business.” Type 1 expressed perceptions such as, “This is our job,” and “That is IS’s job.”

The migration of IT into an integrated role in the conduct of business by Type 3 companies appeared to arise from the processes they employed to identify and exploit opportunities. The key insight of these companies was that achieving competitive advantage from IT meant basic changes in business practices and culture. Therefore, they developed and managed initiatives as a change process, recognizing that technical system changes were only part of the overall change required for success. Executives encouraged wide sharing of new knowledge about

— the system and its potential effects, and

— the operations and information needs of other participating companies.

System designers and programmers were directed to spend time observing and discussing operations that would be affected by system use—both within their own company and in other VAC organizations. Care in planning and executing system rollout produced early successes that were used to build momentum. The processes of building skills and knowledge, systems development, implementation planning, and actual implementation were coordinated by cross functional assignments. Senior management recognized the change process as an opportunity to invest in building capabilities that would continue to produce returns in the future. The continuing ability of the integrated companies to retain and build on early advantages validates management's insight and persistence.

## Summary

Success in utilizing IT to change the rules of the strategic game varied widely among companies in the study. Executives in the successful companies attributed their success to capabilities developed in the process of identifying and realizing opportunities to improve performance in their value added chain. Both line and IS executives believed that line leadership, integration of line and IS objectives, and multi-functional definition and execution of tasks were key to past success. While the methodology makes it impossible to validate a cause and effect relationship between capabilities and results, descriptions of current efforts demonstrate that the capabilities are important to ongoing programs.

It may be surprising to some that there was virtually no indication from either line or IS managers that lack of technical capability or constraints on IS budgets had limited progress on initiatives. Managers interviewed in Type 1 companies generally expressed high regard for the technical competence within IS. There were, however, widely expressed concerns in both Type 1 and Type 2 companies over the extent to which IS employees “understand the business.” The material on developing internal capabilities outlines how the Type 3 companies successfully overcame those concerns.

The experience of these companies suggests that it is important to recognize that establishing electronic linkages between VAC organizations creates potential for ongoing improvement in VAC performance. Leaders who blend organizational learning and action in early implementation efforts establish an innovation process that continues to generate performance improvements and thus, continues to maintain their competitive edge.

# Appendix The Value Added Chain and Its Significance in Deploying “IT” Strategically

The concept of the value added chain (VAC) is used widely in economics. Michael Porter uses a modified version, which he labels the “value chain,” in his work on competitive advantage (Porter, 1985). Clemons and McFarlan demonstrate the utility of this model for opportunity search (Clemons and McFarlan, 1986.). As analysis on this study proceeded, we found the traditional economic concept a useful complementary model for identifying IT opportunities because it provides a structured overview of the entire process for creating value — from raw material to final consumption. That model is illustrated by the diagram below of a simplified VAC for a food product.

FARMER--> BROKER--> PROCESSOR--> 
DISTRIBUTOR--> RETAILER--> 
CONSUMER-->

The background section cites four articles describing various ways companies have used IT to alter competitive balance in favor of the sponsor of the IT initiative. Virtually all of these systems and the systems in the study that were competitively successful produced improved performance of the VAC. Most of the performance improvement resulted from

— lowering transaction costs and/or improving the coordination between players at two or more levels in the chain or

— using information resources that exist at one level of the chain to improve performance at another.

Order entry systems lower transaction costs and improve coordination between levels. Some retail chains and distributors sell information collected from POS devices to consumer packaged goods manufacturers, who use it to fine tune marketing and manufacturing programs.

The sponsor of an information initiative that improves the effectiveness or efficiency of its VAC has the same choices that other innovators typically have (increased margin per unit or increased volume). The sponsor can charge a price for use of the IT innovation that approximates its value to the user, or it can pass on a portion of the increased value added to other participants.

Choice of the later alternative rests on the expectation that the shared benefits of the innovation will either

— provide the entire VAC with competitive advantages over VACs without the innovation and therefore allow the sponsor's VAC to gain market share or

— provide part of the VAC with an incentive to increase the volume of business that it does with the sponsor (e.g., create a payoff for a distributor to push the sponsor's products harder)

Using the power of information to improve the functioning of the VAC enables the sponsor to achieve many of the advantages of vertical integration without the primary disadvantages. Hayes and Wheelwright's (1984) concise review of these advantages and disadvantages can serve as a useful stimulus to ideas about how to leverage information power in a particular VAC.

Expanding capability to network distributed computers and databases is leading to recognition of the VAC as a powerful model for identifying opportunities for productivity improvement. Companies are using IT to link backward to suppliers (in some cases all the way to basic raw material producers) and forward through distributors or retailers to end users. Driving information from one point in the chain to all affected players makes a VAC more responsive to changes at any level (e.g., changes in market demand or availability of key supplies).

Emerging IT options support VAC integration. Remote order entry systems increase coordination and interdependence between buyer and seller. Programmable toll-free 800 service provides ability to respond to different users with varied offerings while collecting direct customer information. POS scanners lower transaction costs and provide data that has value throughout the VAC. While some options are being created by new technology, many valuable innovations to date have been implemented initially with technologies that were fairly pedestrian at the time. Competitive advantage has resulted more from innovative thinking about the business than from tomorrow's technology.
