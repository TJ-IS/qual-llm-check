# Matching Computer-Based Information Systems with Organizational Structures

By: Richard Leifer
School of Management
Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute
Troy, New York 12180

## Abstract

Results of studies of the organizational impact of computer-based information systems (CBIS) are contradictory and uncertain. One reason is that what is meant by a CBIS differs from one study to another. In an effort to clarify linkages among CBIS designs and organizational contexts of use, a number of CBIS organization matches or “fits” are suggested based upon a four-part categorization of CBIS, consisting of centralized systems, distributed systems, decentralized systems, and stand-alone systems. Appropriate CBIS are then determined for different organizational configurations. The impact of CBIS on organization structure will depend on whether a CBIS structure fit exists. This approach should provide guidance for researchers in discussing CBIS-organization linkages as well as provide practitioners an additional criteria for ensuring CBIS success.

Keywords: Network architecture and design, organization structure and design, systems design, fit

ACM Categories: C.2.1, H.2.4, K.4.3, K.6.1, K.6.4

## Introduction

Proposed reasons for successes of computer-based information systems (CBIS) range from adequate design to the processes and functioning of the systems project team (White and Leifer, 1986). The match or “fit” of CBIS design within the organizational design is one underappreciated aspect of the equation determining CBIS success. It is the contention here that successful implementation of CBIS architectures requires that system users match the style and process by which jobs in the organization are performed. Markus and Robey (1983) refer to this as organizational validity, which is the fit between an information system and its organizational context of use. Thus, successful systems tend to fit organizational patterns designed for those tasks (Robey, 1977, 1981). The purpose of this paper is to suggest characteristics of the fit among alternatives of CBIS architectures and organizational designs for CBIS success.

## Rationale

The evidence concerning the relationship between CBIS and organizational structure is fragmentary and mixed (Attewell and Rule, 1984). This is due primarily to an inadequacy in the way computerized systems are operationally defined as variables in CBIS-structure linkages. Attewell and Rule's (1984) review of the literature suggests that a number of variables are important when studying computerized information systems in organizations. These include patterns of information usage and information flow, variability of work, industry type, etc. (p. 1189). However, nowhere do they suggest that a critical variable, computerized systems, is itself in need of identification and further study.

As Kroeber and Watson (1984) point out, the term computer-based information system (CBIS) is generic. There are classes of systems known collectively as CBIS, just as highway systems, railway systems and airline systems are different types of transportation systems (p. 4). The multitude of studies linking computerization with organizational structure have conceptualized computerization as the generic information system or as some combination of transaction systems, office systems, management information systems, electronic mail systems, etc. These systems might consist of mainframes, microcomputers, or work stations, and might have a standalone or networked configuration. Due to this lack of agreement about what the terms computerization or information technologies mean, there is little consensus on computerization-structure studies. Adding to this confusion is that computer-based information technologies are changing rapidly. Not only are price-performance relationships changing, but capabilities are also changing. For example, electronic mail, telecommunications and other communications systems which were not widely available five years ago are commonplace today.

Due to the lack of agreement about a typology of CBIS, or in many cases a lack of precision about what authors mean by “computerization,” the results of linkage studies provide little help for those who have a theoretical interest in such linkages and for practitioners who must make choices about how best to integrate information systems into their organizations. Although recent work by Markus and Robey (1985), Huber and McDaniel (1986), Leifer and McDonough (1985), Specht and Robey (1985), and Carter (1984) have attacked the computerization-structure relationship, there is still much confusion because the variables in question have not been clearly defined. The objective of this paper is to present a taxonomy of CBIS and of organizational structures, and to suggest “natural” matches or fits between the two variables. The taxonomies presented in this paper should: (1) provide a framework by which researchers can make comparisons across research studies, and (2) assist practitioners in their efforts to enhance the acceptability of CBIS in their organizations.

In the following section, a way of categorizing CBIS is proposed. Then a typology of organizations is presented. Finally, a mapping of CBIS onto organizational structures is suggested.

## Categorizing Computer-Based Information Systems

Since this discussion pertains to linking CBIS and organization structure and design, CBIS are categorized by their architecture or topology (Burch, 1985), which is a set of interconnections of nodes in a network. Categorizing CBIS architecturally is appealing since it is not idiosyncratic to particular settings, and, further, CBIS architectures are fairly well-established and accepted. As a taxonomy, the four CBIS architectures are: centralized systems (which includes batch systems), distributed systems, decentralized systems, and stand-alone PC-based systems. Each is discussed below:

Centralized Systems: These systems are designed around a central processor or mainframe with “dumb” terminals, which allow interactive, information processing activities, mostly of a transactional nature. Centralized systems also include centralized mainframe installations, which are largely used for batch processing or updating files. These systems are the basis of the term EDP, and still account for a large proportion of the systems used and sold today.

Distributed Systems: These systems, referred to as peer-to-host systems (Durr, 1987), are designed as “spokes” or terminals around a central processor or mainframe. Spokes might have their own processor, storage device and terminals that have their own computing capabilities and data bases. Terminal users can communicate with others but only through the central hub.

In contrast to the centralized processing of online systems, there is program/data independence. However, there is a similarity between centralized processing and distributed processing in that both imply a form of central management, control and authority, requiring communication with some other function or process and rules governing how that communication will take place (Nack, 1982). In a distributed processing environment, some processing of data occurs in multiple machines and results are exchanged, thus requiring rules. Ignoring or not realizing this dependence upon central management is what leads many corporations into difficulty.

Decentralized Systems: Referred to as “peer networks” (Durr, 1987), there is no central processor through which communications must pass, and hence there are more degrees of freedom in communication. Although terminals or other systems communicate through bridges or gateways and require rules for connectivity, these constraints are substantially less than for distributed systems.

Electronic mail, local area networks, telecommunications systems, group decision making systems, etc. allow messages to be sent through the network in an interactive mode. This results in an increase in the quality, quantity, reliability and capability of the system to process information (Leifer and Triscari, 1987). The ability to selectively communicate with particular individuals, groups, skill areas, or those with needed information, allows the flexibility to build ad hoc, non-face-to-face groups or work teams. This flexibility gives decentralized systems the capability to cope with a wide variety of informational demands.

Stand-Alone Systems: Composed of standalone PCs used in individual departments or as the information systems in small organizations, stand-alone systems may function as the “mainframe” for those organizations. Since PCs are a relatively low-cost tool, most larger organizations do not plan for them (LaPlante, 1987). Further, their effect is on the work of individuals rather than on the organization as a whole (Lee, 1986).

## A Typology of Organization Structures

There has been a range of organizational structure typologies suggested in the past few years (Daft, 1986). The one adopted here, suggested by Mintzberg (1979), has been used in a number of research studies. Mintzberg proposed five organizational forms, described below. Although not discussed by Mintzberg, a variation of the divisionalized form has been added to the typology as it reflects more recent trends in divisionalized companies.

## Simple structures

Simple structures are characteristic of both young, start-up, entrepreneurial organizations and well-entrenched autocracies. They are generally small, operating in a market niche within a dynamic environment with few rules, but are centralized in the sense that the founder/entrepreneur/CEO makes most or all of the important decisions. Competitive advantage lies with either a unique product service or fast response to environmental demands. Examples of simple structures include an automobile dealership with a flamboyant owner, a new government department, a middle-sized retail store, a corporation run by an aggressive entrepreneur, or a school system in a state of crisis.

## Machine bureaucracies

Machine bureaucracies are characterized by standardization, functional structural design, and large size. These structures are generally differentiated both horizontally and vertically, and are naturally associated with mass production technology where the products, process and distribution are rationalized around repetition and standardization. These rationalization efficiencies find expression in the standardization of functions and processes, utilizing rules and policies to underline the obsession for control. Even though current notions of organizations find bureaucracy out of favor (Naisbett and Aburdene, 1985), continuing requirements for mass produced goods and services make this type of organization the most prevalent of the configurations. Examples of this type of organization include the Post Office, a large automobile company, an airline, a security agency or a steel company.

## Professional bureaucracies

Professional bureaucracies rely on standardization of skills as the basis for coordination, whereas the machine bureaucracies rely on standardization of work functions. While the products/services of machine bureaucracies have a high physical component, the products/services of professional bureaucracies have a high informational component. Since these products/services depend for their effectiveness on skilled people who must be given considerable control over their own work, the organization must be decentralized down to those professionals responsible for carrying out the organization's tasks. As a result of their training, professionals act rather independently, but are consistently in accordance with the goals of the organization, requiring few formal rules and policies. Independence of action is productive if no concerted action is required by organization members to act as a whole. Examples of professional bureaucracies include law and accounting firms, and academic institutions. At a recent academic meeting, an academic administrator stated that managing an academic department was akin to herding cats.

## Divisionalized forms

The divisionalized form in contrast to the professional bureaucracy, is an integrated set of semiautonomous entities loosely joined by an administrative framework and is characteristic of older, more mature, very large organizations. The semiautonomous entities, often referred to as strategic business units (SBUs), determine the strategic portfolio of the organization. Divisionalized structures might operate in two ways, which will be referred to as Form A and Form B.

Form A, as discussed by Mintzberg (1981), is a market-based set of divisions — decentralized from the perspective of the total organization, but centralized within the division. In this form, divisionalization might be composed of multiple machine or professional bureaucracies. In short, this organization might be seen as a set of small bureaucracies held together loosely by a formalized set of budgets or goals as performance control systems. Examples are usually drawn from the private sector of the industrialized economy — the vast majority of the Fortune 500.

Form B, not discussed by Mintzberg, can also be viewed as a set of semiautonomous divisions. However, rather than being loosely tied together by performance control systems, they are bound together by a strong culture. While the semi-autonomous units develop in response to product or geographical diversity, they are tightly coupled to each other through strong values, beliefs, shared perceptions and meanings (Deal and Kennedy, 1982; Peters and Waterman, 1982). Hewlett-Packard might be such an organization; the divisions are bound by the Hewlett-Packard "Way" (Packard, 1981).

## Adhocracy form

The adhocracy form might be construed as a more adaptive form than the divisionalized Form B. Being small and having the characteristics of a young organization (but not necessarily young in years), the adhocracy operates effectively as a cohesive group working together. Like the professional bureaucracy, adhocracy relies on trained and specialized experts, but, unlike the professional bureaucracy, these experts must work together to accomplish the organization's goals. For this reason mutual coordination and cooperation are critical, and cause these organizations to behave like project teams. The structure is organic with little formalization of behavior. Examples might be a factory making complex prototypes, a space agency, or a job shop design agency.

## Levels and Units of Analysis

Organizations are heterogeneous and to some extent are mixtures of the ideal forms presented here. Especially with machine bureaucracies and divisionalized forms there may be mixtures with other organizational forms such as simple structures and adhocracies. For example, in Soul of the New Machine, Kidder (1981) describes an adhocracy that resides within what is essentially the professional bureaucracy of Data General. Similarly, we should recognize that several CBIS, as a portfolio of systems, can and often do coexist within an organization (Swanson and Beath, 1986). There may be word processing systems using PCs that coexist separately, but in parallel with distributed or networked systems.

This suggests a problem involving levels or units of analysis. At what level or unit of analysis should we study the CBIS and organization interaction? That is, would it be helpful to investigate the CBIS of a divisionalized form organization if the divisions operate independently and are only linked with the administrative apex by reporting requirements? The lack of communications among the units in a divisionalized organization suggests that the level of analysis for studying the interaction of CBIS and organizational structure would be at the division level, rather than the organization as a whole.

The unit or level of analysis problem is not well-studied (Pfeffer, 1982). However, from the structural point of view taken here, characteristics of organizations can be viewed as properties of networks among people or subunits. The interaction, which links organizational members with emerging social structures (Mayhew, 1980) suggests that the unit of analysis is defined by mechanisms that influence actual or potential communications activities among organizational members.

At the same time, interactions at sublevels in an organization are influenced by the context within which the subunit is embedded. For example, a research unit in the military might function somewhat as an adhocracy with a CBIS designed to fulfill the mission of that unit. However, by virtue of being in a large divisionalized bureaucracy one might expect a high degree of formalization (rules and policies).

The unit of analysis suggested in this paper, then, is the largest organizational unit wherein task-related communications regularly take place, with the understanding that the larger, superorganizational context will somewhat constrain the activities and functions of the CBIS-structure relationships within the unit.

## Organizational Design, CBIS Design and Information Processing: Basic Conformations

The primary point of this paper is that an effective CBIS is one that fits the organization's culture and style. Appropriately, the mapping of CBIS will achieve balance and harmony for the organization if the characteristics of the organization's culture and style are complementary (Burch, 1985).

In this section, natural matches or fits between organization and CBIS designs are discussed.

Table 1 summarizes natural matches between CBIS and organizational designs in the sense that the actions and behaviors required by the CBIS will be complementary to the actions and behaviors of the organization and to its culture (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984). Thus, a match exists if the CBIS automates or supports tasks that are currently being performed, either manually or with outdated technology, and results in the tasks being done essentially the same after computerization.

## Simple structures and stand-alone systems

Given that in such organizations the founder/entrepreneur makes the important decisions or even all the decisions, information processing capabilities are generally limited to the CEO's own information-processing capacity, and most data gathering is done informally through personal contacts, a "seat-of-the-pants" managerial style. Information systems in simple structures are crude and informal (Miller, 1986) and are predominantly stand-alone systems, either PCs or small system networked PCs. These systems are good information-processing solutions because of the organizations' small size and limited needs for large amounts of information processing. Most of the PCs are used to enhance individual job performance in such areas as word processing, technical developments, sales, or inventory control, etc., rather than to contribute to an overall MIS. Many small organizations, such as these, use face-to-face meetings or the telephone for their information processing systems. In terms of routine accounting needs or other, more comprehensive activities, many small organizations probably find it cost-effective to use local service bureaus.

## Machine bureaucracy and centralized online systems

The machine bureaucracy's concern for efficiencies and rationalization results in centralized decision making coupled with many rules and policies governing actions. Their computer based information systems must be reliable and consistent in monitoring the organizations' multivariate functions and processes for enhanced control (Burch, 1985). CBIS in a machine bureaucracy is primarily concerned with computerization of the paperwork processes of the firm. These activities range from accounting functions to systems aiding first line personnel, such as online ordering systems or other transaction processing systems (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984).

Since many online CBIS tasks are routine, such as data entry, centralized CBIS topologies are well-suited to monitoring, control, and routine data processing and fit well into machine bureaucracies where control and monitoring are strategic necessities (Miller, 1986). Implementing a centralized system in a machine bureaucracy requires few changes on the part of the organization (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984). The centralized CBIS will tend to not only reinforce the existing organizational structure and “way of doing things,” but might even drive units using the technology to be more formalized than units that are not computerized (Leifer and McDonough, 1985). In summary, effective performance of centralized CBIS tasks require rules and policies that match the tasks of the machine bureaucracy organization.

Perhaps, more importantly, access to external sources of specialized information/databases, such as medical case studies, stock market activity, or legal briefs, is of greater value than internal information/databases. For these reasons, both centralized and distributed information systems might be best suited for the professional bureaucracy.

Distributed systems in academic environments, for example, allow access to data bases residing on a mainframe. The database can be downloaded, worked with, and once again uploaded, and can be processed using the speed and capacities of the large machine. The administrative information system provides for support and completion of more routine tasks such as copying, work processing, accounting, personnel functions, etc., which do not require node or terminal processing. Information systems that provide administrative support might actually use the same hardware system as the technical users, but administrative and professional users utilize the hardware systems in parallel and do not interact with each other. In such a system, the professional's need for autonomy and localized information processing is preserved, as is the need for control by the administrative function.

## Divisionalized designs and information systems

## Form A Divisionalization

Divisionalized organizations, composed of poorly integrated, loosely coupled, semiautonomous units, might have divisions that are machine bureaucracies, professional bureaucracies, or adhocracies, all coexisting within one corporate shell.

While Mintzberg (1979) suggests the most obvious divisional form is the machine bureaucracy, we should recognize that since divisions are loosely coupled to the strategic apex of the organization, divisions might also be professional bureaucracies or adhocracies. For example, IBM's Entry Systems Division operated almost as an adhocracy for the first few years of its existence, unlike the rest of IBM. Another example might be Shearson-Lehman which operates as a professional bureaucracy and is very different from other parts of American Express. Depending on the form of the division, we would expect the CBIS to vary from division to division.

These semiautonomous units (strategic business units or SBUs) are large and homogeneous enough to exercise effective control over most factors affecting their businesses (Gluck, et al., 1980), even though they exist within the umbrella of the enterprise as a whole. The CBIS of these SBUs reinforces the strategy and structure of the SBU rather than the rest of the organization (Frohman, 1982). From the preceding discussion, one would expect a centralized CBIS, if the SBU is a machine bureaucracy, and a combination of centralized and distributed CBIS if the SBU is a professional bureaucracy. Similarly, in an adhocracy SBU, one would expect a decentralized CBIS. (Adhocracy and CBIS design will be discussed below.)

## Form B Divisionalization

Form A divisions, operating semiautonomously with separate CBIS and databases, have the potential for interunit distrust and competition. In Form B divisionalization, there is a greater sense of interunit cooperation and support, and use of cross-divisional CBIS. If Huber's (1984) conjectures are borne out, increasing environmental turbulence will lead to an increase in organizational complexity (a view shared by others, such as Scott (1974), and Pondy and Mitroff (1979). As organizational complexity increases there are increasing needs for greater information processing capacity and capability (Tushman and Nadler, 1978). These needs can be accommodated by changes in CBIS architectures, particularly telecommunications, LAN, voice-data integration, terminal emulation, and electronic mail. For example, in 1982, Digital Equipment Company's (DEC) electronic mail system had 6000 subscribers (Crawford, 1982) allowing information to flow throughout the organization. Leifer and Triscari (1987) found that decentralized CBIS leads to increased information processing capability, increased volume of information processing, more personalized contacts, and more task focused interactions. Decentralized systems, by virtue of their flexibility and high information processing capabilities, assist the organization in adapting to environmental changes (Olson and Lucas, 1982).

In summary, Form B divisionalized structures are associated with decentralization, organizational flexibility, and increased information processing needs, and would be best served by decentralized CBIS for increased information processing capabilities.

## Adhocracy and decentralized systems

Adhocracies rely on team members working together to accomplish the organization's goals. For this reason, high information processing capabilities are needed, which can be accomplished with mutual coordination, task forces (Galbraith, 1973), and, in addition, decentralized CBIS.

In order for communications to flow throughout the organization to where the expert knowledge resides, communications need to be widely-dispersed and available. For this to occur successfully the size of the adhocracy must remain relatively small. Adhocracies might be divisions of divisionalized organizations, as discussed above, or may be internal entrepreneurial units within large organizations, operating somewhat autonomously of organizational constraints. An example is Convergent Technologies, where, in an effort to tap into the entrepreneurial forces and energy, a “company within a company” was formed (Larson and Dolan, 1983). This kind of “strike force” was also described at Data General (Kidder, 1981) in the course of designing a new computer. As a way for organizations to manage uncertainty and change, they can divide themselves into relatively autonomous divisions, each with its own general manager and its own staff heads for each major function (Kanter, 1983). The resulting information flows and unit flexibility means there is a relatively flat organizational pyramid (Bleicher, et al., 1983), allowing innovation and fast response to function and flourish.

Due to limited opportunities for face-to-face communication, decentralized information systems should be perceived as being instrumental in increasing the volume, extent, and availability of information. Foster and Flynn (1984) found that a communication network in a General Motors research and development laboratory resulted in more personalized contacts, an evolved hierarchy based on competence, and increased time to devote to their jobs. Decentralized information systems contribute and enhance the quality and quantity of information, becoming a vital component of the adhocracy's decentralized structure.

## Implementation Issues

While there continue to be tremendous advances in technology, new technology may not be appropriate in all cases. For example, optimistic expectations for electronic mail appear to have fallen short, as the 1986 projections for 1988 usage of electronic mail are about 40% of 1984 projections for 1988 (Infoworld, 1986). Machine bureaucracies, in particular, may find the use of electronic mail to be threatening to traditional, centralized control systems and prevalent culture. Thus, it is expected that electronic mail would find highest acceptance where the culture endorses vertical and horizontal communications, project group solutions to problems, and decentralization. Since successful implementation is based as much on feelings and politics as on the rationality of task accomplishment (Markus, 1983; Markus and Robey, 1983; Solomon, 1985), matching the CBIS with the organization reduces resistance to the CBIS because when there is a good match, power structures, responsibilities and job definitions do not change much.

## Organizational Impact of CBIS

If the CBIS is not matched with the organization, we must not ignore the ability of information systems to change organizational structures (Markus and Robey, 1983). What would happen if the new CBIS routinized formerly decentralized decision making, such as portfolio analysis models, aggregate production scheduling systems, or personnel selection models? Schultz and Slevin (1975) and Franz, Robey and Koeblitz (1986) found that when the new system required numerous changes in user attitudes and behaviors, successful implementation of a CBIS was reduced.

The distinction between CBIS and structure as independent or dependent variables has not been made clear, but there has been an implicit assumption that structure was the independent variable to which the CBIS should conform for an acceptable match. This is largely because structure and culture have more pervasive influence on organization members than does a CBIS, and since a CBIS costs less and is more controllable than the cost of changing an organization's style and culture (Strassman, 1985), it is easier to modify a CBIS design than an organizational design. Keep in mind, however, that there may be hidden costs attached to a new CBIS, often ignored by managers, such as interruptions of established organizational routines or changes in organizational power relations (Attewell and Rule, 1984).

Management may choose a CBIS for strategic or competitive reasons, and in doing so may even realize that it is not well-matched to the organization. There are basically one of two possible outcomes of such a mismatch. Either the system will not be used as intended (e.g., it will be ignored, sabotaged, or manual systems will continue to be used in parallel to “check the system”) and hence will not live up to managerial or user expectations, or the organization will make the desired changes, ultimately resulting in a CBIS-organization match.

Many writers have stressed the need for effective implementation programs (e.g., Markus, 1983; Ginzberg, 1981; Alavi and Henderson, 1981) so that detrimental outcomes associated with CBIS-organization mismatch be avoided, and that new, successful CBIS-organization matches be reached. These programs generally have users involved in the design process and planning and promote effective communication between designers and potential users. Another approach suggests linking the MIS plan with the corporate strategy to ensure that the CBIS will be viewed as valid and important (Pyburn, 1983; Markus and Robey, 1983). It seems reasonable to assume that unless the CBIS is perceived by top management to be important in the achievement of organizational goals, any support for changing the organization to fit the requirements of the new CBIS will surely fail.

In fact, most of the organizational change and development literature (see, for example, Robey, 1986) suggests that for change to be effective, it must be supported by top management. While the impact on organizations of information systems has not gone unnoticed (Keen, 1981; Robey, 1977; Attewell and Rule, 1984), the dynamics of the process of changing organizational structure and organizational culture have not been fully appreciated. As Schein (1984) points out, change in a mature organization “will always be a painful process” (p. 14). For example, it took five years for an avionics research unit of the United States military to effectively change from a set of stand-alone systems to a decentralized system (Leifer and Triscari, 1987).

## Conclusion

Changing organizational structure to more adequately match the CBIS is neither easy nor quick. Senior MIS and organizational managers should modify their expectations accordingly.

Given the competitive nature (Ives and Lear-month, 1984; Porter and Millar, 1985) and strategic nature (Young, 1984) of information systems, changing organizational structure to bring the CBIS and organization into a more natural fit is a strategic necessity for many organizations. By matching the CBIS to the organization a more effective CBIS results — the system is accepted and used. But in some cases, it may be best to implement a system that is not matched to the organization, resulting in new organizational activities that yield increased strategic or competitive advantages. This suggests that the decision to fit or not to fit a CBIS with the organization should be a conscious one, where the costs and benefits are appreciated as part of the CBIS design process.

In focusing on a taxonomy of the CBIS-organization design matches, two outcomes are needed. First, there must be greater comparability among research studies so that the contingencies of CBIS effectiveness can be determined and more useful theories can be developed. Further research is needed to test the taxonomy presented here in a variety of settings to determine its empirical validity. Second, hopefully practitioners will utilize the notions presented here as an additional filter for evaluating new or revised CBIS in their organizations. Focusing on the interaction between CBIS and the organization will result in more effective CBIS and more effective organizations.

## About the Author

Richard Leifer teaches and conducts research in organizational behavior and management information systems at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, New York. He holds a bachelor's degree in Psychology, masters degree in Engineering Science, and a Ph.D. in Organizational Design from the University of Wisconsin at Madison. His current research activities concern the organizational impacts of information systems.
