## Abstract

Innovation is considered to be an important quality in many successful organizations. The nature of the innovation adaptation process within the organization, however, is not well understood. This article examines the efficacy of McFarlan and McKenney's diffusion model as a descriptor of actual behavior. Specifically, the responses of 412 companies were examined with respect to the integration of personal computers into their organizations. The results imply that McFarlan and McKenney's model provides, at least in general, an explanation of technological diffusion.

Keywords: Technology diffusion, personal computer integration, resource allocation

ACM Categories: K.6.2, K.7.2

## Introduction

In the current turbulent business climate, information technology has shown the potential to change whole industries by fundamentally altering competitive relationships. The combination of less expensive information technology and increasing competitive forces has generated what has been called the “economic imperative of the information technologies” [2]. Companies have become compelled to view their information systems (IS) as strategic assets and to use their IS technologies to maintain a competitive edge.

In spite of the strategic importance of keeping pace with technological advances, the ability of most businesses to assimilate and apply information technology lags far behind the available opportunities. Keeping up with technology and controlling its assimilation is a significant challenge facing information systems managers in this decade.

The key to effective technological assimilation is exercising control over its diffusion through the organization. The successful organization needs to simultaneously strike a balance between stagnation and unbridled, counterproductive growth. As the IS role has increased in importance, so has the effort to create a suitable framework to identify and manage particular attributes of an IS, to create an environment where information resource utilization is an active rather than reactive component of organizational culture.

Several studies have been conducted to examine the diffusion of IS technology through the organization. One stream of research examined attributes or characteristics influencing diffusion (formalization, centralization, management attitudes, etc.) [21, 22]. A second stream of research attempted to provide models describing the overall diffusion process of IS technology within the organization [3, 11, 15].

This article will address the latter works, that is, those dealing with overall diffusion models. Everett M. Rogers [17, 18] provided, arguably, the first comprehensive view of organizational innovation by examining and categorizing the many studies of innovation that have been reported in a variety of disciplines (social sciences, agriculture, education, etc.). From the vast body of diffusion literature, Rogers concluded that the empirical evidence showed diffusion of innovations through an organization takes place in several distinct stages.

Gibson and Nolan's stage theory of EDP growth is perhaps the earliest and most extensively cited attempt to create such a framework in the IS literature [3]. Their model attempts to simplify the complex evolution of the EDP department into definable and discrete stages by using IS budgetary changes as stage indicators. Upon subsequent examination though, criticisms [1, 8] of various aspects of the Gibson and Nolan model emerged.

Viewing IS technology from a more encompassing perspective than Gibson and Nolan, McFarlan and McKenney [11] proposed a somewhat different model of how change occurs in the organization. They addressed their theory not to the stages of the evolution of an IS department but to the phases by which an organization can incorporate new ideas before it ultimately evolves in form. An IS department's nature may or may not be altered by the assimilation of a new concept. In fact, many new concepts may be necessary before the magnitude of growth suggested by Gibson and Nolan would be perceived.

McFarlan and McKenney created a procedural model to enable an organization to manage the diffusion of new technologies. Their model segmented the diffusion process into four phases $[11, 12, 13]$ .

Phase 1. Technology Identification and Investment: The emphasis is on the learning and application of the new technology. Staff skills are built by means of pilot studies. In this phase the experience with the technology is so limited that participants are unable to perceive long-term implications. A lack of management attention, incompetent project management, or poor choices in vendors or hardware can cause stagnation to occur and impede movement to more advanced phases.

Phase 2. Experimentation, Learning and Adaptation: The focal point of this phase is on raising the user's awareness of the new technology and the problems it can solve by building on past experience. Success in this phase generally leads to increasing requests for service. Decision making in this phase is concerned with assessing staffing and skill requirements, equipment acquisition and the generation of appropriate financial data. Much care must be taken to remain flexible in this phase because it is essential for the encouragement of innovative applications. In many cases, the most advantageous adaptations are developed at this time. It should be noted that these new developments may differ drastically from the original conceptions of the first phase. If managers are not allowed to develop and refine their understanding, stagnation will occur and halt progress to the next phase.

Phase 3. Rationalization and Management Control: This phase is characterized by the pursuit of short-term efficiencies. Management and operational control are sought, rather than the strategic emphasis of the preceding phase. Management controls are implemented at this time to monitor the growth of the new resource. Activities in this phase center on upgrading staff to acceptable knowledge levels, reorganizing to develop further projects, determining appropriate technology, and cost-effective implementations of the new technology. As in the previous phases, stagnation can occur if too much standardization takes place.

Phase 4. Widespread Technology Transfer: In this phase the benefits and experience of the new technology are disseminated to other units within the organization. The technology base is installed, learning is relatively complete, and long-term analysis and planning are emphasized.

It should be noted that conceptually McFarlan and McKenney's model more closely matches Rogers' general model of the innovation process than did the earlier Gibson and Nolan model. Further, McFarlan and McKenney's model directs managerial attention toward two important considerations. First, it allows attention to be focused upon new, individual innovations whose integration may have previously been left to chance. Secondly, the manager can anticipate potential diffusion problems and commit appropriate resources to a specific situation at the most advantageous time [10].

From a broader perspective, McFarlan and McKenney's model can be viewed as forming a connection between the micro and the macro aspects of organizational assimilation. It forms a link to models by such theorists as Schein and

Lewin, who described the organizational learning process of individuals, and the overall organizational evolutionary process. Schein and Lewin described the learning process as an unfreezing in response to an external pressure, then a changing in the individual's thought process to eliminate the pressure, and finally, a refreezing of the individual's attitudes [9, 19]. As McKenney further notes, "The nature of learning and the functioning of management groups causes organizational learning of new technology to occur in a threshold rather than incremental manner" [14, p. 17].

A crucial observation, though, is that not everyone will feel or respond to the pressure to change and unfreeze at the same time and “if the shift is unsupported it may not be permanent” [14, p. 40]. Conversely, if enough people (a critical mass) incorporate the new principles into their thought processes, the organization may enter into the initial phase as described by McFarlan and McKenney [11]. Each member of the IS team working with the new technology might experience several freezings and unfreezings during the learning process. This may have to occur repeatedly before enough force is generated by the new technology to pressure users to unfreeze their attitudes to enable the commencement of the next phase of learning and adaptation [13].

There may be literally dozens of freezings and unfreezings for a single person alone to incorporate a new technology into his or her thoughts and actions. When one views the assimilation process from an organizational perspective, the enormity and complexity of the process presents a formidable challenge for management at all levels. McFarlan and McKenney's model acts as a bridge to span the chasm between individual learning and organizational evolution, but more importantly, it gives the IS professional a way to address the technology assimilation of large groups of people as well as the specifics of a single technology's diffusion within the organization.

McFarlan and McKenney's model can be viewed more specifically in terms of Schein's writings on the organizational learning process. Schein's learning process acts as a subunit for the phases of individual technology assimilation. Consequently, education of users becomes a critical factor in phase evolution.

In phase one (identification and initial investment), building staff skills becomes the initial consideration. As the technical issues become better understood, the organization subsequently moves into the second phase (experimentation and learning) by making users conscious of the new technology, building upon previous experiences and implementing pilot experiments. When the organization enters the third phase (rationalization and management control) the use and knowledge of the new technology has risen to a level where demand for the new technology may accelerate rapidly. Emphasis must be directed at developing controls to monitor growth and avoid counterproductive proliferation. By the time the organization enters into the last phase (widespread technological transfer) the awareness of the technology has permeated the organization.

## The Study

To effectively assimilate new technology into an organization, management must have a basic understanding of the diffusion process. Organizational diffusion consists of “(1) an innovation, (2) which is communicated through certain channels, (3) over time, (4) among elements of the social system” [17, p. 10]. The organizational challenge is learning to adapt new technologies in the quickest, most efficient manner in order to outpace actual and potential competitors. In the existing business environment the key variable becomes the timing of the placement of resources to insure the expeditious assimilation of the new technology [10].

The focal point of this study is examining the efficacy of using McFarlan and McKenney's diffusion model as a descriptor of actual organizational behavior in relation to the integration of the personal computer (PC). Based on the phase characteristics presented by McFarlan and McKenney, one can make a priori categorizations of organizations into phases based upon characteristic concerns or problems being encountered with PC integration in existing operations.

"Innovation invariably requires the reallocation of (usually scarce) organizational resources" [21, p.

[729]. As a consequence, certain levels of resource commitment would be expected to be associated with a particular level of IS technology diffusion. If the process of diffusion is concerned with the transmission of knowledge and the process of learning over time, it then becomes obvious that educational resource commitments become crucial in the assimilation of new technology.

Specifically, the following null hypothesis was generated from the theoretical framework previously discussed:

H0: There is no association between the problems or concerns that organizations are experiencing with PCs and the educational commitment (forms of PC related training) that are undertaken by the organization.

By measuring the relationship between the perceived problems and concerns with PCs and actions related to educational activities (according to McFarlan and McKenney's classification), an empirical assessment of how organizations are responding to the diffusion process can be addressed.

## Methodology

To investigate the null hypothesis, a survey of IS practitioners was undertaken. It was designed to view the assimilation of a single new form of information system technology—personal computers.

The population examined was the membership of the Data Processing Management Association (DPMA). A survey questionnaire was sent to a nationwide sample of 2000 DPMA members with different organizational affiliations chosen by means of a systematic random sampling. Overall, 412 usable questionnaires were returned for an effective response rate of 20.6%.

The respondents to the questionnaire occupied eight different job title categories ranging from traditional titles to new and unique job titles (e.g., Micro Center Manager, Coordinator of Microcomputer Systems, etc.). On the average, the individuals responding have been employed 14 years in information systems fields. Over 40% of the respondents were classified in the Director or Manager of Information/DP Systems category.

The study gathered data relevant to the phases of the diffusion process in the following areas: 1) current perceived problems or concerns encountered with respect to integrating PCs, and 2) the organizational commitment currently being undertaken to integrate PCs, as indicated by development of educational programs.

## Results and Discussion

In order to develop major problem categories, the problem statements were independently classified by the authors. After a comparison of the independent classifications, a set of eight major classifications was developed as being representative. The set of major problem areas with their descriptors is shown in Table 1.

## Problem Categories

The problem categories were independently rated by three judges. The judges attained an interrater reliability of 0.88.

The first problem category was concerned with PC operating standards (how the PC would fit into current organizational operations). Specific concerns included PC to PC and PC to mainframe compatibility, use of appropriate software, and licensing issues.

The second concern was organizational constraints related to PC development. The most commonly cited areas were financial constraints (e.g., capital and cost data gathering), manpower constraints, and hardware constraints. In addition, concerns were voiced over the diffusion of responsibility arising from the preceding constraints.

The third area was end-user education, particularly the coordination of training and development of user education. The fourth problem category was the user perception of PCs in the organization. High PC visibility has caused many potential users to form invalid ideas about the necessity, ability and need for support of PCs before they actually encounter the technology.

The fifth general problem category was a concern with the rate of technological change that the respondents were experiencing. The sixth problem category was a concern about the lack of managerial attention or support. The seventh major category consisted of responses stating that there already was an existing policy for integrating PCs into their organizations. The final category was the concern over data security brought about by the increasing usage of PCs.

Using McFarlan and McKenney's phase descriptions, the respondents were classified into the four phases according to the problems they were experiencing in integrating PCs into their organizations. Those respondents specifying rate of technological change or lack of management attention were placed in the first phase (identification and initial investment). A typical comment of this phase was “Some key individuals still view the PC as a ‘toy’ and not a serious tool.” Comments such as this illustrate a basic lack of understanding of the PC and its long-term organizational implication and are symptomatic of reactions occurring in many organizations in the first stage of technology assimilation.

The problems of organizational constraints and user perceptions of the PC are key characteristics of phase two organizations (experimentation and learning). One sixteen year veteran director of information systems relates, “The development of technology [in this particular company] is not ready to implement certain desired functions, i.e. networking . . . word processing (etc.). User needs outstrip available solutions." Another manager of information systems adds, "This is because it is perceived that this will be less costly than traditional technologies." Thus, as user awareness is increased so is the demand for the new technology. It is being demanded and used in ways perhaps not thought of during phase one. This usage may be far from productive, though, as is illustrated by the second comment.

The problems dealing with PC operational standards are normally found in phase three (control) which is characterized by concern with the development of policies at the operational level. The following comments provide a representative view of this phase. In one instance a manager responded, “. . . establishing and enforcing guidelines for the acquisition and use of PC hardware/software technologies [is necessary] to achieve a cost effective and compatible implementation of such technologies.” Another respondent cites the following specific example, corroborating the general observations of the previous respondent, “Line managers are avoiding EDP involvement in systems development via PCs. In several cases poor programming practices have developed through use of ‘rookie’ programmers who happen to be in the user department.” Phase three actions try to clean-up problems created during phase two and anticipate future operational problems.

The lack of current problems or concerns dealing with end-user education and data security are all indicative of phase four organizations (wide-spread technology transfer). Many respondents in this phase have developed comprehensive PC policies. One such respondent states, “We don’t have a problem—at the outset we initiated policy guidelines and standards for equipment that allows for successful integration.” However, other concerns arise as the technology spreads throughout the organization. For example, one MIS manager relates, “Users frequently wish to implement mainstream data processing functions on them (PCs) . . . Integrating and controlling corporate data becomes impossible if this is allowed.”

The second aspect of the study was concerned with assessing organizational commitment toward education. To assess educational commitment, the authors felt greater accuracy would be obtained by observing actual educational programs in place rather than using financial criteria as Gibson and Nolan used. While financial criteria ostensibly have greater precision, it was believed that there would not necessarily be a reliable correspondence between financial outlays and actual programs because of the variations (size, industry, and so forth) present in the sample. From McFarlan and McKenney's model, one views an escalating level of educational commitment. As we move through the phases, we would expect increasing levels of educational activities to be provided to organizational members.

## Educational Commitment Categories

No formal training programs are initiated at an organizational level in phase one. In these companies, individuals are just becoming acquainted with the technology. The need would not, as yet, even be perceived at the organizational level. While organization-wide educational programs are not available, specific areas within the company may have varying levels of training $[12, 13]$ .

In phase two, one might describe the organization as assuming a passive role toward education where only a minimal educational commitment is made. The second phase of the diffusion process is associated with a working pilot experiment whose purpose is to develop experience and interest in the new technology. This phase is characterized as one where “both users and developers lack familiarity with the technology and how it can best be applied.” [13, p. 148] Success in this phase “1) . . . starts with a ‘rough and dirty’ test to educate IS staff and users rather than a long period of introspection concerning design and potential benefits; and 2) . . . attracting the interest of potential users of the technology” [13, p. 148]. Given the preceding situation, most organizations would not want to commit the level of resources needed to set-up a formal education program for a technology that is neither proven nor accepted. Communication of information and generation of interest is carried out informally or through external sources.

Internally directed educational programs are developed in the third phase of technological diffusion. In this phase, the organization takes a more responsive and active role by emphasizing management and operational control. It becomes desirable and necessary to set educational standards from an internal perspective, that is, based on corporate goals and objectives. By this time the level of user awareness of and organizational commitment to the new technology dictate the need for and the appropriateness of locally developed programs.

With widespread diffusion of the technology within the organization, a strategic perspective needs to be developed which is future-oriented and related to other systems in the organization. In this phase, “firms take experience gained in one operating division and expand its rise throughout the corporation” [13, p. 40]. Thus, an integration process becomes necessary to transfer the knowledge and experience gained in one part of the organization to all other parts. Significant organizational commitment is given in this stage and typically formalized by conferring functional status to the new technology. The need to educate many end users, combined with the desire to facilitate the use of specialized applications, requires the focusing of resources and energy to achieve this end.

## Actual Educational Commitment

The next action in this study was to assess the actual organizational commitment in the form of educational programs provided to organizational members. The responses ranged from no support for the PC user to assigning a team to set up a department to support PC users. Similar forms of educational activities were also reported in a recent MIT study [16].

The educational activities undertaken in the responding organizations were independently rated by three judges. The judges attained an interrater reliability of 0.94 when rating the responses into the categories in Table 2.

The first level of commitment consisted of organizations that supply no educational support for the PC. Organizations in this category typically either are not aware that the PC exists or still consider it to be a toy rather than an organizational tool. The PC is unrecognized, therefore the idea of training has been seen as unnecessary and wasteful of organizational resources.

The second level of commitment consisted of organizations that provided only minimal commitments. Within this classification, education was defined as programs that did not emanate from within the formal organizational system. Programs were developed by informal support groups and sources external to the organization. In fact, consultants and vendors appeared to play the major educational roles for organizations in this classification. As one respondent commented, "There is no standard training or education yet. Therefore, PC vendors are company-wide setting up courses for users to attend." Use of informal groups and external sources exhibits a degree of organizational commitment, though minimal, as well as being a device to further disseminate knowledge and information about the new technology.

At the next level of support organizations provided an internal direction. Education was defined as the establishment of standardized, on-going programs. General introductory programs and specific training were the primary activities of companies in this classification. Out-of-house training was used when necessary programs were not available internally. The key criteria in this classification were in developing uniformity based on corporate objectives.

The final level of commitment—strategic—was defined as companies offering full-featured, integrated educational programs for PC users within the organization. The highest degree of PC educational support is reflected with the creation of special internal PC support departments or resource centers. One professional described his current environment as, “Many in-house classes, on-line tutorials, and videotape classes are provided through our PC information centers.” A distinguishing feature within this classification is the capability to provide both standardized and customized programs at either group or individual levels.

With both the problems and educational activities independently classified, we can view McFarlan and McKenney's model from two different perspectives. If the model is an accurate description of organizational behavior, we would expect a congruence between the independent classifications. The respondents were classified by the problems they perceived that they were experiencing with PC integration and their respective educational activities. This relationship is shown in Table 3.

Based upon the categories delineated in Table 3, the null hypothesis was tested using the chi-square and phi statistics. The resultant chi-square = 127.67, with 9 degrees of freedom, was significant at p < .001. Statistically, the phase of technological diffusion, as defined by characteristic problems in relation to the level of organizational educational resource commitment, is significantly different from what would be expected by chance. In addition to being significantly different from chance, Table 3 also had a phi statistic = 0.56 showing a moderately high degree of association among the data. Thus, there appears to be a relationship between phase of technological assimilation and level of educational activities.

Further, when viewing Table 3 we observe several trends. First, the total number of firms in all levels of educational commitment decreases as we move to more advanced phases, as we would expect with a new technology such as the PC. Second, organizational educational commitment follows a pattern that we would anticipate, based on McFarlan and McKenney's model. That is, greater levels of educational activities are undertaken as an organization progresses through the phases. For instance, it can be observed that over twice as many phase one organizations provide no education than any of the other phases. The exact opposite trend is seen among organizations in the final phase of technological assimilation. That is, over twice the number of organizations provide maximal levels of education than at any other phase. There is a correspondence between level of educational activity and the particular phase. That is, we would expect phase one organizations to provide no educational resources, phase two organizations to provide only minimal resources, etc., and observe this to be happening.

However, as one views Table 3 some discrepancies are seen. While a correspondence between level of resource commitment and phase does exist, within each of the phases there are many organizations that either provide more or less resources than anticipated. The middle two phases, especially phase two, appear to have organizations more uniformly distributed throughout the differing levels of educational activities than in the first and last phases.

One might ask what would cause these discrepancies. One argument might be that there are more than four phases. We observe that the number of organizations providing no organizational commitment was greater than the number of organizations in all of the other educational levels combined in phase one. Likewise, we observe that more organizations provided the maximum level of commitment than the number of organizations in all of the other educational levels combined in phase four. With the first and last phases showing directional trends that are so much more sharply defined than the middle two phases, one is forced to consider that there might be more than the four postulated phases. For example, Rogers' [17] general model contains five stages, and a later model by Nolan [15] and an empirical study employing a limited sample by Huff and Munro [5] both postulate the existence of six stages.

Another argument might be that the four phase model does not fit all companies equally well. For example, Kimberly and Evanisko [7] found that “adoption of technological innovation tends to be most prevalent in organizations that are large, specialized, functionally differentiated, and decentralized.” And while size explained most of the variance, “cosmopolitan” administrators were also shown to be a significant variable in organizational innovation. A study by Ziegler and Reynolds [20] presents still another moderating variable, the maturity of the industry. Thus, specific individual and organizational variables might define a somewhat different phase model than the current model of McFarlan and McKenney.

## Implications

What are the managerial implications arising from this study? Perhaps the most significant one is that with an understanding of the diffusion process within the organization managers can begin to take a more active stance when dealing with new technological applications. That is, assimilation of new technologies can be controlled, thereby increasing organizational effectiveness.

Some important observations about the diffusion process for managers are: 1) a new technology goes through certain distinct phases as it diffuses through the organization, and 2) the natural processes of diffusion can slow down or even stagnate if efforts aren't taken to ensure that assimilation takes place.

A managerial mechanism to avoid stagnation and waste of company resources has been presented in Henderson and Treacy's [4] decision-making framework. This framework incorporated technological diffusion into the overall organizational processes, emphasizing the educational process as a primary organizational variable.

By committing educational resources at the introduction of a new technology, the dysfunctional and nonproductive effects occurring naturally in the diffusion process are decreased, as is the time within each phase. Productivity is thereby enhanced by bringing the new technology online more quickly.

One successful implementation of PCs, using a strategy similar to the one presented by Henderson and Treacy, was employed at General Foods Corporation. The General Foods strategy included personal executive education first, followed by the establishment of a personal computer center, and finally a general education program for all employees [6].

In conclusion, the findings of this study appear to show that the diffusion model postulated by McFarlan and McKenney is a viable concept and real phenomenon in the business environment. The existence of a repeating diffusion phenomenon is important in many areas of managerial decision-making. In particular, an assimilation strategy allows the organization to anticipate the possibly adverse reactions to new and useful technologies. Management at all levels can initiate action to ensure that appropriate resources are mobilized on a timely basis to strategically support corporate goals and objectives. The value of McFarlan and McKenney's model is that it provides an important starting point for effective planning in a complex business environment that presents challenges from domestic and international competitors.
