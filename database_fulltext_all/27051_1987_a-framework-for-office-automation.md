# A Framework for Office Automation

By: Raymond McLeod, Jr.
College of Business Administration
Texas A&M University
College Station, Texas

By: Jack William Jones
Neeley School of Business
Texas Christian University
Fort Worth, Texas

## Abstract

Much attention currently is being aimed at office automation. Proponents are recognizing its potential value to the manager as an information source, in addition to its initial focus on improved clerical operations. However, proponents are recommending office automation as an information system without citing any basis, theoretical or empirical, for their claims. The authors construct a theoretical framework that may be useful to the manager, or the system designer, in channeling office automation efforts in the right direction. The framework is based on Henry Mintzberg's concept of managerial roles, and is presented in the context of data gathered from five senior executives.

Keywords: Office automation, theory, framework, decision support

ACM Categories: H.1.1, H.4.1, H.4.2, H.4.3, J.1

## Introduction

The purpose of this article is to report the result of research aimed at the incoming information flows of senior executives. An understanding of these flows—where they originate, the form that they take, and their ultimate use—enables the development of a theoretical framework that can assist the manager in evaluating office automation (OA) as a means of gathering information for decision making.

OA is one of the most popular topics in business today. Hardware and software vendors are touting their products as the way to achieve a long overdue increase in office productivity, and some applications, such as word processing and electronic mail, are being implemented on a wide scale. If the increasing interest persists, the money spent to automate the office could soon dwarf the investments that firms are making in data processing and decision support systems $[6]$ .

The initial focus of these office automation efforts was on increased productivity of clerical and secretarial workers, but recent attention has been aimed at how management can benefit from OA through better information for decision making $[8, 11, 22, 26]$ . While recognizing the value of applying office automation to increase the productivity of secretarial and clerical workers, our concern in this paper is to address the information needs of managers. There are two reasons for this attention. First, management applications are the best way to justify office automation expenditures because the benefits of improved decisions far outweigh the economies of increased clerical productivity $[3, 11, 13]$ . Second, office automation offers hope of capturing some of the informal information flows that have eluded past EDP (electronic data processing) and DSS (decision support system) efforts. Our approach will be to identify the overall flow of information to the manager for use in decision making, and then to evaluate the extent to which OA might contribute to those flows.

When one reads the literature of office automation, two points stand out. First, there is no theoretical base that ties everything together. Office automation did not evolve in response to a well-defined need. Rather, the technology was developed first, then an application was found. As Peter Keen [16] points out, "Office technology has been tool-obsessed and product-obsessed. But the . . . problem is this issue that office technology doesn't have a concept of architectural integration." The second point that characterizes office automation is that little has been offered in the way of specific benefits that can be expected. When OA is presented as a way to increase secretarial and clerical productivity, there usually is little elaboration about the form that this productivity will take. Likewise, when OA is presented as a way to improve management decision making, little is offered in the way of specifics. Most assuredly, it is difficult to measure the informational value of OA [11], but that difficulty should not prevent a detailed prescription of the manner in which OA should and should not be applied.

This paper addresses these two points. We describe a research project dealing with the flow of management information that office automation seeks to capture. The project data provide the empirical basis for a theoretical framework that can be used as a blueprint for office automation systems intended to provide decision support for managers. The framework enables current technology to be aimed at places where it will do the most good, and serves as a guide for future research and development in theory and technology.

## Office Automation

Office automation has been defined as the application of electronic and electro-mechanical devices for the purpose of increasing the productivity of office workers $[13]$ . The devices include general-purpose computers, special-purpose office machines and communications equipment. The office workers include managers, professionals, secretaries and clerks. The professionals are salaried persons who do not manage, yet have a need for information. The term “knowledge workers” has been used to describe both managers and professionals $[26]$ . Secretarial/clerical employees achieve increased productivity in their ability to create, store, retrieve and transmit business communications. Knowledge workers achieve increased productivity in the form of better decisions.

When OA is seen as a means of providing information for decision making, then the line separating OA from DSS becomes vague. In fact, OA proponents regard everything happening in the office, including decision support, as a possible OA application [22, 26].

We recognize the contribution of OA to decision making, yet retain the DSS as an information system concept, by taking a narrower view of OA. We regard OA as simply a means of communicating data and information; the processing and presentation of that information is the bailiwick of DSS. This view does not belittle the importance of OA. If OA can contribute to the information flow by capturing some of the flow that has escaped the MIS and DSS concepts, then it becomes a pearl of great price.

## OA applications

The most widely known OA applications are word processing, electronic mail and electronic calendaring. All three are computer applications currently being spotlighted as ways to use personal computers. Word processing facilitates communication by increasing the ease with which printed documents can be prepared. Electronic mail substitutes electronic storage and transmission for the paper form. Electronic calendaring facilitates communication by increasing the ease with which an appointment calendar can be created, shared and maintained. With the exception of word processing $[20, 31]$ , little effort has been expended to explain whether the three most popular applications fit into an overall MIS or DSS context and, if so, how.

Other OA applications include voice mail, videotex, image storage and retrieval, facsimile transmission, and teleconferencing. Voice mail is just like electronic mail, except that you speak your message into your telephone instead of typing it using a keyboard. The message is stored in a computer until the other person uses his or her phone to retrieve it. Videotex is a way of entering document-type material into computer storage and then retrieving the material using a visual display terminal.

Image storage and retrieval is the use of microfilm and microfiche to capture document images for storage compactness; the computer facilitates quick retrieval by advising the physical location of an image, such as reel and frame number. Facsimile transmission is a way to transmit a document image over great distances; it could be called “long-distance copying.”

Teleconferencing consists of three technologies — videoconferencing, audioconferencing, and computer conferencing. Videoconferencing can be one- or two-way, and can utilize full-motion or still pictures. Audioconferencing is two-way, and provides sound communication only. Computer conferencing enables groups of persons to interact using their computer terminals. The unique feature of computer conferencing is that all members do not have to be online at the same time—the members review the previous discussion and add their contributions at their own convenience. This ability is termed “asynchronous” communication, contrasted to the “synchronous” style of video- and audioconferencing where all participants must be engaged in the communication at the same time. Computer conferencing also can be used in a synchronous manner.

## OA communication

Technology has shaped the information that is communicated by OA in terms of both where the information originates (the source) and the form that it takes (the medium). Most of the OA applications aim at internal rather than environmental flows. One reason is incompatibility between data communications networks using different vendors' equipment. Many of the applications involve linking several pieces of equipment electronically, which for reasons of incompatibility are restricted to in-house networks. This restriction does not work to the advantage of OA, and is felt especially at the executive level where information from the environment is so important.

The media employed by OA also play a critical role in acceptance of OA by managers. The managers' media preferences have received the attention of several researchers, the most publicized being Henry Mintzberg's study of five executives [23]. Kurke and Aldrich [19] replicated the Mintzberg study, and Daft and Lengel [7] addressed the topic of information "richness." All of these studies concluded that managers prefer oral to written information.

Only three of the OA applications feature oral media. These applications are voice mail, audio-conferencing and videoconferencing. Whether these electronic applications can be expected to match the inherent appeal of informal, face-to-face oral communications remains to be seen. However, if managers do prefer oral media, regardless of how that media is communicated, one would expect these three applications to have a special appeal.

A further appraisal of OA media relates to printed or displayed output. Computer and communications technology enables outputs to be enhanced with graphics, color, and even (in the case of videoconferencing) animation. These visual enhancements, coupled with the inherent “user friendliness” of the applications, help to explain the success that vendors of OA hardware and software have experienced.

In some respects OA has characteristics that offer strong appeal to managers, and in other respects it does not. The reality is that OA is not a panacea. It requires considerable effort to sell to management and to implement it once sold. If the sellers, implementers and managers are to understand where OA should and should not be applied, some type of master plan or framework is needed. The absence of such a framework is one reason, perhaps the most important one, why the benefits received from OA have thus far been spotty.

## Need for an OA Framework

All of the major computer applications prior to OA have had the benefit of master plans early in their life cycles. EDP evolved as an application of computing technology to well-defined accounting systems. It is true that there were problems in implementing early EDP systems, but the problems stemmed from the newness of the technology. There was never any doubt about what the systems were to achieve. MIS had no overall plan, but there were “islands” of structure in the MIS movement $[4, 17, 24, 34]$ .

The most clear cut example of the value of a theoretical foundation is DSS, where the framework of Gorry and Scott-Morton [9] actually initiated the DSS concept. Subsequent efforts contributed additional frameworks, providing different views of how to channel implementation efforts in the right direction [1, 30].

If OA is to have such a foundation it would seem wise that it possess the characteristic that is so apparent in the DSS framework—a user orientation [2]. In the case of DSS, Gorry and Scott Morton recognized unique needs of levels of managers and unique characteristics of problems that the managers solve. If OA is to be regarded as a communication medium channeling information for decision making, then the framework should be oriented toward the flows of information—where they originate, what form they take, and how they are used. The framework should be the mold that OA must fit if it is to be considered seriously as a means of improving decision making.

## A General Model

The conceptualization that we employed in studying the linkages between information sources, flows and use is based on Henry Mintzberg's managerial role model $[23]$ . We expanded Mintzberg's basic model as depicted in Figure 1 to integrate information flows from five separate sources with the roles that managers play. (The figurehead role is not relevant to our study and is not included.)

Through the interpersonal roles of liaison and leader the manager acquires information from sources located external and internal to the organizational unit. As liaison, the manager makes contacts outside his or her vertical chain of command and acquires information from other operating units, the external environment, and internal support units such as the accounting and computer departments. As leader, the manager acquires internally oriented information from superiors and subordinates. Thus from the perspective of an information monitor, the manager is perpetually scanning the environment looking for problems to solve and opportunities to seize. Information gathered through the monitoring activity can either be routed to superiors (the spokesman role) or to subordinates (the disseminator role), or used in the four decisional roles—disturbance handler, entrepreneur, resource allocator, or negotiator [23].

It would be an easy matter to overlay onto the arrows of the Figure 1 model the OA technologies that we have identified, implying that all of the technologies can be used to transport information along all of the paths. However, we would have only a laundry list of OA applications for each flow. We would not know the relative importance of each of the flows to the manager, nor would we know which media are preferred for each flow. An understanding is needed of how information really flows to the managers, enabling the proper positioning of OA as a means of communicating that information.

## The Study

A study was designed to focus on the flows of information from the five sources to the four decisional roles. Data were gathered by logging all of the information inputs of five experienced senior executives during a two-week period.

## The executives

The executives included a president of a life insurance company, a corporate officer for a wholly owned subsidiary of an international petroleum services company, a corporate officer for an energy company, a chairman and CEO of a city bank, and a president and CEO of a 300-store retail chain. All organizations are headquartered in the Southwest. All of the executives are in their 40s, and have college degrees (two with Masters and one with a Ph.D.). All have been in their current positions from 1 to 8 years and are characterized as energetic, innovative, and successful. As an indicator of success, since the time of the data gathering the petroleum services officer has assumed the presidency of a similar company, and the bank president has assumed responsibility for all of the holding company's banks.

## Survey Instrument

A form was designed for the executive's secretary to use in logging the executive's incoming information transactions for a two-week period. The secretary logged incoming computer and noncomputer reports, letters, memos, periodicals, scheduled and unscheduled meetings, and telephone calls. Additionally, it was necessary for the executive to log those transactions taking place away from the office that the secretary could not observe, such as tours to other locations, social activities, and business meals.

Each logged entry identified the media type, the source, the topic, the decisional role where the information likely would be used, and the executive's perceived value at time of receipt. The secretary entered only the media and source, the executive entered the topic and value, and the researchers, working in conjunction with the executives and secretaries, entered the decisional role.

## Information Characteristics

Volume. A total of 1454 transactions were logged, with the average daily volume varying considerably from executive to executive. The retail CEO had the highest average (41), and the petroleum services officer the lowest (14). The two CEOs and the president had the highest daily volume, and the two corporate officers the lowest. The volume also varied from day to day for each executive.

Value. The values that the executives assigned to each transaction ranged from zero (for no value) to ten (for maximum value). High values were assigned sparingly; only 6% of the transactions received values of 9 or 10. The executives were much more generous in assigning low values. They felt that 13% of the transactions had zero value—the most frequently assigned number. Another 13% were assigned values of 1 or 2. The overall average information value was 4.48.

Source. In terms of volume, the firm's environment and subordinates were the major sources, together providing 75% of the transactions. The executives obtained the least information from superiors, a not surprising result considering the top-level status of the participants. It should be recognized, however, that each executive had one or more levels of superiors in the form of board chairpersons, holding company executives, and so on. Therefore, the opportunity existed for a higher volume of upper-level information had the executive elected to take advantage of it.

Information received by the executives was analyzed in terms of both volume and value for each of the five sources in the Figure 1 model. This analysis is summarized in Table 1.

Table 1 also lists the average transaction value for each of the sources. You will note that the ranking based on value differs considerably from that based on volume. The environment provided the largest volume, but the lowest average value. The superiors provided the lowest volume, but the second highest value.

The environmental sources initiated information flows with letters and telephone calls, and the executives supplemented these with information gained from periodicals and scheduled meetings. Information from subordinates employed the widest media mix. The subordinates triggered flows by writing memos and letters, "dropping in" for unscheduled meetings, making telephone calls, and forwarding noncomputer reports. On a few occasions subordinates forwarded computer reports. The executives stimulated flows from subordinates by scheduling meetings and taking tours. Internal support units communicated primarily by providing computer and noncomputer reports, supplemented by memos. Other operating units relied most heavily on memos, telephone calls, and noncomputer reports. Superiors communicated information to the executives in the form of memos and both scheduled and unscheduled meetings. The executives acquired some information from superiors by touring.

The six circled entries in Table 2 account for more than half of the executives' total information value—letters from the environment, telephone calls from the environment and subordinates, memos from subordinates, and both scheduled and unscheduled meetings with subordinates. These information linkages are the heart of the executive's information system. The many other linkages are all relatively minor in comparison, but cannot be ignored. By utilizing a wide media mix, it is as if the executive were saying "I have my favorite media that I know I can rely on, but I'm going to supplement them with as many more as I can."

## Media of decision making

The executives concentrated on certain media in handling disturbances and making entrepreneurial improvements, as indicated in Table 4. Letters and telephone calls were important in both roles. Memos, noncomputer reports and unscheduled meetings provided the most support in handling disturbances, and periodicals and scheduled meetings provided the most support in making improvements. The executives showed no strong preferences for media used in resource allocation.

The striking pattern from Table 4 is the manner by which the executives drew on the different media types when playing the disturbance handler, entrepreneur, and resource allocator roles. With the exception of social activities and business meals, the executives took advantage of all media opportunities. The message seems clear: If an executive is to have full information support while performing the various decisional roles, the information system must be flexible enough to provide a wide mixture of media.

Table 4 dramatically illustrates the fallacy in the infatuation with computer-based systems at the expense of other media. The executives all had computer resources at their disposal, but none based their information systems on computer output. Rather, the executives relied primarily on others in their organizations to provide most of their information. Those persons made use of computers to varying degrees, emphasizing that the computer cannot be ignored as a means of obtaining information. The key points to this discussion are that (1) computer output is only one of several ways to communicate information to executives, and (2) the executives' computer output did not go to the executives directly, but, rather, was transmitted through other persons.

## Toward an Office Automation Structure

The understanding of the executives' information flows provided by the study enables the development of a structure showing where OA can be applied to enhance managerial decision making.

OA as a new way to handle established communications. A starting point is to relate the communications media to the OA applications and this is done in Table 5. The shaded boxes identify the OA applications that correspond to each medium. The OA applications could replace or supplement the medium.

Of all the OA applications, word processing is the most firmly established in terms of handling the media designated in Table 5. The wide acceptance of word processing as a means of producing written communications is evidenced by the variety of word processing software and hardware that currently is in use. Manager's secretaries, and in some cases managers themselves, use word processing to prepare written communications. However, in these instances word processing usually is regarded as a means of communicating information from the manager to someone else. Word processing also should be viewed as a corporate resource for building a strong intra-firm communication network facilitating the flow of information to the managers. Therefore, managers should be just as interested in their internal and environmental information sources developing word processing systems as they are in developing their own.

Electronic calendaring can facilitate the gathering of information by enabling the manager to schedule meetings, social activities, business meals and tours. Electronic calendaring also enables internal sources to identify appropriate times to communicate information to the manager by telephone calls and unscheduled meetings. Electronic calendaring facilitates the flow of oral information from both environmental and internal sources, making it especially valuable to the manager in establishing and maintaining those linkages.

Electronic mail and voice mail can be used for letters, memos and telephone calls where the intent is simply to relay information, not engage in conversation. Electronic mail also can be used to relay both computer and noncomputer reports, an especially effective application when managers are located some distance from where the reports are prepared.

Image storage and retrieval can be used to obtain microform images of all types of written documents. Of all the OA applications, videotex is the one that currently is the most difficult to link to decision support. The bulk nature of the data retrieved requires analysis time that the manager, especially the top-level manager, might not want to expend. It would appear that videotex, image storage and retrieval, and facsimile transmission are the OA applications that the manager will most likely delegate to someone else (clerical employees and knowledge workers) to use and glean the meaningful information for communication to the manager in another form.

If it is inconvenient for the persons to meet at a central site, teleconferencing can make a contribution. Firms that have implemented teleconferencing claim to have achieved a number of benefits. Although justified initially as a means of reducing travel costs, teleconferencing users have recognized better preparation for meetings, more effective meetings, more information communication, and increased personal productivity as the benefits derived [10].

## A Framework for OA as a Decision Support System

In assembling our structure we are influenced by Mintzberg's role model in Figure 1, the relationships between OA applications and communications media in Table 5, plus our study findings.

Figure 2 is the type of framework that has been missing in previous efforts to apply OA as a management tool. The structure identifies the OA applications that appear to offer the most potential for contributing to the various flows of information. The most important point to recognize when studying the framework is that it is based on the data gathered from individual executives. Each manager is likely to have a unique mixture of preferred OA applications. Figure 2 applies OA technologies to the information flows of the study executives. The OA technologies identified in the figure are those that the researchers believe would be most readily accepted by the study executives, based on the detailed data that were gathered plus the researchers' understanding of the executives' preferences for particular information sources and media.

In tailoring the model to a particular manager, the task of the OA system designer is to become familiar enough with the manager's flows and media preferences to help that manager identify his or her unique mix. The Mintzberg-based framework provides the overall blueprint that the manager and designer can use in tailoring OA to the manager's information flows. Rather than follow the inductive approach of trying to find a use for a particular OA application as has been the pattern up to now, the framework establishes the starting point for a deductive approach. For example, the manager and designer can address a specific flow (such as the flow of information from subordinates into the leader role) and evaluate each OA application in terms of how it fits the needs and preferences of the manager for that flow.

![](/api/attachments/GNE2TX99/fulltext/images/31c0451a23dc0b717f81b921b0b4f28bfe3f649dd9d0341209be1c640412c2e1.jpg)  
Figure 2. Office Automation as a Communicator of Decision Support Information

There are 10 possible OA applications, and none of the study executives' flow paths use them all. OA support in terms of the number of potential applications is the greatest for routing information from superiors and subordinates, and to the decisional roles of disturbance handler and entrepreneur. In contrast, only three OA applications (electronic calendaring, videotex, and word processing) link the manager to the environment. The manager can use electronic calendaring to keep track of appointments with external sources, and can purchase material such as tables of economic statistics in videotex form. External sources can use their own word processing systems when communicating by letter.

The path connecting the manager to internal support units likewise does not lend itself to many OA applications. The reason is that most of the information flows come automatically in the form of periodic computer and noncomputer reports. Facsimile transmission can be used to transmit graphical material, and persons in the support units can use electronic mail along with word processing to prepare the explanations that often accompany the reports. Also, some periodical data can be retrieved in videotex form. In communicating with other operating units, the manager can use teleconferencing as an alternative to scheduled meetings. The other units can utilize voice and electronic mail, as well as word processing, as alternatives to letters, memos and telephone calls.

The same tailoring of OA applications to certain media can be seen in the flows to the decisional roles. The study managers showed strong preferences for certain media in certain decisional situations. $^{1}$ As an indication of the value of the study data, an OA application may be preferred in one situation and not in another. For example, a manager would use teleconferencing to obtain information for handling disturbances and making entrepreneurial improvements where scheduled meetings are preferred, but not use teleconferencing for the other two roles where information is less likely to be communicated in a group setting. In the absence of a framework, this behavior appears irrational to the observer (often the designer). The system fails and the designer does not know why.

We feel quite strongly about the pairings of the applications with the flows shown in the framework, as they are supported by the study data. However, users of the framework are cautioned concerning two points. First, the study managers were all executives; lower-level managers most likely will emphasize different flows. For example, the top-level executive is like a mother hen looking out for her baby chicks, constantly scanning the environment for information that someone in the firm might be able to use. The incoming network of the executive, therefore, is more pervasive than that of the lower-level manager. By addressing the incoming flows of the executive, we feel that we have included all of the types of flows coming to lower-level managers as well.

The second major point to consider is the opportunity that OA offers as a new way to communicate. Although our matching of OA applications to media might imply a one-for-one substitution, that is not the intent. Our framework does not present OA applications strictly as substitutes for currently favored media. Instead, it shows where OA can be applied to replace or to supplement those media. The end result can be a much more powerful information network than is available currently to the manager. The manager will be able to “have the cake and eat it too” by electing to replace a current medium only when OA can do a better job.

Our framework regards OA as a means of facilitating information flows. As such, it minimizes the chance of making serious errors in applying OA to achieve other, less important, and potentially counter-productive objectives. As an example, OA proponents often quote statistics of how much of the manager's time is spent in meetings. The implication is that this is wasted time and OA can reduce it $[13, 14, 26]$ . Our study revealed that the average value of information gained in scheduled meetings was the highest of all media at 7.4, followed by unscheduled meetings at 6.2. The objective of OA ought to be to increase the value of the information communicated at meetings, rather than reduce their time. This framework of OA as a communicator of decision support information, based on study data, can steer OA applications in the right direction.

## Evaluating OA as a provider of decision support information

Where does this analysis lead us? Can OA provide the linkages to the sources that managers regard as valuable? Can it communicate information in a form that managers will use? Can it be counted on to contribute as a communicator of information to be used in decision making?

OA currently suffers two basic weaknesses. One is its internal focus. This weakness should become less of a factor in the future as data communications standards become more accepted and as a wider variety of environmental data becomes available to videotex users. The other weakness is OA's emphasis on visual rather than oral media. If managers do indeed prefer oral media, as the studies consistently indicate, then OA could be geared to the wrong media. But this issue calls for additional study. Perhaps the “written” form of OA communications, such as computer conferencing and electronic mail displays, overcome the disadvantages of the traditional paper-document written communications.

Looking at the individual OA applications, two appear to offer only marginal utility as decision support tools—videotex, and image storage and retrieval. Videotex is very similar to database querying, which is a popular way of obtaining decision support information, but videotex is characterized by storage of contents of lengthy documents. The bulk does not lend itself to management by exception, which is the way that the computer-based DSS usually is applied. The same can be said for image storage and retrieval—the contents are very detailed and selection and synthesis are difficult.

Another point reducing the number of potential OA applications is the fact that several are tailored to geographically dispersed organizations. Audioconferencing, videoconferencing, and facsimile transmission apply only when distance is a factor. Combined with the marginal potential of videotex and image retrieval, a small firm may have only word processing, electronic mail, voice mail, electronic calendaring and computer conferencing from which to choose. For the manager of a small, local organization the selection of potential OA applications is not as bountiful as it first appears.

In spite of these weaknesses, OA does have some characteristics that make it a legitimate candidate to participate in the manager's information system. Word processing is firmly established, and electronic and voice mail likely will become very popular with the installation of LANs (local area networks) and digital PBX systems during the next few years. The broadband capability of LANs will stimulate the adoption of videoconferencing, the application most closely resembling the face-to-face contact that managers prefer.

OA could well offer the most as a package of entirely new communications opportunities. The asynchronous nature of computer conferencing, permitting participation at one's own convenience, makes it possible to conduct a conference in an entirely new manner. This characteristic of convenience will make computer conferencing popular even in local firms where the task is not to span distances in geography, but rather in time. Cases have been recorded where computer conference members enter their dialog on a Saturday afternoon or at 3 in the morning [13]. Likewise, videoconferencing opens new doors to communication as described earlier.

Sales of OA hardware and software currently are booming and are expected to increase. If OA is to serve as an information communicator for management, the system designers must not only know the managers' information flows, but understand why those flows are preferred. Perhaps our structure based on information flows to decisional roles will contribute to this understanding. Perhaps managers can improve decision-making performance through a carefully conceived and executed program of implementing OA as a new way to communicate in a traditional manner and as an entirely new communication opportunity.

## Applying office automation for decision support

The task of applying OA as a decision support system is too complex for a simple formula. Much depends on the organization (whether it is global or local), on the nature of the manager's work, and on the manager's preferences (whether they are for external or internal information, in an oral or written form). In spite of these complexities, it is possible to produce a reference guide showing the OA options. Such a guide appears as a decision tree in Figure 3.

A manager who is contemplating OA as a DSS element should take the following steps.

1. Work jointly with his or her secretary, administrative assistant, and/or consultant to take an inventory of incoming communications, identifying the sources, the media, and perceived value. It is necessary that an actual count be conducted since gut-feel estimates may be misleading [21].

2. Become familiar with the main features of the different OA applications by attending seminars offered by OA vendors and/or industrial associations, and by reading the current literature. It is necessary that the manager personally gain this familiarity in order to consider OA as a package of new communications opportunities. Only the manager knows his or her needs and preferences well enough to make this determination.

## Conclusions

The claims of OA hardware and software vendors are taking their toll, and organizations are purchasing the wide assortment of products. Initial successes with word processing tempt users to try for more. But OA as a package of computer and noncomputer technologies has no central focus. First it was improvement of clerical productivity, and now it is better decisions. Although the goal of better decisions is proper, the proponents are knocking on the wrong door—emphasizing efficiency while overlooking the value of better information.

A focus can be achieved by gearing decision-based OA programs to the needs and preferences of managers. A solid theoretical base can be built, applying time-proven principles that have worked so well in the DSS area—frameworks based on how managers use information in decision making. We draw on the concept of managerial roles, as described by Mintzberg [23], to provide the basic framework. This framework is fleshed out with data gathered from a study of the information gathering and use patterns of five senior executives. The result is a framework identifying which OA applications seem to offer the most potential depending on sources, media preferences, and decisional roles.

Armed with the framework, the manager can go into an OA feasibility study with eyes open—realistically recognizing the potential that OA has to offer when used in certain ways, but not expecting the impossible. This clear view from the outset will reduce the possibility of failure and disappointment, without setting up roadblocks to those uses where office automation can pay real dividends as a new way to communicate using traditional media, and as a completely new communications opportunity.
