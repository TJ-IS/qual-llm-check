## Abstract

Building on previous research, an instrument for measuring information systems effectiveness was designed and tested. Factor analysis of the results revealed several underlying constructs that are simple to interpret and encourage adoption of the instrument for general use in firms.

It was found that performance varies widely between firms and that the better performers were those where the level of performance matched the perceived importance of the particular aspect of IS. Discriminant functions applicable to the chosen sample are discussed and a process for evaluating IS effectiveness in individual firms is presented.

Keywords: MIS management, strategic planning, measurement, effectiveness, evaluation, computer-based information systems

ACM category: K.6, K.6.4, H, I.5.2

## Introduction

Measuring the effectiveness of information systems activity is a difficult task. As success in information systems (IS) becomes a key strategic issue in sectors such as financial services, the task becomes more important. As information systems support starts to encompass the whole range of operational and decision-making activity, it also becomes more difficult. And as information systems budgets grow and resource allocation challenges IS managers and steering committees, reliable methods to assess IS effectiveness, both in absolute terms and relative to competitive firms, and to identify causes of poor performance assume great importance.

In this article the effectiveness of the overall information systems effort in 21 firms in the financial services sector is evaluated using an instrument based on previous work. The reliability and construct validity of this instrument are examined using factor analysis, and measures of overall effectiveness are related to the particular factors derived.

Alloway and Quillard [1] have asserted that firms performing well in areas they consider especially important do better overall than firms for whom this relationship does not hold. This hypothesis is tested and a subsidiary hypothesis, also deriving from this work, is tested as well; namely that firms consider inquiry and analysis systems more important than monitoring and exception reporting systems, but are developing too few systems in the former area compared with the latter. Both of these hypotheses are of considerable importance to firms wishing to maximize the effectiveness of their overall systems effort.

The objectives of the research reported here are thus:

\- To develop and test a modified instrument for measuring information systems effectiveness and to evaluate its reliability and construct validity.

\- To identify those attributes most closely linked to overall information systems effectiveness in the financial services sector in South Africa.

\- To test the hypothesis that the overall effectiveness of IS is a function of the correlation between the perceived importance and performance of individual IS attributes.

\- To test the hypothesis that inadequate attention is being paid to the development of analysis and enquiry systems in the chosen sector.

## Developing the Instrument

Several attempts have been made to measure “user information satisfaction” as a surrogate for the overall effectiveness of information systems in the organization. Ives, Olson and Baroudi [4] review and critically analyze some of these studies and argue convincingly for the adoption of the instrument designed by Bailey and Pearson [2] on the basis of reliability, content, and predictive and construct validity. They also test and recommend adoption of a shorter and enhanced version in the same article.

Alloway and Quillard [1] developed and applied a similar instrument, consisting of 26 items. Where Bailey and Pearson used six scales (a set of semantic differentials) for each item, they called for responses regarding just “performance” and “importance” in each case.

The instrument used in this research consists of 38 items, 24 of which derive from Pearson, 12 from Alloway, and two additional items. The composite instrument is designed to meet the general need for assessing information systems effectiveness, to enable a specific test of the hypotheses stated above, and to reflect our perception that certain issues such as database technology, steering committees and strategic planning must be addressed in today's IS arena. The two scales of performance and importance are measured for each item and in the last part of the questionnaire respondents are asked to rate their firm's overall information system on a single scale from “complete failure” to “very successful.”

## Research Methodology

A pilot test of the composite questionnaire was conducted using 22 user managers and DP professionals attending an information systems executive course at one of the authors' institutions. Some ambiguities were removed and a general discussion of the responses confirmed the reasonableness of the instrument for measuring IS effectiveness (see Appendix 1 for final form).

The CEO's of 32 firms in the financial services sector were then invited to participate in the study. The firms included all the commercial banks and building societies, the top ten life assurance companies by annual premium income, and several large general banks and investment companies. Twenty-six firms agreed to participate. The six that did not covered the full spectrum of institutions and size and there was no indication of non-respondent bias.

Batches of questionnaires were delivered to an appointed liaison officer at each site with the request that they be distributed on a confidential basis to 10-15 user and DP managers, endeavoring to cover a variety of management levels and functional areas.

Twenty-one usable sets of responses were obtained, with five firms missing the deadline, or having to be excluded for other administrative reasons. The 276 individual responses are characterized in Table 1. Close contact was maintained with liaison officers and there is no reason to suspect lack of independence in responses within a firm.

The 276 usable responses were subjected to a variety of uni- and multivariate statistical procedures in order to explore the construct validity and reliability of the instrument. $^{1}$

## Analysis of the Instrument

## Construct Validity

The principal components method of factor analysis using varimax rotation $[7]$ was applied to the “performance” and “importance” parts of the questionnaire. Reasons for choosing this technique included: its value as an objective and powerful procedure for examining the constructs underlying the chosen instrument $[5]$ ; its reproducibility for other sectoral analyses planned; and, given the very wide range of attributes typically used in evaluating IS effectiveness, the appeal of determining a small set of orthogonal (independent) factors for subsequent data reduction and substantive analysis $[3]$ .

Applying the method to the “importance” responses resulted in 10 factors which accounted for 55% of the overall variance. Few items had high factor loadings and the constructs implied by the factors did not make immediate “sense.” In contrast, factor analysis of the “performance” set yielded 7 factors which accounted for 62% of the total variance. Table 2 shows the 38 items and selected factor loadings. A cut-off level just below the conventional 0.50 was chosen since this allowed the unambiguous assignment of all items to one or other of the first seven factors. Inspection of the items composing each factor led to the generic factor names given in the figure.

While the grouping indicated by the factor analysis on performance responses is very appealing, it was nevertheless considered necessary to perform a check using the so-called complete linkage method of hierarchical cluster analysis. This approach imposes severe conditions on the formation of clusters of similar items $[3]$ and, as it turned out, provided valuable confirmation of the factor analysis. It became apparent that the “importance” clusters were indistinct and different from the factors derived. Also, there was considerable similarity between the clusters and factors derived for “performance” measures.

## Reliability

In light of the above, the performance set looked promising as an instrument to measure IS effectiveness and, therefore, its statistical reliability was examined. Two tests, based on assumptions of independent and normally distributed responses, were carried out.

First, the reliability of the set of performance ratings was measured by calculating between and within-respondent variability. The one-way analysis of variance procedure $[3]$ yielded a highly significant F-ratio of 15.31. In similar fashion to Bailey and Pearson this outcome may be expressed as a reliability coefficient of 0.94.

Second, a composite measure, being the arithmetic mean of the 38 performance ratings for each respondent, was calculated. These 276 values were analyzed to compare between to within-firm variability for the 21 firms in the sample. Variance analysis again yielded a highly significant F-ratio (8.12) and a reliability coefficient for the overall instrument of 0.88.

The outcome of the factor analysis of performance responses strongly supports the construct validity of the modified instrument tested in this research. The reasonably high proportion of the overall variance accounted for, and the assignment of virtually all of the original items to factors that have intuitively sensible meaning suggest that several independent dimensions governing information systems effectiveness have been identified. This will be discussed later in this article.

The poor outcome of the same analysis on importance items requires interpretation and two hypotheses are put forward. Since respondents were required to tackle these items first, it could be that there was some confusion with the ideas and requirements and, particularly since all the scales were in the same direction, a tendency to respond mechanically. Having gained the full “gestalt,” responses to the performance set might then have been more ordered in the minds of the respondents. Alternatively, it may simply be that reflection on relative importance is more abstract and challenging than on performance, which is a tangible, day-to-day issue for users and DP personnel alike.

## Application to Financial Services Sector

## Effectiveness Measures

Overall information systems effectiveness was examined as follows for the 21 responding companies:

\- the single response to the question in part E of the questionnaire—how would you rate your organization's overall CBIS: complete failure . . . very successful.

\- the composite success measure, being the arithmetic mean of performance ratings for all items and all respondents in each company, as described in the previous section.

The two measures are shown in Table 3 where it can be seen that they correlate very closely. $^{2}$

## Grouping of Firms

For subsequent analysis, it was decided to split the 21 firms into groups. To avoid prior assumption of any ranking by the effectiveness measures and to arrive at a reasonable number of groups, a nodal clustering technique known as the "K-means" method (6) was applied to the full set of performance items. Two, three, four and five node passes through the data were conducted and the groups were then placed in order on the basis of the composite success measure (see Table 3).

There is a clear association between the success measures and clustering of groups. For further analysis the 21 firms were assigned to the four groups as shown. (The apparently erratic behavior of the clustering algorithm can be seen in the figure, but the outcome is acceptable for the purposes of this study.)

## Testing of Primary Hypothesis

After examining over a thousand user and DP managers in 19 very large U.S. firms, Alloway and Quillard [1] concluded that overall information systems effectiveness was relatively poor in the sample they studied. Further, they concluded that the reason for this generally poor performance was not to be found in disagreements between IS and users regarding performance or importance of particular items, but rather that “neither group of managers sees any relationship between the two critical components of success, the importance and the performance of a particular criterion.” In view of the strategic and planning implications of this conclusion and possible differences over time, place and industrial sector, this study set out to test the above hypothesis.

Figure 1 shows scatter diagrams for the items studied, comparing perceived performance and importance separately for the four identified groups of companies. Item 8 (IS department profitability) is omitted from this and subsequent analysis because many respondents noted “not applicable” on the questionnaire. Cluster analysis on the 38 items associated this item closely with item 34 (overall cost-effectiveness), so excluding item 8 eliminates very little information. It will be noted that two items (volume of output information and development of more monitor systems) are consistently rated lowest in importance and appear to be out of line. Spearman’s r was calculated for the four full data sets as well as for the sets excluding items 4 and 35. Table 4 shows these correlations.

All coefficients for the first three groups are statistically significant at the 3% level or better, while neither of the coefficients for group four is. The downward tendency from group one through group four is noticeable, as is the improvement in correlation through exclusion of items 4 and 35.

To further test the primary hypothesis, the arithmetic averages of each respondent's ratings on performance and importance were calculated and grouped according to the seven independent factors derived earlier. The same underlying constructs are thus used to arrive at reduced sets of performance and importance data. The values are shown in Table 5, aggregated by company group and for clarity of subsequent interpretation, are listed in order from highest to lowest performance in each group. (The ringed numbers in Figure 1 are the aggregated values for these seven factors.)

As a final step in data analysis, discriminant analysis was applied to the values in Table 5 in order to identify the linear function of performance or importance factors that discriminated most strongly between the four effectiveness groups. As had been found previously in this study, performance ratings yielded clearer results than importance. While statistically significant functions discriminating on importance ratings were found, eigenvalues were low and most constituent factors were fairly evenly represented.

## Discussion

Based on a simple semantic differential scale, the 21 firms in this sample rate themselves roughly over the range “poor” to “good.” It appears that an average of ratings of perceived performance is adequate as a measure of information systems success, yielding results comparable to the more elaborate equation due to Alloway and Quillard. (In theory a weighting of performance by importance is appealing; in practice the many items and rather narrow range of importance ratings appears to cancel out any material differences).

For analytical purposes it has been convenient to classify the sample firms in groups and, using accepted clustering techniques, four groups were identified. These groups arrange themselves clearly in terms of the composite success measure and concomitants of information systems success can thus be studied in relation to these groups.

The primary hypothesis, that overall IS success is correlated with the relationship between perceived performance and importance has been proven in this study, certainly for the financial services sector in South Africa. There is a good correlation between these elements for the most successful firms, and this correlation steadily deteriorates until it becomes insignificant for the least successful firms.

The secondary hypothesis is also proven, namely that there is a mismatch between perceived importance and performance in developing more of the different basic systems types. Given the perceived importance of having more analysis and enquiry systems, on average all the firms in the sample have performed better in producing traditional transaction-processing and reporting systems.

By aggregating the data in this study in terms of the seven underlying and independent dimensions of information systems activity derived from the field questionnaire, it has also been possible to identify some areas where good performance is more important for overall success. While Table 5 shows that the better firms do better in all areas, their perceived success in the conventional systems area and reliability of service appears to be more important as a discriminator. It is also noteworthy that all firms rate their performance in the areas of conventional systems, reliability of service, and IS staff quality more highly than in the “newer” and more management-orientated areas of strategic management, user involvement, responsiveness to new systems needs and end user computing.

Finally, it should be noted that the numerical ratings for importance are consistently higher than those for performance in this study (see Table 5). While this may be due to differences in the semantic scales, the possibility that this reflects a general feeling that performance does not measure up to importance should not be overlooked.

## Conclusions and Recommendations

This article has addressed two issues—the development and testing of a modified instrument for measuring information systems effectiveness in organizations, and the application of this instrument to test certain hypotheses regarding systems effectiveness in the financial services sector in South Africa.

Following on previous research in the field, the modified instrument presented here possesses an underlying set of independent dimensions that is intuitively most appealing. For instance, the seven factors map well onto the four critical success factors for the information systems function determined by Rockart [8]:

![](/api/attachments/WQ2U9TWX/fulltext/images/359eec766d6e2f3ca26390d3e1aeca7838a09b1ab2d64c4a5d8e850f99e43ba7.jpg)

Considering the totally different research methodologies and sectoral and international differences, this relationship strengthens the validity of the basic constructs.

With specific reference to the financial services sector studied, important confirmation has been obtained that IS effectiveness here is, at least in part, a function of the relationship between perceived importance and performance on individual information systems attributes. It may be concluded that those companies that concentrate their resources in the most important areas will achieve greater success than those that spread their resources too thinly, focusing on areas of historical success only, or worse yet, losing sight of the need to plan and deteriorating into day-to-day firefighting and crisis management.

Of particular importance to those firms enjoying less success in this sector is the needed emphasis on quality of conventional systems (as opposed to numbers of systems) and reliability of service. This finding is consistent with the growth stage of the financial services sector in this country. Banks are making a major transition to automatic banking, building societies are competing vigorously on the same ground, and the life assurers are vying with each other to improve service and offer maximum flexibility to the individual client. Corporate success will depend in large measure on excellent and reliable transaction processing systems.

It is to be expected that the many differences between industries, companies, growth in information systems, and so on will all dictate the very specific variables influencing information systems success in the individual company. On the basis of this article's findings, however, a management strategy for assessing and improving IS effectiveness can be recommended.

In the specific case of a financial services firm in this country, the instrument can be applied to locate the firm in its particular “success” group. Performance in each of the factor areas can then be assessed and special attention paid to those factors where performance is most poor relative to group 1 results. For firms in general, the following approach can be adopted:

1. Assess the level of information systems performance using the given instrument.

2. Derive performance and importance ratings for each of the underlying factors determined in this article.

3. Correlate performance with importance ratings.

4. If necessary, refocus information systems efforts in line with the findings, establishing specific priorities accordingly.

5. Review the effectiveness of the IS function on an on-going basis, informing both DP and users of shifts in perceptions.

Clearly much research needs to be done to identify all the many possible concomitants of IS effectiveness in the modern firm. $^{3}$ This work, and the studies upon which it was based, give encouragement that the problem is tractable. IS and user management can adopt survey instruments such as that offered here to obtain a quick and reliable assessment of their IS function in order to focus their efforts in the most crucial areas for success.

## PART B. Future Needs for Information Systems

Monitor, Exception, Inquiry and Analysis Systems are described as follows:

Monitor: The system monitors daily detail activity producing standard reports on a FIXED SCHEDULE (daily, weekly or monthly) for example, daily customer transactions.

Exception: The system processes daily detail but produces EXCEPTION reports where the definition of EXCEPTION CONDITIONS IS FIXED, for example, listing of overdraft violations.

Inquiry: The system provides a database with FLEXIBLE inquiry capability, enabling MANAGERS to design and change their own monitoring and exception reports.

Analysis: The system provides powerful DATA ANALYSIS capabilities (modeling, simulation, optimization or statistical routines) and the appropriate database to support MANAGERIAL decision-making.

## PART D. Organizational Performance in Developing New Systems

Monitor, Exception, Inquiry and Analysis Systems are described as follows:

Monitor: The system monitors daily detail activity producing standard reports on a FIXED SCHEDULE (daily, weekly or monthly) for example, daily customer transactions.

Exception: The system processes daily detail but produces EXCEPTION reports where the definition of EXCEPTION CONDITIONS IS FIXED, for example, listing of overdraft violations.

Inquiry: The system provides a database with FLEXIBLE inquiry capability, enabling MANAGERS to design and change their own monitoring and exception reports.

Analysis: The system provides powerful DATA ANALYSIS capabilities (modeling, simulation, optimization or statistical routines) and the appropriate database to support MANAGERIAL decision-making.
