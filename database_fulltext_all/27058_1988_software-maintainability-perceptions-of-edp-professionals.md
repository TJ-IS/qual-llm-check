# Software Maintainability: Perceptions of EDP Professionals

By: Chai Kim

Stu Westin

College of Business Administration

## Abstract

Software maintenance continues to be one of the most complex problems facing much of the software industry. This study examines EDP professionals' perceptions of what makes software maintainable. Perceptions were assessed through a series of factor analyses on the data collected from a survey of 149 professionals from 20 organizations of varying sizes. The respondents were asked to rate the relative importance of various features which contribute to the ease of maintenance. Results showed that EDP professionals do not share a common perception of software maintainability: software project managers have different perceptions of what contributes to the ease of maintenance than those who are directly involved in maintenance operations. If those who manage and those who actually maintain software do not, in fact, agree on the importance of maintenance factors, then the maintenance problem is much more complex than EDP professionals or software engineers might have originally thought. Perhaps this is the reason why, according to the survey, EDP professionals are less than optimistic in general about the ability of state-of-the-art approaches to solve the problems of software maintenance.

Keywords: Software maintenance, software maintainability, project management

ACM Categories: K.6, K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

More than a decade into the so-called “structured revolution,” many organizations still spend 60% to 70% of total software dollars for software maintenance. General Services Administration’s government-wide estimate of software maintenance cost is \$1.3 billion per year (General Accounting Office, 1981; Martin and McClure, 1983), despite the fact that more than 200 software tools are available to help increase the productivity of the development and maintenance activities (Hanson and Rosinski, 1985).

The problem of maintenance is exacerbated by the already large inventory of software systems and the ever-growing backlog of software development requests. No doubt, the demand for software maintenance is expected to continue increasing. To offset this increase, not only must new systems be designed with ease of maintenance in mind, the productivity of maintaining existing software systems must also be dramatically increased. This raises two important questions that will be addressed in this study: What are the factors that programmers view as important in maintaining software? What are the software features that software managers view as important in increasing overall productivity of maintenance operations?

For purposes of this study, software maintenance is defined as the activities performed on software after the programs have been installed. These activities are often categorized into three types: (1) corrective maintenance — performed to identify and correct defects in software; (2) adaptive maintenance — performed to adapt software to changes in software requirements; and (3) perfective maintenance — performed to enhance software performance, cost effectiveness, or processing efficiency (Swanson, 1976). It should be noted that maintenance often involves reiteration of requirements analysis, design, coding, and testing.

It would seem that certain software and environment features can be emphasized to create an effective environment for maintenance operations, although there is no reason to believe that they will be common for different software organizations. It is the authors' belief that such features will prove insignificant unless the maintainers and the managers agree that they are important in enhancing the effectiveness of the maintenance work.

What then are the perceptions of software maintainability held by EDP professionals? Most of them focus a great deal of their efforts towards problems related to software maintenance, yet few of them have been formally trained for this task. Therefore, they might have different perceptions of what software maintenance is all about. What are the factors that contribute to software maintainability? Do perceptions differ among EDP professionals? These are important questions to practitioners and researchers of software maintenance. To address these questions, this study investigated (1) the nature of the perceptions of software maintainability, (2) the extent to which those who manage and those who are managed agree on software maintainability factors, and (3) the degree to which professionals in the industry are optimistic about their ability to deal with software maintenance. This study hopefully provides valuable insights to both researchers and practitioners who deal with the problems of software maintenance.

## Previous Studies

Due to the magnitude of software maintenance problems, maintenance is pivotal in increasing overall software productivity. To decrease the efforts focused on maintenance is to increase software productivity; they are largely the same issue. The features that seem to have potential for increasing maintainability of software, and thus productivity, have been investigated from various perspectives.

Arthur (1983) classified these features into methodology, technology, people, organization (management), and external variables. Doke (1985) classified the features into management, organizational structure, methodology, software, and hardware. Features such as performance evaluation system and training program are listed under “management.” End-user programming, programming assistant, independent test group, etc., are categorized under “organizational structure.” Features including prototyping, structured design and analysis, and structured programming are under “methodology.” Such features as database management system, data dictionary, and report generator are listed under “software.” Finally, online developmental environment, and similar features are classified under “hardware.”

Arnold (1984) organized the works reported at a conference on software maintenance under tools for software maintenance, software maintenance and testing practices, management of software maintenance, understanding and documenting software, and maintenance in a database environment.

The National Bureau of Standards (1984) classified the features for controlling and improving software maintenance in its Guideline on Software Maintenance under three general categories: controlling software maintenance, improving software maintenance, and managing software maintainers. Factors such as rotating staff assignment and keeping the staff well trained, etc., are listed under “managing software maintainers.” Coding conventions, structured and modular programming, documentation, etc., are listed under the heading “improving software maintenance,” and finally, the factors such as establishing a maintenance policy and enforcing documentation and coding standards are categorized under “controlling software maintenance.”

Research on programmer productivity, both in software development and in software maintenance, has focused largely on productivity metrics and tools. Specific objectives of such research have been to find ways of designing a programming environment that is conducive to enhanced productivity (Arnold, 1984; Glass and Noiseux, 1981; Jones, 1981; Martin and Osborne, 1983; Girish, 1981). Many researchers have emphasized the importance of features which relate mainly to the way code is developed or maintained. They attempt to isolate the specific methods or techniques of coding and testing that would significantly improve productivity (Girish, 1984).

Some researchers put more emphasis on the activities which precede the coding phase of the software lifecycle such as requirement analysis and software design (Munson, 1978). They are concerned primarily with how to build ease of maintenance from the start rather than attempting to add it later during the coding stage.

Others have concentrated on the importance of using very high level languages (Harel and McLean, 1982; Martin and McClure, 1983). Still others have concentrated on the importance of using certain software tools and techniques such as data dictionaries, reusable code, computer-aided software design techniques, and the programmer workbench (Hanson and Rosinski, 1985; Paul, 1981). Overton, et al. (1971), concentrated on the problems faced by programmers who must maintain programs written by someone else. Lientz and Swanson (1980) provided the results of a survey on maintenance practices and management attitudes.

There seems to be no consensus across studies on the meaning of software maintenance. The authors could discover no research on investigating the nature of the complexity of doing software maintenance. Consequently, the research on software maintenance has not been reported within a common framework, much less under a common theory.

There is widespread agreement that software maintenance is a key to software productivity. This study attempts to provide a real world meaning of the maintenance problem by uncovering perceptions of the problem held by practitioners of the industry.

## The Survey

Data for the analysis was gathered through the field survey method. The seven page questionnaire (see Appendix A) was developed and tested by the authors specifically for the purpose of this study. A small pilot study $n=9$ followed by a debriefing session, was used to pretest the instrument and to identify any ambiguities or faults in the method. Subjects were assured anonymity.

## Subjects

Subjects were EDP professionals working in various firms throughout the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic regions of the United States. A total of 149 responses from 20 different organizations were analyzed. These firms were sampled from a regional mailing list compiled from the membership list of a major association of data processing professionals. A systematic cluster sample was used in which organizations were the primary sampling units.

The organizations varied in size and industry, and included both government and private sector firms. Examples are software houses, major banks and insurance companies, large defense contractors, military EDP organizations, consulting firms, and others. The size of the programming staff in the firms ranged from 14 to 875.

The overall response rate, following a second request, was 61%. In addition to the strong response there were indications of a high degree of interest in the topic. A surprising number of respondents — more than half — requested results of the research.

Managers, analysts, and programmers each accounted for about 20% of the sample. The remaining 40% were programmer / analysts. Respondents had an average of 4.6 years of formal, post high school education. Nearly 60% had attended continuing education programs in software engineering or related fields within the past five years.

In terms of age, 45% of the sample were 30 or younger. Approximately 75% were 40 or younger, and 93% were 50 or younger. About 56% of the sample had less than ten years of experience. More than 25% had greater than fifteen years of experience. Average tenure in the EDP field for all respondents was about ten years.

COBOL was by far the most commonly used language for projects involving respondents. Nearly 70% of the respondents indicated that they presently work, at least partially, in a COBOL environment. FORTRAN (22%) and BASIC (18%) were the next most commonly used languages. More than 14% of the sample indicated that their present projects involved Pascal or Ada. A substantial number of respondents cited the use of very high level languages such as NOMAD, RAMIS, and FOCUS. Several lower level languages such as BAL, Assembler, and C were also cited.

## Measurement

The survey instrument comprises two major sections. The first, and larger, section focuses on measuring the perceived importance of various features of software and the perceived importance of the EDP environment for improving the maintainability of software. The second section deals with demographic data.

## Measuring Perceived Importance

The major portion of the questionnaire contains twenty features of software or of the EDP environment. A concise operational definition for each feature is also provided. The cover letter stated that the features “may contribute, with varying degrees, to the ease of maintenance, or the maintainability of software.” Each item and definition is accompanied by a 1-to-7 scale relating to the importance of the item in increasing maintainability (see Appendix A). Subjects were asked to indicate the importance of the item by circling a digit on the scale.

The twenty features were taken from Guideline on Software Maintenance of the National Bureau of Standards (1984). The placement order of the items on the instrument was random. Table 1 displays the twenty items.

## Analysis

Analysis of the maintainability features involved a two-level factor analysis. In the situation described here, the phenomenon under study deals with software maintainability — specifically, the way in which maintainability is perceived by EDP professionals. The first order factor analysis therefore uncovered latent perceived dimensions of software maintainability.

The second order factor analysis uncovered patterns in the latent perceived factors, thereby providing an even more parsimonious description of maintainability. At this level of abstraction only three underlying constructs were involved. Factor scores on these high level constructs were the basis of further analysis.

An interpretation of the factor analyses is provided in the next section. The initial factoring is presented first, followed by the second order factor analysis. Factors are numbered and discussed in order of importance as defined by the percent of variance attributed to the factor. Factor loading values, shown in parentheses, indicate the degree of involvement of an item in the factor. A more rigorous description of the method of analysis is provided in Appendix B.

## First order dimensions of maintainability

The initial factor analysis uncovered seven latent dimensions of software maintainability as perceived by EDP practitioners. These dimensions are described in the following sections and results are summarized in Tables B1 through B3 in Appendix B.

## Factor 1: Maintaining Control and Standards During Coding

Factor 1 (17% of the extracted variance) appears related to the enforcement of standards while developing software (i.e., the coding phase of the life cycle). The highest loading feature is "Use of External Design Documentation" (.79), which forces software to comply with precoding specifications. The next feature, "Use of Standard Data Definitions" (.72), forces control over the data items and data structures of the project. The next two items force control on the code itself. "Coding Conventions" (.55) help to maintain consistency within and across software systems. "Code Audit Procedures" (.55) are intended to trap violations of coding conventions. The final significant loading is "Avoiding the Use of Compiler Extensions" (.52). This also deals with maintaining software control and standards.

It is interesting to note that “Use of Code Review Techniques,” which does not load significantly on any factor, does have a reasonably strong loading (.36) on this dimension. Code reviews are somewhat related to coding standards, but their major focus, as specified in the operational definition, is on software quality and correctness. The moderate positive loading does, therefore, make sense.

Another interesting aspect of the first dimension is the moderate negative loading (-.37) of "Use of Modular Software Design." A negative loading on an item denotes, in this context, that the software feature somehow impedes the dimension of maintainability which is captured in the factor. The authors have long recognized the importance of the software module concept, but have also questioned whether the concept can be defined so precisely that it makes the specification of modules in a system unambiguous. What is a module to one programmer/analyst/designer may not be a module to another. Indeed, it is this very problem that has been such a hindrance to the successful implementation of the reusable code concept. It is likely that this negative loading reflects the contention between designers and coders on how the system should be ideally partitioned.

## Factor 2: Methods for Understanding Code During Maintenance

All high loading features in the second factor (15% of the extracted variance) aid the maintainer, either directly or indirectly, in assimilating the software. The highest loading is “Use of Machine Generated Aids” (.82). As cited in the operational definition, these aids include such items as cross-reference maps, load maps, and variable tables. The next highest loading is “Use of In-Source Comments” (.66). These items are employed by the maintainer in his or her attempt to comprehend the workings of existing software.

The remaining high loading is “Use of the Method of Rotating Personnel” (.63). This method allows the people who maintain software to experience the process of designing and coding software. Likewise, it allows those who design and develop software to experience the maintenance process. In terms of software maintainability the aim is twofold. First, the maintainer has a better understanding of how software systems are designed and coded and thus a better comprehension of the software itself. Second, software is (hopefully) created with maintenance in mind, i.e., easier for the maintainer to comprehend.

"Use of Code Review Techniques" also loads moderately on this factor (.38). These techniques that are aimed at improving the quality of software involve persons other than the author of the code. The result is the elimination of ambiguities and excessive complexity in the software.

## Factor 3: Coding Style

Each high loading feature of Factor 3 (13% of the extracted variance) deals with the shape or character of the software itself. The first two features are “Use of Modular Software Design” (.83) and “Use of Structured Programming” (.64). The fact that these two items cluster might be an indication that modular software is viewed as a coding issue rather than a design and specification issue (recall the negative loading of the former item on Factor 1). The final high loading is “Use of Standard Library Functions/Routines” (.49). Considering the three items together shows a common thread of emphasis on hierarchically organized modules. Structured programming calls for top-down organization of code. Modular design stresses hierarchically organized cohesive functions with low coupling. Library routines are cohesive functions — software blackboxes — which are invoked from above.

Although it is not extreme, the negative loading of "Use of External Design Documentation" (-.26) might be a further indication of the previously mentioned contention between designers and coders. Recall that the item on modular design loaded negatively on the “Control and Standards” dimension of maintainability. This is viewed in the same vein as the negative loading of “External Design Documentation” on the “Coding Style” dimension. Perhaps this documentation is viewed as a hindrance to the creative abilities of the programmer.

## Factor 4: Human Relations Management

Factor 4 (13% of the extracted variance) relates to some of the sociological aspects of the maintenance process. The single most important feature is “Presence of Open Communication Among Users, Managers, Analysts and Programmers” (.81). The remaining items are “Use of the Chief Programmer Team Approach” (.56), and “Use of Formal Testing Procedures” (.51). All three items deal with teamwork and interaction among involved parties. The latter two items relate closely to the concept of “egoless programming,” as described in the National Bureau of Standards (1984). The item “Use of Rotating Personnel” loads moderately (.35) on the factor supporting the interpretation of the dimension.

## Factor 5: Controlling Quality and Clarity of Code During Maintenance

Factor 5 (11% of the extracted variance) deals with control of software after the initial coding. "Use of Formal Software Change Procedures" has a large loading of .85 on the factor. The second and final qualifying item is "Use of Straightforward Approach to Coding" (.59). Formal change procedures affect control of software. However, unlike the other control features — those captured in Factor 1 — the procedures deal with existing code which is being maintained.

Change procedures fight the proliferation of incongruous, poorly designed, undocumented, "quick fix" modifications of the system. The end result of employing the procedures is that the quality and clarity of the software is sustained in the maintenance phase of the life cycle. The "Straightforward Approach" item also relates to the clarity or understandability of software.

If the interpretation of the factor is correct, then it should be related in some way to Factor 2, "Methods for Understanding Code During Maintenance." Inspection of the inter-factor correlation matrix reveals that this is indeed the case (see Appendix B). In fact, these two factors hold the strongest coefficient that exists between any factor pair. In the authors' judgement this provides further evidence that the interpretation of this factor is correct.

## Factor 6: Interaction Between Authors and Maintainers

Factor 6 (10% of the extracted variance) relates to the importance of communication between the maintainer of software and the original creator of the code being maintained. A single feature loads highly on the factor, “Availability of Original Author(s) or Previous Maintainer(s) of the Software” (.88). The operational definition of this item states that the original authors are available for consultation concerning the present maintenance activity.

Two positive, moderate loadings lend credence to the interpretation of the factor. The first of these items is “Use of In-Source Comments” (.26). These comments are a form of communication between the author and the maintainer of software. In fact, of all the forms of documentation that are spawned during the life cycle, in-source comments deal exclusively with these two parties — original coders and present maintainers. The remaining positive moderate loading (.25) relates to the “Open Communications” feature.

Although their absolute values are not excessive, several items that load negatively on this item might be an indication that the use of formal policies and procedures somehow inhibit the free interaction between authors and maintainers. These items are “Adherence to Formal Testing Procedures” (−.36), “Adherence to Code Audit Procedures” (−.25), and “Use of Machine Generated Aids” (−.22). Several other items dealing with formality and structure also have negative, albeit less substantial, loadings. One explanation for this might be that as the degree of formality exercised in the process increases, the acceptability of open, informal interaction between technical personnel diminishes.

## Factor 7: Reliance on the Machine

The final factor (9% of the extracted variance) deals with trading off tasks from man to machine; this trade-off can take place at various points in the life cycle.

The highest loading item is “Use of the Highest Level Programming Language” (.87). When the level of language used is higher, detail work handled by the machine is greater. The use of a higher level language can thus rely more on the system and less on the human programmer to complete the application.

The second most important items tend to validate this interpretation, though their loadings are not extreme. “Use of Machine Generated Aids” (.33) relates to the reliance on the machine (rather than on the human) in tracing and cataloging the contents of the code. “Use of Standard Library Functions / Routines” (.29) deals with the coder’s reliance on the system to provide prepackaged chunks of code to perform functions in the application.

This completes the discussion on the seven extracted factors. Higher order factors will be discussed in the next section.

## Higher order dimensions of maintainability

The second order factor analysis revealed three higher order perceived dimensions. In essence, these three factors provide a more abstract, less detailed model of software maintainability. It should be noted that, in terms of comprehensiveness of the factors, the three factors are about equal since each accounts for approximately one third of the extracted variance. Tables B4 through B6 in Appendix B provide synopses of the results.

## Factor 1: Understandability of Code

Two first order factors load strongly on Higher Order Fact 1 (34% of the extracted variance). These are “Methods for Understanding Code During Maintenance” (.81) and “Controlling Quality and Clarity of Code During Maintenance” (.77). Recall that the former dimension relates to those techniques, both managerial and technical, that aid the technician in the maintenance activity; the latter dimension deals with formalizing maintenance in an attempt to sustain the clarity of code.

The dimension “Reliance on the Machine” has a moderate negative loading (−.37) on this higher order dimension. This negative coefficient could be an indication that hardware/software techniques aimed at simplifying the maintenance process are not perceived as improving the situation. Instead, it is the human coder that is the key.

## Factor 2: Design of Code

The second higher order factor (33% of the extracted variance) has two high loadings. These are “Coding Style” (.83) and “Maintaining Control and Standards During Coding” (.75). The first component, style, relates to the morphology of software (e.g., structuredness, modularity). The other component deals with the degree to which formalized standards and conventions are followed in creating the software (e.g., use of external design documentation, standard data definitions, code audits).

Note that Higher Order Factor 2 seems related exclusively to the pre-implementation phases of the life cycle. Specifically, it relates to the design and creation of software. Higher Order Factor 1, on the other hand, relates to post-implementation phases of the life cycle — that period in which software is maintained. The authors do not find the time-wise partitioning of maintenance issues surprising. Software implementation is the time when the “responsibility baton” is handed from one group of technical personnel to another; it shifts from development personnel to maintenance personnel.

## Factor 3: Shaping the Environment for Productivity

Higher Order Factor 3 (30% of the extracted variance) deals with the environment in which software is created and maintained. The factor has three loadings which are high in absolute value. One of these coefficients, in fact the strongest one, is negative — “Interaction Between Authors and Maintainers” (−.72). The two positive loadings are “Human Relations Management” (.70) and “Reliance on the Machine” (.48).

The “Human Relations Management” component deals with fostering open communications and teamwork between all involved parties (managers, users, analysts, programmers). The “Reliance on the Machine” component proposes recruiting the machine as a member of the team; that is, using higher level languages and system debugging aids to improve performance in software maintenance. The “Interaction” dimension, which loads negatively, considers teamwork quite differently, however. This component deals with two and only two key personnel — the development programmer (author) and the maintenance programmer (maintainer) — in explaining maintainability.

The polarity of these components suggests that those managerial approaches which formalize and stress group effort of all concerned parties are somehow inhibitive to the value of interaction between author and maintainer. One explanation is a teamwork approach to developing software, for example, a chief programmer team setting in which detailed program specifications are used, where the personality and style of any one author is absent from the code. Rather, the software combines the approaches of a number of contributors (analyst, programming team, code review team, etc.). No single individual is the sole creator. There is little doubt that consultation with the original “author” would be of less value in this case than it would be had a programmer/analyst solely designed and created the piece of software. Another explanation is that in some environmental settings interaction between author and maintainer is impractical.

The positive direction of this final higher order factor relates to sociological (managerial approaches) and technical (hardware/software) avenues that form an environment which fosters productivity. The factor was therefore named “Shaping the Environment for Productivity.”

## Perceptual differences

A relevant question at this point is whether there is agreement among professionals about the value of the various components of maintainability. Factor scores provide a means for investigation. In this particular study, a score on a given dimension is an indication of the perceived importance of that facet in contributing to the overall maintainability of software. Higher order scores were computed for each respondent for each higher order factor.

The scores on the first two higher order dimensions (Design, and Understandability) were added thereby creating a composite measure called “Software Features.” This score reflects the degree of importance awarded to the features of software by an individual and enables an analysis of maintainability from the perspective of two underlying dimensions: software and environment.

## Differences Across Jobs

An interesting point investigated in this study concerned whether consistency of perceptions differed across areas of responsibility. For example, do managers perceive the environment component as more important than do technical people? Four different areas of responsibility were considered: programming-development, programming-maintenance, systems analysis/design, and project management.

Table 2 shows the correlation coefficients and significance among the four areas of responsibility and the two high level components of software maintainability. The most striking finding presented in the table shows that EDP professionals tend to discount the value of those factors for which they are most responsible (critical significance = .05).

Software features scores of development programmers are significantly lower than those of other professionals. In other words, the development programmers in this study viewed characteristics of software as having less potential in solving the maintenance problems. The same can be said for the maintenance programmers. Persons who perform this task seem to have less faith in software features than do other professionals.

Project managers, on the other hand, appear to have less faith in the worth of the “Shaping the Environment” dimension. These professionals tend to discount the value of such things as human relations management and team effort in producing code.

## Pessimism Among EDP Professionals

In another phase of the analysis the scores on the three high level factors were added in order to generate a measure of general optimism regarding software maintenance. Higher scores on this measure indicate more faith in the various techniques and approaches to increasing software maintainability. This measure includes both technical and environmental factors. Correlation coefficients between this measure and various demographic and job related variables were calculated in order to determine if certain categories of individuals are more or less optimistic about the maintenance problem. Two statistically significant correlations were found; both were negative.

The maintenance programming activity correlated negatively with the general optimism measure (r = -.16, p = .05). This suggests that the maintenance programmers in this study tended to be less optimistic than other professionals. The measure also correlated negatively with the number of years the respondent had worked in the EDP field (r = -.18, p = .03).

## Summary of Findings

The major thrust of the study was to uncover and analyze the latent dimensions of software maintainability as perceived by EDP professionals. A summary of these latent dimensions is provided in Figure 1. The tree structure represents the way in which concepts (components) relate to higher level, more abstract concepts. The root of the tree, i.e., the most abstract concept, is software maintainability.

The analysis uncovered a multi-tiered latent structure of software maintainability in which lower level constructs form three general higher level construct clusters. One higher level construct deals with understandability of software, one with design of software, and the final one with the environment in which software is produced and maintained. According to the portion of variance captured by these high level constructs, they are about equally important in explaining maintainability (see Table B6). This structure may provide a general framework for future investigations into software maintainability.

Two of the higher order factors, “Design” and “Understandability,” can be tied to specific portions of the life cycle. As stated before, these factors relate to the pre-implementation and post-implementation life cycle phases. The other factor, “Environment,” most likely pervades the entire life of the application. It should be noted that this environment factor is not strictly a human relations dimension. One aspect of the factor, albeit the least significant of the three, deals with the technical environment in which the software is developed and maintained (e.g., language level, software tools).

Based on the analysis of the factor results, six specific findings can be enumerated. These are summarized below.

## Inseparability of tasks

In several instances the duties and responsibilities of the coder vis-a-vis those of the designer are raised as a point of possible contention. There is repeated evidence that modularity is seen as a coding rather than a design responsibility. In a similar vein, use of external design specifications is perceived as having a negative impact on coding style. Most likely, these findings are an indication that a clean separation of software design and software coding is either impossible or undesirable. Perhaps the EDP professionals believe that the finer syntactic details of the programming language must be considered when deciding how to ideally partition the system.

## Importance of environment

According to the perceptions of EDP professionals in this study, maintainability is not merely a technical issue to people who deal with the realities of the maintenance situation on a daily basis. Rather, both managers and technical practitioners view the environment as playing a role as important as any software factor.

## No common perception of maintainability

Building easily maintainable software and maintaining that software are obviously contingent on the well coordinated efforts of everyone involved (system analysts, programmers, and managers). Yet, according to the factor scores, these professionals do not seem to share a common perception of what the problems of software maintenance really are or how to enhance software maintainability (see Table 2).

## Less faith in software features by technical professionals

The results indicate that programmers and analysts have less faith in utilizing software features to increase software maintainability than do managers.

## Less faith in environment features by management professionals

The results indicate that managers have less faith in utilizing features of the environment to increase software maintainability than do the technical professionals.

## Pessimism among experienced EDP professionals

Professionals who are the most pessimistic about software maintenance are the most intimately involved in maintenance activity (namely maintenance programmers) and have the longest tenure in the EDP field.

## Discussion and Recommendations

## Discussion

What are the managerial implications of the six specific findings addressed in the previous section? First, the fact that EDP professionals perceive design and coding as being inseparable reemphasizes the iterative nature of the systems development life cycle phases. Movement from phase to phase does not occur in a strictly sequential fashion, and it is, perhaps, impossible to strictly define where one phase ends and another begins.

Second, the perceived importance of the environment suggests the need for a refocusing of the current research efforts. The major emphasis of literature on software maintenance to date has been largely technical in nature. Researchers have focused on the development and evaluation of tools and approaches assuming that these efforts will hold the key to improving the present maintenance situation. The result of these efforts has been a proliferation of tools and technical approaches. A finding of this study suggests that a more balanced approach to research in this area is desirable; both technical and nontechnical issues must be tackled.

Lack of consensus among the EDP professionals about what features contribute significantly to the maintainability of software could imply that these professionals do not correctly understand the real problem. If this situation continues, it will be difficult to foresee a solution in the near future.

The next findings seem to further amplify the inability to grasp the essence of the maintenance problem. The features under the subjects' direct control are not perceived by them as key factors of maintainability. Why do designers, coders, and maintainers see less value in software features (e.g., modularity, use of design documentation) than those who do not come in contact with software firsthand, namely, project managers? There is another question which is perhaps the other side of the same coin: Why do project managers see less value in managing those features that are within their direct responsibility?

If managers do not recognize the importance of such environmental features as human relations and team effort in developing and maintaining code, then who should?

The lack of faith in the very features over which managers have direct control could imply that they are attributing software maintenance problems to other professionals. Perhaps they believe that these features under their direct control have already been well handled by themselves and, since they have been well handled, they may feel the only important features that still need attention are those of other professionals. In this way, these two groups of professionals might be justifying the quality of their own work while attributing the present maintenance problems to someone other than themselves. A situation develops where managers blame the programmers/analysts and they in turn blame the managers.

On the other hand, if managers truly believe their work has little to do with software maintainability, then the implications are indeed serious. Why should they try to do a good job in carrying out their responsibilities in the first place if they feel their efforts have marginal value in increasing software maintainability?

Which is the case? The reason why, in this study, managers and those who are managed have a diametrically opposed view is difficult to determine. In any event, the view on software maintainability in this study is less than optimistic. In fact, another significant finding concerns pessimism and software maintainability.

The very people expected to have the best understanding of software maintenance — maintenance programmers and more experienced EDP professionals — are less optimistic about the ability to improve the maintenance situation than those who are relatively new to the profession. In our study, this attitude relates to features of software as well as to environment. This suggests that the ways in which available tools and techniques of maintenance are utilized require careful reexamination.

In conclusion, it seems clear that many implications of the findings relate to communication problems among professionals. Perhaps the real problem of maintenance concerns disagreements between those who perform maintenance and those who manage maintenance and lack of communication to uncover real problems of software maintenance. Until all EDP professionals operate based on a similar perception of the problems and potential solutions, all the software tools and techniques of designing, coding, testing, and maintaining will have little chance of being effective.

## Recommendations

What can be done to improve the situation? Different suggestions are offered to practitioners and researchers. The recommendation to practitioners involves improved communication. It seems imperative that those who manage and those who are managed begin talking to each other to establish a consensus on which factors contribute most effectively to software maintenance. It would seem naive, however, to expect that an optimum mix of factors aimed at improving maintainability would be uniform across all organizations. Therefore, they must strive to establish a consensus for their particular shop.

To researchers, it is recommended that research regarding software maintainability should emphasize environmental as well as technical factors. In the minds of the EDP professionals in this study, software maintainability clearly involves more than the technical issues. Further research must be undertaken involving both software managers and software maintainers before definitive conclusions can be drawn about the utility of various factors of software maintenance and thus, software productivity. Future research should be conducted to further understand the nature of communication problems between those who manage and those who are managed in the real world setting of software.

Weinberg (1971) has made significant contributions through research and writing regarding the psychology of programming. This study suggests the importance of sociology of EDP professionals.

# Appendix B Statistical Generation

This appendix details the methodology used to derive the latent perceptual dimensions of maintainability.

## Data adequacy

Kaiser's (1970) Measure of Sampling Adequacy (MSA) was used to verify that the set of variables under consideration, i.e., the rated features, were suitable for factoring. The set of 20 items provided a combined MSA score of .71 indicating that the variables are well suited for factor analysis. One item, “Use of a Single Programming Language,” fell below the minimum acceptable level of .5, however. This item was removed from further analysis thereby increasing the overall MSA score to .72. Eleven of the 149 responses had missing values which made them unsuitable for factoring.

## First order factor analysis

The unaltered correlation matrix of the 19 features was factored using the technique of principal components, one of the most popular approaches to the factor analysis of behavioral data (Green, 1978). Of the 19 components extracted, seven were retained under the criterion that the eigenvalue of the factor must exceed 1.0. The scree-test clearly validated the selection of seven components. These components account for slightly more than 62% of the variation in the original data.

The pattern solution was then rotated in the reduced space to an orientation which was more interpretable. An oblique rotational technique, promax, was employed since there is no a priori reason to believe that the latent perceptual dimensions of software maintainability should be uncorrelated with one another, although they may be.

## Inter-Factor Correlations

As described above, the previous phase of the analysis included an oblique rotation to the final factor solution. Such a rotation allows for (but does not force) the nonorthogonality of factor patterns. The inter-factor correlation matrix, i.e., the matrix containing the correlations between the seven factors described above is shown in Table B3. Several correlations are substantial; one approaches .4. Most of the coefficients are too large to be ignored.

The fact that the seven components are non-orthogonal shows that these latent dimensions of maintainability overlap in some way. One dimension cannot be studied without considering other dimensions simultaneously. In short, these aspects of maintainability are related to one another.

Higher order factor analysis is a means of dealing with this complexity (see Green, 1978). Just as factor analysis exposes latent patterns of variation in the original variables, higher order factor analysis can expose latent patterns of variation among the factors and in many cases provides a more parsimonious solution than is provided by ordinary factor analysis (Cattell, 1966).
