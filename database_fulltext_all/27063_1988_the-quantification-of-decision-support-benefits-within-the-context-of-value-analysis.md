## Abstract

Traditional cost-benefit approaches to evaluating the effectiveness of decision support systems are generally regarded as inadequate. More recent approaches account for the intangible benefits overlooked by traditional cost-benefit analysis, but still attempt to express benefits in terms of costs. This paper focuses on a value analysis. It advocates a methodology for (1) identifying significant intangible benefits for a specific decision support system, (2) quantifying these benefits in value terms, and (3) establishing a decision rule for identifying the significance of the proposed system. Finally, an alternative approach at deriving the benefit utilities is examined.

Keywords: Decision support benefits, value analysis, conjoint measurement, self-stated utility

ACM Categories: H.1.1, K.6.2

## Introduction

The influence of the new information technologies, particularly since the advent of the microcomputer, is increasingly being felt in the higher echelons of the management hierarchy (Benjamin, 1982). This upward penetration of the new information technologies such as decision support systems (DSS) raises issues which previously did not exist. One of the most significant issues identified involves the evaluation of the effectiveness of the information technologies on the decision-making processes (Ball and Harris, 1982; Dickson, et al., 1984; Keen, 1986; Meador, et al., 1984). The traditional cost-benefit approach, which seeks to reduce all variables in a DSS evaluation study to monetary terms, and other approaches that ignore the intangible benefits are considered no longer valid (Keen, 1981; Melone and Wharton, 1981; Oxenfeldt, 1979). While appeals have been made for a more rigorous evaluation methodology for DSS that would be consistent with the methodology used in the evaluation of any other investment proposal (Alavi, 1982; Boczany, 1983; Curley, 1984; Harrison, et al., 1983; Partow-Navid, 1987; Vaid-Raizada, 1983), this subject remains largely unresearched in the literature (Bennett, 1983; Hamilton and Chervany, 1981; Keen, 1981; Radford, 1978).

Initial research into user perceptions of information characteristics for systems improvement did not propose any formal evaluation model (Munro and Davis, 1977; Neumann and Segev, 1979). Papers which have subsequently addressed this issue more formally can be classified as versions of the scoring model (Kleijnen, 1980). Scoring models have as their basic premise the integration of economic data with the qualitative factors and intuitive judgements of management which have an effect upon a system's successful implementation and use. This integration has been attempted through the inclusion of the concept of subjective evaluations as applied to the intangible benefits (Boczany, 1983; Ghandforoush, 1982; Litecky, 1981; Vaid-Raizada, 1983). However, the general approach of these evaluation models that produce indices scores for alternative IS/DSS proposals involves the assignment of monetary values to the intangible benefits. Thus, these models are primarily cost oriented.

Support, however, appears to be growing for the value-based approach to DSS evaluation (Hurst, et al., 1983; Keen, 1981; Melone and Wharton, 1984). An important premise of the value analysis approach proposed by Keen (1981) is that the perceived benefits of a DSS are significant determinants in justifying a specific DSS. This requires a greater focus on value and the recognition that qualitative benefits are of central relevance to the evaluation of a specific DSS. Melone and Wharton (1984) relate value to the nonquantitative factors of the decision choice and indicate that management must determine and establish a consistent set of criteria for project evaluation. Hurst, et al. (1983) note that “it is the lack of perceived value of a DSS which generally keeps it from being built, not its cost” (p. 127). If the potential user(s) does not perceive the proposed DSS to have value, even if it produces all the “right” answers, the system should not be developed or acquired. Keen (1981) too believes that “the benefit of a DSS is the incentive for going ahead” (p. 11). Value analysis is considered applicable at each stage of the adaptive design process for a DSS (Keen and Gambino, 1983). Consequently, a more rigorous interpretation of the perceived benefits of a DSS, which are a measure of value of the proposed DSS, should greatly facilitate the decision on whether to proceed with the next stage of development or acquisition.

The primary objective of this paper is to present a structured evaluation methodology to DSS that:

a. emphasizes value rather than cost,

b. focuses on intangible benefits, and

c. is applicable to a single investment proposal.

The proposed evaluation methodology is intended to give substance to the value analysis framework, in particular the second stage (Keen, 1981). It does not focus on cost as a determinant in the evaluation process for two reasons. First, the initial development of a DSS takes place as the context of a research and development (R&D) project that is below the capital investment level, thus minimizing cost considerations (Keen, 1981). Second, available software for DSS development, particularly on microcomputer-based systems, is relatively inexpensive and unlikely to be a significant factor in any DSS evaluation decision (Keen and Wagner, 1979; Hurst, et al., 1983). The proposed evaluation methodology will also be developed and explained as it relates to a specific

DSS application in the area of compensation planning as described in Money, et al. (1987).

The secondary objective of this paper is to examine the results of two alternative methods of utility generation which form an integral part of the proposed evaluation methodology. The two approaches considered are (1) a direct, self-explicated method and (2) the conjoint measurement method.

## A Value-Based DSS Evaluation Methodology

The evaluation of a proposed DSS may be equated to new product evaluation studies in the marketing field (Cattin and Wittink, 1982). Much research in this field has been devoted to how consumers trade off multiple product attributes when making a purchase decision. $^{1}$ Conjoint measurement has emerged as one of the major tools for uncovering consumer perceptions (Cattin and Wittink, 1982; Holbrook, 1981; Haggerty, 1985). Conjoint measurement decomposes the more easily formulated, overall ranked judgements of the users concerning an object into utility scores for each object attribute (Green and DeSarbo, 1978). Research into the technical aspects of the technique has identified it as a fairly robust procedure for assessing an individual's preferences (Malhotra, 1982; Segal, 1982). While initial studies of the conjoint approach concentrated on consumer preferences, research has shown that consumer perceptions can be adequately accommodated through this technique (Green and DeSarbo, 1978). Further useful insight into technical aspects of the technique can be acquired from a number of texts (Jackson, 1983, chpt. 3).

Using an approach similar to these marketing studies, it is possible to develop a DSS evaluation methodology that uses utilities (Ahituv, 1986; Kleijnen, 1980, chpt. 4) derived from the conjoint measurement method to uncover DSS user perceptions. The resultant interval-scaled utility scores (Green and Rao, 1971; Green and Srinivasan, 1978) for the individual benefits may then be used in further analysis to establish the perceived value of the specific DSS under consideration.

The proposed evaluation methodology which draws on the above concepts can be presented in four phases:

Phase One: The identification of appropriate DSS benefits using the Delphi approach and a literature search.

Phase Two: The grouping of the individual DSS benefits into homogeneous categories.

Phase Three: The generation of utility scores for the intangible benefits through the application of conjoint measurement.

Phase Four: The use of the numeric utility scores to propose a decision rule for establishing value.

Each of these phases will be discussed and illustrated in the next section.

## Application to a Compensation Planning DSS

Data for the proposed methodology was gathered from three sources:

1. Thirty-six MBA (Manpower) $^{2}$ students from the Stellenbosch Graduate School of Business for the benefit identification process (Phase One);

2. Twenty-four separate MBA (Manpower) students from the Stellenbosch Graduate School of Business for the benefit grouping process (Phase Two);

3. Fifteen compensation planners who attended a workshop on a specific compensation planning DSS (COMPLAN) at the University of Cape Town for the benefits quantification process (Phase Three).

Since DSS benefits are considered to vary (Keen, 1981), it is necessary to identify those benefits that appear related to the compensation planning function. Two approaches were examined.

## Phase two: DSS benefit groupings

Product features are clusters of identifiable attributes which impact a user's perception. Such grouping exercises have been performed for, among others, IFPS benefits (Keen and Wagner, 1979), DSS classifications (Bennett, 1983), IS benefits (Miller and Doyle, 1987), and expert systems evaluation criteria (Liebowitz, 1986). Similarly, the intangible benefits for the compensation planning DSS are examined for any natural groupings which can identify their primary areas of impact on an organization.

Three potential areas of impact for DSS benefits were proposed by the Delphi subjects of Phase One. Each benefit is likely to be oriented to either:

a. the operational level,

b. the managerial level, or

c. the personal (individual user) level.

To validate these a priori groupings of product features for the compensation planning DSS, an independent group of 24 MBA (Manpower) students was asked to assign the benefits identified from Table 1 to each of the three proposed areas of impact. Cluster analysis using the BMDP2M package (BMDP Statistical Software, 1983) was applied to the respondents' ratings. ## Phase four: value-based inferences using benefit utilities

The decision facing managers in justifying the continued development of a specific DSS is whether the proposed system has value, i.e., whether the benefits are perceived as significant by the potential end users. This phase of the study provides substance to the benefit threshold concept in the value analysis model (Keen, 1981).

The evaluation decision approach involves the following three steps:

1. The DSS evaluators must select, from among the list of identified benefits, a set of critical benefits considered by them to represent value for the proposed DSS (referred to as the value benefit set).

2. A threshold level for the value benefit set is established using the null benefit set of utilities. Since the null benefit set of utilities is a function of the perceptions of the potential end users, the threshold level is not an absolute score.

3. Using the inferential statistical hypothesis testing procedures for dependent samples (Siegel, 1956; Wonnacott and Wonnacott, 1977), the value benefit set utilities are tested against the threshold level. The difference in utilities between the two benefit sets (value benefits and null benefits) is translated into a statistical measure (the t-statistic) which is examined for significance.

A decision rule would then be formulated as follows:

1. If the resultant t-value is statistically significant (i.e., p < 0.005), the hypothesis that significant value is perceived in the proposed DSS by the potential DSS users would be supported. Consequently, development or acquisition should proceed.

2. Conversely, if statistical significance is not established (i.e., p > 0.005), then development or acquisition should cease or, at the very least, a reappraisal of the proposed DSS project should be initiated (Keen, 1981).

The strength of the perceived value attached to the proposed DSS can also be gauged from the magnitude of the calculated t-statistic as measured by the p-value. A p-value that is below, but close to, 0.005 indicates a statistically significant, but weak, perceived DSS value. A p-value that is significantly below 0.005 implies a significant and strong perceived DSS value.

## Derivation of Benefit Utilities: An Alternative Approach Using Self-Stated Ratings

## Discussion

The utility scores used in the above illustration were derived indirectly through the decomposition of rankings of benefit combination using the conjoint measurement approach. To establish whether similar results of individual benefit utilities can be computed from a more direct measure, a self-stated utility generation approach is examined. A literature search conducted by Holbrook (1981) showed that each of the two approaches has been developed and advanced independent of the other, but that each is concerned with essentially the same objective of uncovering perceptions toward product attributes through evaluative judgements. Neslin (1981) established that “previous research has demonstrated that in practical applications, statistical and self-stated importances often do not agree” (p. 80). The purpose of Neslin’s research was to compare the predictive validity of statistical and self-stated procedures for linking features to perceptions. His findings “support the use of statistical rather than self-stated methods for linking features to perceptions” (p. 85). However, he declares that the use of self-stated procedures are desirable because “they are simple to analyze and are completely idiosyncratic.” He believes that the development of better self-stated procedures are a necessity.

This research examines a self-stated procedure (Goldberg, et al., 1984) as a useful measure of benefit utilities rather than conjoint-derived utilities.

The self-stated method is a compositional approach requiring weights and ratings to be assigned to individual benefits in order to arrive at a utility measure. Using the same benefit groupings as the conjoint approach, the 15 respondents from the COMPLAN workshop were required to assign weights to each benefit group that reflected the relative importance attached to each group. Thereafter, each benefit was considered in turn and a rating based on a Lickert scale was assigned. This rating measured the respondents' strength of perception of the value of the specific DSS benefit.

The model to construct a respondent's self-stated utility is given by:

$$
U (i, j) = W (i) ^ {*} u (i, j)
$$

where $U(i, j)$ is the self-stated utility for benefit j in benefit group i; $W(i)$ is the weight assigned to each benefit group i; $u(i, j)$ is the assigned Lickert scale rating for benefit j in benefit group i.

To compile the self-stated utility for a benefit, a respondent is requested to:

1. assign a weighting factor, W(i), which reflects the relative importance attached to each of the three benefit groups as noted in Table 2; and

2. rate each individual benefit on an “importance” scale.

## Results

The resultant utility values can be interpreted and used in the same manner as those generated through the conjoint approach. To establish the extent to which there is correspondence between the utilities generated from the two approaches, correlations are computed. On an individual-by-individual basis all respondents except one generated self-stated benefit utilities that correlate significantly with their conjoint derived counterparts.

When the responses of all subjects are compared on an aggregated basis, the correlation is 0.5851 ( $t_{calc} = 16.736$ , n = 540), which is statistically significant at p < 0.0005. This represents however, only a moderate association between the two approaches.

The OLS regression method can be used to predict conjoint-derived utilities from self-stated benefit utilities. For the aggregated responses, the predictive model is given by:

$$
\hat {Y} _ {l, k} = 0. 3 5 8 7 + 0. 3 1 8 7 X _ {l, k}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \hat {Y} _ {l, k} & = \text { predicted   conjoint - derived } \\ & \text { benefit   utility } \\ & \text { for   respondent   k,   and } \end{array}
$$

$$
X _ {l, k} = \text { self - stated   utility   l   for   respondent   k. }
$$

Neslin (1981) suggests that the predictive ability between the two utility generation approaches could possibly be improved through the development and testing of clustering schemes among individual respondents.

Using data from the 15 COMPLAN respondents, a cluster analysis was performed using the ranked values of the benefit combinations to establish whether any natural groupings of respondents existed in which the within-group variation of benefit utilities was small. ## Conclusion of the self-stated approach

Previous research has demonstrated that in practical applications, statistical and self-stated importances often do not agree (Neslin, 1981; Jaccard, et al., 1986). These conclusions appear not to be entirely substantiated from the above results. The two approaches tend to show a moderate to good correlation on average for individual respondents. On an aggregated basis using data from all respondents, the findings are less encouraging. However, there does appear to be a moderate improvement in aggregate prediction of the conjoint-derived values using the self-stated values when homogeneous clusters of respondents are formed. These findings suggest that greater convergence between the utility scores derived from the two approaches can be expected if respondents have similar perceptions toward the object under review — in this case a proposed DSS.

## Conclusion

This study has addressed both the problem of incorporating intangible benefits into the mainstream of DSS evaluation decision making and the lack of a systematic framework for DSS evaluation which focuses on value. The methodology proposed involves an identification and quantification of intangible benefits normally associated with DSS, and, consequently, the use of these derived utilities in a decision rule to establish value for the proposed DSS. Through the literature it has been established that a DSS that is perceived to have value by the potential users is more likely to be adopted and implemented than one for which no significant value is perceived. The approach is also applicable to the evaluation of a single investment proposal.

The technique used, namely conjoint measurement, is considered a robust procedure for converting subjective judgements about the relative importance of intangible benefits into numeric scores for further analysis.

The results of this study have implications for both the DSS researcher and practitioner. For the researcher, alternative forms of benefit utility generation should be investigated. While the conjoint approach has been used in this study, it has the potential to be cumbersome, with respect to data capture, as the number of benefit groupings and benefits within each grouping increase. The study did examine the appropriateness of using self-stated utilities as opposed to conjoint-derived utilities. Since the two approaches do not produce consistently high correlations, further research on suitable methods is needed before the two approaches can be used interchangeably. An alternative statistical approach that could be considered is the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) (Saaty, 1980). It is felt that further research on the convergence properties of different utility generation methods, similar to that conducted by Jaccard, et al. (1986) would contribute much to establishing the proposed approach as an integral part of DSS evaluation decision making.

For the practitioner, the structured nature of the methodology offers the opportunity for both consistency in DSS evaluation decision making an enhancement of the information base from which such decisions are made. It is envisaged that through repeated application of and reporting on the proposed evaluation methodology, a common pool of DSS benefits may emerge and their associated utilities may provide a database for the development of a theory of evaluation based on empirical observations.
