# Factors Affecting Information Satisfaction in the Context of the Small Business Environment

By: Ali Reza Montazemi
Assistant Professor of Information Systems
Faculty of Business
McMaster University
Hamilton, Ontario, L8S 4M4, Canada

## Abstract

This study reports an empirical investigation of some of the relationships that exist between organizational characteristics and end-user satisfaction associated with computer-based information systems (CBIS) in small businesses. Eight hypotheses were tested using data collected in a two-phase study of 83 small firms. The major findings show that end-user satisfaction is correlated with the number of systems analysts present within a firm, with the degree of analysis of information requirements, with the level of participation, and with end users' level of computer literacy. In addition, it was found that decentralization of the decision-making process tends to create a need for more effective CBIS. As a result, end-user satisfaction was found to be higher in firms that were less centralized. Furthermore, there was a strong correlation between the end users and data processing personnel's satisfaction with the CBIS environment.

Keywords: MIS satisfaction, organizational context, small business computer usage

ACM Categories: H.4, J.1, K.4.3, K.6

## Introduction

The success of information systems depends on interacting factors. The influence of these factors varies among different organizations and even among different units within an organization. The major questions raised, in relation to the adoption of a successful CBIS, are: "How is computer technology being absorbed within the organizations?" "What problems are being encountered in absorbing these technologies?" and "What are the possible strategies for providing a better fit between organizational needs and the available technologies?"

In search of an answer to the above questions, Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) identified ten organizational variables with direct or indirect influence on the success of CBIS. These variables are organizational size, organizational maturity, organizational structure, organizational time frame, the psychological climate in the organization toward CBIS, the extra organizational situation, organizational resources, rank and location of the responsible executive, and the steering committee. Interaction among these variables can produce a variety of situations that can affect the utilization of CBIS. It was found that organizational size had special importance because, as an uncontrollable variable, it had major impact on the requirements necessary for integration of different professional units within an organization, degree of formalization of organizational systems, resource availability, and lead time for planning and implementation of CBIS projects. In recognition of these contextual variables, Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) proposed that CBIS projects are less likely to succeed in smaller organizations than in large ones. But the question remains, “Is such a proposition binding?”

Lack of resources in small firms is always a major factor in influencing the effective utilization of CBIS. The real question is whether the effects of other contextual variables also depend on organizational size. Previous investigations indicate that there are features specific to the small business CBIS environment (DeLone, 1981, 1983; Raymond, 1985). However, there are inconsistencies among the findings of these studies. Thus, the purpose of this study is to further investigate not only the state of information systems, but also the characteristics of pertinent factors in the context of the small business environment. To achieve this, the analysis was performed in two phases. In phase one, the information systems environment of 83 small business firms was investigated by means of face-to-face interviews with 164 employees. Then, based on an analysis of the data obtained from these interviews and from the findings of previous studies, eight hypotheses concerning the impact of organizational context variables on end-user satisfaction were postulated. In phase two, these eight hypotheses were tested by means of a structured questionnaire.

## End-User Information Satisfaction in the Small Business Environment

## Previous investigations

The recognition of possible differences between “small” and “large” organizations (DeLone, 1981, 1983; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978, 1982; Raymond, 1985) provides the basis for generating several hypotheses that concern the success factors of consequence to CBIS in small businesses. DeLone (1983) grouped these factors into three categories: fixed, semi-fixed, and controllable. Fixed factors included the duration of CBIS operation and the composite CBIS experience of the small firm’s personnel. Semi-fixed factors included vendor knowledge of the small client’s business, personnel acceptance of CBIS systems, characteristics of the hardware and software, top management’s knowledge of computers, and the type of computer services used (i.e., on-site or off-site). Factors of influence in the short run were labelled as “controllable” and included top management’s involvement in computers, the level of external software support, the level of firm-sponsored CBIS training, the relative spending on software and CBIS personnel as opposed to hardware, the level of CBIS planning, and the use of computer controlling systems practiced.

DeLone (1983) postulated no less than 22 hypotheses concerning the effects of the cited thirteen factors on the operation of information systems. In his study, two questionnaires were used to collect information from 93 small businesses. This information was then used to test these hypotheses. Computer success was defined as a composite measure of a chief executive's use of computer-generated reports and the impact of the application of CBIS. Chief executive knowledge was computed as a composite measure of computer experience and formal training. All other success factors were based on single questionnaire items. The analysis of data collected from the 93 small firms indicated that a chief executive's knowledge of computers and the degree of his or her involvement in the CBIS was directly associated with the success of computer operations. While a chief executive's knowledge and his or her involvement were significantly interrelated, it was indicated that the computer use (albeit on-site or off-site) was directly associated with success. However, involvement of a chief executive was shown to be greater in businesses with on-site computers. When computer resources were located on-site, the small businesses had better control over the application of that resource in terms of business operations. Additionally, cross-classification analysis determined that employee training was higher in small businesses with on-site computers and that more planning was done in firms that developed most of their software in-house. Thus, low levels of control were associated with low acceptance, whereas high levels of control generated only moderate employee acceptance of CBIS.

Raymond (1985) postulated and tested seven hypotheses by using data collected from 464 small manufacturing firms. Two dependent variables used as surrogate measures of CBIS success were (1) user's information satisfaction and (2) level of system utilization. User information satisfaction was measured by a twenty-item instrument that utilized a seven-point semantic differential rating scale. This instrument was adapted from the questionnaire developed by Bailey and Pearson (1983). The level of system utilization was measured by having users rate two items on a seven-point scale: perceived frequency of CBIS usage and regularity of CBIS usage. These measures related to CBIS use in its entirety rather than to a specific application. The hypotheses in the Raymond study postulated that CBIS satisfaction and usage increase in a small business that:

1. has greater CBIS experience;

2. develops a greater percentage of their applications in-house;

3. operates applications in-house;

4. implements a greater number of administrative applications;

5. implements interactive applications;

6. has a high ranking CBIS function; and

7. is located in a less remote region.

The analysis of Raymond's data confirmed hypotheses 2 through 6; the result of his analysis was contrary to the first hypothesis, which showed a slight negative association between duration of CBIS experience and user satisfaction. In the case of hypothesis 7, no significant regional differences were found in terms of user satisfaction or system utilization.

## Post-analysis of results

Analysis of the results of the DeLone and Raymond studies reveals inconsistencies which are described below:

1. Raymond found a slight negative association between the number of years of CBIS experience and end-user satisfaction. He also contended that firms with greater CBIS experience tended to develop a greater proportion of their applications internally and to process their application externally. Conversely, DeLone found that the number of years of CBIS experience had no relationship with the success of CBIS, the type of computer operation (i.e., on-site or off-site), the percentage of application programs prepared externally, and employee acceptance of computers.

2. Raymond contended that end users were slightly more satisfied in firms that developed a greater proportion of their applications internally. In contrast, DeLone found that a decision to acquire application programs developed either internally or externally had no effect on computer success.

Such contradictions could be attributable to differences in (a) the sample population (both in terms of geography and timespan) and (b) the methodological issues involved. It should be noted that (a) is difficult to avoid in independent studies. In addition, significant variations such as those cited are not expected to occur within a timespan of one or two years when considered across a fairly similar population. In these two studies then, the reliability of the instruments used to measure the success factors of information systems is questionable. Studies show that because of the complexity of the task, there is weak agreement about the attributes of a successful information system (see, for example, Bailey and Pearson, 1983; DeLone, 1983; Gallagher, 1974; Ives, et al., 1983). In particular, the study of success factors by DeLone (1983) and Raymond (1985) utilize different constructs for collecting the required data, thereby making it difficult to compare results and to locate the possible cause(s) of the contradictions.

Finally, the goals of the DeLone and Raymond studies are directed toward classifying the small business information systems into a “success” or a “failure” category. In this context, the validity of the test can be characterized by its effectiveness in predicting success in a pertinent organizational environment. The typical statistic used to express “effectiveness” has been the correlation test score and the standing on some criterion indicator that serves to represent success. Correlation can add to the body of information about the construct validity and test, insofar as it confirms or fails to support the “theory” of what the test measures. But, at this juncture, the interest of this study centers on the pragmatic concern of identifying factors which can affect the end-user satisfaction.

## The Objectives of This Investigation

Basic goal

Success is complex and many-faceted (Ferratt and Short, 1986; Franz and Robey, 1984; Srinivasan, 1985; Zmud, 1979). Thus, to formulate any complete definition of the successful performance of an information system, much less to develop a measure that adequately represents it, is difficult. This is particularly true in smaller organizations where little is known about their information needs, opportunities, and problems. To this end, an information analysis of 83 small business firms was performed through face-to-face interviews with 164 employees. The purpose was to obtain insight into their means of assessment and adoption of information technology (Montazemi, 1987). The outcome of the study revealed areas that were at odds with the findings of other investigators. In addition, there seemed to be major factors that affected end-user information satisfaction that had been overlooked previously. Based on these findings, eight hypotheses were postulated and tested by means of a structured questionnaire. A brief description of the relevant information obtained from the interviews, which led to the postulation of the eight hypotheses, is presented in the next section.

## Information technology assessment and adoption

A semi-structured questionnaire was used to systematically collect information about the hardware, the policies governing the analysis, the design, the development, and the acquisition of application software. In addition, information was collected about the level of computer literacy of end users. The questionnaire was first tested and modified by 11 employees of the first four firms. Information from the remaining 79 firms was used in the subsequent analysis.

Only two firms did not have access to on-site computer hardware. The management personnel of all 79 organizations were actively involved in ongoing CBIS activities. The major reason for high level managerial participation was the need for tight control over computer-related costs, which were high when compared to other expenditures. Therefore, management participation in CBIS activities and the impacts of on-site computer hardware, identified previously as factors with a positive effect on end-user satisfaction (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982; DeLone, 1983; Raymond, 1985), were evident in almost all of the sample organizations. In addition, the possible effect of regional disparity (Raymond, 1985) among the firms in the sample was absent since the firms were selected from fairly limited geographical boundaries.

## Systems Analysis

Few organizations had access to a qualified systems analyst. The term “systems analyst,” as defined by Vitalari and Dickson (1983), refers to an individual who analyzes organizational requirements for information and who designs a CBIS to collect, store, and disseminate data in support of organizational goals. Among this sample, only 19 percent of the firms studied had access to at least one part-time systems analyst. As a result, inappropriate policies were adopted in assessing the required information systems technologies. For example, only nine percent of the firms engaged in a complete information requirements analysis, which included extensive information needs analysis, data flow analysis, and long range planning of the organizations' information requirements. There were six firms (eight percent of the sample) that carried out data flow analysis, whereas 42 percent of the firms analyzed the data requirements for each individual application, 19 percent copied systems used by similar organizations without any consideration of their own specific needs, and 22 percent of the firms simply automated their manual systems.

Even though access to a systems analyst did not automatically entail complete systems planning, there was correlation between the two (see Table 1). Therefore, one can argue that, although the presence of a systems analyst can induce a more favorable environment for the adoption of information technology, the nature and degree of assessing the information requirements of the firm has importance in its own right (Griese and Kurpicz, 1985; Huff and Munro, 1985; McFarlan, et al., 1983; Nolan, 1979; Vitalari, 1985). Two of the eight hypotheses in this article test the effect of these two factors on end-user satisfaction. They are:

H1: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the presence of a systems analyst(s) in the firm.

H2: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the level of information requirements analysis performed.

## End-User Involvement

End-user involvement in the systems development process, which is believed to have a positive effect on satisfaction with CBIS (Bruwer, 1984; Buss, 1983; Hirschheim, 1985; Mumford and Henshall, 1979; Rockart and Flannery, 1983), was measured during face-to-face interviews through a five-point Likert scale. Little end-user participation in the systems development process was apparent in the sampled organizations. Lack of specialized systems analysts and deficient information systems planning may have contributed to low end-user involvement. In fact, there was a strong correlation among end-user involvement in the systems development process, the level of information requirements analysis performed, and the number of systems analysts in the firm (see Table 1). ## End-User Computer Literacy

End users with computer experience were more at ease participating in CBIS activities. Also, analysis of the data indicated that the level of an end user's computer literacy correlated with his/her participation in the systems development process and with the level of information requirements analysis performed (see Table 1). It is therefore conceivable to assume that the level of an end user's computer literacy directly affects satisfaction with a CBIS (Benson, 1983; Bruwer, 1984; Hirschheim, 1985; Lee, 1986; Nelson and Cheney, 1987; Rockart and Flannery, 1983). Because there was a lack of detailed quantitative analysis in previous investigations, it was decided to test this phenomena more rigorously through application of the following hypothesis:

H4: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with his/her level of computer literacy.

## Online Application

A majority of the in-use applications were either batch systems such as payroll and inventory systems (37.40 percent) or merely systems oriented to extract particular data items from a database to answer simple queries (45.70 percent). However, Raymond contends that end-user satisfaction is positively associated with the implementation of interactive application systems.

Therefore, given the chance, end users should prefer to change the batch systems to an interactive mode. In this sample, end users indicated that 20 percent of the application systems used for simple queries would have served them better if modified to a “what-if” mode of operation that could be utilized for both inquiry and simple analysis. This finding was not sufficient to draw any conclusion about Raymond’s fifth hypothesis and thus was adopted for further testing. The fifth hypothesis states that:

H5: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the proportion of online application systems adopted by the firm.

## Application Systems

Lack of experienced information systems personnel and/or the backlog of required application systems created an environment in which most applications were either developed through general purpose (fourth generation) application generators such as Lotus 1-2-3 (41.70 percent), or were special purpose commercial software tailor-made to meet the specific needs of the organization (39.60 percent). Firms also indicated a desire to increase reliance on outside software houses in order to acquire special purpose software (55.90 percent), mainly at the expense of general purpose application generators (27.10 percent). The increase was mainly attributed to two factors: (1) the difficulty experienced by the organizations in developing complex application systems, and (2) the lack of integration among the different application generators in use.

The increasing attraction towards purchase of special purpose application software is contrary to Raymond's second hypothesis, which states that small businesses must develop a greater percentage of their applications in-house. The cause of this contradiction may be attributable to a lack of specialized data processing personnel and to the deficiency in end-user computer literacy apparent in this study's sample. Development of application systems, even through a user-friendly fourth generation language, requires basic computer literacy; end users in the sample lacked this in spite of the bias adopted in the selection of end users who were major users of the CBIS. Only 13 percent of the end users had ever directly utilized command or procedural languages for their own personal information needs. Twenty percent had utilized report generators and/or a limited set of commands from languages such as SQL, SAS, LOTUS 1-2-3, or dBASEIII. The remainder of the end users had either accessed computerized data through a menu-driven environment (47 percent) or had received required information through an intermediary such as a data processing analyst (20 percent). Based on this skewed distribution of end-user computer literacy and on the lack of specialized DP personnel, use of special purpose application software seemed the logical choice. Thus, if acquired with care, special purpose application software can be of great benefit. In fact, Martin and McClure (1983) pointed out that the share of this type of software is likely to grow from 4 percent of total computer expenditure in the 1970s to 10 percent in the 1980s. The question really is whether the interest in special purpose application software is due solely to its cost advantage or whether, as well, end-user satisfaction plays a role in their adoption. To understand more about end-user effect, a sixth hypothesis was postulated:

H6: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the proportion of special purpose software acquired by the firm.

## Duration of CBIS Operation

As was pointed out earlier, controversy surrounds the relationship between the duration of a company's CBIS operation and end-user satisfaction. This analysis shows no relationship between the duration of the company's CBIS operation and the number of systems analysts employed, the level of user participation, the user's level of computer literacy, and the number of application software used (or required). However, larger firms, as well as less centralized ones, tend to have CBIS for a longer period of time. Furthermore, the older organizations in the sample employed more computer programmers and, as a result, developed a greater proportion of their application programs internally. Thus, if Raymond's contentions were correct, CBIS satisfaction and usage should increase in small businesses where a greater percentage of their applications are developed internally, and therefore, end-users in the older organizations should be more satisfied with their information systems. However, as a result of the equivocal outcomes of previous studies and as a result of the findings of this investigation, it was decided to further test the effect of duration of CBIS operation on end-user satisfaction. Based on this, the following hypothesis states that:

H7: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the duration of the company's CBIS operation.

## Organizational Size

Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) posited that CBIS projects are less likely to succeed in small organizations than in large ones. Justification for this proposition was based on a number of organizational context variables. For example, it was argued that larger organizations tended to have a less centralized structure, a more favorable psychological climate toward CBIS, and more resources to allocate to CBIS. Although Raymond did not find any significant association between organizational size (i.e., number of employees) and end-user satisfaction or systems utilization, there seems to be some association between organizational context variables and CBIS environment (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Raymond, 1985). An analysis of data from phase one of this study substantiates some previous findings (see Table 2).

As noted from Table 2, larger populated organizations tend to spend more resources on CBIS by employing systems analysts and by performing a more comprehensive information requirements analysis. Also, larger organizations have exposure to CBIS for a longer period of time. However, the number of employees is not associated with either end-user participation, computer literacy, interface, or source of application. In contrast to the number of employees, the number of managers in the organization is associated with all research variables.

It has been found that, to adapt and innovate most effectively, organizations must delegate authority for decision making to professional managers and experts (Miller, 1983; Miller and Friesen, 1984). However, such an action increases the diversity of goals, values, and task orientation among managers (i.e., it increases the degree of organizational differentiation). Consequently, interdepartmental conflict escalates and a greater need for integration and liaison devices that foster the necessary collaboration among departments is created (King and Schrems, 1978; Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967; Milles, 1980; Miller and Toulouse, 1986; Mintzberg, 1979). Given that one measure for the degree of decentralization of decision making is the number of managers in a firm (Burns and Stalker, 1961; Miller, 1983; Miller and Friesen, 1984; Zaltman, et al., 1973), the relationship between the number of managers and other research variables indicates that the significance of CBIS is a device for integration (see Table

2). In response to a need for specialization and integration, less centralized organizations tend to make more use of specialized systems analysts, to perform a more thorough information requirements analysis, to encourage end-user participation in the development process, to make more use of special purpose software, and to have a greater number of online application systems. This seems to be congruent with the findings of Ein-Dor and Segev (1982), who postulated that decentralized organizations tend to have more integrated CBIS and more propensity to aid in producing and using new kinds of software. Considering the positive relationship between the decentralized decision environment and other pertinent research variables, it seems reasonable to assume that end users in less centralized firms are more satisfied with their CBIS. Thus, the following hypothesis is adopted for further tests of this phenomena:

## H8: End-user satisfaction is positively associated with the degree of decentralization in a firm.

## Methodology

## Environment

First, a definition for categorizing organizations as “small” was adopted. Since private firms tend to refrain from disclosing their total capital and/or their revenue, the number of employees, as defined by the Canadian Small Business Guide (1984), was selected as a proxy for categorizing a firm as “small.” Next, 242 small firms located in Southern Ontario were contacted by telephone and asked to participate in the study. To encourage participation, each firm was promised a brief written report of an information systems analysis specific to its organization, as well as a report based on the overall findings of the study.

The small business firms that participated comprised 47 service industries and 36 manufacturing firms. On average, these firms had seven managerial positions, 22 office workers, 38 blue-collar workers, and 5 secretaries, from a total of 164 employees interviewed. The first 11 subjects from four firms was used to test the semi-structured questionnaire for the first phase of the study. The remaining subjects consisted of 86 end users and 67 DP personnel.

## Instrument

In recognition of the difficulties inherent in measuring the attributes of a successful CBIS, researchers have resorted to a surrogate measure of the quality of computer use, namely the satisfaction of end users with the application systems (DeBrabander and Thiers, 1984). To this end, based on the data collected during the face-to-face interviews, the Bailey and Pearson (1983) questionnaire was modified and reduced to 35 items in order to fit the specific needs of the environment. Each of the 35 factors comprising user satisfaction construct, which was based on a seven point semantic differential domain, was rated on five scales. Four of the scales were used for evaluation measurement. The fifth scale, defined by “important - unimportant,” was used to weight the factor in the overall score. This questionnaire was mailed to 153 employees who had already been interviewed during the first phase of the study. Forty end users and 37 DP personnel returned completed questionnaires.

## Analysis

## Assessment of reliability and validity of the questionnaire

A comparison of the weighted and unweighted satisfaction scores revealed a strong correspondence with the Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.988. The high correlation coefficient is in line with Pearson's (1977) findings, thereby indicating a perfect linear relationship between the scores. Therefore, the use of a weighted function conveys the same information as the less complex unweighted satisfaction score.

The reliability of a measure refers to its stability over a variety of conditions (Thorndike, 1982). One measure of reliability is the amount of error in the measurement as determined by Cronbach's alpha test when applied to interitem scores and to overall measures. The overall reliability of the questionnaire was 0.94; the reliability of individual factors ranged from 0.88 to 0.98. Thus, very little variance in response was due to measurement error. This indicates that the reliability of the questionnaire is an acceptable instrument.

An instrument must be valid in order to provide an accurate representation of an abstract concept. Generally, any instrument is valid if it does what it is intended to do. To determine the homogeneity of the questionnaire, the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients were calculated for each pair of scales for each factor. All coefficients were significant at the p<0.0001 level, thereby indicating that the questionnaire was internally consistent. A factor analysis confirmed the unidimensionality of the scales in the measurement instrument as well.

To measure the predictive ability of the questionnaire, the overall satisfaction score derived from the questionnaire was correlated with a single self-assessment score obtained during the face-to-face interview. The correlation between these two measures was 0.42 (significant at the p<0.001 level). The results obtained by others (Ives, et al., 1983; Pearson, 1977) and by the author are indicative of the questionnaire's predictive validity.

Construct validity is the extent to which a particular measure relates to other measures that are consistent with theoretically derived hypotheses concerning the concept (or the construct) being measured. Thus, a measure of construct validity is the extent to which the total score correlates with each factor (Kerlinger, 1973). The correlation between the total satisfaction score and the score for each factor ranged from 0.47 to 0.89 (all significant at p<0.001).

Based on the above findings regarding the reliability and validity of the questionnaire, the overall satisfaction score, as well as the score for each construct, can be used as a meaningful measure of the factors under consideration.

Since the respondents consisted of 40 end users and 37 data processing personnel, there may have been significant differences in the degree of satisfaction with and/or the level of importance attached to each factor. Thus, the data were categorized into two groups: end users and data processing personnel.

The average degree of satisfaction with the state of each factor was computed (see Table 4). A high satisfaction with several salient issues such as accuracy, top management involvement, currency, reliability, timeliness, format of output, and relevancy was indicated. However, the average satisfaction of end users was relatively low for other important factors such as flexibility of systems, documentation, and degree of training.

The concern in this article is to investigate whether in fact it is appropriate to employ user satisfaction as an index of success.

As pointed out earlier, user satisfaction has been used as a surrogate measure of CBIS success. However, it is quite probable that lack of knowledge on the part of the end user regarding the technical aspects of the underlying CBIS can create a biased perception of the usefulness of the CBIS (DeBrabander and Thiers, 1984). The question is whether there is any difference between DP personnel's and end users' satisfaction with their CBIS.

Table 4 presents the response of the two groups (DP personnel and end users) to the structured questionnaire. Kendall's rank correlation indicates a high concordance between the two groups of respondents. Kendall's tau for the degree of satisfaction with each group's factor was 0.77 and for the level of importance was 0.64 (both significant at p<0.0001). This analysis is based on the average satisfaction of the two groups of respondents. A further analysis was performed to measure the degree of association for each pair of DP personnel and end users from the same organization. Twenty pairs of comparisons were performed. Kendall's tau for the degree of satisfaction with each factor ranged from 0.68 to 0.86 and for the level of importance ranged from 0.66 to 0.83 (all significant at p<0.0001). This indicates a strong correlation between the perceived importance and the satisfaction of DP personnel and end users.

The close proximity of end-user and DP personnel perceptions of their CBIS environment give credence to using end-user satisfaction as a reasonable macro measure of CBIS success. Nonetheless, such a finding may only be applicable to small organizations and may result from environmental factors such as ease of communication among members, which, in turn, can reduce problems associated with the semantic gap (De-Brabander and Thiers, 1984). Semantic gap, as used in this article, means that different people have different concepts of reality. The greater the semantic gap, the more effort in communication is required by the parties in order to establish semantic equivalence and provide mutual understanding in the systems development process.

## Test of the hypotheses

Table 5 presents the results of testing the research hypotheses. As shown, end-user satisfaction is positively associated with the presence of systems analysts. The need for systems analysts to assess and adopt information systems is well recognized in large organizations (e.g., Rockart and Flannery, 1983). Their effect on end-user satisfaction is also substantiated for small firms by testing the first hypothesis. However, it may be a myth that small business firms, due to their size, “know” their requirements and that user friendly software generators can easily be selected through simplistic media (Lemons, 1985). The owner/manager’s basic knowledge of computer systems may also be an incentive to ignore the need for the services of specialized information systems personnel (Nickell and Seado, 1986). Contrary to this, however, more than 50 percent of the end users in firms that did not use systems analysts were dissatisfied with a number of important issues such as systems flexibility, systems integration, language interface, security, and documentation.

The presence of a knowledgeable systems analyst alone does not guarantee a match between the CBIS and the information requirements of an organization. To achieve this match, a detailed plan of action is needed to understand the information requirements of the end users, to choose appropriate hardware and software, and to oversee the operation and management of CBIS resources. Three levels of planning can be identified: operational, strategic, and policy planning (Boynton and Zmud, 1984). Operational planning involves those activities required to implement a specific CBIS; strategic planning attempts to link an organization's strategic interest with information technology; and policy planning aims to establish an organizational culture that can be used to utilize information products and services appropriately. Requirements for such a detailed information systems plan is well realized in large organizations. This can be attributed to factors such as strong pressure for coordination and integration of diverse units within the organization (this will be described later) and to the availability of more resources to facilitate implementation of CBIS (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978). End-user satisfaction also correlates with the level of information requirements analysis performed in small firms (see Table 5). Thus, it seems reasonable to expect that a lack of a unified information system plan and an assessment of end-user information requirements can have the same negative impact on small firms as is reported for large organizations (McFarlan, et al., 1983; Nolan, 1979).

Moreover, the positive effects of end-user involvement in the systems design processes on end-user satisfaction is also substantiated, thereby confirming the observation made by Hirschheim (1985). The positive effects of end-user involvement can be attributed to a number of factors such as a better fit of the information systems with the requirements, ease of operating the systems due to learning experience during the design phase, and a feeling of ownership (Hirschheim, 1985).

An end user's level of computer literacy also influences end-user satisfaction and appreciation of CBIS. This observation substantiates the perception of other researchers (Hirschheim, 1985; Nelson and Cheney, 1987; Rockart and Flannery, 1983). However, as already noted, the computer literacy levels of the participating end users were low, and none of the organizations had any formal strategy to deal with this factor. In fact, more than 32 percent of the end users and 24 percent of the DP personnel were completely dissatisfied with the degree of end-user computer training provided by their organization.

The positive relationship between end-user satisfaction and the proportion of online application systems reflects previous findings (Dickson, et al., 1977; Lucas, 1975; Raymond, 1983) which show that end users prefer interactive systems. However, the power of interactive application systems is lost if lack of adequate computer literacy in end users causes a failure to take full advantage of an application system. In fact, deficient computer knowledge seems to cause organizations to increase their reliance on special purpose commercial software instead of on general purpose application generators. Nonetheless, selection and use of special purpose commercial application systems must be carefully planned, otherwise it can create significant problems (Martin and McClure, 1983; McFarlan and McKenney, 1983). This may be the cause of the inconclusive evidence concerning hypothesis six (see Table 5). In the absence of a relationship between end-user satisfaction and the use of special purpose commercial application systems, it seems that the interest in this type of software is mainly a response to its cost advantage.

Analysis of the data did not show a relationship between end-user satisfaction and the duration of a company's CBIS operation. This lack of relationship relates to DeLone's (1983) finding and may explain the reason for the weak negative relationship acknowledged by Raymond (1985). The only conclusion that can therefore be drawn is that it is of little consequence how long CBIS has been in operation, but rather how it is managed. The need for integration among different professional management units in an organization seems to be a driving force for more effective utilization of CBIS resources (see Table 2). As organizations increase in size and complexity and as applications of information systems technology increase in scope and complexity, companies need to implement more formal planning processes to ensure the broad-based dialogue essential to the development of an integrated vision of CBIS (McFarlan, et al., 1983). Consequently, as was postulated in hypothesis eight, end-user satisfaction tends to be higher in less centralized firms (see Table 5).

## Other findings

As was discussed earlier, this investigation indicated a strong correlation between satisfaction scores reported by the two groups of respondents (end users and DP personnel). Table 6 presents the association of the DP personnel score of satisfaction with relevant research variables. It can be noted that these associations are similar to those reported for end users, except for the relationships related to the source of application and the duration of experience with CBIS.

The similarity of associations between research variables and scores of satisfaction between the two groups of respondents substantiates the findings of Ferrat and Short (1986): IS and non-IS employees have similar motivation with respect to doing their job. Attributes such as the intensity of information requirements analysis, end-user involvement in information system development processes, and end users' level of computer literacy directly affects the level of communication between end users and DP personnel. This, in turn, directly affects the quality of underlying CBIS, which then influences the satisfaction of the parties involved. However, in contrast with end users, DP personnel were dissatisfied with the application systems obtained from the outside software vendors. This can be interpreted as a result of their inability to directly control maintenance and integration with other systems. Furthermore, DP personnel in firms with longer CBIS experience seem to be less satisfied. This can be attributed to the lack of technical challenge and progress through rank and file.

## Discussion

Successful operation of CBIS depends on a variety of interacting factors (DeBrabander and Thiers, 1984; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; King and Kraemer, 1984; Mason and Mitroff, 1973). This study investigated the effects of eight such factors on end-user satisfaction in the context of the small business firms (see Figure 1), and found that end users in less centralized firms are more satisfied with their CBIS. This may result because less centralized firms are more in need of integration devices. Furthermore, because they have diverse needs, and they formalize goals, less centralized organizations require information systems that are tailored to the needs of individual decision makers. Increased complexity of decisions and more intricate coordination needs create the requirement for specialized systems analysts to perform in-depth information systems planning. The presence of systems analysts and the degree of information requirements analysis tend to have a positive influence on end-user participation in the development process of information systems. Mutual cooperation consequently tends to have positive influence on an end user's level of computer literacy, on satisfaction with the application software developed, and on mutual understanding of the problems and limitations inherent within information systems environment. However, due to limited resources, very few small firms are able to employ systems analysts. Furthermore, even firms which systems analyst support generally avoid information systems planning and proper information systems requirements analysis to reduce expenses. As a result, inconsistencies occur between the user's requirements and systems capability.

Ease of use and the relatively low cost have made general purpose software an attractive option for generation of new applications. The organizations visited in this study utilized general purpose software mainly for simple standalone systems, thereby reducing the flexibility and the capability of a total system. In addition, whereas general purpose commercial software requires only minimal knowledge of information systems, difficulty arises when the application developer faces more involved systems that need detailed understanding of the capabilities of available software and hardware systems (McFarlan and McKenney, 1983). Therefore, it seems more cost-justifiable to use special purpose commercial software that is tailormade to the specific needs of an organization. Even if the special purpose commercial software is tailored to the complete satisfaction of the specific environment of the firm, difficulty arises when integration with other application software is attempted (McFarlan and McKenney, 1983).

Another important finding is the strong correlation between DP personnel and end users' perceptions regarding CBIS environment. Therefore, it seems reasonable to use end-user information satisfaction as a macro index of CBIS success. However, such a phenomena may be specific to small firms, arising as a result of ease of communication among the interested parties. In this case, large organizations with decentralized CBIS may also expect similar results. Obviously, this requires further investigation.

## Concluding Remarks

The results of this study have strong implications for both researchers and practitioners. With respect to the factors affecting satisfaction with CBIS, the study has examined a series of relationships that previously have either been posited with little proof or have created ambiguity in response to contradictory propositions. In particular, this analysis shows that, contrary to previous beliefs, small and large firms alike react to factors that affect their CBIS environment. The distinguishing factor for the small business seems to be its simple organizational structure, which can enhance communication among its members. This concept can be used as a means to establish an effective CBIS.

In addition, management strategists of small firms should note that, contrary to popular belief, a simplistic choice of CBIS can have a devastating effect on daily operations and can prove to be highly expensive in the long run. In particular, lack of specialized systems analysts was apparent among the firms that participated in this study. The major reason for this phenomena may be the lack of the financial resources needed to keep a proper mix of DP personnel. Furthermore, lack of environmental dynamics and limited opportunities for promotion through rank makes it difficult for small firms to attract well trained DP personnel. Thus, exploration of these compounded problems is a highly challenging area of research for the specialists in the field whose purpose should be to devise procedures that reduce the burden.

## About the Author

Ali Reza Montazemi is assistant professor of information systems at McMaster University. His research interests include information requirements analysis and the design of decision support systems. His papers on these topics have been published in the MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transaction on Man, Systems and Cybernetics, Journal of the Operational Research Society, and Journal of Information Systems and Operational Research.
