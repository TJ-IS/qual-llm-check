## Abstract

There is very little empirical research available on the effectiveness of decision support systems applied to decision-making groups operating in face-to-face meetings. In order to expand research in this area, a laboratory study was undertaken to examine the effects of group decision support systems (GDSS) technology on group decision quality and individual perceptions within a problem-finding context. A crisis management task served as the decision-making context. Two versions of the experimental task, one higher in difficulty and the other lower in difficulty, were administered to GDSS-supported and nonsupported decision-making groups, yielding a 2 X 2 factorial design. Decision quality was significantly better in those groups that received GDSS support. The GDSS was particularly helpful in the groups receiving the task of higher difficulty. Members' decision confidence and satisfaction with the decision process were, however, lower in the GDSS-supported groups than in the nonsupported groups. These findings expand knowledge of the applicability of GDSS for decision-making tasks and suggest that dissatisfaction may be a stumbling block in user acceptance of these systems.

Keywords: Decision support, group decision support systems, problem solving

ACM Categories: K.0, K.m, H.0, H.4.2, H.4.m

## Introduction

The development of systems to support the activities of face-to-face group meetings is a relatively new application of computer technology. Gray, et al. (1981), Huber (1982, 1984a, 1984b), and others (Kiesler, et al., 1984; Bui and Jarke, 1984, 1986; Kraemer and King, 1986; Applegate, et al., 1986; Stefik, et al., 1987; Cook, et al., 1987; Johansen, 1987; DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1985, 1987) have articulated the design, functioning and expected impacts of these systems. Only a modest number of empirical studies related to group decision support systems (GDSS) have been conducted, however. Case study and anecdotal evidence have been reported by Kull (1982), Gray, et al. (1981), and Quinn, et al. (1985). In these studies, the authors conclude that computer systems can improve the quality of decisions made by groups in face-to-face meetings.

More rigorous experimental methods have been employed by Steeb and Johnston (1981), Turoff and Hiltz (1982), Lewis (1982), and Beauclair (1987). These studies have been conducted in the laboratory and have used “crisis management” settings (e.g., survival in arctic conditions) in which small groups of people have been asked to generate action plans or choose from alternative solutions to the crisis. For some of the studies, software has been made available to decision-making groups for soliciting and aggregating members’ ideas or preferences, and the performance of these groups have then been compared to groups without access to the software support. Each of these experiments has demonstrated a performance advantage for groups using group decision-making software. The current study aims to further explore the domain of applicability of GDSS technology in two ways: (1) by examining the effectiveness of decision support for a problem-finding task, and (2) by considering the role of task difficulty in the usefulness of a GDSS.

The GDSS research to date has concentrated on the later phases of the decision process, namely alternatives generation and alternatives evaluation. In fact, virtually all empirical tests of individual decision support systems (DSS) have failed to examine initial decision-making phases as well. This research departs from the traditional focus and explores the utility of a GDSS in an early phase of the decision-making process, namely identification of the cause of a problem (problem-finding). Consistent with prior GDSS studies, this study continues the use of a crisis situation (in this case, declining profits that, if not arrested, will cause a change in management of the enterprise). The task faced by the decision-making group in this study is to find the underlying cause of this unsatisfactory state of affairs.

In addition, this research considers the importance of task difficulty as a variable relating to the effectiveness of a GDSS. Two versions of the experimental task, one higher in difficulty and the other lower in difficulty, were employed to ascertain if the effect of a GDSS on group decision quality was different under these two conditions. Groups with access to a GDSS were compared to groups without technical support for their ability to generate alternative possible problem causes and apply appropriate rationales for isolating the cause of the problem. The perceptions of the group members regarding discussion conflict, as well as their satisfaction with the group meeting and degree of agreement with the final group decision were compared between GDSS and non-GDSS groups in this problem-finding task context.

The study begins with a discussion on the nature of group decision support and the purpose of the study in light of available research. Next, the research hypotheses on which this study is based are outlined and the appropriate independent and dependent variables are described. The research method is then discussed, followed by an examination of the results of the study. The article concludes by discussing the implications of the research and possible future research directions.

## Research Issues

## The GDSS concept

Group decision support systems combine computer, communications, and decision technologies to support problem finding, formulation, and solution in group meetings (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). Because decision making in groups occurs through interpersonal communication (Bedau, 1984; Poole, 1983), the most fundamental goal of a GDSS is to support the exchange of ideas, opinions, and preferences within the group (Bui and Jarke, 1984; DeSanctis and Dickson, 1987). A GDSS that provides communication-support features such as anonymous messaging or voting is a Level 1 system (for a thorough explanation of GDSS levels, see DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). When decision modeling or mathematical techniques are added to the system, the GDSS becomes an enhanced, or Level 2, system. DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) argue that GDSS research should begin with the study of Level 1 systems, only proceeding to study higher level systems after some understanding of lower-level systems has been achieved.

Decision support technology aims to ease the decision-making process by, among other things, reducing the cognitive load, or mental effort, associated with group meetings. Level 1 systems facilitate interpersonal sharing of ideas, opinions, and preferences by providing structure and automation to this process. Even a rudimentary Level 1 GDSS should ease some of the communication burden of open verbal exchange, thus simplifying the decision task. It follows that the more difficult the group decision task is, the greater the potential benefits of GDSS support should be in terms of decision processes and outcomes.

## GDSS research and the current study

Research concerned with the impact of group decision support systems on group functioning must, in the long run, account for an array of variables of potential significance to group meetings and the organizations in which they occur. For example, the impact of computer-based information systems can depend on a variety of factors such as: (1) the type of system, (2) the particular task involved, (3) management policy, (4) existing organizational structure, and (5) the stage of computerization. The amount of user experience with the system can also be a factor (for reviews of the literature on the impacts of information systems see Attwell and Rule, 1984; Rice, 1980). Knowledge regarding the impacts of a GDSS on users is far less understood at this time. The initial empirical research in this area has been confined to laboratory settings which provide the control necessary to begin understanding the relationship between the presence of GDSS technology and group outcomes.

Thus, the myriad of potential influences such as organizational contexts and group learning have, to this point, been avoided. Additionally, the studies have been restricted to the aforementioned task setting that deals with a crisis situation of some type. Frequently the selection of this task environment by GDSS researchers is supported by the notion that groups in such circumstances are very susceptible to groupthink (Janis, 1972), which might be lessened by the use of a GDSS.

The current study aims to build on previous GDSS research findings by considering the first two of the five categories of variables mentioned above. $^{2}$ More specifically, the effectiveness of a Level 1 GDSS in a problem finding context is explored under two levels of task difficulty.

By manipulating the degree of task difficulty, the study attempts to determine the ability of GDSS to improve decision-making performance in group meetings. Consistent with prior research, a crisis management situation is employed, but a task setting is used which requires a group to find the cause of a problem rather than a solution to a problem. ## Task setting: problem finding

Almost thirty years ago Simon (1960) identified stages of the process of making management decisions. His well known three-stage formalization of decision making (intelligence, design, and choice) is actually six stages when one considers Simon's elaboration of design activities to include inventing, developing, and analyzing various courses of action. Other researchers (e.g., Mintzberg, et al., 1976 and Bass, 1983) have suggested that individual and group decision making go through various stages. About twenty years ago Pounds (1969) first focused attention on how the first stage in decision making — problem finding — occurs in management. More recently, several authors have noted the lack of scholarly attention paid to the early stages of the managerial decision-making process (see Lyles and Mitroff, 1980; Cowan, 1986). The task which provides the setting for our research is best described as “problem finding/identification” (or “problem defining” as used by Kilmann and Mitroff, 1979). In other words, the groups participating in this research were very aware of the symptoms of a problem (the case write-up described a number of symptoms such as declining profits). Their task was to find the underlying cause of the symptoms; that is, find the problem.

Using Pounds' (1969) definition of problem finding, each group member had their own general "model" for acceptable company performance. They compared that model to the economic realities that the company faced (as described in the case). The group members identified differences between their own models and actual company performance and attempted to reach agreement as to which differences determined the major problem in the case. Thus, this study explores the intervention of technology in support of a very important and relatively neglected phase of decision making — problem finding.

## Task difficulty

One of the most important factors affecting decision making in groups is the nature of the task that the group confronts (Poole, 1983; McGrath, 1984). Group tasks can be characterized by, among other things, their relative degree of problem-solving difficulty (Shaw, 1981). Task difficulty refers to the degree of cognitive load, or mental effort, required to identify a problem solution (see Payne, 1976). Difficulty is a relative concept in the sense that the difficulty level of any given task can only be assessed with respect to other tasks. Assessment of task difficulty can be made by asking decision makers their perceptions of relative difficulty. But evidence of difficulty is more objectively implied in (1) decision makers' relative performance in tasks (more difficult tasks will be properly completed by a smaller proportion of people than less difficult tasks), and (2) the amount of time spent in completing the task (more difficult tasks will take longer for people to complete than less difficult tasks). In the current study, relative task difficulty was manipulated by using one task (a business case) involving a “crisis” in profit performance and varying the data contained in the exhibits accompanying the task to make one version of the case more difficult to solve than the other (see the section on Research Method for a detailed explanation).

## Hypotheses

The major question addressed by this research can be stated as follows: How does a GDSS affect group decision making for problem-finding tasks of varying difficulty? This question was examined using four main hypotheses. The hypotheses were organized into two groups: decision performance and group members' reactions to the group decision process. All hypotheses predict an interaction effect between GDSS use and task difficulty on group outcomes. In other words, the effects of GDSS on group outcomes is expected to vary as a function of the difficulty of the task.

## Decision performance

Decision performance refers to those variables that attempt to measure the attributes of the “finished product” of the decision-making process. For this study decision performance includes decision quality (defined in the dependent variables section) and number of alternatives generated. The hypotheses are described in terms of the impact of the two independent variables (GDSS/non-GDSS, task difficulty) on two dependent variables (decision quality, number of alternatives generated).

## H1: Decision Quality

H1a: Under conditions of higher task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will make decisions of higher quality than those not supported by a GDSS.

H1b: Under conditions of lower task difficulty, there will be no difference in decision quality between groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

The more difficult the decision task, the greater the intellectual effort (cognitive load) required to analyze and evaluate the information used in making the decision. This implies that a greater number of issues and alternatives may need to be considered (Shaw, 1932; Hackman, 1968). The more issues and alternatives to be considered, the more a group is expected to need decision support to focus attention on the decision to be made. By focusing the group's attention on the decision task, use of a GDSS is expected to lead to higher quality decisions. Decision tasks of lower difficulty are expected to require less information analysis and information exchange among group members than more difficult tasks (Shaw, 1932; Hackman, 1968). This implies that there is expected to be few advantages to using a GDSS for decision tasks of lower difficulty; in fact, use of a GDSS may actually impede the group in their decision making if the technology “overwhelms” the problem or “becomes more trouble than it’s worth.”

## H2: Number of Possible Alternatives Generated

H2a: Under conditions of higher task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will generate more alternatives than those not supported by a GDSS.

H2b: Under conditions of lower task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will generate an equal number of alternatives compared to those groups not supported by a GDSS.

Idea generation, or identification of possible causes or solutions to a problem, occurs early in the group decision-making process (Bales and Strodbeck, 1951). Researchers of group dynamics have long documented the fact that naturally interacting groups often overlook or pass too quickly through this important stage (Hackman and Kaplan, 1974; Hirokawa, 1983; Park, 1982). The stage is important because the purpose of a decision group is to bring a variety of viewpoints to bear on a problem. However, the process of idea generation is often stifled by time constraints, undue pressure to conform, or failure to incorporate the views of all group members (Van de Ven and Delbecq, 1974). The technology within a Level 1 GDSS, by soliciting, storing and displaying ideas, and by allowing anonymity of expression, should encourage more ideas to generate from the group than would otherwise occur.

This notion, that GDSS will increase the total number of ideas considered by a group, has been proposed by Steeb and Johnston (1981) and Huber (1982). Initial laboratory research using a relatively small sample size tends to support this notion (Nunamaker, et al., 1987). In the current study, a GDSS may be particularly useful for more difficult tasks where more alternatives may have to be considered before the group reaches a consensus decision. In lower difficulty decision tasks, a GDSS is expected to be no more efficient than support provided by a piece of paper or a blackboard (given the relative simplicity of the analysis and communication of alternatives in these tasks). Relatively few issues are considered for easy tasks (Shifflett, 1972) and therefore the GDSS is anticipated to be of little help.

## Perceived reactions to the decision process

The perceived reactions to the decision process are defined as the group members' feelings about the decision process and the decision they have made. For this study, decision perception measures include decision confidence, amount of agreement in the final solution, satisfaction in the decision process, and amount of discussion conflict.

## H3: Decision Confidence

H3a: Under conditions of higher task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will have greater confidence in the decision made than those not supported by a GDSS.

H3b: Under conditions of lower task difficulty, the amount of confidence in the decision made will be the same for groups supported by a GDSS and those not supported by a GDSS.

Maier (1963) argues that decision acceptance, which can be thought of as confidence in the decision or commitment to the decision made, is as important in group decision making as decision accuracy or decision quality. A GDSS is expected to assist the group in examining more alternatives and issues in the decision task (see hypothesis 2). In addition, it should also enable the group to focus on specific alternatives for detailed analysis. The implication is that individual group members, and hence the group as a whole, are expected to be more confident that the best decision has been made. The use of a GDSS where it is less appropriate may cause frustration with the decision-making process (Huber, 1982), as in easier tasks, if the technology overwhelms the task or interferes with efficient task completion. This may lead to no increase in confidence following use of a GDSS or less confidence that all aspects of the problem have been considered, hence less confidence in the decision made.

## H3.1: Amount of Agreement with the Final Solution

H3.1a: For the higher difficulty task, a greater amount of agreement with the final solution will occur in GDSS-supported groups than non-GDSS-supported groups.

H3.1b: In the lower difficulty task situation, there will be no difference in the amount of agreement with the final solution between GDSS-supported groups and non-GDSS-supported groups.

The use of GDSS in the higher difficulty situation is expected to lead to a more detailed analysis of alternatives and therefore a greater chance that one alternative will be agreed upon as the best solution. In the lower difficulty task, there should be little disagreement on the final solution so there will be little, if any, impact on member agreement from using a GDSS.

## H4: Satisfaction with Decision-Making Process

H4a: Under conditions of higher task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will be more satisfied with the decision-making process than those not supported by a GDSS.

H4b: Under conditions of lower task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will report the same amount of satisfaction with the decision-making process as those not supported by a GDSS.

Because the more difficult tasks require group members to analyze information with greater care than for less difficult tasks, more conflict, and some dissatisfaction with the decision making process, may occur in the more difficult (Maier, 1970). To the extent that a GDSS aids the group in information analysis and group communication, improvements in the overall satisfaction of the group with the group decision-making process are expected. For a lower difficulty task with less potential GDSS benefits members may feel that the decision task does not need GDSS support and may find that it is only marginally useful or gets in the way. Satisfaction with the group decision-making process may be no greater, or perhaps lower, in those groups that utilize a GDSS for lower difficulty tasks. A number of researchers have found that frustration with the group process leads to dissatisfaction in group decision making (Hackman, 1968; Shifflett, 1972; Hirokawa, 1983). If the group sees the GDSS as an impediment to their group activities, they may become frustrated and dissatisfied with the group decision process.

## H4.1: Amount of Discussion Conflict

H4.1a: Under conditions of higher task difficulty, groups supported by a GDSS will experience less discussion conflict than those not supported by a GDSS.

H4.1b: Under conditions of lower task difficulty, there will be no difference in the amount of discussion conflict between groups supported and those not supported by a GDSS.

The use of a GDSS in the higher difficulty situation is expected to lead to more thorough analysis of issues and alternatives in the case. The use of a GDSS is expected to facilitate consensus formation by providing the group with techniques to generate, rank and vote on alternatives. This implies that consensus will be reached with less discussion conflict. In the lower difficulty task, consensus formation is expected to be much less difficult to achieve so there will be little if any impact from using a GDSS on amount of discussion conflict.

## Research Method

## The experimental task

The case that created the task setting for this research was “The Bonanza Business Forms Company” case (Jarvenpaa and Dickson, in press). The case describes the Bonanza Business Forms Company, a firm that sells continuous paper forms for businesses in three markets (the small business market, the hospital market, and the financial institution market). During the previous three quarters the profits of the company were steadily declining while total sales dollars were increasing. As described in the case, Bonanza's management could not determine the cause of the declining profits and consequently asked for help. The group's task was to find the cause of the company's problem using a series of business reports, and to correctly identify why the problem was occurring.

Simon's (1964) phases of decision making and Guzzo's (1979) categorization scheme were used to determine the various properties of the task. Simon's phases showed the task to be an "intelligence" task, or a problem finding task, because it required the group to find the cause of the declining profits for the Bonanza Business Forms Company. It was also a "business" task, in particular, a marketing task. It was similar to the "trouble-shooting" types of tasks that teams of managers in organizations face repeatedly. Guzzo's scheme showed the task to be a technical/factual task with crisis elements because essentially all the data was available to make the decision, and there was one "best" solution. It was a crisis task because the survival of the company was at stake, and the problem had to be found immediately so that a plan-of-action could be implemented to rectify the situation. This task was not an emotional/political decision task because conflicting interests of the participants were minimized. It was also not a programming task because the time-frame was short term, not long term.

The levels of difficulty of the decision task were based on the work of Shaw (1973) and Simon (1964). The more difficult version of the task required more information analysis (hence a greater cognitive load on group members) than the easier version of the task. Difficulty was not based on knowledge of the steps/procedures to identify the problem (the steps/procedures were the same for both difficulty levels) but was based on the depth of data analysis required. Therefore, difficulty levels were relative and not derived from an accepted theoretical scale because no scale could be found that could take a specific task and derive two levels of difficulty from it.

The less difficult version of the “Bonanza Business Forms Case” had exactly the same written description as the more difficult version. The differences between the versions were in the data exhibits attached to the case. Each version of the task had the same graphs and tables but in the more difficult version, numeric differences in data provided by tables and graphs were smaller, requiring more cognitive effort on the part of the decision maker in analysis of the data. Also, in one important exhibit dealing with sales calls by company salesmen, the number of sales calls was shown as an absolute number for the more difficult version, and as a percentage of total sales dollars for the easier version, which required that extra analysis of the data be performed in the former version in order to relate the data to other exhibits. As an example, Figure 1 illustrates the differences between the levels of difficulty for one factor, “Sales Calls by Company Salesman” (Exhibit 5 in the Case). In all, each version of the case involved ten data exhibits.

The two versions of the task were the results of pilot tests in which the task was iteratively refined to differentiate difficulty levels. For a comprehensive discussion of the pilot testing (carried out at two universities using 160 subjects), see Gallupe (1985). In order to quantify the difference in difficulty between the two versions, it was determined in the early stages of the pilot testing that the lower difficulty task had to be correctly solved by pretest subjects 70% of the time. The higher difficulty task had to be solved correctly by pretest subjects 20% of the time. The exhibits accompanying the case were manipulated by the researchers during the pilot testing to achieve the 70%-20% differentiation between levels of task difficulty. Pilot test results and ratings by a team of marketing experts (marketing faculty members at both universities where the pilot testing took place) indicated that the two versions of the task represented two levels of difficulty. In addition to the pilot test results, the team of marketing experts evaluated the two versions of exhibits that were employed. The team agreed that two levels of difficulty in identifying the problem were present in the materials developed.

## Dependent measures

The dependent variables were the decision performance and perceived reactions of group members for the decision-making session. Two aspects of decision performance were examined: decision quality and number of alternatives generated. Group members' perceptions consisted of self-report ratings of the group's decision confidence, agreement with the final solution, satisfaction with the decision process, and perceived amount of discussion conflict.

## Decision Performance

Decision quality was measured along two dimensions: (1) decision content — how close the group's decision was to that made by experts; (2) decision reasoning — the degree of similarity between the group's reasoning in arriving at their decision and the expert's reasoning. This approach, which attempts to incorporate the reasons for a decision into a measure of the decision quality, was similar to the approach used in earlier GDSS research conducted by Steeb and Johnston (1981). Consistent with that study, a panel of three experts used the pilot testing to establish criteria to evaluate decision quality. The criteria for evaluation was based on: (1) how close the solution was to the one recommended by the experts, and (2) how close the line of reasoning came to the one the experts recommended in terms of issues considered and the order in which those issues were considered.

In the actual experiment, the decision quality of each group was rated by the experts using a 1 to 10 scale with a score of 10 being the highest quality.

Decision alternatives were defined as those issues that the group considered to be possible major problem causes in the case and hence, possible solutions to the decision task. Solutions (possible causes) that could have been considered by the groups included: misallocation of advertising expenditures, poor economic conditions, misallocation of sales personnel, and others. Decision alternatives were enumerated by analyzing the video and audio tapes of the experimental sessions. It should be noted that in the experimental instructions, the group was asked to generate as many alternatives as possible. The intent of this measure was to determine if the number of different alternatives generated by the groups was related to the use of a GDSS and/or task difficulty.

## Group Members' Perceptions

Group decision confidence was assessed in two ways: (1) a post-test, seven-point Likert scale was given to each group member to determine the individual's confidence in the decision made; and (2) the entire group was asked during the post-experiment debriefing session, how much confidence they had in the decision (a seven-point Likert scale was also used). The intent in measuring group confidence using two methods was to obtain a group confidence measure that reflected the group as a whole. Previous research (Boje and Murnighan, 1982) used the individual measure aggregated to a group measure and found that the procedure was satisfactory. For this experiment, individual point values were summed and then divided by the number of group members to get the aggregated group confidence score. This was compared with the verbal statement of group confidence given in the debriefing session. It was found that the aggregated score correlated highly with the verbal score in all cases (r = .86). The aggregated measure was used in the data analysis.

Group satisfaction was recorded using the same procedure as that used for group decision confidence. First, individual group members were asked in the post-test questionnaire how satisfied they were with the decision-making process, using a seven-point Likert scale. Individual values were summed and divided by the number of members in the group to give an aggregated group satisfaction score. Second, the entire group was asked in the post-experiment debriefing session (and after they had filled out the post-test questionnaire) how satisfied they were as a group with the decision-making process. The verbal measure was compared with the aggregated group measure to obtain a more reliable estimate of group satisfaction. Again, it was found that the two measures correlated highly in all cases (r = .89). The aggregated group measure was used in the data analysis.

Two other group measures were obtained. These variables were considered less important in the study, but they were measured because of their potential relevance to hypotheses 3 and 4. Group members were asked their perception of the amount of discussion conflict experienced by the group. In the post-test questionnaire, individual group members were asked to rate the amount of group discussion conflict on a seven-point Likert scale. The other measure was the group's perception of agreement with the final solution. In the post-test questionnaire, individual group members were asked to rate their degree of agreement with the group's final solution on a seven-point Likert scale.

## Setting and subjects

## Setting

The setting for this experiment was a “decision room” that was designed and set up to accommodate face-to-face group interaction (Figure 2). The computer terminals were put on movable desks. This enabled them to be moved into position for those groups that had computer support, and moved out of the way for groups that had no computer support. Each computer terminal had a movable keyboard so that individuals could be comfortable and see the other group members as well as their own terminal screens. The only difference in the setting between groups that had GDSS support and those that did not was the placement of computer terminals in the appropriate positions. The GDSS treatment entailed the use of one computer terminal per group member so that the GDSS could be used to support group decision making. In both the GDSS and non-GDSS treatments, each group member had the use of a pencil, paper, a small hand calculator, and a blackboard.

## The GDSS

The GDSS called “DECISION AID FOR GROUPS” (DECAID) was designed, coded, and tested by the authors before it was used in the experimental setting. The software was developed rather than purchased because the available commercial GDSS software was either chauffeur-driven (not allowing each group member to interface with the system), too limited in scope (message storing only, without voting or preference ranking), or too hardware dependent (running only under specialized operating systems). Lack of standard commercial software in the GDSS area has led most researchers to develop their own systems.

The GDSS software performed the basic functions of recording, storing and displaying alternatives that were entered by group members, aggregating and displaying preference rankings that had been entered for those alternatives, and recording votes (either publicly or anonymously) for the various alternatives generated. These features have been identified as appropriate for supporting the communications needs of the group (Huber, 1984a; DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987).

As noted earlier, our interest was in studying the impacts of a Level 1 system on groups. Consequently, no modeling techniques were included in the system. ## Subjects

Eighty-four senior undergraduate business administration students participated in the study. These subjects had taken at least one course each in management science/decision analysis techniques, marketing, and management theory/organizational behavior, and all had exposure to case analysis techniques. Extensive pilot testing (see Gallupe, 1985) indicated that subjects would perform the task and that the task had face validity. Since the subjects had taken the same courses, some of them had worked together before and some had not. Random assignment of individuals to groups closely simulated a practical situation where some members had worked together before but some had not, and increased the likelihood that unknown individual difference factors such as decision-making ability were spread among the groups. The subjects were randomly assigned to groups of three, and each group was randomly assigned to one experimental situation. Three-person groups are very small. The impacts of a GDSS may be more dramatic in larger size groups. But the average number of people attending an organizational meeting is five or less (Datamation, 1986), and, more importantly, our interest was in determining whether impacts of GDSS could be detected in very small groups.

A total of 24 groups was used (six groups per treatment). This yielded sufficient degrees of freedom for a statistical analysis of the data. In order to prepare subjects for the experiment, all subjects (in both GDSS and non-GDSS) were given an overview of the GDSS and encouraged to try it out before the actual experiment took place. All the groups were exposed to the concepts of alternatives generation, preference ranking, and voting, and all groups were given training on the use of the GDSS so that any unanticipated effects due to training would be constant across treatment conditions. At the beginning of each experimental session, if the group was a GDSS group, the group members were given a brief refresher course in the use of the GDSS.

## Experimental procedure

The following procedure was used for each of the groups in this experiment:

1. Subjects were asked to fill out a consent form and a demographic data form.

2. Subjects were asked to fill out a pretest questionnaire that asked about attitudes towards computers and group decision making.

3. All groups were asked to follow the same general decision-making steps:

a. read and analyze the case individually,  
b. initiate discussion about the case,

c. generate alternatives,

d. rank alternatives,

e. resolve any conflict,

f. vote on alternatives to reach consensus.

This placed some structure on the decision-making session but allowed free-flowing discussion. Groups that were supported by the GDSS were instructed to use whatever features of the system they thought might help the group decision making (all GDSS groups used at least one system feature). The agreed upon solution was written on the decision forms provided.

4. Individual group members were asked to fill out a post-test questionnaire that asked about confidence in the decision, satisfaction with the process, amount of discussion conflict, and agreement with final solution.

5. A debriefing session was given to each group to determine if the group members had any questions, comments, or suggestions regarding the decision-making session. The group was asked to provide a rating of their decision confidence and satisfaction with the decision process.

The groups were not given a time limit for completing the task. On average, the groups met for an hour and 17 minutes before reaching a decision. The GDSS groups tended to take longer than non-GDSS groups, and groups with the higher difficulty task tended to meet for a longer time than those with the lower difficulty task. There were no significant differences, however, across groups with respect to decision time.

## Results

This section summarizes and interprets the research findings. The first part examines the statistical findings. The second part outlines some observational findings. In the statistical analysis, a fixed-effect two-way analysis of a variance model was applied to all dependent measures. Tables 3 and 4 summarize the major statistical findings of this study. Table 3 shows the means and standard deviations for the dependent variables. Table 4 shows the results from the analysis of variance.

## Impacts on decision performance

## Decision Quality

Hypothesis 1 of this study deals with decision quality. The hypothesis states that: for higher difficulty tasks, a GDSS is expected to improve decision quality, while for lower difficulty tasks a GDSS is not expected to improve decision quality (and may even decrease decision quality). In fact, use of a GDSS improved decision quality for lower difficulty as well as higher difficulty decision tasks. In particular, the use of a GDSS significantly improved decision quality for higher difficulty decision tasks. So H1 was only partially supported. Use of the GDSS improved decision quality in both task conditions. A significant relationship was also found between decision task difficulty and decision quality, a result that has been found generally in group decision-making research (the more difficult the decision task, the lower the decision quality).

The general finding that use of a GDSS improves decision quality is consistent with the experimental studies listed in Table 1. These studies all reported higher quality decisions in GDSS supported groups. Thus, the results of the current study suggest that the applicability of GDSS may be expanded to include problem-finding task domains.

Using the GDSS seemed to impose an implicit “agenda” on the group that acted to structure the group process. Finally, the GDSS acted as the “group memory” and enabled the group to analyze information without unnecessarily repeating the analysis later in the deliberations. Although other researchers have found that use of a GDSS improves decision quality, the variety of support features used, the number of different GDSSs studied, and the differences in experimental tasks across studies point to the fact that more research needs to be done to determine exactly what features of a GDSS provide the most effective and efficient support for specific types of decision tasks. ## Number of Alternatives Generated

The hypothesis that use of a GDSS will increase the number of alternatives generated by the groups with the higher level difficulty task was partially supported (H2). It was found that the use of the GDSS increased the number of alternatives in both the higher and lower difficulty tasks. This finding is consistent with that of Lewis (1982), Turoff and Hiltz (1982) and Steeb and Johnston (1981), who found that the use of a GDSS tended to increase the alternatives generated in other task settings.

Our observations of the group meetings suggested that use of the system allowed the group to make more efficient use of time in the decision-making session. This facilitated the consideration of more alternatives. The system kept track of alternatives that had already been discussed so that repetitive analysis of the same limited number of alternatives was avoided. The use of the GDSS also tended to allow group members to disassociate themselves from the alternatives they had entered into this system. This allowed for more objective evaluation of the alternatives without the necessity of possibly defending previously stated postures. This again led to more effective use of time and generation of more alternatives.

## Impacts on group members' perceptions

Analyses of variance for each of the four perceptual variables (shown in Table 4) revealed no statistically significant interaction effects for the GDSS and task difficulty variables. Thus, hypotheses 3 and 4 are not supported. The effect of GDSS support on group perceptions did not vary as a function of task difficulty level. In several cases there are significant differences in perceptions between GDSS and non-GDSS groups. Of particular interest is that the findings were in the reverse direction of what was anticipated. Use of the technology resulted in negative sentiments, despite improvements in decision quality.

## Decision Confidence

The hypothesis that use of a GDSS in the higher difficulty task would increase confidence in the decision made was not supported at the p<.05 level (H3). The raw data results, however, indicate that groups who used the GDSS were slightly less confident in the decision they had made compared to other groups (see Table 3). This finding is not consistent with the Steeb and Johnston study (1981) or the Turoff and Hiltz study (1982) that found that groups supported by a computer-based group decision aid had more confidence in the decision made.

The observed trend of less confidence in the GDSS-supported groups is interesting and may be explained by “post-decision apprehension.” Groups supported by the GDSS tended to generate more possible decision alternatives. They also considered those alternatives in more detail. Because of this condition, these groups had a more difficult choice to make, and once they made a decision, they were possibly less confident because of the number and quality of the choices.

Decision confidence is an important outcome variable for group decision making. One of the reasons for using groups to make decisions is to allow a number of individuals to participate in the decision-making process and thus enhance confidence in the decision made. If use of the GDSS tends to lessen the group confidence in the decision, then questions about the appropriateness of using a GDSS must be raised. Further study of the effects of GDSS technology on group confidence is needed.

## Agreement with Final Solution

Contrary to our expectations, the effect of GDSS on member agreement was not a function of the level of task difficulty. Groups with the more difficult task had less agreement on the final decision, as expected. However, groups using the GDSS had significantly lower agreement among members on the final decision than groups without GDSS support. Observation of the groups indicated that more diversity of ideas, hence more effort to reconcile competing views, occurred in the GDSS-supported groups.

Use of the system tended to enhance idea-generation and heighten the group's awareness of multiple viewpoints. A higher level of conflict, less agreement with the final decision, and less satisfaction with the decision process then occurred. Results for the remaining two hypotheses are consistent with this view.

## Satisfaction with the Group Decision-Making Process

Groups with the higher difficulty task were less satisfied than those with the lower difficulty task; however, this difference was not significant. The anticipated interaction effect between GDSS support and task difficulty was not observed. In other words, the effect of GDSS on satisfaction with the group decision process did not vary as a function of task difficulty. Surprisingly, the only meaningful finding for this variable was that GDSS support resulted in a significantly lower level of satisfaction with the group decision-making process. Despite better quality decision making in the GDSS groups, their satisfaction with the decision-making process was low relative to the nonsupported groups. This finding contradicts the findings of Steeb and Johnston (1981) who found that groups with GDSS support reported higher satisfaction with the decision process.

Further research might clarify the discrepancy in the results on decision satisfaction between the current study and Steeb and Johnston's. We can speculate that differences in results are due to the different nature of the tasks and support systems used in the two studies. The crisis planning scenario used by Steeb and Johnston required the participants to do risk analysis — an exercise that lends itself to modeling support. In their study, the GDSS provided a structured risk analysis procedure. Consequently, higher satisfaction in the supported groups may have been a result of the specific modeling support provided by the software. Non-GDSS groups had no modeling support, which is a substantial disadvantage in a mathematically-laden task. The task employed in the current study was solved without using special mathematical models. Consequently, non-GDSS groups were not at such a severe disadvantage as GDSS groups. The Level 1 GDSS in the current study provided support for only the communication needs of the group. The features provided, i.e., voting and preference ranking, are fairly generic and of potential usefulness for a variety of group tasks (Huber, 1984a).

The results of this study raise a rather disturbing question: Why did the GDSS groups report lower satisfaction, and lower confidence, despite better quality performance than the non-GDSS groups? Some insight into this matter may be found when the results on perceived agreement and conflict are examined.

## Discussion Conflict in the Group Decision-Making Process

This variable was added to support “satisfaction with the group process” data. A significant effect in this study involved the perceived conflict experienced by GDSS groups versus non-GDSS groups. Independent of decision task difficulty, the use of the GDSS tended to increase the perceived conflict experienced by the group during the group decision-making session (H4.1a and b are rejected). This finding was consistent with others in this study (confidence, satisfaction) because the use of the GDSS provided a “richer” environment for decision making. A “richer” environment means that the decision-making group has the support to examine more alternatives in more detail and, therefore, conflicting views have more chance to be aired. A review of the videotapes of the experimental sessions indicated that this conflict tended not to be destructive but actually facilitated the resolution of differing views. The fact that more conflict was experienced with GDSS groups seemed to be related to higher decision quality.

## Observations made during the experimental sessions

A general observation based on analysis of the videotapes of the experimental sessions was that previous experience with computers or keyboard familiarity had little to do with actual GDSS usage. A reason for this finding was simplicity of the system and its usability. It seemed that all individual group members could use the system if they wanted to. This finding has implications for GDSS use. To be effective, this type of GDSS must be capable of being used by all participants.

Another observation was that a structured decision-making approach that utilized the strengths of the GDSS resulted in higher decision quality. Those groups that used such an approach were specifically analyzed after the overall analysis was conducted. It was found that those GDSS groups that used a structured approach made better quality decisions when compared to other groups with GDSS support, and when compared to other groups without GDSS support within the same level of difficulty. The number of groups that used this approach was very small (four GDSS groups in total; two at each level of difficulty), but they all performed better within their level of difficulty or GDSS/non-GDSS treatment than the other groups. This decision-making approach had the following six steps:

1. group members analyze the task information independently,

2. each group member enters his or her alternatives into the system before group discussion,

3. the first round of group discussion centers on clarifying alternatives and eliminating redundant ones that are shown on the public screen,

4. group discussions modify alternatives, add new alternatives,

5. group members have brief discussion, then preference weighting,

6. group members repeat steps 4 and 5 until it is clear what the choice of the group is, or until a vote must be taken to break a deadlock.

This approach appeared to increase the efficiency of the group decision-making session, reducing the time spent with the system itself and enabling more discussion about the alternatives shown on the public screen. This approach can be thought of as an automated combination of the Nominal Group technique and the Delphi technique. The use of the GDSS facilitated the group decision process by speeding up the clerical work necessary for the functioning of the process, and at the same time acting as a “memory” for the group. In addition, the system enabled each group member to sit back and look objectively at the alternatives they had entered. This tended to reduce the “ego-baggage” that alternatives in group discussions carry. Reducing this “ego-baggage” reduces the destructive conflict that so often paralyzes rational group decision making. Some conflict is necessary for effective group decision making (Shaw, 1981). The GDSS appears to facilitate the interchange of diverse ideas and at the same time, appears to reduce the destructive conflict that comes from rigid, inflexible positions. Those GDSS groups that followed a structured decision process seemed to realize these benefits. The precise factors causing some groups to find and use a structured process and others to not use any obvious structure are not known.

## Discussion

Use of a GDSS was found to increase the number of alternatives considered and to improve the resulting decision quality of small groups for a problem finding task. Although use of the technology improved decision quality for both higher and lower task difficulty conditions, the effect was particularly strong in groups exposed to the more difficult task. The results extend knowledge of the effectiveness of GDSS to the problem finding task domain and demonstrate the importance of considering task difficulty level when studying the usefulness of group decision support technology. Furthermore, the findings of this study demonstrate that the effects of GDSS can be observed even in very small group meetings.

Perhaps the most noteworthy finding of this study was that use of the GDSS resulted in more negative sentiments from group members regarding decision confidence, agreement with the final solutions, satisfaction with the decision process, and inter-member conflict. Ratings on these variables by the GDSS groups were not extremely low, in the sense that the average scores were on the positive side of a midpoint of four on a seven-point scale. Relative to groups with no computer support, however, the GDSS groups had lower decision confidence, agreed less with the group's final decision, were less satisfied with the decision process, and perceived more conflict in the group discussion. Group researchers have shown that member acceptance of a group's decision often is poorly correlated with its objective quality (Hoffman and Maier, 1961). Ideally, however, the goal of decision support systems should be to enhance user confidence and satisfaction, as well as to improve decision quality (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Sanders and Courtney, 1985). Positive sentiments following use of GDSS may be particularly important in organizational settings where group meetings are used to gain consensus and acceptance of ideas, rather than to gain better ideas per se.

All of these findings must be interpreted with caution, given the limited scope and sampling used in this study. As a controlled experimental study the findings cannot necessarily be generalized to all levels or types of GDSS software or to actual organizational use of GDSS technology. In particular, although the subjects were senior business students with the background and ability to analyze the task given, they were not practicing managers with a number of years experience in making similar decisions. More extended laboratory testing and field studies involving a variety of GDSS designs over a longer period of time, with actual managers, and with larger groups than the current study, are required before firm conclusions regarding the impacts of these systems for specific task types can be truly understood.

This research adds to the growing body of empirical literature that demonstrates the ability of GDSS technology to improve the decision quality of groups. At this point the precise reason for the positive effects of the technology on groups is not altogether understood. In other words, how is the GDSS affecting group activities to yield higher quality decision making? Our observation was that the system provided a suggested structure to the decision process, and that groups who used the system features in a systematic fashion were most successful in their decision making. All groups including the non-GDSS groups were exposed to the technology in a training session, so that all had the opportunity to consider use of voting, preference rating, and ranking in group meetings. Many of the non-GDSS groups chose to use a systematic decision process. But the GDSS groups were more likely than the non-GDSS groups to find a step-by-step decision process, and thus, on average, were more successful in their final decisions. In addition, the GDSS groups had anonymous input of ideas and a method of organizing their ideas, ratings, and votes. The GDSS did not force groups into a structure, but rather suggested opportunities for structuring group discussion. An avenue for further research is to more precisely identify the influence of GDSS characteristics, particularly suggested structure and anonymous communication, on the group decision process.

The anticipated interaction effect of task difficulty with GDSS support on group outcomes was not observed in this study. In other words, the effect of GDSS support did not vary significantly as a function of level of task difficulty. Several explanations are possible. Certainly there were strong trends in the data in the direction of effects indicated in the experimental hypotheses. For example, the advantage of GDSS support for decision quality was greater in the higher difficulty task condition than in the lower difficulty task condition, and the difference in user satisfaction between GDSS and non-GDSS groups was greater in the lower difficulty task than in the higher difficulty task condition. A larger sample size, resulting in higher statistical power, may have resulted in significant interaction effects for some of the dependent variables. Another possibility is that the lower task difficulty condition was not low enough to yield the hypothesized impacts for the GDSS intervention. Every effort was made to empirically differentiate the levels of difficulty. Difficulty, as we defined it, was a relative construct dependent on the nature of the task and the subjects and not on some theoretical scale. Further research might go beyond the two levels of task difficulty considered in this study. In particular, the question of whether GDSS can be helpful in even very easy tasks should be pursued.

Of course, further study and replication of all of the findings of this research may be of interest. In addition, the results of this study raise the following questions for future research:

\- What are the precise causes of more negative sentiments on the part of group members who use a GDSS?

\- How can a GDSS be designed to result in both improved decision quality without reduction in positive feelings about the group meeting?

\- How will extended experience with GDSS on the part of decision makers affect decision quality and user perceptions?

\- Can GDSS be useful to groups confronting highly emotional, or politically-laden tasks, in addition to more rationally-based tasks?

Finally, future research might refine and enhance the approach used in this study by examining different tasks with varying levels of difficulty, considering groups of varying size, precisely isolating the nature of GDSS impacts on group processes, and taking the methodology out of the laboratory and investigating the use of GDSS in actual organizational settings.

## About the Authors

R. Brent Gallupe is associate professor of information systems at the School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Ontario. He holds a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. His research interests include group decision support systems, management of end-user computing, and the evaluation of information systems. His research has been published in Management Science, Data Base, and the Proceedings of the 1986 International Conference on Information Systems.

Gerardine DeSanctis is associate professor of management information systems at the University of Minnesota. She holds a Ph.D. from Texas Tech University. Her research interests concern the design of systems to support managerial decision making and the effects of information system use on individuals and groups within organizational settings.

Gary W. Dickson is a professor of management information systems at the Curtis L. Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota.
