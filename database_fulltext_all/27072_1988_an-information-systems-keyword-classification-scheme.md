## Abstract

Information systems researchers, while helping others build better information systems, have done little in supporting their own information needs. The large amount of knowledge accumulated in IS today has created a situation where the researcher needs some sort of organizing mechanism to map the IS territory and relate its different components. A keyword classification scheme performs such a task through its content descriptors, systematically listed to show their relationships. This paper proposes a keyword classification scheme for IS. The scheme contains over 1,100 keywords and consists of nine top-level categories (reference disciplines, external environment, technological environment, organizational environment, IS management, IS development and operations, IS usage, information systems, and IS education and research), each of which is divided into subcategories.

Keywords: Information search and retrieval, IS research frameworks, diffusion of IS research, IS literature

# Need for an IS Keyword Classification Scheme

Considerable developments have occurred in the IS field over the last two decades. While in 1968 the University of Minnesota's doctoral program in IS was the only one of its kind, nearly 60 schools were recently reported as having Ph.D. programs in IS (MIS Interrupt, 1986). In the early 1970s there existed only a handful of journals where IS research papers were published; in 1983, more than 35 such outlets were listed (Hamilton and Ives, 1983). The increases in the number of IS conferences and IS research centers are further evidence of the expansion that has taken place in the field. Along with this increase in IS research activity came an increase in the number of papers and articles published; a rough calculation indicates that currently there are over 2,000 IS papers already published in journals with more than 300 new papers appearing every year.

This growth in IS research is currently putting a heavy burden on the IS researcher. In earlier days a researcher could get a good grip of the field by reading most, if not all, IS papers being published, and keep abreast of the small number of research projects being carried out. Today, however, it is impossible to achieve such a high degree of awareness. In addition to the increases in the volume of research output, the growth of the IS field has meant greater specialization and division into subfields. Faced with such an information overload, many IS researchers have sought assistance from computerized information retrieval systems. Unfortunately, currently available databases do not adequately cater to the needs of the IS research community. From an IS viewpoint, one of their major weaknesses is the lack of an agreed-upon list of keywords for describing IS contents. Moreover, when asked to assign keywords to their articles, IS researchers face an added difficulty, that of identifying terms describing the contents of their paper without the benefit of having a list of descriptors specific to the IS field.

An IS keyword classification scheme would not only help alleviate the problems mentioned above, but would also provide a description of the field, introduce a common language, prevent the proliferation of synonyms, and enable the development of IS research databases with high levels of user performance characteristics. Such a keyword classification scheme is proposed here. The article first describes some of the salient design considerations for building a classification scheme, followed by an assessment of the current status of existing IS classification schemes and related information retrieval tools. Then, the procedure followed in developing the proposed scheme is described. Subsequently, the resulting scheme is presented. Finally, a study conducted to assess the scheme and the results of the study are discussed.

## Design Considerations

## The usefulness of keywords

The content identifiers that are assigned to articles and papers serve researchers in a number of ways (Communications of the ACM, 1981). Keywords can be used for quickly identifying topics covered by a given research document, thus allowing the researcher to focus on specific areas of interest. Libraries and individuals use keywords for filing or bibliographic purposes. Studies that analyze the content of research publications and the research trends in a given domain (Hurt, Elam and Huber, 1986) can also rely on content identifiers. When researchers make requests from computer-aided information retrieval systems, keywords become valuable search tools. Keywords assist researchers in locating documents of interest, in relating documents to each other (thereby relating similar topic areas), and in predicting the relevance of individual articles through the use of index terms (Salton and McGill, 1983).

The assignment of keywords to a document is the outcome of an indexing process by which, according to Salton and McGill (1983), “the documents or information items are first converted to a special form using a classification or indexing language” (p. 11). The indexing language is a set of terms and rules for assigning terms to documents. Indexing terms can be controlled or uncontrolled. According to Salton and McGill (1983):

Many experts feel that an uncontrolled indexing vocabulary which in principle can include the whole variety of the natural language introduces too many opportunities for ambiguity and error. Hence, a limited indexing language is often advocated in which the terms available for content identification are rigidly controlled. This permits the control of spelling and the elimination of synonyms by referring to unique accepted terms for each synonym class, and by identifying semantically related terms (p. 54).

A keyword classification scheme is such a controlled indexing language. To be successful, it should be exhaustive, clear, systematic, flexible, expandable, and have a clear terminology.

## Scheme components

A keyword classification scheme includes four components: schedules, a notation, an index, and an organization for maintaining and revising (Foskett, 1977). The schedules, the central part of the classification scheme, are the index vocabulary (i.e., the set of index terms, listed in a systematic manner to show their relationships).

The notation is a shorthand symbol for a category in a classification scheme and may be composed of numbers, letters, arbitrary signs, or a mixture of these (Wynar, 1972). The function of the notation is to show the existing order of the schedules, thus enabling easy and rapid identification and organization of the different categories.

Conducting a keyword search in a classification scheme, which may contain several thousands of systematically arranged terms, can be relatively complex. An index is therefore necessary so that terms employed in the classification scheme can be easily found. An alphabetical list of all terms and subjects included in the classification scheme showing the relationships existing between the descriptors is called a relative index. In addition, it is also possible to construct a specific index, which would contain one entry for each subject of the classification scheme, to provide further support to the search process.

As experience with the Computing Reviews classification scheme (Communications of the ACM, 1981, 1982) has shown, revision and maintenance are of utmost importance if the scheme is to remain useful and usable. According to Foskett (1977), continuing revision requires an organization rather than an individual. He has found that “those schemes which have relied on the genius of their compilers without the backing of an adequate organization, have gradually fallen into obsolescence, whereas those schemes which have adequate backing continue to progress" (p. 196).

## Schedule descriptors

The major effort in building a classification scheme is the construction of its principal component, the schedules. This activity consists of specifying the set of descriptors and arranging them in a meaningful structure.

According to Wessel (1975), there are three classes of descriptors. Content descriptors describe the contents of a document. These can be single-term or multiple-term expressions, but each should be regarded as an indivisible whole. In any given field,

the number of descriptors should range from 500 to 5,000. When one approaches or goes below the lower limit, the resulting indexing will probably be too general and lacking in detail for useful retrospective searches. When one approaches or exceeds the upper limit, great complications arise within the systematization often leading to formal inconsistency and even more often producing inconsistency of usage. Once again the benefits of systematization and transferability of indexing and search procedures are lost. One is back in the chaos of free-text indexing (p. 48).

The systematic arrangement of the content descriptors is far from being trivial. In developing a classification scheme, one of the critical design questions is whether to organize the domain (e.g., the IS field, or the literature) (Wynar, 1972). Ideally, the organization should be based on the domain in order to make the classification scheme conceptually complete and logical to the researchers in the field. This approach also avoids the shortcomings of building a scheme by organizing the existing literature. For one thing, such a classification scheme misses out on those aspects of the domain that have not yet been researched. In addition, the scheme would be biased toward the interactions between the elements of the domain (e.g. management of end-user computing) rather than the elements themselves (management, end-user computing). Furthermore, since the scheme would represent both the elements and their interactions, it would need constant adjustments as new interactions are researched. Thus, the scheme would not reflect the conceptual structure of the domain and would need constant updating as the field evolved.

Document descriptors describe the physical characteristics of a document, such as its format (e.g., journal), type (e.g., microfilm), bibliographic information (e.g., date, author).

Meta-descriptors describe descriptors' relational attributes with other descriptors. For example, RT (related term) is a meta-descriptor that indicates that the descriptor for which it is used is conceptually linked to another descriptor.

## Current Status of IS Classification Schemes

Currently there exists no keyword classification scheme specifically developed for IS. A number of related tools and sources are available, but they each have important flaws from an IS perspective. These tools and sources are grouped into three categories and reviewed below.

## IS literature reviews and research frameworks

The first category consists of IS literature reviews and research frameworks on specific topics. A literature review on a specific topic usually provides some conceptually meaningful organization for that topic while at the same time provides in-depth coverage. Examples of these in IS include a review of the determination of information requirements (Bariff, 1977), implementation of management science models (Ginzberg, 1974), individual differences in (Zmud, 1979), and user involvement (Ives and Olson, 1984). The shortcoming of such reviews is that they cover only a limited number of topics and lack the breadth of research frameworks, which essentially define the IS field.

A number of IS frameworks exists today, and some of the better known include those of Chervany, Dickson, and Kozar (1971), Gorry and Scott Morton (1971), Mason and Mittroff (1973), Lucas (1973), Mock (1973), Nolan and Wetherbe (1980), Ives, Hamilton, and Davis (1980), and more recently, Culnan (1986, 1987). The problem with frameworks, however, is that by necessity they are too broad and have too few categories. Still, they provide natural starting points for developing a domain-based scheme.

## Databases and library search tools

The second category includes databases and library search tools containing IS keywords. While these are not based on a comprehensive IS keyword classification scheme, they nonetheless employ IS keywords organized in some way. Examples of such databases are The Business Periodical Index, Infor II, and Social Sciences Citation Index. In addition to their inadequate coverage of the IS literature (i.e., not indexing all IS journals), these databases often structure their keywords in ways that do not make sense from an IS perspective. This frequently leads to difficulties in information retrieval efforts, as illustrated by the following example.

A researcher wanted to review previous research on user information satisfaction. For the keyword user information satisfaction, the database provided few or no references. On the other hand, using satisfaction as the keyword resulted in an avalanche of references, the large majority of which the researcher did not need. If the researcher had tried user satisfaction as the search term, however, the articles dealing with user information satisfaction would have probably been missed, thus forcing the researcher during the search process to use all the synonyms he or she could remember. Given the large number of synonyms in the IS field (a problem discussed next), the researcher faces a serious risk of not finding all of the relevant material. An IS document retrieval system developed around an IS keyword classification scheme would considerably reduce these problems.

## Keyword lists and existing classification schemes

The third category of search tools available to the IS researcher consists of keyword lists and classification schemes. With the exception of the Computing Reviews classification scheme (Communications of the ACM, 1982), MIS Quarterly's list of terms (MIS Quarterly, 1985), and the taxonomy proposed by Hurt, Elam, and Huber (1986), there are no published keyword lists or classification schemes specific to IS. Some journals require the listing of keywords, but the common practice is to leave the number of and choice of keywords to the authors of an article. This lack of control on the choice of indexing terms has led to an anarchic proliferation of synonyms. For example, the following terms have all been used by various authors as keywords for information requirements determination: systems analysis, user needs assessment, information analysis, requirements specifications, requirements definition, information requirements analysis, detailed study, requirements analysis. Numerous examples of similar term multiplicities can be found in the literature. These redundancies provide little use to researchers conducting literature reviews and to developers of computerized information retrieval systems.

The Computing Reviews classification scheme (Communications of the ACM, 1982) is the only published keyword classification scheme that explicitly recognizes IS topics. Unfortunately, this keyword list and classification scheme is not very useful for IS researchers because it was designed for computer science, which views IS as a subtopic. The IS descriptors used for the scheme are too few in number and scattered across several subtopics. As such, the scheme does not cover in sufficient detail the extent and variety of IS research. For example, consider the category computing milieux (K) and its subcategory, management of computing and information systems (K.6). The bulk of IS research to date fits into this subcategory, but both the small number of entries contained and their ordering do not reflect the way IS research and IS textbooks generally treat those subjects. An examination of K.6, a portion of which is shown in Table 1, illustrates these points.

Given the realities of the IS field, a more meaningful ordering of these topics could be achieved by creating a category called IS development, which would have subcategories such as development strategies (with subsubcategories iterative design, prototyping, etc.), life cycle activities (which would include the subsubcategories of information requirements determination, IS design, programming, testing, documentation, and post audit), IS development methods and tools (again, with subsubcategories such as data flow diagrams, structured analysis, etc.), IS project management, and implementation. As exemplified by the subcategory K.6.1, the Computing Reviews classification scheme does not treat most IS subjects in sufficient depth and is flawed from two design perspectives: its IS descriptors are very few in number, and the structure used to organize the descriptors is not meaningful from an IS viewpoint.

MIS Quarterly's keyword list (MIS Quarterly, 1985) provides another source of keywords for the IS researcher. While this list contains keywords describing topics solely of interest to the IS research community, it lists only 115 terms. This is too small a number compared to the minimum of 500 keywords that would be required to do justice to the extent and variety of IS research. Furthermore, since the list is only structured alphabetically, it does not reflect the organization of IS knowledge established in the IS community.

Finally, while the taxonomy proposed by Hurt, Elam, and Huber (1986) provides a structure to its keywords, its scope is limited in that it was developed for DSS research and contains only 25 keywords. In view of these weaknesses, none of the three schemes examined meets the requirements for a comprehensive IS keyword classification scheme.

## Development of an IS Keyword Classification Scheme

The development of a keyword classification scheme is, in many ways, an introspective look into the field for which it is being built. In IS a number of efforts have been made that reflect the nature and status of the field. These include conceptual studies focusing on IS and its underlying disciplines (Data Base, 1982), proposals for research frameworks (Chervany, Dickson, and Kozar, 1971; Gorry and Scott Morton, 1971; Ives and Davis, 1980; Lucas, 1973; Mason and Mittroff, 1973; Mock, 1973; Nolan and Wetherbe, 1980), surveys attempting to identify key issues as seen by practitioners (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Hartog and Herbert, 1986), and empirical studies examining researchers' formal communication systems (Hamilton and Ives, 1980, 1982, 1983) and assessing the intellectual development of IS (Culnan, 1986, 1987; Culnan and Swanson, 1986). Among these studies, some are useful in that they help specify the domain of IS research and provide a foundation for the classification scheme. More specifically, previous frameworks provided an initial structure for the scheme, while the other types of studies complement and help fill the gaps in the structure.

We examined the frameworks suggested by Chervany, Dickson, and Kozar (1971), Gorry and Scott Morton (1971), Mason and Mittroff (1973), Lucas (1973), Mock (1973), Nolan and Wetherbe (1980), and Ives, Hamilton, and Davis (1980). This comparison led us to conclude that the Ives, Hamilton, and Davis framework was the most complete and suitable structure as a starting point for the development of a classification scheme for IS.

Several factors contributed to this decision. The lves, Hamilton, and Davis framework groups IS variables into three large categories: the environments, the processes, and system characteristics. This is a simple, yet comprehensive coverage of IS topics at the highest level. A second factor was the general acceptance and relative recency of the framework. Its recency ensures that the evolved state of IS is being captured. In addition, its general acceptance, as evidenced by the lack of rejection or questioning of their framework in the seven years since it has been published, indicates that it conforms to many IS researchers' view of the field. Another important factor was that the framework has been tested by examining how well it accommodates IS dissertations. The generally positive results attests further that the model adequately captures the structure of IS research.

Subsequent to the literature review and the decision to employ the Ives, Hamilton, and Davis framework as the first version of the classification scheme, we evaluated this initial structure in terms of how meaningfully it could incorporate keywords from the IS literature. The first evaluations were conducted in brainstorming sessions, during which keywords were nonsystematically picked from various issues of top IS journals. In addition, textbooks on various topics (e.g., database, behavioral science) were consulted to create and order many subcategories. During these sessions, as more and more keywords were being introduced the restructuring of the overlapping categories of the Ives, Hamilton, and Davis framework, as well as the creation of numerous subcategories, became necessary. As a result of these initial evaluations, a rough, but workable classification scheme containing about 600 keywords emerged (Barki, et al., 1986).

Subsequently, the authors began a process of expanding and refining the preliminary scheme. With this objective, all the keywords used in articles published in MIS Quarterly since the first issue, and in Information & Management since 1977 were compiled. In addition, keywords listed by IS papers in Communications of the ACM, Management Science (keywords listed since August 1978), and Decision Science (keywords listed since January 1980) were assembled. After eliminating duplications, the list contained close to 2,000 keywords. Then, the authors examined and discussed each keyword in the list until they agreed on where in the classification scheme it fitted best or whether it necessitated the creation of a subcategory.

A relative index and an alphabetical index of the scheme have been prepared, but in view of their length they are not presented in the paper.

This is due to the overlap between some of these categories. For example, in the Ives, Hamilton, and Davis model, IS management activities are common to both IS development environment and IS operations environment. Also, organizational characteristics are included both in the user environment and in the organizational environment. We recognized these overlapping categories and reorganized to create the following top level categories: technological environment, IS management, IS development and operations, and IS usage.

## Reference disciplines

The creation of a reference disciplines category was necessitated by the need to incorporate the relevant concepts that IS researchers import from those disciplines. Examples are: cognitive style and role conflict (from behavioral science), utility of information (from information theory), strategic planning (from management theory), and goal programming (from management science). Various papers on IS and its underlying disciplines (Data Base, 1982), as well as empirical studies conducted by Culnan (1986, 1987), provide conceptual and empirical support for the creation of this category.

## Environment component of IS

In the scheme, the environment component of IS is divided into three top-level categories:

the external environment (B), the technological environment (C), and the organizational environment (D).

The external environment covers such areas as economic environment (e.g., public sector), legal environment (e.g., software protection), political environment (e.g., regulation), and social environment (e.g., computers and society).

The technological environment incorporates hardware and software concepts, such as computer organization, computer networks, operating systems, DBMS, programming languages, and software packages.

The organizational environment includes organizational characteristics (e.g., size), organizational functions (e.g., marketing), task characteristics (e.g., complexity), and organizational dynamics (e.g., organizational change).

## Process component of IS

The process component of IS is represented in the scheme by three top-level categories: IS management (E), IS development and operations (F), and IS usage (G).

The structuring of IS management (E) in the scheme reflects a view of management that sees its functions as including planning (e.g., critical success factors), organizing (e.g., centralization/ decentralization of IS), staffing (e.g., data administration), evaluating (e.g., cost/benefit analysis), and controlling (e.g., chargeback). To these are added several second-level categories, including hardware resource management (e.g., hardware selection), software resource management (e.g., software selection), data resource management, IS security (e.g., public key encryption), and IS management issues (e.g., information technology adoption).

The next top-level process category is IS development and operations (F). The development part of this category includes second-level categories that relate to systems development subjects such as: development strategies (e.g., prototyping), life cycle activities (e.g., information requirements analysis), testing, development methods and tools (e.g., data flow diagrams), project management (e.g., chief programmer teams), and implementation (e.g., user involvement). The operations component of this category incorporates activities such as input operations (e.g., data validation), data processing (e.g., file updating), and database operation (e.g., retrieval).

The final top-level process category of the scheme is IS usage (G). This category groups the IS research topics in: organizational use of IS (e.g., competitive use of IS), users (e.g., end-users), type of IS support (e.g., personal IS), type of access (e.g., chauffeured access), and type of processing (e.g., online).

## Information systems

Information systems (H) contains IS keywords and topics related to IS products. As such, it includes types of IS (e.g., transaction processing systems, MIS, DSS, expert systems, computer-based communication systems, and document management systems). In addition, IS products related to various application areas are also listed (e.g., marketing IS, health IS, human resource IS). Also, this category incorporates components of IS products (e.g., user interface, knowledge base), and IS characteristics of IS products (e.g., graphics).

## IS education and research

The IS education and research (I) top-level category was created to include self-referential research in IS (i.e., inquiries into the status of the IS field itself), educational aspects (e.g., IS curriculum), trends in IS research (e.g., diffusion of IS research), and historical aspects of IS (e.g., history of computing). The MIS curriculum factor found in Culnan (1987) shows the relevance of this category.

## Assessment of the New Classification Scheme

In order to incorporate the reviews of a large number of people, the research team presented the classification scheme to other IS researchers during the final stages of its development process. While the feedback obtained was highly positive and indicated that the scheme was essentially sound, a more formal assessment was also undertaken. This evaluation had three objectives; evidence was sought for the scheme's completeness, the soundness of its structure, and its convergence and standardization potential.

To perform the assessment, a three-part questionnaire was designed. The purpose of Part I was to assess the scheme's completeness. Here, the respondents were asked to read five abstracts and, without referring to the classification scheme, to list five keywords for each abstract. Whether these keywords or their synonyms are contained in the scheme gives an indication of its completeness. Part II of the questionnaire provided the respondents with a top view of the classification scheme (the two highest hierarchical levels), and asked them to assign each of ten randomly chosen keywords to the appropriate class. The extent to which the respondents assigned the keywords to their actual classes provides an indication of the soundness of the scheme's structure. Part III was similar to Part I in that it asked the respondents to assign keywords to abstracts. However, this time they were asked to select keywords for five additional abstracts using the complete classification scheme, which was separately provided. The degree to which the respondents choose the same keywords for a given abstract suggests the extent to which the scheme can bring convergence and standardization to the indexing of abstracts.

Five sources were used for abstract selection: all articles, published in MIS Quarterly (1977-1987), Information & Management (1977-1987), ICIS Proceedings (1980-1986), Management Science (1970-1987) and Communications of the ACM (1970-1987). From each of the five sources, two abstracts were randomly chosen to create a sample of ten abstracts. In the case of articles chosen from Management Science and Communications of the ACM, a consensus among the authors was required for deciding whether a given article was to be considered an IS article or not.

The questionnaire was distributed to 86 IS researchers (faculty and IS doctoral candidates) across Canada and the United States. Forty-eight questionnaires were returned, giving a 55.8 percent response rate. Tables 4 through 6 summarize the results obtained.

## Assessment of completeness

Table 4 shows for each abstract the number of different keywords the respondents selected without using the scheme. The classification scheme was searched in order to find these keywords or their synonyms. As Table 4 strongly suggests, the classification scheme's degree of completeness is highly satisfactory: out of a total of 343 keywords proposed by the respondents only nine were not contained in the scheme. Furthermore, three of these nine — research paradigm, research program and clinical studies — have closely related terms in the scheme (research frameworks, IS research agenda, and case study and action research). The remaining six keywords were research variables, MIS definition, MIS theory, social psychology, industrial psychology, and prescriptive model. While these keywords are not actually contained in the classification scheme, several related terms are: research frameworks, information systems, psychology, and models.

## Assessment of structure

The results of Part II are shown in Table 5. When asked to indicate the class to which each of the ten keywords belonged, the respondents identified the correct class (i.e., the actual top-level category in the scheme) at an average rate of 68 percent. The percentage of correct responses ranged from a high of 96 percent to a low of 39 percent.

Given the diversity of perspectives existing in the IS field and the respondent's lack of familiarity with the scheme, these results are encouraging. The below-average percentages were for the following keywords: message systems, data description languages, database management systems, and construction industry. An examination of the classes to which the respondents assigned these keywords helps explain the low rate of correct classification.

## Assessment of convergence

Table 6 shows, for each abstract in Part III of the questionnaire, the three keywords most frequently selected from the classification scheme, and the percentage of respondents having chosen each keyword. On the average, over the five abstracts, the most frequently chosen keyword was selected by 74 percent of the respondents; the second most frequently chosen keyword averaged 62 percent; and the third averaged 43 percent. It should be noted that prior to completing this part of the questionnaire, the respondents had not yet seen the full classification scheme, which contained over 1,100 keywords and for which no alphabetical index was provided. Under these circumstances, the results obtained show a remarkable degree of convergence.

## Conclusion

The level of maturity reached by the IS field has made the development of a classification scheme for indexing its literature both feasible and desirable. Currently, most IS researchers seem to have their own schemes. These usually consist of a small list of 50 or so terms, with each term written on a file folder containing papers and articles. The researchers use their expertise to search their files and to relate article contents. Some researchers have developed more complex schemes to support personal computerized data bases of IS research literature. Some of these schemes are fairly advanced and are organized hierarchically.

While useful to the individual researcher when searching in his or her personal (computerized or not) database, such classification schemes have the major flaw of being customized to their users, not to the field. When the need arises to explore an unknown territory in the literature, there are no guidelines to help the researcher who often wanders about, wasting valuable time and effort. A classification scheme specifically developed for IS can go a long way in supporting IS researchers. The authors feel that the scheme presented here, reached after more than three years of evaluation and refinement, has evolved into a robust structure that comprehensively reflects the status of IS and IS research. The general feedback received from IS researchers and the assessment results support this view.

However, the proposed scheme is by no means final. Like any large-scale software, it contains and will continue to contain “bugs,” no matter how much it is improved. In a soft and evolving domain, this is inevitable and, to a certain extent, natural. It is felt that the scheme has now reached such a state that further enhancements can be made only through the feedback of the IS community at large. The authors welcome all critiques, comments, suggestions, and recommendations.
