# IMPACT OF ORGANIZATIONAL MATURITY ON INFORMATION SYSTEM SKILL NEEDS\*

By: Izak Benbasat
Albert S. Dexter
Robert W. Mantha

## Abstract

This article presents the results of a study which analyzes skills perceived as useful by information systems (IS) managers and systems analysts in IS organizations of different levels of maturity. These IS skills were examined under two major subgroups of generalist/managerial and specialist/technical skills as well as under more detailed categories of organizations, people, society, systems, computers, and models skills. Generalist, i.e., organizational and people skills, were rated highest. Based on these rankings, which deviated little between managers and analysts and across the maturity spectrum, recommendations concerning graduate IS curriculum are suggested.

Keywords: Information systems curriculum, information systems maturity, information systems stage hypothesis

ACM Categories: 1.52, 3.51

The objective of this study is to examine the skills deemed useful to information systems managers and systems analysts in information systems (IS) environments of different levels of maturity, and based upon the results, make recommendations for IS curriculum.

## Introduction

In 1972 the ACM Curriculum Committee on Computer Education for Management presented its report of curriculum recommendations for graduate professional programs in IS [1]. The report outlined the need for professional programs in IS and determined the knowledge and abilities required by IS specialists to work effectively in their field. These abilities were grouped into six skill categories: people, models, systems, computers, organizations, and society.

A study undertaken by the University of Minnesota's Management Information Systems Research Center [4] sought to empirically test the ACM Curriculum Committee recommendations. The researchers surveyed IS practitioners to determine what skills they possessed, what skills were thought to be useful, and how employees, supervisors, and users varied in their perceptions of the skills that were possessed and deemed to be useful. They selected and clustered 97 skills into the six skill categories mentioned in the ACM report and argued that three of these skill categories (organizations, people, society) contained many skills which were more closely related to the information analysis activity, and three others (systems, computers, models) were more closely related to the systems design activity. They re-grouped the skills into two major clusters which correspond to these two basic activities, calling them generalist and specialist skills. The Minnesota study also introduced a new skill category called “performance” which did not overlap with the generalist or specialist skills. The performance category included fourteen items, such as accepting responsibility, performing tasks accurately, and cooperating with others.

Subjects from fourteen firms were categorized as employees, supervisors, or users within one of twelve IS positions ranging from MIS director to junior programmer. The firms surveyed had mainframe hardware expenditures averaging slightly over \$75,000 per month. The IS director generally reported through the vice president of Administration or Management Services. By perusing the Minnesota Study, one could infer that these were mature MIS environments. Table 1 gives the rank ordering of skills by employees, supervisors, and users.

This study concluded that the ACM course recommendations appear to be too technically oriented, and that based on the survey there was a more pronounced need for performance, people, and organizational skills. The researchers speculated that specialist skills would continue to be in demand in small to medium size data processing organizations. However, they did not have enough empirical results to support this claim, since they had only surveyed larger and more mature organizations.

The study described in this article is an attempt to determine what skills are required by organizations of various levels of maturity. Can the Minnesota study conclusions be applied to smaller and less mature IS organizations? Are the needs for skills different in IS organizations of varying levels of maturity?

These are valid questions to be asked in today's IS environments, where there is an ever increasing proliferation of systems implemented in smaller organizations. The implications of such questions for curricula in Information and Computer Systems are important. Universities may be training graduates in IS who do not possess the skills required to function effectively in the area. To answer the questions posed above, this study analyzes the impact of the maturity levels of IS organizations on the skill requirements of two IS positions: the IS manager and the systems analyst. Based on these findings, recommendations for IS curricula are presented in the final section of this article.

## Organizational Maturity of IS

The stage hypothesis originally stated by Nolan in 1973 [12] contends that the pattern of EDP expenditures, when plotted over time, is “S” shaped. This “S” hypothesis was later questioned by Lucas and Sutton who wrote, “…however, the results do not invalidate the concept of a stage model. The analysis suggests an alternative model of budget growth and separation between models of budgeting growth and growth stages in the development of the computer resource," [6, p. 254].

Nolan wrote that the “S” shape reflects how the organization learns to assimilate EDP technology. Based on this curve, Nolan distinguished four stages of growth: Stage I—initiation, slow steady growth; Stage II—contagion, high exponential growth; Stage III—control, absolute declining growth; and Stage IV—integration, managed steady growth. At each stage the characteristics of the tasks for managing the EDP organization (controlling, organizing, and planning the EDP effort) are different.

The stage hypothesis was restated by Gibson and Nolan after conducting a more intensive study of the “growth processes” at work at each stage [3]. They defined three growth processes: 1) building an applications portfolio, 2) building an EDP organization, and 3) building an EDP planning and control system. Nolan then introduced a fourth growth process called “user awareness.” This process represented the extent to which users develop an understanding and awareness of the opportunities and limitations of computer technology.

Nolan has now developed a six stage model $[13]$ . However, what is important for this study is not the number of stages per se, but the “growth process” characteristics as the organization moves from lower to higher stages.

It is assumed that as an organization moves from Stage I to higher stages, it becomes more mature. Organizations displaying characteristics of Stage IV, or VI, are considered to be more mature than organizations displaying characteristics of Stage I. Therefore, the “growth process” characteristics describing the stages can be used as a means of measuring an organization’s maturity in IS.

We used the Nolan stage model and processes at these different stages to develop eleven maturity criteria, shown in Table 2. These criteria could be described in the following manner:

More mature IS organizations as compared to less mature organizations:

1. have larger expenditures on hardware;

2. have more experience with computers and computer technology;

3. are located at a higher level in the organizational hierarchy, rather than being placed under functional area authority;

4. have users who are more interested and capable of participating actively in the systems development effort;

5. have senior management who plays a greater role in the overall planning and control of the IS organization;

6. have more formalized objectives which are tied to overall organizational objectives;

7. have a formal budgeting process integrated with the overall organizational priorities;

8. have evaluation criteria based less on clerical cost savings and more on overall contribution to the organizational goals;

9. have a formal plan integrated with the overall organizational plan;

10. have chargeout systems, enforce documentation standards, and require periodic progress reports; and

11. have an applications portfolio which includes a greater proportion of tactical and strategic level applications.

## Research Methodology

## Organizational maturity questionnaire

We used these eleven criteria to develop a maturity questionnaire which classified the companies participating in the study as more or less mature in terms of their IS activities. All eleven criteria were weighted equally to arrive at a composite maturity score.

Each question tried to measure the organization against the criteria by requesting the manager to select an alternative which best described the situation in his/her organization. In the questions the alternatives were ranked in ascending order of maturity. As an example, the question related to “objective setting” is shown below:

Which of the following best describes the means of determining the objectives in your IS organization?

1. Objectives are set informally by the IS manager.

2. Objectives are set formally by the IS manager.

3. Objectives are set by top management.

4. Objectives are derived from overall business objectives in cooperation with top management.

Each question was given a maturity score which corresponded with the choice numbers on the left. The sum of the maturity scores on questionnaire questions was used to classify the organization into a more/less mature category. These procedures are explained in the “Research Study” section later in this article.

## The skill questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of 99 skill items. Table 3 illustrates the distribution of the skills within the various categories. A sample of the skill items is shown in Appendix 2.

The majority of skills items surveyed were a subset of the 111 skills developed for the Minnesota study. Fourteen of these 111 skills belonged to a category called “performance.” After closer analysis it was observed that these performance skills were so essential for their tasks that the respondents consistently gave them a high usefulness score. Examples of these were: "Ability to perform tasks accurately," and "Ability to plan and organize work assignments." Since these skills failed to discriminate among respondents and were consistently ranked above the other skills, they were not included in this study's skill set.

## Hypotheses Tested

## Hypotheses concerning IS managers

H1: More Mature Organizations: Generalist vs. Specialist Skills

Managers of more mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than for specialist skills.

## H2: Less Mature Organizations: Generalist vs. Specialist Skills

Managers of less mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for specialist skills than for generalist skills.

## H3: Generalist Skills: More vs. Less Mature Organizations

Managers of more mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than will managers of less mature organizations.

## H4: Specialist Skills: More vs. Less Mature Organizations

Manager of less mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for specialist skills than will managers of more mature organizations.

Nolan indicates that the more mature the IS organizations, the more managerial/generalist skills and the less technical/specialist skills the data processing manager will require $[11]$ . He argues that less mature IS organizations will require managers to perform many of the technical functions of systems analysts and programmers, whereas more mature organizations will require managers to perform more managerial and administrative functions.

## Hypotheses concerning systems analysis

H5: Generalist Skills: More vs. Less Mature Organizations

Systems analysts in more mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than systems analysts in less mature organizations.

H6: Specialist Skills: More vs. Less Mature Organizations

Systems analysts in less mature IS organizations will perceive a greater usefulness for specialist skills than will systems analysts in more mature organizations.

One of the main characteristics of mature organizations is the increased penetration of computerized IS throughout the organization.

Consequently, systems analysts in more mature organizations will need to effectively interact with people in many functional areas. They will need a better understanding of the organization's structure, functions, and information needs. It is hypothesized that the analysts of more mature organizations will require more generalist skills than analysts in less mature organizations, since most applications in the latter organizations would be serving one functional area. One of the characteristics of Nolan's stage hypothesis is that organizations in Stage I, which are less mature, use the computer in one functional area, usually accounting. Therefore, analysts in such organizations will require less generalist skills than analysts in more mature organizations.

## H7: More and Less Mature Organizations: Generalist vs. Specialist Skills

Systems analysts from both more and less mature IS organizations combined, will perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than for specialist skills.

Systems analysts design systems to satisfy organizational needs and therefore must be competent with social systems, related to generalist skills, as well as with technical systems, related to specialist skills. In the Minnesota study, it was written that “specialist skills would be elevated, for example, over generalist skills for both senior and junior programmers. At all other positions generalist skills were deemed more useful,” [4, p. 15]. It is therefore hypothesized here, in accordance with the results of the Minnesota study, that systems analysts will have a greater need for generalist skills than for specialists skills.

## Hypotheses concerning differences of IS managers and systems analysts

H8: Generalist Skills: Managers vs. Analysts

IS managers will perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than will systems analysts.

## H9: Specialist Skills: Managers vs. Analysts

Systems analysts will perceive a greater usefulness for specialist skills than will IS managers.

H10: People, Organization, Society Skills: Managers vs. Analyst

IS managers will perceive a greater usefulness for people, organization, and society skills than will systems analysts.

H11: Systems, Computer, Model Skills: Managers vs. Analysts

Systems analysts will perceive a greater usefulness for systems, computer, and model skills than will IS managers.

The Minnesota study did not compare skill requirements across job positions. Because of inherent differences between the tasks performed by IS managers and systems analysts, it is expected that there will be a significant difference in the usefulness attributed to the various skills. In particular, managers are expected to perceive a greater usefulness for generalist skills than systems analysts, and analysts are expected to perceive a greater usefulness for specialist skills than IS managers. In order to make a finer distinction between skills, Hypotheses 10 and 11 were formulated using the six skill categories. It is hypothesized that people, organization, and society skills will each be more useful to managers than to analysts and that systems, computers, and model skills will each be more useful to systems analysts than to managers.

## The Research Study

The mail questionnaire approach was selected as the means to collect data for the study. Questionnaires were mailed to the participating organizations in a package containing a cover letter, one organizational maturity questionnaire, $^{1}$ three skill questionnaires, and a return envelope.

The package was personally addressed to an executive in the information systems department of the organization surveyed. This executive, the IS manager, was requested to fill out the organizational profile questionnaire and one skill questionnaire. The executive was also asked to give the two remaining skill questionnaires to two of his/her systems analysts for completion.

## The organizations surveyed

Thirty-five companies returned the questionnaires. $^{2}$ A sensitivity test was performed on a subset of these 35 companies by eliminating three of the companies which had scored immediately above and three which had scored immediately below the median maturity score. There was no significant difference in the statistical results, described in the next section, when all 35 companies versus a subset of 29 was used in the analysis. Consequently, it was decided to use the whole sample rather than use a subset. These companies were classified into 17 more mature and 18 less mature organizations based on the median score of the organizational maturity questionnaire scores distribution. $^{3}$

The descriptions of a typically more and typically less mature IS organization in the survey is outlined in Table 4. The typically more mature organization shows many of the characteristics belonging to Nolan's Stage IV, however, it cannot be classified as a fully mature Stage IV organization. The typically less mature organization exhibits many characteristics of a Stage I organization. Therefore, this article compares “more” or “less” mature organizations rather than “mature” and “immature” organizations.

Although the Minnesota study did not give many details describing the organizations surveyed, it mentioned that the average monthly mainframe hardware expenditures averaged slightly over \$75,000, which is more than the \$40,000 hardware average obtained for the more mature organizations in this study. However, it is important to note the reduction in the cost of hardware and emphasis on software expenditures during the last five years. For non-budgetary characteristics, such as reporting structures, organizations in both studies could be considered equivalent in terms of maturity.

## Statistical methodology

Two types of different but complementary statistical analyses were performed for testing the hypotheses: t-tests and profile analysis. $^{4}$ The means for testing various hypotheses by using t-statistics were generated by a large number of individual skills, e.g., about fifty skills were aggregated to calculate generalist and specialist skill means. However, it is possible to have similar means and variances for two treatment groups, e.g., managers and analysts, and at the same time have different patterns of individual skill distributions within a treatment group. An extreme case would be if analysts viewed ten questions in a category of absolute necessity and ten questions of no use. If managers viewed the same questions in exactly opposite fashion, the overall means and variances for managers and analysts would be equal. Therefore, profile analysis was used to test if mean profiles of skill items followed the same pattern in different treatment groups.

As a basic example of the type of results which can be obtained from profile analysis the following figure is presented.

The first hypothesis tested in profile analysis is that of parallelism. In Figure 1 the mean scores of both systems analyst and managers on society skill items seem to be following a parallel pattern. In the above case, the hypothesis of parallelism was not rejected, significance level 0.72. The next question of interest, when the profiles are parallel, is the difference between treatment means. The farther apart the lines in Figure 1 are from each other, the greater difference between the treatment means. In the above case, the hypothesis of equal means for the society skill set of managers and analysts was rejected at the .007 level of significance.

If the profiles are not found to be parallel, for example, if the profile lines cross each other, then there is a treatment response interaction. In this case, the test of equal treatment levels has no meaning (see [9], pp. 153-154 for details).

## Discussion of the Results

The tests performed on the hypotheses were divided into three groups: those concerned solely with IS managers, those concerned solely with the systems analysts, and those comparing skill requirements of both.

## IS managers

The findings relating to hypotheses 1 through 4 are summarized in Table 5. As was the case with the Minnesota study [4], generalist skills were perceived as more useful to IS managers of more mature IS organizations than were specialists skills (p < .001; hypothesis 1 was supported). $^{5}$ The same was true for managers of less mature IS organizations (p < .001; hypothesis 2 was not supported).

Nolan has indicated that the skill mix of managers of less mature organizations would contain more technical skills, and that the skill mix of managers of larger organizations would contain more managerial skills $[11]$ . The discrepancy between Nolan's hypothesis and these findings could be explained in two ways. First, Nolan was describing “skills contained” whereas this study has examined skills perceived to be useful. Secondly, in less mature organizations, managers might have little use for the highly specialized skills (e.g., model skills) included in this study. Consequently, the “technical” skills of Nolan's hypothesis might not correspond to the “specialist” skills of both the Minnesota study and the present study. One can only try to explain this discrepancy since Nolan did not describe explicitly what was meant by the word “technical.”

The results also indicated that generalist skills were perceived to be more useful to managers of more mature organizations than of less mature organizations (p < .03, hypothesis 3 was supported). This reflected the additional administrative and managerial tasks performed by managers of more mature organizations. The perceived usefulness of specialist skills remained comparatively low and was not significantly different between managers regardless of organizational maturity (hypothesis 4 was not supported). This suggests a basic knowledge of the more specialized aspects of IS is necessary for managers, but that the requirements for these skills do not vary across organizations of varying levels of maturity. In addition, some of the specialist skills were of little use to managers of both more and less mature organizations (e.g., most of the model skills and many of the computer skills as shown in Table 8, p. 31). Consequently, these skills did not differentiate between the two groups of managers and therefore contributed to this lack of difference in perceived usefulness for specialist skills.

## Implications for University Curriculum

The study described in this article examined if the maturity level of an IS organization has any influence on the type of skills required of managers and systems analysts. It is an extension of the Minnesota study $[4]$ which sought to empirically test the ACM Curriculum Committee recommendations $[1]$ . Whereas the Minnesota study sample contained only more mature organizations, this study analyzed data from IS organizations on a wider maturity spectrum. The findings indicate that for both IS managers and systems analysts, generalist skills are perceived to be more useful than specialist skills, regardless of maturity levels.

The ACM curriculum report proposed that IS would be successful only if a balance was achieved between the emphasis placed on organizational and technical factors, and that systems in the past had failed because organizational factors had been too often ignored. Based on this premise, a thirteen course graduate curriculum was proposed. Of the 13 courses, 6 courses were related to generalist skills and 7 courses were related to specialist skills. This program was augmented by an additional generalist course suggested by McFarlan and Nolan [8]. The Minnesota study made recommendations to stress the people and organizational materials in the curriculum.

The results of this study also indicate the ACM curriculum recommendations place too much emphasis on the technical aspects of information systems. More emphasis should be placed on acquiring people, organizational, and society skills rather than specialist skills. This recommendation is fully supported by Keen and Scott Morton ([5], pp. 52-53) who wrote:

This ACM curriculum contains comparatively little material on managerial and behavioral topics. As a technical curriculum, it is intimidating even for an incoming student let alone for a busy manager who can update her or his knowledge only casually.

At the same time there appears to be a trend in organizations to put a non-technical individual, frequently a former line manager, in charge of the information systems and data processing activities of the firm. A recent informal survey of one major hardware manufacturer's principal customers shows that 22 out of 45 major accounts had appointed a new head of the MIS function within the past 12 months. This reflects, in part, the organization's attempts to get computer-related activities focused on real business problems. It suggests a recognition of the need for the individual in charge of data processing to be a real manager; technical competence is less important than ability to supervise personnel, plan, and maintain a business perspective.

Graduate programs in information systems which place a heavy emphasis on the managerial and administrative aspects of IS appear well suited to satisfy the present skill requirements of IS managers and systems analysts. Emphasis should be put on course content which combines the knowledge of both IS, organizational behavior and organizational development, which is concerned with the introduction of change and modification in organizations.

Social researchers in the IS field have long argued that the IS professionals who have come up through the programming/technical ranks to reach analysis/managerial positions have tended to search for solutions within a narrow technical framework. It is therefore encouraging to find out, as the results of this study show, that the practitioners in the analysis/managerial ranks concur with the researchers that generalist skills are necessary for the successful IS practitioner. IS organizations should encourage their programming/technical people who want to advance through the organization to enroll in graduate or continuing education courses to make up for the requisite generalist skills they lack. It is hoped that with the better trained specialist, the IS solutions can be found within the broad technical, managerial, and behavioral context of the organization.

## Appendix 2. Examples of Skill Items

## People

— Communicate and interact with non-computer oriented people

— Recognize and remove personality problems which interfere with job completion

## Organizations

— Identify in an ongoing organizational situation the key issues and problems of a given functional area (production, finance, marketing, etc.)

— Determine the positive and negative impacts of a specified information system on specified parts of the organization

## Society

— The privacy issue and its implications on databanks (both private and public)

— The impact of computers on industrial, clerical, and managerial positions

## Systems

— Prepare effective user documentation for either a portion of a system or an entire system

— Evaluate system performance and make needed adjustments to a system after implementation

## Computers

— Develop (design and implement) databases using a generalized database management system (IMS, TOTAL, ADABAS, IDS)

— Develop structured (or modular) programs

— Analyze communication systems (estimate line and terminal requirements, volume and message length, queues, etc.)

## Models

— Recognize the appropriate management science (operations research) model for situations commonly encountered

— Formulate and solve complex simulation models
