# User Response to an Online Information System: A Field Experiment

By: Charles R. Franz
University of Missouri
Department of Management

## Abstract

This study examines the impact of an information system on user attitudes and perceptions of job characteristics. These impacts were assessed with a quasi-experimental research design for a sample of nurses in a major urban hospital. After testing for and rejecting possible confounding influences of the pretest scores, comparisons were made between pre-implementation and post-implementation attitudes and perceptions. Results showed little change in job perceptions and no significant change in attitudes toward the system. The importance of these findings is discussed within the context of alternative models for explaining the organizational impacts of information systems.

Keywords: Computer systems implementation, organizational impacts, project management

ACM Categories: C.5.1., K.4.2., K.6.1.

Concern over the organizational impacts of computer-based information systems has been a prevailing issue in the literature since the 1950s. Early speculation and research stressed the impact which systems had on job design, user motivation, and attitudes [1, 25, 34]. One scenario portrayed the computerized workplace as inhuman and workers' jobs as robbed of their enriching elements. These deskilled jobs were predicted to produce alienation, job dissatisfaction, and lowered motivation to perform [7]. An alternate scenario portrayed the computer as liberator, able to remove monotony and make jobs more enriched and satisfying [4, 14]. Unfortunately, research findings have been too inconsistent to support either scenario strongly. As a result, we know little more about the computer's impact on work today than we knew in 1960.

In a recent review of research on computer impacts, Attewell and Rule [2] concluded that such inconsistencies could potentially be overcome through new research strategies which consider situational causes of computer impacts. Attewell and Rule were especially critical of the job impacts area because it has tended to focus on public bureaucracies, has failed to distinguish between different levels of computer-based technology, and has not accounted for “novelty effects,” that is, for the temporary increase in interest that occurs when a new technology is first implemented. Their primary recommendation was to control for these problems by including other possible explanatory variables such as firm size, ownership, industry type, and nature of the workforce. By collecting data on impacts from a large number of work sites, the influences of these variables could be assessed in conjunction with the variable of primary interest, the impact of computer-based information systems. Thus, they suspect computer impacts to vary depending on the situational factors that apply to different work settings.

A second strategy for improving the quality of job impacts research is to establish more rigorous controls through experimental design. Research on information systems has used experimental methods, but most often in laboratory settings where the ability to generalize to real work sites is questionable.

A more promising strategy is the field experiment to determine computer impacts in a particular site while controlling for other possible causes of changes in work behavior. The field experiment establishes control through research design rather than through statistics, as in the survey approach recommended by Attewell and Rule. Ideally, both types of studies should be done to improve the current cloudy picture of computer impacts on job content and user response.

The purpose of this article is to examine the impact of an online information and communications system on users' attitudes and their perceptions of task scope. The research was conducted in a hospital using nurses engaged in primary health care as subjects. The study employed a quasi-experimental research design to assess the change in nurses' perceptions of task characteristics and their attitudes toward the information system. Previously developed and validated questionnaires were administered before and after system implementation. The results show very little change in user response, leading to the conclusion that the system had little impact on nurses' jobs or their attitudes. These findings are discussed in the light of three different models of computer impacts: the technological imperative, and organizational imperative, and the interaction perspective.

## Prevailing Models and Previous Research

Relevant previous research includes studies of computers and job content and studies of users' attitudes toward computer-based information systems. This research has been valuable in providing instruments for conducting field experiments and in establishing general expectations about computer impacts. However, previous models and research findings are incapable of supporting directional hypotheses about the way in which computer systems may affect job design and user response. Past research is based upon the notion of a technological imperative, that computer technology will have some impact in the work-place, but the nature of that impact has been difficult to predict. We will review the prevailing model of a technological imperative, and then look at selected studies of computer impact on jobs and user attitudes.

## The Technological Imperative

Most of the early research on computer impacts in organizations was based implicitly on the notion of technological imperative. This idea argues that computer technology is the direct cause of changes in the organizations which employ computing. Job content, control, organizational structure, and interorganizational relations are among the targets of technological change which the computer can affect. The technological imperative has been most convincingly argued by Ellul [13], who viewed technology as the cause of its own applications in society. Technology is self-sustaining and autonomous rather than a response to existing problems. Computers, in particular, seem capable of finding new problems and requiring application to a larger and larger set of activities.

While this idea generally serves the critics of modern technologies, such as Winner [39], many computer advocates express the belief that computers are the cause of positive change in organizations. Certainly most computer vendors convey this image with their portrayal of office automation as liberating and enhancing the work of clerks and professionals. This “dual scenario” is symptomatic of the more general problem of formulating hypotheses about computer impacts. Studies are easily found which support both positive and negative outcomes of computer implementation. Thus, while the technological imperative prevails as a dominant paradigm influencing research on computer impacts, the inconsistency of empirical findings sheds doubt on any attempts to specify directional hypotheses about impacts.

## Research on Computers and Job Content

The technological imperative is clearly reflected in the literature on computers and job content. There is an implied assumption that computing is the causal variable affecting job content, but the research shows conflicting findings about the extent and direction of impact. For example, Leavitt and Whisler [25] predicted that management jobs would become more routine as a result of computing being used to centralize management decisions. Anshen [1] offered the opposite argument that jobs of managers would become more enriched as the computer took over routine aspects of their work. The few studies that addressed the issue offered little definite evidence of an impact one way or another. These inconsistencies continue to the present.

In more recent research, Björn-Andersen [5] examined the impact of an online data entry system on work design and job satisfaction in a bank. The implementation of the computer system required substantial changes in several of the jobs. About half of the clerks affected felt that the variety of their jobs had increased; more than half felt that feedback had increased; and most felt that their autonomy had remained about the same. Overall, 83% of the clerks felt that the computer implementation was a positive event for the bank and themselves. Satisfaction with the psychological content of the jobs was over 70% for all clerks. Much of the satisfaction with the system seems to have resulted from an upgrading of many positions from strictly clerical work to work which involved a variety of interactions with the computer system.

In another study, Bjørn-Andersen, Eason, and Robey [6] studied users of computer systems in eight European and American organizations and reported an increase in complexity, feedback, ability to address new challenges, and new methods and procedures. Users were satisfied with these changes. However, these researchers also reported increases in routine, standardization, work pace, and work load for the same tasks. Paradoxically, these changes were also evaluated favorably, a finding that is somewhat inconsistent with theories of job characteristics which predict positive evaluations with enlarged task scope but not with standardization.

These findings were confirmed in an independent study by Kling [22, 23], who used a modified version of the Job Diagnostic Survey [18] to assess the impact of computer use on managers and clerks in municipal governments. Generally, respondents perceived computing to enlarge their jobs by increasing skill variety, feedback, ability to deal with others, and task significance. However, Kling also reported increases in job pressure associated with the introduction of computing. As Bjørn-Andersen, et al. [6] had found, computing simultaneously enriched and routinized work, while producing positive reactions to both kinds of change. The extent of these changes was not large in Kling's study, but the computer was perceived as being responsible for them.

## Studies of User Attitudes Toward Systems

While usually related to research on job content, studies of user attitudes are more concerned with attitudes toward more specific system features and impacts. These attitudes are often found to be associated with the degree of acceptance and use of the system.

Lucas [26] presented a descriptive model of implementation which posited relationships between user attitudes and decision style, system use, and various situational and personal factors. His research showed that user attitudes toward the computer system were related significantly to ratings of system quality. He also argued that system use correlated strongly with user attitudes. A study of attitudes toward the implementation of a medical information storage and retrieval system revealed that leaders' attitudes toward the system were likely to affect actual use of the system.

Swanson [37] studied the relationships between a priori involvement, MIS appreciation, and system use. MIS appreciation was measured as group attitudes. Swanson found that a priori involvement was related to high system use and that favorable attitudes acted as a positive intervening variable. He also found that a priori involvement led to more favorable attitudes, which in turn led to higher system use.

Schultz and Slevin [35] developed an instrument to measure attitudes toward OR/MS models. They maintained that if a model required a large change in user attitudes, the possibility of a successful implementation would be reduced. They theorized that the greater the fit between model (or information system) and the organization using it, the higher the probability that the organization would use the model. The smaller the attitudinal change required, the higher the probability of users accepting and using the system.

Robey [32] used Schultz and Slevin's measure and found a high correlation between attitudes and voluntary use of a system designed to support industrial sales personnel. In particular, attitudes about the system's impact on user performance, goals, interactions with designers, and urgency of project schedule were strongly associated with actual use.

In an earlier study, Robey and Bakr [33] examined attitudes toward the implementation of new information technology in an office environment. Respondents preferring intrinsic job aspects reacted positively to the new technology initially. Their attitudes toward the new system, however, were negatively correlated with time spent using it. Respondents with extrinsic (social) work preferences reacted less favorably than the intrinsics, but their attitudes were positively correlated with number of hours spent using the system. This research emphasized the importance of novelty effects in implementing systems.

The literature reveals that most of the attitude research in implementation focuses on the relationship of user attitudes and system usage. There seems to be a general consensus that favorable attitudes are associated with successful implementation. However, few research studies have examined the concept of attitude change as a result of system implementation, although Schultz and Slevin [35] emphasize the importance of measuring pre- and post-implementation attitude as a way to access system impacts. Also, while the MIS literature advocates post-implementation evaluations to ensure that the new system is meeting its original goals, post-implementation evaluations of attitudinal changes are virtually non-existent.

## Hypotheses

Based on the literature, it is difficult to hypothesize the direction in which attitudes and perceptions of task characteristics should change. The literature on task changes shows evidence of both negative and positive changes, but the extent of change is often minimal. The attitude studies are primarily concerned with associations between attitudes and computer use and do not consider how attitudes about a job may change after system implementation. For these reasons, two sets of nondirectional hypotheses were formulated about the effect of implementing an online information system on job perceptions and attitudes. Each set includes hypotheses for the specific job characteristics and attitudes measured. Stated in null form, these hypotheses are:

H1: The implementation of an online information system will not have significant impact upon users' perceptions of six job characteristics:

1.1 Variety

1.2 Autonomy

1.3 Feedback

1.4 Dealing with others

1.5 Task Identity

1.6 Friendship

H2: The implementation of an online information system will not have significant impact upon three attitudes of users toward the implemented computer system: 2.1 Performance

2.2 Goals

2.3 Urgency

The research method described below attempted to reject these null hypotheses, thereby supporting the theoretical hypothesis that computer technology does have an impact on workers' perceptions of job content and workers' attitudes.

## Research Methodology

## Field Setting

The research took place in a large hospital with over 400 beds, delivering more than 150,000 patient days of health care. The hospital had been using data processing for many years, but not in the nursing area where a new system was to be implemented. Very few computer systems had been installed in the operational units of the hospital. Data processing was used primarily for financial accounting and billing.

The primary area of the hospital where the new system was to be installed and studied was in nursing. The nursing function required thousands of communications with other functions throughout the hospital, such as the pharmacy, clinical laboratory, dietary department, and admitting desk. Thousands of documents and phone calls were manually issued daily. The old system was error-prone, resulting in many problems and much frustration for the nurses.

To cope with the errors and delays the hospital decided to acquire an electronic communications and order entry system known as the Patient Care System (PCS), developed jointly by the Duke University Medical Center and IBM. The initial functions of the system emphasized data collection, communications and electronic message sending. Specific functional features of the PCS included collection of admissions data, electronic communication between nursing departments and the admitting department, and a generalized message sending capability among all locations having a printer.

The PCS operated through a configuration of menu-driven screens located in each nursing station. Users would select menu items with a light pen and make subsequent choices when prompted by the system. The terminals were located at the desk of each nursing station, and use was required, not optional.

## Research Design

A quasi-experimental research design was employed in this field study. Data were collected both before and after implementation of the Patient Care System. The data collection periods were separated by approximately eleven months, with the first data collection taking place during December 1982, and the second taking place in November 1983. The design is described by Campbell and Stanely [9] as a separate-sample pretest-post-test design and is diagrammed below.

Groups A and B represent randomly selected sample groups. Observation 1A is the pretest, intervention is the treatment (which in this case is the PCS implementation), and observations 2A and 2B are the post-tests.

This design is not a true experiment because both groups received the treatment, and there was no control group. For practical reasons, the hospital did not wish to delay the introduction of the system for any subset of nurses. The design did, however, permit an assessment of the effects of the pretest on post-test scores, a common threat to internal validity in experimental design [9]. Also, the effect of randomization was to control for other systematic sources of variance in the perceptual and attitudinal variable.

The treatment in this study consisted of learning system features and operating characteristics of the PCS system. It is difficult to consider system implementation as a classic experimental treatment because implementation occurs over a long period of time. However, all of the events that occur between a pretest and a post-test in any experiment are potentially capable of influencing post-test scores and thus might be considered as part of the treatment. The duration of this interval is usually longer in field experiments than in the laboratory and is a potential weakness of the design.

## Sample

Approximately 500 nurses were available for testing the effects of the PCS system. Prior to system implementation, 258 randomly selected nurses completed questionnaires (pretest measures). After the PCS system was implemented, 218 randomly selected nurses completed questionnaires (post-test measures). This quasi-experimental design produced the following three subgroups of users:

Group 1A: Nurses who only completed pretest questionnaires (n = 156)

Group 2A: Nurses who completed both pre- and post-test questionnaires (n = 102)

Group 2B: Nurses who served as a control on the questionnaire instrument and who completed only post-test questionnaires (n = 116)

Group 1A constituted 156 nurses who did not complete post-implementation questionnaires and were excluded from the data analysis. Because this is a large dropout rate, the possible differences between Group 1A's pretest scores and those of Group 2A were compared using a t-test. Four of the possible nine comparisons showed a significantly higher mean score for Group 2A than for 1A. The variables affected were Autonomy, Feedback, Dealing with Others, and Friendship. This suggests that results on these variables might be biased by the more positive perceptions of the nurses who continued in the experiment. It might also indicate that nurses with more negative job perceptions were less motivated to participate in the second round of questionnaires.

## Measures

A questionnaire was used to collect perceptual and attitudinal data. The job perception measure was the Job Characteristics Inventory (JCI) developed by Sims, Szilagyi, and Keller [36]. The JCI measures six dimensions of job characteristics which were originally developed by Turner and Lawrence [38] and later revised by Hackman and Lawler [17]. The six dimensions are Variety, Autonomy, Feedback, Dealing with Others, Task Identity, and Friendship. While conceptually similar to the more popular Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) [18], the JCI was felt more appropriate for this study for two reasons. First, the JCI was tested and validated in a health care environment. Second, previous research has revealed that the factor structure for the Job Diagnostic Survey is highly dependent on the idiosyncratic characteristics of the respondents [12, p. 184]. This finding was confirmed by a later study [16] which concluded that the JCI was a superior instrument to the JDS.

Responses to the JCI were scored with a five-point Likert scale. For example, one of the items in the variety scale asks “How repetitious are your duties?” Possible responses range from 1-very little, 3-a moderate amount, to 5-very much. Higher scores indicate that the respondent perceives a job characteristic to be present in greater degree on his or her job. For more complete information on the scale and its construction, see [36].

User attitudes toward PCS were selected from a seven factor instrument developed by Schultz and Slevin [35]. The three factors selected were performance, goals, and urgency as these were frequently found to be important in earlier research. Performance measures the perceived effect of the new system on the employees' job performance and performance visibility. Goals measures the perception of the new system's effect on making goals more clear, more agreeable to the employee, and more achievable. Urgency measures the perceived need for results even if costs are involved, the importance to the employee, their supervisor, and to top management. The Schultz and Slevin questionnaire also uses a five-point Likert scale. A sample item from the performance scale is "The accuracy of the information I receive will be improved by the PCS." Possible responses range from 1-strongly disagree, 3-uncertain, to 5-strongly agree. The higher the score for an item, the more favorable the attitude toward the PCS. For more complete information on the Schultz and Slevin instrument, see [35].

## Discussion

These results indicate little change in nurses' attitudes and perceptions caused by implementation of PCS. The general expectations of computer impact on job design and user attitudes is thus not supported. There appears to be little evidence of a technological imperative operating in the hospital. The only job changes to be affected were in the social interaction area. There was a decrease in nurses' perceptions of their opportunity to develop friendships with other people while performing their job. We shall first discuss the implications of this finding and then proceed to a broader discussion of the lack of significance found in the study.

Previous research has shown that systems can change patterns of social interaction both negatively and positively. Equipment can serve as a physical barrier, reducing opportunity for interaction [33], and long system response times can increase the opportunity to interact with coworkers [3]. In the hospital, probably neither of these situations applied. The reduced perception of friendship opportunities may, in fact, have been due to an extraneous factor, namely, announcement of a 170 person layoff just prior to the administration of the second wave of questionnaires.

With fewer nurses to perform the same duties, each nurse would have more health care tasks to perform, leaving less time for social interaction. Also, the departure of friends as a result of the layoff would undoubtedly affect the post-implementation scores. Thus, the decrease in this job characteristic may not be the result of PCS implementation, but rather the result of factors that could not be controlled in the field experiment.

These results also seem to raise broader doubts about the usefulness of the technological imperative as a general model for understanding computer impacts. While these findings cannot be used to support hypotheses from models other than the technological imperative, they are more consistent with alternative models of impacts that are less prevalent in the literature. Specifically, the results appear to fit the organizational imperative and the interaction perspective models. Hopefully future researchers will consider them worthy of more complete theoretical development and testing. Clearly this cannot be accomplished within the scope of this article.

## The Organizational Imperative

In contrast to the technological imperative, the organizational imperative suggests that computer systems should be developed to fit the organizational context for which they are intended. The basic idea behind this imperative can be found in the MIS literature where different types of systems are recommended for different decision environments [21] or organizational levels [15]. The objective of this imperative seems to be to reduce the extent of impacts which computer systems may have. By prescribing user involvement and user-friendly systems, the organizational imperative strives to create systems which fit the existing needs of users without changing the structure of the organization or of people's jobs.

Some modest empirical support can be found for the organizational imperative. Daft and Macintosh [10] showed that the information processing requirements of organizational departments varied according to the technology used by those departments to convert raw materials into outputs. They recommended that different types of information systems be used to process the information in these different departments. For example, where departmental work is easily analyzed and presents few exceptional cases, a routine transaction-processing system may be used. Since the work in the department is already likely to be routine, the information system is not likely to change jobs within the department to any significant extent. Where departmental work is not analyzable and presents many exceptional cases, larger amounts of qualitative information must be processed. In this case a different type of system, such as a DSS or communication system, might better fit the existing structure. Because the emphasis is on fitting the system to the information-processing needs of the organization, little change in the organization is predicted by the organizational imperative.

The hospital data are partially consistent with this perspective. The objective of the system was not to change major portions of the nurses' primary health care tasks, but rather to automate routine aspects of communication between departments. Given such modest objectives, it would be surprising if the PCS had produced major changes in nursing tasks at the hospital. Communication and order entry are important elements of the task, but they are by no means the primary responsibilities of nursing jobs. Health care functions were not affected by the PCS. Rather, the PCS was implemented in a fashion where it would not affect the salient aspects of nursing care.

The presence of any change in the organization would seem to violate the prescriptions of the organizational imperative. In this case, the reduced opportunity for friendships would seem to pose problems for the model of the organizational imperative. Such changes are perhaps best explained as unintentional or accidental impacts [6]. While we have tentatively attributed the reduced opportunity for friendships to the announced layoff, it is conceivable that the PCS itself had features which inadvertently created difficulties in making new friendships. From the standpoint of the organizational imperative, such a change would be considered a misapplication of the principle of minimum impact. Systems should be designed to fit the organization, not to change it.

Interpreted in this way, the organizational imperative imposes rather strict conditions on the design of information systems. In trying to avoid negative impacts, the approach risks becoming unrealistic. Whether the change in friendship opportunities was caused by the PCS or not, there are numerous instances of systems being implemented where there are organizational changes. This leads us to consider the organizational imperative to be of limited value in guiding research on the organizational impacts of computing.

## The Interaction Perspective

The interaction perspective model explains impacts as organizational changes which result from the interaction of an existing organizational context with the system design process [29]. Changes may or may not occur, depending on the objectives of the various participants in the design process. Changes may be evaluated as neutral, positive, or negative depending on who is making the evaluation. Instances of no impact may also be evaluated as neutral, positive, or negative. The interaction perspective draws attention to the process by which organizational changes occur when an information system is introduced in an organization.

Most recently, the interaction perspective has been used by Buchanan and Boddy [8] and by Dawson and McLaughlin [11] in research on computer impacts. Buchanan and Boddy studied the effects of computerized controls on operators in a biscuit-making company in Scotland. Through an intensive case study of the development and implementation of computerized controls, they determined not only what impacts occurred but why they occurred. Different effects were found for different jobs. For example, the use of computer-controlled dough mixing replaced the craft skills of the doughman, but the ovenman's skills were enhanced by the computerized packet weighing because it gave him more information and required more judgement and discretion. Buchanan and Boddy conclude that technology, management objectives, and existing physical and organizational structures combine in a complex way to produce impacts.

In a similar case, Dawson and McLaughlin studies the impact of a computer-based information system on British Rail [11]. Management's objective was to control the movement of empty freight cars within and between the marshalling yards. By implementing a system to monitor freight car positions in real time, British Rail removed power from the yard foremen and gave it to area freight assistants who operated the computer system. The organization structure was, therefore, modified to reflect the changes in information processing which the system enabled. By investigating the history of operations and policy making at British Rail, the researchers were able to establish the reasons for these changes and to explain why the impacts occurred.

Kling and Scacchi [24] have used the term "web model" to describe the interaction perspective and have contrasted web models with "discrete-entity" models. Discrete-entity models attribute outcomes of computer implementation to a set of discretely identifiable causes such as user acceptance, features of the technology, top management support, data availability, equipment configurations, and so on. By contrast, according to Kling and Scacchi, "web models make explicit the salient connections between a focal technology and its social and political contexts" [24, p. 3]. This requires an appreciation for the complex interactions among managers, system designers, and users, all of whom may have conflicting interests. Such an understanding is not likely to come about unless the researcher knows a great deal about the organization, the design history of the system, and the nature of the technology.

The interaction perspective requires a new theory about the organizational impacts of information systems. Mohr [33], in writing about the field of organizational behavior, has recommended that complex phenomena such as innovation and organizational change require “process” theory instead of “variance” theory.

Variance theory is like trying to explain a dependent variable by adding more and more independent variables into a large regression equation. Variance theory seeks to improve the predictive power of the equation by carefully choosing the right predictors and modeling possible interactions among them. Attewell and Rule [2] have recommended this approach for the computer impacts area.

Alternatively, process theory hopes to explain how and why outcomes occur in one situation by tracing the process through which various predictor variables interact with one another over time. Qualitative, longitudinal methods which capture the nature of the system development process are needed, rather than large sample methods which attempt to explain variance with regression equations.

In our study the ability to explain the absence of impacts would have been dramatically improved if we had collected data of this type. As it was, we formally conducted surveys at two discrete points of time. Access to the implementation process was more informal and depended on one of the authors' direct involvement as the Vice President of Information Systems. From this informal observation it is possible to identify some aspects of the development and implementation of PCS which help to explain the apparent absence of impact on the nurses' jobs and attitudes.

The nature of the implementation process at the hospital was characterized by favorable management attitudes and support of the system. The administration began approximately two years before system installation to create favorable impressions of the benefits of using online information systems in nursing duties. Almost a year before installation, a head nurse was made the leader of a user design team composed of five nurses. The head nurse was a very strong supporter of the new system and encouraged its use within all the nursing units. This degree of management support and user involvement is normally considered to be a factor in promoting positive attitudes among users $[26, 27, 28]$ .

Training was another process factor that may have been responsible for the absence of impacts. Three stages of training were conducted. First, a professionally produced videotape presentation was given to the nursing staff to explain the system to be installed. A question and answer period followed. Second, a live lecture and hands-on demonstration was provided by the head nurse. Nurses paired up and shared terminals to practice using the system. The final stage consisted of making a model system available to all nurses on a 24 hour basis. The model system contained online programmed instruction on how to use the system and a “help” mode to answer questions. The nursing design team also developed their own display screens for the system, including the logical sequence of screens and the decisions about display screen content. No system specialists were involved. After system implementation, maintenance functions to display screens were also performed by the nursing team.

While none of this information was collected systematically, we feel that it is important to include it in this discussion because it helps to explain, in retrospect, why there was little impact of PCS on job perceptions and attitudes. In research designed to test the interaction perspective's basic premise about the way impacts occur, this type of information would need to be collected more systematically over time. Our design is longitudinal, but not process-oriented. To explain organizational impacts (or just as important, the absence of impacts), more needs to be known about the interaction between the organization and the system development process.

## Conclusions

Two sets of conclusions can be drawn for both managers and researchers. First, our findings indicate a smooth implementation results in little reaction from the users. This finding is important because it means that a critical set of well established workflows was substantially changed without adversely affecting approximately 500 users. It infers that the social aspects of the system were sufficiently integrated with the technical aspects to avoid the type of major resistance that is often reported in the literature. The socio-technical perspective of information systems regards system implementation as successful if it achieves both social and technical objectives simultaneously. In other words, we may consider the PCS to be “organizationally valid” to the extent that it fits with its organizational context [30]. Achieving this fit was apparently facilitated through supportive management, user involvement during system design, and systematic training.

For researchers, we suggest that attention to the details of system implementation can help to explain why impacts happen and why they don't. This approach to research on impacts, however, is more demanding than either the technological imperative or organizational imperative models. To fully explain how and why a system affects an organization, research must be thorough enough to describe the process by which systems are implemented. The use of longitudinal research which employs both quantitative and qualitative methods is recommended to investigators interested in improving their ability to explain computer impacts.
