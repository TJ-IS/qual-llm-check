# The Use of Cognitive Mapping for Information Requirements Analysis

By: A.R. Montazemi
Management Sciences and Information Systems Area
Faculty of Business,
McMaster University
Hamilton, Ontario
Canada

By: D.W. Conrath
Department of Management Sciences
Faculty of Engineering
University of Waterloo

## Abstract

To complement and augment the present methods used for information requirements analysis, the application of cognitive mapping is introduced. The proposed technique, which involves the recognition of cause and effect relationships, appears to provide three benefits. It aids in the identification of irrelevant data. It can be used to evaluate the factors that affect a given class of decisions. And, most importantly, it enhances the overall understanding of a decision maker's environment, particularly when it is ill-structured. These observations are supported by a real world application of the approach to the evaluation of the performance of insurance claim representatives.

Keywords: Information requirements analysis, information evaluation, cognitive mapping, decision analysis.

ACM Categories: H.1.1, H.4.2, I.2.8, J.1

## Introduction

Designing an information system, whether it be manual or computer-based, is seldom a simple task. The relevant environment is likely to be complex, and often the decision maker lacks a full understanding of this complexity. Yet if one is concerned that an information system is used, the decision maker should be convinced that the system reflects both his needs and his perceptions of how the environment operates (e.g., [15]).

Several approaches already exist which recognize the above constraints and our purpose is not to replace them. Rather, we are suggesting that the use of the cognitive mapping technique might prove to be useful in a number of cases, particularly those involving ill-structured decision environments. In addition, we will show how this technique has been applied in the development of individualized information systems designed to assist in the evaluation of the performance of insurance claim representatives by their managers.

## Information Requirements Analysis

A number of tools are available to assist information analysts in the design of suitable information systems $[4, 7, 17, 18, 30]$ . However, most are useful primarily for routine, well-structured decision-making. The task becomes formidable when one considers the information needs of decision makers faced with ill-structured decision environments. To handle such situations, information requirements analysis (IRA) was developed (e.g., $[6, 13, 21, 22, 23]$ ).

An example of this approached is provided by King and Cleland [13]. They suggest that the entire procedure can be accomplished in eight steps:

1. Identification of the user set and interfacing organization,

2. Identification of decision areas,

3. Definition of decision areas,

4. Development of a descriptive model of the system,

5. Development of a normative model of the system,

6. Development of a consensus model of the system,

7. Decision model identification and specification, and

8. Specification of information requirements [13, pg. 288].

King and Cleland point out that the sequencing of these steps, as in most such processes, is in terms of the initiation rather than the completion of each.

The successful execution of this procedure requires a detailed understanding of the decision maker's decision environment and its information requirements. As Mintzberg, et al., [19] have noted, the solution to ill-structured decision problems rests mainly on the cognitive efforts of the decision makers. For this reason, the approach used by IRA typically involves a high degree of interaction between the end user (decision maker) and the analyst (e.g., [13, 23, 24, 25, 26]). Extensive user-analyst interaction is assumed to result in an in-depth understanding of the activities and accompanying decision processes within an organization.

Such an assumption may be valid when the decision environment is reasonably transparent and the nature of its complexity can be perceived by the decision maker. Difficulties arise when the complete structure of the decision problem is not apparent, and even with the aid of an analyst one can come to grips with only a fraction of its complexity. Despite the fact that this is a common occurrence for most managers, little work has been done on the process of determining information requirements $[30]$ . We know much more about how to model a decision situation and how to provide a decision maker with appropriate information, given that the parameters and their relationships have been identified, than we do about the processes of collecting the relevant data. While inputs and outputs may be correctly identified, little advice is given on how to determine the appropriate structure of the inputs to lead to desired outcomes. We present our work as a step in this direction.

While cognitive mapping is intended essentially to provide insight into the structure of a decision problem, it also provides advantages for information requirements analysis. The procedure that IRA follows, for example, makes use of values or weights for the information that is to be included in the information system. There are indications, however, that decision makers have difficulty in quantifying the value of the information content of their decision variables $[3, 11, 15, 28]$ . This is likely to be particularly true for decision environments which are not well understood. While reasonable values may be obtained in a rather straightforward manner for many well-structured decision environments, this is unlikely to be the case when the structure is unknown, especially since the structure itself may be the basis for the values. A cognitive map not only yields insight into the decision structure, but it can be used directly in several different ways to obtain relative values for the structure's elements.

Another issue, which is really an aspect of the above, is whether or not certain data are necessary for a decision. To ensure that decisions are in line with the goals and objectives of the organization as a whole, Munro and Wheeler [23] have suggested the use of critical success factor (CSF) analysis. Their approach gives structure to the decision maker-analyst interaction (interviews), thereby reducing the chance of including irrelevant data in the information system. Nevertheless, once a factor has been accepted as being “critical,” it would be useful to have another means to investigate whether or not this really is the case. A well developed cognitive map can provide such a test.

## Development of a Cognitive Map

A cognitive map is a representation of the relationships which are perceived to exist among the elements of a given environment. Taking any two of these elements, the concern is whether the state or movement of one is perceived to have an influence on the state or movement of the other (both static and dynamic relationships can be considered).

These are similar to the CAUSE relationships identified in the Event-State (ES) model of De and Sen [8]. Note that we are dealing with a mental or cognitive (i.e. “subjective”) condition rather than objective reality. Nevertheless, the link between the two is not spurious, as our perception of how an environment behaves is the result of personal observations (experience) and education.

The cognitive mapping technique has been usefully employed in the fields of international relations $[1, 12]$ , administrative sciences $[27]$ and management sciences $[9, 10, 14, 29]$ . Figure 1 displays a simple cognitive map.

To develop a subjective representation of the relationships between factors in semi- or unstructured decision problems, Eden, et al., contends that:

The principle criteria which need to be met by any technique which could be useful for this purpose should be that it could, first, capture complexity; second, capture peculiarity and idiosyncracy; third, capture generalities; fourth, capture particularity; and fifth, that it could be represented comprehensibly [10, p. 53].

As we will demonstrate, these criteria appear to be satisfied by our application of the cognitive mapping technique. Our approach essentially follows that taken by such researchers as Eden, et al., [10] and Klein and Cooper [14].

To construct a cognitive map, it is important to start with the purpose for which the model is being built. If the purpose is not well defined, the search for relevant variables (factors) is likely to lack direction and the model can easily grow to an unmanageable size. The next step is to identify the relevant factors, those which may influence a decision and can vary in their values. Constants are of little interest. Here is where the approach used by Munro and Wheeler [23] to identify critical success factors comes into play.

The final step, the actual construction of the cognitive map, requires finding causal relationships among factors. In the literature these have usually been derived from decision makers' statements [1, 10]. Another method is to use questionnaires structured specifically for this purpose (see Appendix A). Typically one only seeks to determine whether the relationships are positive or negative.

Figure 1. Cognitive Map for Director of Public Health

While it may be possible to develop more complex functional cause and effect relations, in most cases these would be based on such limited tangible evidence that their use would be questionable.

The procedures used for constructing cognitive maps satisfy the five criteria noted at the beginning of this section. For example, a cognitive map is based on the perceptions of a given decision maker, those which reflect his/her particular conditions. This individual identification of factors and their relationships captures “peculiarity and idiosyncracy,” the second criterion. Determining these relationships reflects the required “complexity,” the first criterion. In fact, the network of such relationships is often used as a measure of environmental complexity $[14]$ .

The capturing of “generalities” (criterion three) is precisely what the cognitive mapping procedure accomplishes, as most such maps are the product of an accumulation of knowledge over time. The intent is not to model a unique situation, but to build a framework that describes a decision environment that will be (and probably has been) faced a number of times.

Still, to be useful, this environment ought to be restricted in its purpose. This satisfies the fourth criterion, “particularity.” Where it is violated the relationships are likely to be so vague that they would be of little value in the development of an information system. Finally, if the cognitive map is restricted as suggested, it can be represented comprehensively. The contents of a map can be readily understood and its development, as we shall demonstrate, is not difficult. There is no mystery among the individual parts. The only surprise may come from some of the chains of influence that can be observed from the whole.

## The Value Of Cognitive Mapping

The principal reason for developing a cognitive map is to obtain insight into the structure of the decision environment that might be difficult to obtain otherwise. At a minimum the approach that we present provides a means for gathering information about a decision environment that enhances our understanding of it. If the map did no more, the cognitive map would be an information system. But it is more than that, it is capable of yielding insights that might otherwise be overlooked. In addition, the map may be used as direct input into the design of an information system. Please note that the intention here is not to replicate the decision process. Cognitive mapping provides a tool to support decision-making.

Let us consider a simple example. Suppose Figure 1 is the cognitive map of a city's Director of Public Health. The director has assumed that if the city's sanitation facilities were improved, the incidence of disease would go down. This happened in the short run, but the incidence of disease per 1000 residents once again continued to increase. While each direct relationship could be justified, the indirect effects could not be understood until the entire map was displayed. Better sanitation facilities, causing an initial improvement in health, led to an increase in the city's population. This growth led to more garbage, more bacteria, and therefore more disease, overcoming the initial advantages of improvements in sanitation. The apparent contradiction of one of the relationships (better sanitation facilities lowering the amount of disease) indicates that the problem should be investigated. The larger map suggests why this is the case. What it does not do is suggest what should be done. This is the realm of the decision maker and the reason for the Director's existence.

As we have mentioned, another major reason for developing a cognitive map is to gain insight regarding the relative information value of the various factors involved. For example, look at the cognitive map portrayed in Figure 2. It is a simple one, indicating just positive and negative causation. Let us assume that we are interested in comparing the value of B versus that of D. We find that B influences only E, whereas D affects B, C and G. Furthermore, D has an indirect effect (one step removed) on A, E, F and G. Unless the state of E is our primary concern, it is likely that information about D is far more valuable than information about B. This may be less obvious, and certainly more difficult to explain, without the cognitive map.

There are a number of different ways in which a cognitive map could be used to provide a specific measure of value of information for each element. The value of knowing the state of B versus that of D in Figure 2 is dependent on more than just the number of elements that each affects. The relative importance of each of those affected elements (in terms of their use for the set of decisions that are to be made) is also of concern. In addition, the impact of a cause and effect relationship depends upon the number of other causal links that influence the same element. The smaller the number of such links, the greater will be the value of each. The measure of value is a subject which could be discussed at considerable length, it is therefore being covered in a separate paper [5]. One aspect of this question, however, deserves mention here — the identification of irrelevant data.

Irrelevant data can be defined as those data pertaining to factors which have no effect on the decision outcome. The identification of critical success factors should limit this problem. Nevertheless, the use of a cognitive map, by showing the connectivity between factors, can aid in determining whether or not data about a particular factor are relevant to a given decision.

Returning to Figure 2 to illustrate this, suppose we are concerned about the state of F as it will affect a decision to be made. Furthermore, as is often the case, we want to know why F is as it is. We notice that both A and C influence it directly, and D has an affect on F through C. On the other hand, neither B nor E nor G influence F either directly or indirectly. They are irrelevant in terms of its outcome. Such a determination is frequently difficult to make by direct inquiry or observation. With the use of a cognitive map it becomes quite clear.

We may also wish to know if the system as a whole is acting as we expect. If not, this may signal trouble. For example, let us assume that one finds that the value of state F is relatively high, but those of A and C are relatively low. The cognitive map suggests that low A and low O would lead to a low F, and yet we find that this is not the case. Thus, we may wish to investigate further to determine what is happening within the system since it is not behaving as we perceive that it should. It may be that other factors ought to be considered as affecting F in this particular situation, factors that were not originally thought to be relevant.

We now describe a real world application of the cognitive mapping technique, both to demonstrate the procedures used and to verify its value empirically.

## An Empirical Study

## Purpose

The objective of the study was to develop an operational information system that would support decision makers facing an ill-structured environment. In addition, we wanted to construct systems that would be used by a number of persons facing similar decision environments. This would permit us the opportunity to conduct an empirical analysis of the effectiveness of the application of the cognitive mapping technique and, in particular, to undertake an experiment using hypothetical data.

The appropriate circumstances were found in a large Canadian property and automobile insurance company. The general problem was the evaluation of the performance of subordinates. The specific issue examined was the evaluation of insurance claim representatives by their superiors — the district claim managers. The decision environment was clearly ill-structured. The evaluation exercise was complex, subjective, and each manager had his or her own approach. Systems designed to support performance evaluation were developed and tested for ten district claim managers.

## Choice of Factors

To start, one claim manager was asked to indicate which factors he used to evaluate the performance of the claim representatives in his district. We were seeking the critical success factors pertinent to the performance of claim representatives. It was explained to the manager that these factors might include both hard data (e.g. number of new files handled) and soft data (e.g. work habits or personality). He was interviewed after he had had two weeks to consider the matter. The list developed was then discussed with him to define precisely the meaning of each factor.

The second step was to determine if these same factors were taken into account by other claim managers in the sample when reviewing the work of their claim representatives. To this end, a letter, along with the initial list, was sent to each manager asking them to add or delete items as they saw fit. In their replies all of the remaining nine managers unanimously agreed that seven of the eight items on the list were the factors that they used. These are shown in Table 1.

## Developing the Cognitive Map

Each subject was asked to indicate all the possible cause and effect relationships which might exist among the relevant factors. Since all ten subjects were in agreement on the factors, the same structured questionnaire (see Appendix A for an example) was used for everyone. This was administered individually by one of the authors to ensure that each subject fully understood what he or she was to do.

A question was posed for each possible cause and effect relationship. As one can see from the example given in the appendix, all we sought was to determine the existence and direction of the relationship. We did not attempt to get the managers to gauge its strength.

The final step was to derive a valancy matrix representing the cognitive map of each subject. The entries were +1 (positive cause and effect relationship), 0 (no cause and effect relationship), and -1 (negative cause and effect relationship). An example taken from one of our subjects is shown in Table 2. This claim manager felt, among other things, that the more open files a claim representative has, the fewer will be the number of files handled. Also, the more complex the files being handled, the greater will be the number of solicitors involved. In addition, the manager perceived no effect caused by changes in the payout/reserve ratio.

The valancy matrix was then incorporated into an information system designed to detect "exceptional" performance by each claim representative. Exceptions were identified in two distinct ways (for a more complete description see [20]). One concerned performance as measured by a given critical success factor. Limits of acceptable performance along each factor were established for each claim representative by the application of another structured questionnaire. For each instance the manager in question was asked for upper, expected, and lower limits of acceptable performance. The basis for choosing the upper and lower limits was that the manager wanted to be notified if these were exceeded. This procedure was undertaken individually for each of the claim representatives because the limits varied considerably according to the experience and education of the representative.

The second exception occurred when the cause and effect relationships in the cognitive map were not consistent. As an example, if factor C was related positively with both factors A and B, and the values for the latter two were both high, the value of C would be expected to be high as well. If it were low, performance would be considered to be exceptional and thus brought to the attention of the manager.

Note that an information system was developed for each district claim manager. Each manager had a support system that reflected his/her own cognitive map and definitions of factor limits indicating exceptional performance. The systems were based on individual perceptions of information needs and not on some normative model. We recognize that the appropriateness of such an approach is an issue subject to debate, but that is outside the focus of this article.

## Results

Each subject identified sufficient cause and effect linkages so that no element appeared to be irrelevant in terms of all of the others. Each factor was directly related to at least one other factor. Hence, the cognitive maps suggested that the initial set, based on critical success factor methodology, was appropriate. This result, however, does not indicate if the cognitive map was of value. To do this we examined its role in the context of an information system.

Each subject was provided a number of hypothetical situations regarding the performance of their claim representatives. In 120 of these cases their performance, as indicated by measurements presented for the relevant factors, was acceptable. None of the limits defining “exceptional” performance were exceeded. In 39 cases, however, all of the relationships affecting a given factor were opposite in sign to that expected. In the other 81 situations there was at least one consistent (supporting) cause and effect relationship.

For each of the 120 hypothetical cases the managers were asked if they “saw a problem,” one that warranted further investigation of a subordinate’s performance. In every one of the 39 cases where a factor had a value that was not supported by at least one cause and effect relationship, the claim managers responded yes. In all of the other 81 situations the said no. Thus, even though the values of all factors taken individually were acceptable, a claim representative’s performance was perceived to be acceptable if an only if there were no factors with values inconsistent with the values of every one of the factors supporting it.

Among the 39 cases which aroused suspicion, the managers were typically unable to express exactly why they detected a problem in the subordinate's performance. They recognized individual inconsistencies, but seldom were they aware of the total pattern which led them to question performance. Yet, independent of the situation's complexity, this could be ascertained quickly by an analysis of the manager's cognitive map.

## Discussion

What the cognitive mapping technique does is improve our understanding of complex decision environments by the application of simple data collection and analysis techniques. While there are other means for doing the same thing (one reviewer mentioned that he/she used group meetings), they require that the participants be capable of recognizing the complex relationships. Based on data collected about pairwise relations, a cognitive map can display the fact that the significance of the value of a given factor is dependent upon the values of all its causal factors. The map can also display a chain of complex indirect relationships that might be important in understanding the information content of a given system. In essence, the cognitive mapping technique is somewhat akin to the use of simulation techniques. It permits the building of a complex, interactive system based on simple, understandable components.

The use of cognitive mapping, in the context of information requirements analysis, provides the system designer with several advantages. First, as we have shown, he/she can better understand the value of one factor as it relates to the values of other factors. Information that is dependent upon complex relationships can be more easily defined. This would allow system designers to better identify each pertinent decision area, as well as develop a more complete model of the decision environment (steps two through four of King and Cleland [13]).

Second, although not demonstrated empirically, the cognitive map might be used as a basis for determining, independently, the information value of any given factor based on the extent to which it directly or indirectly influences other factors. In particular, a cognitive map can identify factors that are irrelevant to a particular decision.

Third, because the technique is simple to use, it enables one to individualize the information system, as we did in our example. Each manager could determine the pertinent factors in his/her decision environment, their relationships with each other (the cognitive map), and the control limits which are to be applied. Often the value of an information system lies at the level of an individual decision maker. Where objective criteria for evaluating information are lacking and subjective ones must be used, most decision makers feel that the system should reflect their own perceptions. They, after all, are the ones that must accept the responsibility for the outcomes of their decisions. Steps five, seven and eight of King and Cleland's approach, are now accomplished. However, step six was not accomplished, thus raising concerns about the value of developing a consensus model.

A number of other issues also remain to be resolved as generalizations should not be based on a single experiment, no matter how dramatic the results. One of the key questions concerns the value of information, a main argument for the use of the cognitive mapping technique. Just how valuable is the information provided by the application of this approach? Empirically, we developed an information system that identified 120 out of 120 cases correctly, using a particular definition of inconsistency that appeared to agree with the needs of the managers tested. Yet three issues can be raised.

First, the value of a cognitive map depends upon how the data are analyzed. The definition of inconsistency used here was strongly related, at least empirically, to the managers' uneasiness about subordinate performance. What other forms of analysis should be tried? For example, what about tracing indirect as well as direct influences?

Second, given an empirical identification of analyses which yield valued information, such as the inconsistencies identified earlier, how likely are they to arise? This experimental database was hypothetical, and thus could reflect any distribution of circumstances. Yet, no historical analysis was made of past data (which would have been very difficult given the manner in which they had been recorded) to determine the likelihood of such inconsistencies.

Finally, if all of the above issues are resolved in favor of the application of the cognitive mapping technique, how should it be integrated into information requirements analysis? This article has described one approach. Other approaches deserve examination as well.

This study was undertaken in a limited context. Nevertheless, the results would seem to be sufficiently strong to warrant further attention. If an application of cognitive mapping provides information of value that is otherwise not easily identified, then it would appear to be sensible to include such a technique in the design of an information system.
