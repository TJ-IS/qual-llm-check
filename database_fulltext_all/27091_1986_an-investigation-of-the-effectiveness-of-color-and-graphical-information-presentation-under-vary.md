# An Investigation of the Effectiveness of Color and Graphical Information Presentation Under Varying Time Constraints

By: Izak Benbasat\*
Albert S. Dexter
Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration
University of British Columbia
Vancouver, British Columbia
Canada

\*Currently Marvin Bower fellow Harvard Business School, Harvard University, Boston, Massachusetts

## Abstract

A laboratory experiment was conducted to assess the influence of color and information presentation differences on user perceptions and decision making under varying time constraints. Three different information presentations were evaluated: tabular, graphical, and combined tabular-graphical. Tabular reports led to better decision making and graphical reports led to faster decision making when time constraints were low. The combined report, which integrated the advantages associated with both tabular and graphical presentation, was the superior report format in terms of performance and was rated very highly by decision makers. Color led to improvements in decision making; this was especially pronounced when high time constraints were present.

Keywords: Graphic presentation, information system design, user-machine systems.
ACM Categories: H.1.2, H.4.2, I.3

## Introduction

The recent technological advances in color and graphics hardware and software have spurred interest in understanding their impact on managers. As yet, however, there have been few controlled studies to examine how these new enhancements influence decision making. Various reviews of the literature show mixed evidence as to the efficacy of color and graphics $[8, 10, 13, 16]$ . Research has been conducted to examine the influence of color and graphics in such diverse areas as education $[19]$ , search and identification tasks $[5]$ , accounting $[26, 31]$ and business decision making $[8, 13]$ . Given the diversity of the tasks, one might not expect convergent results; however even with a specific area, such as the design of instructional materials, results were equivocal $[19]$ .

There are several reasons for the contradictory results. One explanation is that there are intervening variables such as the characteristics of the user, the resolution of the medium used, or differences in the medium itself (paper or CRT) that may be the cause of equivocal results. Another explanation may be the inappropriate comparison of multicolor graphs to monochromatic tabular reports $[10, 38]$ . This kind of comparison confounds the effects of color and graphics, thus making it difficult to attribute the results to a specific variable.

Perhaps the most disappointing aspect of the previous studies has been the lack of a priori reasoning as to why graphical or color-enhanced reports are or are not expected to lead to better decision performance in given problem settings. We agree with DeSanctis [8] that the evaluation of color-enhanced and graphical information systems has to be undertaken on the basis of matching the information system to the characteristics of the problem. It is likely that the characteristics of a problem setting, and the appropriateness of graphical and tabular formats within that task setting, might help to explain the contradictory results. In other words, one should not assume graphical or color-enhanced reports will be superior for all problem contexts.

We planned a series of laboratory experiments to examine the individual and joint effects of color and graphics. Graphical and tabular reports were evaluated within a variety of task settings (reading and retrieval of data, communication of facts, comparison of alternatives, trend analysis, recognition and recall, problem finding, problem comprehension and problem solving) in order to identify their comparative advantage. In a previous study [2] we evaluated color-enhanced and graphical information systems for individuals with different decision making styles. In this study we report the findings of a laboratory experiment that was designed to assess the individual and joint effects of color, graphics, and time allotted to complete task in a decision making setting.

While the new advances in technology focus around color and graphics, the managerial environment has long been portrayed as one of varying degrees of time constraints. Mintzberg [25] observed that managerial activities are characterized by brevity, variety, and fragmentation. He noted, in particular, the brevity of tasks:

“Half of the observed activities were completed in less than nine minutes, and only one-tenth took more than an hour. In effect, the managers were seldom able or willing to spend much time on any one issue in any one session” [25, p. 33].

Time is also an important component of information load. Wright notes that:

"A decision maker's need to simplify should become more urgent when he must operate under a heavy information load. Information load is generally conceived as the amount of data to be processed per unit of time. An increase in information load could therefore result from either increasing the amount of data with which a person must cope or decreasing the time available for processing" (emphasis added) [36, p. 555].

Information load has been related to cognitive strain. Cognitive strain is the breakdown of the decision maker's cognitive processes, occurring when he or she suffers from information overload. Overload is the point at which information processing demands exceed the information processing capacity of the individual [34]. As such, by studying the influence of different information presentation treatments on decision making we are also interested in examining ways to alleviate the stress associated with decision making under time constraints.

Time has generally been treated as a dependent variable in experimental studies, but rarely as an independent variable to measure its influence on decision making. The few studies dealing with the aspect $[4, 34, 35]$ observed that decision makers working under time constraints tend to place greater weight on negative evidence than those working under less stressful conditions. These previous studies were mainly concerned with the effect of time pressure on decision making strategies. This study examines the effect of different information representations on decision performance under different time constraints.

## Experimental Framework

## The Task Environment

The task setting was a typical managerial problem involving the allocation of monetary resources across various income generating alternatives. Each alternative had a different rate of diminishing marginal return. Specifically, the task setting used in the experiment was the “Brand Manager’s Allocation Problem” described by $[23]$ . This task involves the allocation of a fixed promotional budget across three territories with the objective of maximizing the resultant total profit. Each of the three territories is represented by an independent response function that determines the relationship between promotion and profit for that territory. Two cases with different budget amounts and response functions were utilized in the study.

The response functions have the following form:

$$
\text { Sales } = \text { tmin } + \text { ts } ^ {*} (a x ^ {b} / (1 + a x ^ {b}))
$$

Profit = sales - x

where,

x = promotional dollars allocated to the territory

tmin = sales in the territory when x = 0

ts = saturation sales minus tmin in the territory

a,b = parameters

For the experimental task, the subjects were shown, on a CRT screen, a report that represented profit at levels of promotion from \$0 to the promotional budget limit for each territory. The report was in one of three forms: graphical, tabular, or combined graphical-tabular (see Figures 1, 2, and 3). The subjects were then asked to allocate the entire budget, or any portion thereof, to the three territories (i.e., they were not required to spend their entire budget). They were told that their performance would be evaluated based on the amount of profit they obtained from this one allocation decision. No simulated allocations or other interaction with the terminal was required during the exercise except for the entry of a final solution, although computations to determine the results of various allocations could be performed independently by the subjects.

## Experimental Design

The major objective of the experimental design was to test the effects of color and graphics on decision making performance under differing time constraints. The independent variables examined were:

1. Information Presentation: tabular, graphical, or a combination of tabular and graphical.

2. Color: monochromatic or multicolor.

3. Time Constraint: low — a fifteen minute time limit; or high — a five minute time limit.

Every attempt was made to balance the graphical (Figure 1) and tabular (Figure 2) reports in terms of their information content. The graphical report was simply a one-to-one transformation of the tabular report. It should be mentioned that while the tabular report could be easily converted to a graphical format, the reverse was somewhat difficult due to the problem of obtaining exact values from the graph. The combined report (Figure 3) contained the same information, and in the same format, that was found on both the tabular and graphical reports.

The reports were generated using the DISSPLA graphics software package $[11]$ . The total screen area used was approximately 11.5 x 9 inches for the graphical report, 7 x 10 for the tabular and 13 x 11 for the combined. The letters and digits used in the display were 0.1 inches, approximately the same size as those used on a typical CRT. The graphical lines used to depict the territories were solid, dotted and dashed lines for territories 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

In the monochromatic treatment, all tabular and graphical elements were in green, the same color found on the CRTs used by the subjects in their computing work. In the multicolor treatment green, cyan (blue) and yellow were used to identify the lines or numbers for territories 1, 2 and 3 respectively. All other lettering, headings and scales on the multi-color reports were in white.

All reports were displayed on a Jupiter CRT having a screen resolution of 1024 x 780 pixels. The terminal could superimpose a grid on the screen to facilitate analysis of the graphical reports. The grid could be turned on or off at the option of the subject. $^{1}$

1 It would be possible to include the use of the grid as an independent variable to measure its effect on report readability. However this was not done here.

The two limits chosen for the time treatment were derived in the following manner. In designing the experiment various time constraints were tested. It was determined that a subject could obtain the optimum solution within a five minute time limit. (This was confirmed to be true after the experiment, given that half of the subjects attained a near optimum solution within the five minute limit.) Less time appeared to induce excessive strain and seriously impair performance. According to Janis and Mann [14] when there is insufficient time to complete the task, decision performance can become quite erratic. We wished to avoid this condition. The fifteen minute treatment was included to provide a “comfortable” amount of time in which to solve the problem.

Immediately prior to the experiment, the subjects worked on the same task with the help of a simulation aid in search of the optimum solution. A maximum of thirty trials were permitted for the subject to explore the solution space, at no cost, by inputting various allocation combinations and receiving feedback on total profit. Note that the report contents of the simulation study (Figure 4) were different than the ones used in this study (Figure 2). As a result of working on a task simulation, subjects could be considered as having experience in a specific problem-solving context.

## Experimental Procedures

Immediately after completing the simulation exercise the subjects were told that they were to perform a short decision making exercise. The case description was identical to the one used in the simulation exercise, however the budget and response function parameters were changed ensuring that the new problem had a different optimum solution (see Appendix 1).

The experiment was administered to one subject at a time. Each was presented with a front-end screen on the CRT terminal, which briefly explained the new task, its report format, budget constraint, and the time limit imposed on the exercise. The subject was given a form on which to write the allocation decision and was reminded that the report would disappear when the allotted time had expired.

One minute before the end of the allotted time, a bell on the terminal would ring and a lab assistant would remind the subject that there were only 60 seconds remaining. Upon expiration of the 5 or 15 minute limit the report on the screen would be replaced by a question asking the subject to key in the allocations to the three territories (and also to write them on the allocation form to provide backup) within one minute. The computer program would then sum up the amounts allocated to the three territories to check that it was not over the budget limit. The program then asked the subject to double check that the figures were keyed in correctly. Upon a positive entry by the user, the exercise would be terminated. Since the subject's elapsed time was calculated based on this last entry, total time could range up to 6 minutes (360 seconds) or 16 minutes (960 seconds). The subjects could take less time than allowed if they so desired by indicating to the computer program that they were finished examining the report and then proceeding to enter their allocation.

Once the subjects had entered their allocation, a questionnaire was administered to elicit ratings of the various information presentation attributes. The questions (see Appendix 2) were developed based on items contained in the Jenkins and Ricketts “Satisfaction Questionnaire” [17]. Responses were scored on a seven point Likert scale and were analyzed using non-parametric statistical methods. Several open-ended questions were also asked with respect to strengths and weaknesses of the reports as well as the decision making strategy used by the subject during the exercise.

Upon completion of the questionnaire all materials were collected, including any notes taken by the subjects. No performance feedback was given immediately in order to prevent task solutions from being shared. In addition, since subjects were randomly assigned to one of two different cases, each with a different solution, the possibility of biased results due to sharing of information was further limited.

## The Subject Population

The subjects were all business school students who voluntarily participated in the experiment. As business students they had the appropriate analytical training required for the successful completion of the problem. $^{2}$ In total, 65 subjects were involved in the exercise: 58 MBA students, five undergraduates, and two doctoral students.

Participants were provided with two incentives to take part in the experiment. First, a monetary prize was offered to the top 3 performers (\$40, 30, 20, respectively) in each of the different treatment combinations. Second, they were given the opportunity to apply their classroom knowledge to an actual problem using a state-of-the-art piece of computer hardware. Subjects indicated in post experimental briefings that this latter opportunity, rather than the potential monetary reward, was the main reason for their participation. In this regard, “experimental realism” [32] appears to be in effect. $^{3}$

All subjects were given a reduced version of the Ishihara color discrimination test $[12]$ to prevent colorblind individuals from biasing the results. Two males who were found to be color deficient were assigned to monochromatic treatments. Given that approximately 10% of the male and 1% of the female population have some form of color deficiency the incidence of colorblindness found in this sample is within reasonable limits.

## Hypothesis Development

Presentation Mode in Task Environment
The allocation problem involved apportioning a given budget to various alternatives such that their combined profits are maximized. The plot of a response function (the amount of return for a given allocation) can be found in Figure 1. The slopes of the three curves in Figure 1 show the marginal return obtained from a \$1 allocation, depending on the level of allocation and the alternatives. A solution to the problem is to identify the alternative that has the highest slope in the range of \$0 to \$X, and allocate \$X to it. If any funds remain, the next step is to compare the slopes of the other two alternatives (starting at \$0) to the slope (starting at \$X + 1) of the alternative chosen previously and to allocate funds to the one with the highest slope. This procedure continues until the entire budget is allocated [20].

## DeSanctis states that:

"If graphs have a summarizing effect and allow an additional perspective on a problem beyond that which is available in a table or narrative, then comprehension of a problem and decision making speed should be enhanced by use of the graphs during the decision process" [8, p. 468].

Figure 1 provides the type of information to support this solution procedure. For example, it is clear from the graph (budget = \$54) that as an initial rough approximation \$20 should be allocated to territory 3 (highest slope), then \$20 to territory 1. Since the slope of territory 2 is less than the ones for the other two territories, the optimum solution would be to allocated \$0 to territory 2, based on the flat response curve.

The same information provided in tabular form (Figure 2) requires that the decision maker either perform calculations to determine the slopes or plot the data to convert them into a form that matches the requirements of the problem. The graphical report enables the decision maker to accomplish the task by merely isolating the information required, whereas the tabular report will require both isolation and computation [21]. Another advantage of this graphical presentation is that it reduces information overload by creating chunks or patterns [24] such as a slope. Thus, with a graph a territory could be described with fewer pieces of information than the 55 numbers needed for a market with a budget allocation of \$54.

There may, however, be some problems associated with the use of graphical reports in this problem setting, especially in determining an optimum solution. Graphical reports might be disadvantageous since it is difficult and time consuming to get an exact y-value (profit) for a given x-value (promotion). This is due to the interpolation problems inherent in graphical analysis. This problem does not exist for tabular formats.

Ghani similarly discusses the importance of considering the advantages and disadvantages of an information system based on the task:

Performance on a particular task is contingent on how well the task characteristics and the mode of presentation reinforce each other. Specifically it has been found that: (i) a tabular representation is best where the task requires the simple retrieval of data, (ii) a graphical representation is best where relationships among the data have to be perceived . . . [10, p. 43].

It could be argued that, given the nature of the task and the information presentation used in this study:

\- Tabular reports will assist in determining the optimum solution by providing the exact figures to calculate the marginal profit figures for different allocations.

\- Graphical reports will provide a quick understanding of the area where a good solution to the problem lies as they display the returns by allocation relationship (marginal return per allocation dollar) over the entire range of possible allocations.

It thus appears that the comparative advantages of both tabular and graphical reports, as identified by Ghani, exist in this environment. However, it can be argued that the graphical reports will be more appropriate for the high time constraint and tabular reports for the low time constraint task environments.

## Color in Task Environment

Color in this study was used as a redundant attribute. According to Christ, target attributes are “considered to be redundant if the targets could be identified (or located) either in terms of their color or their shape; that is, the color and the shape of the target were perfectly correlated” [5, p. 543]. In this study, the graphic lines depicting the promotion and profit figures for territories 1, 2, and 3 were coded in green, cyan (blue) and yellow, respectively. However, since each line was labeled with the name of the territory and each was coded differently (solid line, dotted line, and short line segments) color was a redundant attribute. For the tabular report, the promotion and profit figures for each territory were coded in the same colors used in the graphical report. Since each line was labeled as to which territory it represented, color was a redundant attribute. The effectiveness of color-coding was highly dependent on the task being performed [1].

Based on a literature survey, Christ reports that “the addition of a completely redundant color to size, brightness, and already redundant combinations of size and brightness facilitated absolute identification performance” [5, p. 553). In addition:

“. . . redundant colors can decrease search time for symbolic displays if the subject knows the color of the targets. This advantage of redundant colors increases as the density of the symbols in the display increases . . . [5, p. 561].

Schutz [30] compared the performance of subjects using different graphical report types on point reading and comparing tasks. Point reading involved reading y-axis values for specified x-axis values. The comparing task consisted of finding which line of the multiple line graph had the highest y-value for a specified x-axis value. He concluded that, in general, color improves performance and subjects' preferences for the point-reading task, but not for the comparing task. Considering that point-reading is used to determine the marginal benefit of allocation levels in this task, color might improve the performance of subjects using graphical reports.

Chute states that “color’s ability to delineate figure-ground relationships, to show interrelatedness, and to make discrimination underscores color’s effectiveness in learning” [7, p.

253]. Color might improve the readability and understandability of the combined reports by making it easier for the subject to associate visually the figures and lines belonging to the same alternative. Another reason for the use of color coding is that it is preferred over achromatic codes $[6, 30, 34]$ . For example, Jeffrey and Beck $[15]$ report that color in displays reduces monitoring and produces less eye strain and fatigue. However, this preference does not necessarily mean that color always improves performance $[15, 27]$ . As an example, in a study to measure performance on monitoring the status of 10 different channels of information, Kanarick and Petersen $[18]$ found that redundant color coding of digits did not aid performance.

## Information Presentation and Color Hypotheses

The following hypotheses associated with information presentation differences were formulated (all hypotheses are stated in the null form):

H1: There will be no difference between subjects who use tabular, graphical, and combined reports in terms of:

H1a, profit performance in the 5 minute treatment,

H1b, profit performance in the 15 minute treatment.

We expect the subjects with the graphical reports to achieve a better profit performance than the subjects with the tabular reports in the 5 minute (high constraint) treatment.

We expect the subjects with the tabular reports to have a better performance than those with graphical reports in the 15 minute (low constraint) treatment based on the following rationale:

1) subjects with tabular reports will have enough time to process the data to determine the marginal returns, and

2) it is easier for the subjects with tabular reports to obtain the exact values required to find a solution close to the optimum (99% or better of optimum profit).

The combined group subjects have the advantages associated with both report formats.

However, they are expected to spend some time trying to understand how to integrate the information contained in the two reports. We do not expect that they have enough time to take full advantage of both the tabular and graphical reports in the 5 minute treatment. However, the 15 minute period should provide enough time to take full advantage of both reports.

To summarize, we expect that the combined group subjects will have a performance level equal to that of the graphical group and better than the tabular group in the 5 minute treatment (graphical = combined > tabular). In the 15 minute treatment the combined group will perform the best, followed by the tabular and graphical groups (combined > tabular > graphical).

H2: There will be no difference between subjects who use tabular, graphical and combined reports in terms of:

H2a, time taken to complete task in 5 minute treatment,

H2b, time taken to complete task in 15 minute treatment.

The graphical reports will be helpful in quickly identifying a good, but not optimum solution. At this stage, there is little advantage to continuing the analysis, since the exact values needed to calculate the optimum are not obtainable from the graph. The tabular group subjects will spend more time both in the 5 and 15 minute treatments because they have to compute marginal returns and the time taken for additional analysis brings them closer to the optimum solution.

As indicated previously, subjects using the combined reports may quickly locate promising search areas but must still take the time required to determine the optimum using the tabular data. In the 5 minute treatment they will take the full time to complete the task because of the time needed to integrate both types of information. In the 15 minute treatment we expect the subjects to perform in the middle ranges between graphical and tabular.

To summarize, we expect in terms of time performance that:

1) the combined group subjects will take more time than the graphical and no more time than the tabular group subjects in the 5 minute treatment (time for tabular $\geq$ combined > graph);

2) the combined groups subjects will take more time than the graphical but less time than the tabular group subjects in the 15 minute treatment (tabular > combined > graphical).

H3: There will be no difference between subjects who use tabular, graphical and combined reports in terms of report attribute ratings.

In terms of subject preferences, we expect tabular reports to be ranked higher in terms of report accuracy and graphical reports to be ranked higher in terms of assisting in the problem solving process since the graphical form is directly applicable to the solution process. The combined reports will be ranked at least as high as tabular in terms of report accuracy, and at least as high as graphical reports in terms of assisting problem solving since they combine the advantages associated with the other two.

H4: There will be no difference between subjects who use monochromatic reports and subjects who use multicolor reports in terms of:

H4a, profit performance in the 5 minute treatment,

H4b, profit performance in the 15 minute treatment environment.

H5: There will be no difference between subjects who use monochromatic reports and subjects who use multicolor reports in terms of:

H5a, time taken to complete the task in the 5 minute treatment,

H5b, time taken to complete the task in the 15 minute treatment.

H6: There will be no difference between subjects who use monochromatic reports and subjects who use multicolor reports in terms of report attribute ratings.

We expect color to be helpful and all hypotheses to be rejected for the following reasons:

1) Color helps in decreasing search time and enhancing information acquisition for graphical and tabular reports by making the identification of the information of a particular alternative easier, consequently allowing more time for decision making. For graphical reports, color improves point reading performance.

2) In the combined report, color helps the subject to associate each line of the graph to its corresponding column in the tabular portion. This might reduce the time needed to acquire information.

3) In terms of report ratings, we expect multicolor reports to be rated more highly in terms of those characteristics which relate to visual appeal, such as understandability and report format, rather than on those relating to assistance in decision making.

## Statistical Models

It was originally planned to use the analysis of variance (ANOVA) model to examine the influence of the various factors on profit performance. However, since the profit performance data was highly skewed, the assumption of the ANOVA model that the probability distribution of the dependent variable is normal for each factor level did not hold [28, p. 426]. Therefore, non-parametric statistical procedures, Chi-square, Mann-Whitney, and median tests were used to analyze the profit data.

The chi-square test was chosen because the distribution of the profit performance figures showed that a high proportion of the subjects (39 out of 65) had a performance level very close to the optimum profit. In addition, a natural gap appeared to occur at this level, with no scores being registered in the 98 to 99% interval. It was decided to use a two way classification: subjects who achieved a very close to optimum profit (99% or better) and those who did not, and use a chi-square test of independence to determine if performance level and information presentation (or color coding) type were associated. The average performance level of the > 99% group was 99.5% versus an average of 91.8% for the < 99% group. To complement the chi-square statistics, the Mann-Whitney test or the median test $^{4}$ was used to identify the pairwise differences between the color treatments.

Time performance was evaluated based on the following factors:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {Information} & X _ {1} = 1 \text {for tabular reports} \\ \text {Presentation} & 2 \text {for graphical reports} \\ & 3 \text {for combined reports} \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {   Color   } & X _ {2} = 0 \text {   for   monochromatic   } \\ & 1 \text {   for   multicolor   } \end{array}
$$

This model was run using SPSS ANOVA [29] to determine the differences in treatment means. Since time was an experimental treatment the sample was divided into two groups and the analysis performed separately on those in each of the two time categories. ## Discussion

In the task environment under study, it was possible to obtain an optimum solution using an analytical approach (marginal analysis given that the subject had the precise figures to perform the necessary calculations. The graphical reports were in a form that made it possible to obtain a good solution, but not an optimum one, because of the inherent lack of precision of graphical reports. The tabular reports, on the other hand, provided the information to obtain an optimum solution given that the subject understood the problem solving approach and had enough time to complete the task.

In general, a tabular report is a finer information presentation than a graphical report. A graph can always be derived from the data included in the tabular report; the reverse is somewhat more difficult. Thus, even if the graph is in a form to directly support the required problem-solving approach, it is expected that, given enough time, those using tabular reports will perform at least on a par with those using graphical formats. This was indeed the case in the 15 minute treatment where subjects who had tabular reports outperformed the ones with only graphical reports in terms of achieving a close to optimum solution.

The potential advantage of the graphical information lies in reducing decision making time. The subjects in the tabular group took 44% more time (15 vs. 10.5 minutes) than the graphical group subjects who, on average, had a very good but not close to optimum solution. $^{6}$ The combined report contains both of these advantages: the performance improvement associated with having the precise information provided by the tabular report and the speed in decision making provided by the graphical one. The findings showed that the combined group subjects took less time than the tabular group subjects and performed better than the graphical group subjects given enough time to study the problem in depth (15 minute treatment).

In addition to the performance advantages, the combined report was also preferred by the subjects. Both the subjects who used the combined report and those who had worked with the tabular or graphical report only, but had not seen the combined, expressed a desire to use a combined format. Subjects who utilized the combined report rated them very highly on all attributes shown in Table 6.

There were no time or performance differences associated with information presentation in the 5 minute group, contrary to our expectations that the graphical group subjects would do better. It is possible that it was difficult to complete this task in less than 5 minutes, regardless of the information presentation. Recall that in determining the appropriate time for the high time constraint treatment, 5 minutes appeared as a threshold level, below which considerable stress on the subjects occurred. In addition, in the low time constraint treatment the graphical group subjects, who were the fastest performers, took 10.5 minutes on average.

Color coding was found to influence profit performance significantly. However, the strengths of multicolor reports were only evident in the 5 minute treatment. It therefore appears that in this problem context, the advantages associated with color (identification, discrimination of items, reading points from graphical lines, and association of items) are more pronounced in high time constraint environments.

The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

Given a reasonable amount of time to solve the problem (15 minutes);

1. Combined group subjects had a higher profit performance than the graphical group subjects, but the same as the tabular group.

2. Combined and graphical group subjects completed the task faster than tabular group subjects.

3. Combined and graphical reports were preferred over tabular reports.

Therefore, in aggregate terms the combined report format is superior as it rates as high or higher than the tabular and graphical formats along all evaluation dimensions.

## Guidelines For Practitioners

The objective of the study described here was to evaluate different information presentation methods. Controlled experiments such as this one have, by definition, a narrow focus and produce results that do not necessarily address every aspect of the general problem under study. However, it is important to understand that each result is like a small tile contributing to an emerging mosaic of understanding. A major goal of our experimental program, of which this is a part, is to test the dramatic claims made in the practitioner literature about color and graphics and to catalogue the various findings so that practitioners are provided with some recommendations about how to design presentation formats for different task settings and decision maker types.

One clear finding we have observed is the importance of the match between the task and the information presentation method. A given presentation method is better than others only if it allows an additional perspective of the solution to the task. In other words, contrary to the claims found in the promotional literature, it is not correct to expect graphical reports to be better for any and all problem contexts. Therefore, the information system designer should consider choosing a particular presentation mode based on an understanding of how it supports the nature of the task.

The solution of the task examined in this study required that the decision maker:

a) Calculate marginal returns (slopes) for each territory;

b) Compare the marginal returns (slopes) for different territories; and

c) Acquire exact data values, at the later stages of the problem solving process, to obtain the optimum solution.

Since the graphical report was in a form to directly support the determination of relative slopes, given ample time to solve the problem, subjects with graphical reports completed the task faster than the tabular group subjects and attained a very high profit level. However, graphical reports did not assist in item c) above, acquisition of exact data values, as evidenced by the fact that subjects with graphical reports were less likely to find the optimum solution. It should also be mentioned that in a different study [2] when graphical reports were not in a form that directly supported the task, none of the advantages attributed to graphs described in this study were observed.

A second finding of our studies concerns the value of color-coding. It is clear that color enhances decision making, but it appears that the benefits of color are also subject to qualification. In this study we observed that the benefits of color are more pronounced when the decision maker was under a higher time constraint to complete the task. In another experiment [3] it was also found that color-coded reports were preferred in the earlier parts of the problem solving process and enhanced subjects' understanding of the problem and profit performance at that stage.

In a previous study it was found that color was beneficial but only for subjects who were of a certain psychological type: those who have a perceptual difficulty disembedding parts of a complex figure. If line graphs are considered complex figures whose individual lines a decision maker is trying to analyze, then color would assist in highlighting these line. In summary, color improves the comprehension of graphs, especially when the graph is complex and has overlapping lines and figures, but only for individuals with difficulty in disembedding.

To reiterate our initial comment, laboratory experiments attempt to achieve a high degree of control and internal validity in the investigation of facts. In the long run our understanding of a complex and wide ranging topic such as presentation methods will only be achieved by a series of cumulative research studies using multiple investigation methods to explore it.
