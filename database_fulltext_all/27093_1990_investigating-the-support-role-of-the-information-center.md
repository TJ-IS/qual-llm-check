## Abstract

During the past decade, end-user computing has been subject to several research efforts. One result is common to nearly all the studies on that topic: the importance of providing support to users. Descriptive studies have identified this result as a major issue; critical success factor studies have reported it as a key element, and models of factors of success have found that it is significantly related to user satisfaction with end-user computing. This article reports the results of a study that further investigates user support, in the particular context of the information center. The study examines the relationships among some of the key features of the information center in its support role and reports on user satisfaction. The results are based on the data provided by 172 users and 19 information center managers in 19 organizations. Analysis of the data suggests that the number of information center employees, the number of databases, and the number of software tools provided to users are negatively correlated with user satisfaction, while the proximity of the information center, the diversity of services offered, and the proportion of the information systems budget devoted to the information center are positively related with the various dimensions of user satisfaction.

Keywords: End-user computing, information center, end-user support

ACM Categories: J.1, K.4.3, K.6.1

## Introduction

End-user computing is to the 1980s what the implementation of computers in organizations was to the 1970s—a tremendous change in organizational practices. Statistics show that the spreading of the phenomenon was extremely rapid. Although less than 10 years ago the information center (IC) was a concept known only to IBM and to some of its customers, the American Management Association estimated that by 1985 a third of all American firms had implemented an IC (American Management Association, 1985). When the AMA polled 295 companies in 1988, 58 percent had a discrete unit that supported end-user computing (American Management Association, 1988). Moreover, in 1988, the vast majority (70.2 percent) of information centers received budget increases. Most IC budgets rose by 6-10 percent, but a full quarter went up by more than 20 percent (Greenberg, 1988). Forecasts suggest that the growth in end-user computing will continue; Strassman (1985), for instance, estimates that by the year 2000, there will be more than 200 million workstations installed in organizations worldwide. This estimate may very well be realized. Indeed, a 1988 Computerworld classification of the 100 organizations with the most effective use of their information systems, indicates these companies had on the average 35 PC/terminals per 100 employees; and the first 25 organizations had a mean of 44 units per 100 employees (Sullivan-Trainor, 1988).

End-user computing in general, and the IC in particular, have been subject to several studies. Some of these studies are mainly descriptive in nature (Benson, 1983; Carr, 1987; Rivard and Huff, 1985; Rockart and Flannery, 1983; Sumner, 1985). They are important contributions because they help to define the field under study, to identify the key players and describe their role, and to state the main issues and problems. Other researchers focus on identifying success factors for end-user computing, either by conducting critical success factors studies (Carr, 1987; Leitheiser and Wetherbe, 1985; Magal, et al., 1988) or by building and testing models of factors of success (Bergeron and Bérubé, 1988; Buyukkurt and Vass, 1988; Rivard and Huff, 1988).

From these studies, one factor emerges as being of critical importance: the support provided to users. It was identified as a major management issue by descriptive studies; critical success factors studies found that it is a key factor in the management of end-user computing; and the results of studies of models of factors of success indicate that support is significantly related to user satisfaction with end-user computing.

This article reports the results of a study aimed at exploring further the role played by user support in the context of the IC. A survey of 172 users and 19 IC managers in 19 organizations was conducted. The study examined the relationships between some of the key features of the IC in its support role and user satisfaction.

## Supporting End Users

The end-user computing (EUC) literature devotes much attention to providing support to users. While some studies identify support to users as a critical management issue, others empirically test its relationship with EUC success. These research efforts also contribute to the identification of the major dimensions of support. These various aspects are discussed in the following paragraphs.

In their studies of human-computer interactions, researchers found that even if a user is well trained before using a software package, considerable learning also takes place during the actual use of the package. Some studies indicate that, in order for this learning to occur, ongoing support must be provided to users, either through documentation or human advisers (Eason, et al., 1975; Martin, et al., 1973). When the first studies on end-user computing were undertaken, the need for providing support to users was soon identified as a key issue. Rockart and Flannery (1983), for instance, describe end-user support as one of the three major areas of end-user computing with which information systems management must be concerned; the other two areas are strategy and control. Benson (1983) also found that providing support to users in their end-user computing activities is of critical importance. Reporting the results of a study of usage patterns and sources of assistance of personal computer users, Lee (1986) states, “Most users do need a considerable amount of information and help in order to become proficient with the use of this technology,” and he added, “Our respondents cited almost unanimously that the lack of time and user assistance were critical barriers to more effective utilization of PCs” (p. 324). From a survey of IC managers, Leitheiser and Wetherbe (1985) propose a list of 10 factors they define as being critical to the successful management of end-user computing. More than half of these factors are related to end-user support.

While descriptive studies contribute to the identification of user support as a key issue in the management of end-user computing, studies aimed at testing models of success confirm its importance. Bergeron and Bérubé (1988) surveyed 212 users and found that user satisfaction with end-user computing is higher in organizations that provide full support to their end users than in organizations that provide only partial support. Rivard and Huff (1988) surveyed 272 users who developed applications and found that their satisfaction with the support received from the Information Systems Department is the factor most closely related to overall user satisfaction with end-user computing. Buyukkurt and Vass (1988) report similar results in their survey of 232 users involved in end-user computing activities. They found that support provided to users is the factor that explains the largest percentage of the variance in the model they tested; they also found that this factor is the most closely related to overall user satisfaction.

While investigating end-user computing in the context of information centers, Magal, et al. (1988) adopted a slightly different approach. First, they focussed on IC managers rather than users; second, they attempted to obtain a rating of the importance of several critical success factors rather than test a factor model. Notwithstanding these differences in research design, their results are very close to those previously mentioned; of the five composite factors they obtained, the quality of IC support services was consistently rated as being the most important.

In addition to highlighting the role played by end-user support, these studies contribute to identification of the major dimensions of support. Rockart and Flannery (1983), for instance, recommend four main dimensions of support: (1) the development of a distributed organization for support (rather than a single, centralized organization); (2) the provision of a wide spectrum of products; (3) the development of a substantial education program; and (4) the development of effective data migration procedures (that is, facilitating user access to data). The critical success factors listed by Leitheiser and Wetherbe (1985) also contribute to identification of the main components of a conceptual definition of support. Some of these components are: (1) to provide whatever services are needed in a timely way; (2) to develop a competent staff; and (3) to select and support the “right” software packages. Following a principal component analysis of 26 critical success factors for the IC, Magal, et al. (1988) propose five components for quality of support services: (1) a competent staff; (2) support software packages; (3) end-user training; (4) reliability of applications developed; and (5) training for IC staff.

In their study of factors of success for end-user computing, Rivard and Huff (1988) identify several dimensions of support. These dimensions include help in designing and debugging applications, support in the choice of programming tools, availability of those who provide support, their understanding of user applications, their people orientation, and the quality of training they provide. Buyukkurt and Vass (1988) also propose four dimensions for support. They are competency, availability, cooperativeness, and responsiveness of support staff.

This study is an effort to pursue further investigation of earlier studies. Yet it differs from those studies on three aspects. First, while previous studies are concerned with a number of contributors to EUC success, this study focuses on the role of support itself in the context of an information center. Second, it uses factual measures for the dimension of support instead of relying on perceptual measures as the previous studies did. Finally, its data were obtained from two different sources—users and IC managers—rather than from one unique source. The research model is presented in the next section.

## The Research Model

The research model for this study, illustrated in Figure 1, includes two constructs: IC support and user satisfaction with IC. In order to operationalize these constructs, an analysis of the dimensions of support proposed in the literature was performed. This analysis led to the identification of four features of the IC that are potentially relevant to support and within which the dimensions can be plotted. These features are (1) the IC staff, (2) its location, (3) the services it offers, and (4) the software tools it supports. A fifth dimension, the IC budget, was added because of its expected intrinsic importance. Each of these features, along with the research questions, is discussed in the next several sections.

## IC staff

Much has been said about IC staff. They must provide help in designing and debugging applications, support the choice of programming tools, understand user applications, and be competent and available, responsive, people-oriented, and so on. In this study, two variables are related to IC personnel: the number of people available for supporting users, and the percentage of IC employees with a computer background. These two measures encompass several of the dimensions identified in the literature. The size of the IC staff is related to availability, rapidity, responsiveness, and timeliness of support. In turn, the percentage of IC personnel with computer background is related to competency, ability to provide technical assistance to users, and understanding of user applications. Note that these two variables do not include people-orientation and cooperativeness dimensions. This limitation must be taken into account in the interpretation of results.

## Location

According to several authors, an appropriate location for the IC is critical. Providing users with distributed support services was the first recommendation made by Rockart and Flannery (1983) about support. Leitheiser and Wetherbe (1985) found that proximity of the IC to users played an important role in the success of the IC. In this study, an IC was classified in proximity to users when it was in the same building or within walking distance. Otherwise, the IC was classified as distant.

## Services

Rivard (1987) identifies two dimensions of support: quality and variety. While quality is closely related to support staff (e.g., availability, people-orientation, competency, responsiveness), variety is related to the services offered by the IC. Each of the studies mentioned earlier identify dimensions relevant to variety. It appears from these studies that the IC should provide a wide range of services such as training, development, assistance with the use of software, research of new products, data extraction, consultation, and diffusion of information. In this study, two variables were selected as a surrogate for variety of services. The first is the number of services provided by the IC as measured by the presence or absence of each of the IC services suggested by Leitheiser and Wetherbe (1985). The second is the number of corporate databases made available to users for data extraction. While data extraction is generally part of the range of services available from an IC, it is understood that if a large number of corporate databases are made available to the users for data extraction, then much effort has been provided by the IC staff in giving support to users.

## Software tools

The importance of selecting and supporting end-user computing tools such as electronic spreadsheets, database management systems, and graphic software is well documented. Rockart and Flannery (1983), Leitheiser and Wetherbe (1985), Magal, et al. (1988), and Rivard and Huff (1988), to name a few, put emphasis on this success factor. Because user needs are often quite varied because of user backgrounds, responsibilities, or personal characteristics, IC managers are often faced with deciding on how many software tools to support. The more tools there are available, the more likely user needs will be met; however, providing adequate support for each tool might be difficult. In this study, the number of tools made available and supported by each IC was measured.

## Budget

The percentage of the information systems budget allocated to the IC was added to the list of independent variables. Because the budget affects all operations of the IC and determines the amount of resources to be devoted to user support, we deemed it relevant to this study and added it to the model.

## User satisfaction

The dependent variable in the model is user satisfaction. User satisfaction is widely used as a measure of success in EUC research (Bergeron and Bérubé, 1988; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Rivard and Huff, 1988). In this study, the composite scale developed for measuring satisfaction includes most of the items from Rivard and Huff (1988) as well as from Buyukkurt and Vass (1988). Other items were added in order to encompass most of the dimensions identified in the descriptive studies cited above. The resulting measure is presented in detail in the Research Methodology section.

Based on these operational definitions of IC support and user satisfaction, the study was conducted to examine the relationships between user satisfaction and each of the following variables:

\- the size of the IC staff,

\- the proportion of IC employees with computer background,

\- the proximity of the IC to user premises,

\- the number of different services offered by the IC,

\- the number of corporate databases made available to users,

\- the number of software tools supported by the IC, and

\- the proportion of IS budget allocated to the IC.

## Research Methodology

In order to provide answers to the questions embedded in the research model, a field study was conducted in 19 large Canadian organizations located in two major cities of Eastern Canada. The organizations included in this convenience sample were selected from the membership list of an association of IC managers and from personal contacts. Most large organizations having an information center in these cities were represented in the sample. There was only one IC at each site. In each organization, two questionnaires were completed, one by the IC manager during an interview, the other by mail, sent to users involved in end-user computing activities. A total of 172 users in the 19 organizations completed the questionnaires, for an overall response rate of 21 percent. Beforehand, the questionnaires had been pretested on a group of end users and IC managers.

Most of the questions were of a quantitative or descriptive type. The second questionnaire, completed by IC users, was designed to measure user satisfaction with the IC. The two questionnaires were complementary, each of them gathering data on a specific construct.

The user questionnaire contained 40 statements related to various aspects of user satisfaction with IC. Some of the aspects were user evaluation of the quality of the technological environment, of the technical assistance obtained from the IC, and of the applications developed by the IC personnel; an appreciation of his/her relationship with IC personnel, the improvement in user decision-making processes because of EUC activities, and user-perceived autonomy in the EUC environment. Users were asked to indicate on semantic differential scales—two per statement—how they felt about each statement. The questionnaire also included a criterion item, in the form of a statement, asking users to rate their overall satisfaction with the IC. Two items pertaining to the same statement, which were negatively correlated with the others, were eliminated from the questionnaire.

In order to better capture the dimensions of user satisfaction, a factor analysis was performed. An average was calculated for each of the remaining 39 pairs of items, and this average was used in the factor analysis. A principal components analysis followed by an orthogonal transformation of the axes was conducted. From this analysis, six factors with eigenvalues greater than one emerged; the extracted factors accounted for 60 percent of the total variance in the data. Only those statements with factor loadings larger than .5 were kept for further analysis; of the 39 statements, seven were eliminated. Table 4 presents the remaining 32 statements, along with their factor loadings, eigenvalues, percentage of variance explained, and reliability coefficients; for purposes of clarity, loadings smaller than .5 are not listed. In order to assess criterion-related validity, the correlation coefficients between each factor and the criterion item measuring overall satisfaction were calculated; these coefficients were all significant (Table 4). In addition to the factors, a global measure of satisfaction was also calculated. It consist of the mean of the 32 statements.

Table 5 lists the six factors and the statements included in each. The six factors are interpreted as (1) improvement of decision making, (2) quality of the technological environment put into place by the IC, (3) quality of technical assistance, (4) quality of application development performed by the IC, (5) relation with IC personnel, (6) and user autonomy.

## Positive correlations

Three key features of ICs were found to be positively correlated with dimensions of end-user satisfaction. They are the proximity of services, the variety of services, and the IC budget.

## Proximity of Services

The relationship between proximity of services and user autonomy may be explained by the quick answers users can get to their problems when they are located close to the IC. In the long run, users are likely to become more knowledgeable about computing and be able to do more work by themselves. The fact that distant users appear to feel less autonomous may be due to the difficulty they experience with obtaining appropriate support. The possibility for a user to get personalized face-to-face help from IC staff might make a difference in the satisfaction a user can get from IC services. This finding supports Rockart and Flannery's (1983) claim for distributed support services; that is, when users are geographically dispersed, it might be appropriate to implement several points of support services.

## Variety of Services

Variety of services (e.g., assistance, consulting, training, research) offered by the IC is positively related to the perceived quality of technical assistance. This supports the idea that it is important for the IC to offer various and diversified services. Providing users with a technical environment might thus not be sufficient to assure EUC success. Users also require assistance, and their needs in that respect are numerous. The wider the scope of services provided by the IC, the more users are likely to be satisfied.

## IC Budget

Quality of technical assistance is also related to the proportion of the information systems budget allocated to the IC. By definition, services offered by the IC are much more dedicated to end-user support than the services supported by the Information Systems Department. Therefore, in organizations where a larger proportion of the IS budget is allocated to the IC, end users are likely to be more satisfied. A larger proportion of IS budget allocated to the IC might in fact be representative of top management support to IC, which is considered to be an important success factor by previously cited researchers such as Magal, et al. (1988). Although more money does not immediately translate into better support, it is a means through which IC management can provide the services users require and finally improve the overall quality of IC services. Money can be spent in different ways, such as hiring more competent employees, increasing the variety of services offered, and buying and installing more equipment dedicated to end-user support. These are potentially sound explanations of the relationship between the proportion of IS budget allocated to the IC and the quality of technical assistance; but this particular finding carries two limitations. First, the use of the budget variable may be problematic given the different accounting schemes for capturing information technology services and equipment existing in organizations. Second, the sample for this variable is particularly small because only 10 organizations provided budget information. This result must therefore be interpreted with caution.

## Negative correlations

Three other key features of the IC were negatively correlated with various dimensions of end-user satisfaction. They are the total number of employees working at the IC, the number of corporate databases available for extraction, and the total number of software tools.

## Number of Employees

At first glance, we would expect that a larger staff at the IC would be related to higher end-user satisfaction because more extensive services would be offered. However, the opposite relationship was observed. In these situations, users were less satisfied with the technical assistance and the quality of the applications developed.

They considered themselves to be less autonomous when there was a large staff at the IC as opposed to a more restricted staff. Some of the specific problems we observed were: It is more difficult to obtain personalized service in a large organization; it takes longer to contact the person that can help the user with his/her specific problem; and there may be a turn-over of personnel, which would create a lack of continuity in the services received from the IC. The more general effect of a large IC staff is also reflected in its negative relationship with global satisfaction. To help alleviate the negative effect of large ICs, managers could concentrate on improving the diversity and specificity of services and on the closeness of the IC location to the users.

## Number of Corporate Databases

A second feature of ICs negatively correlated to various dimensions of end-user satisfaction was the number of corporate databases available for extraction. The access to central databases is a particular problem for whoever wants to read, download, or upload data from a micro-computer. Despite technological advances, the transfer of data between applications is still a major problem for end users as well as for analysts who act as consultants to end users. As such, making a large number of databases available to the users is relatively easy; it is in ensuring that they can be accessed smoothly and used easily that the problems might take root. The analysts should ensure that the technological environment perfectly supports the use of each corporate database by the end users and that technical assistance is available before opening up any additional databases to them. Establishing priorities in the databases available for extraction might be part of the solution. In the short run, a lower number of databases would be available, but it would be possible to exploit them more effectively and efficiently. In such an environment, end users could be more satisfied with the applications developed because they would more clearly understand the use of the databases. In the long run, more corporate databases could be made available to the users to the extent that each one satisfies basic criteria such as technological effectiveness, semantic compatibility and user-friendly access.

## Number of Software Tools

In organizations where the IC supported the use of a larger number of software tools, end users were less satisfied with the quality of technical assistance and the quality of applications developed, and felt more dependent on the IC than in organizations supporting the use of a smaller number of tools. Two different reasons might explain these results: the first is related to users, the other to IC staff. The early proponents of end-user computing (Martin, 1982; McCracken, 1980; McLean, 1979) put much emphasis on the importance of providing users with integrated tools. They argue that it is very difficult for a user to switch from one interface to the other. While software tools currently on the market are quite user-friendly, very few are truly integrated. Users who need to learn to use a large number of different tools seem to be less satisfied.

Another explanation for these results is the difficulty IC employees may experience in becoming knowledgeable about and adequately supporting a large number of tools. For example, in a situation where every IC consultant would be required to learn about and support all tools, they could hardly become proficient in the use of each of them. As a result, users may feel that support is not adequate or appropriate. On the other hand, the IC might opt for specialization; in such a case, any one or two people among IC staff would be proficient with a given tool. Once again, users might be less satisfied because they can obtain support from only a few IC employees. This suggests that in order to provide adequate support, it would be more appropriate to limit the number of tools available.

## Limitations

This study carries some limitations. The first is related to sample characteristics. Although a large number of end users (172) participated in the survey, they nevertheless came from only 19 organizations. The results might be representative of these organizations, but any generalization of the results to other organizations in other environments should be made with caution, particularly given the lack of diversity in the industries represented. Care should also be taken in interpreting the results related to the IS budget because the subsample was probably too small to support clear conclusions. Also, while it would be interesting to respond to questions such as: What difference does the type of industry, the size of user organization, and the age of the IC make in the findings? The relatively small number of organizations studied does not allow for such analysis.

## Conclusion

This study attempts to shed light on the relationships between some of the key features of the IC support activities and user satisfaction. Some of the IC characteristics were found to be positively related to various dimensions of end-user satisfaction; they are: the proximity of the IC to end users; the large variety of services offered by the IC; and the proportion of the IS budget devoted to the IC. Other features were negatively related to user satisfaction: a large number of corporate databases; a large number of software tools; and a large number of IC employees. The lack of technological control on the access and use of the databases might be a cause of dissatisfaction for the end user, but this situation may be reversed in the future.

Our analysis also suggests that users are more satisfied when the IC supports a smaller number of tools. This probably reflects the reality that most software packages on the market accomplish basically the same functions. In addition, it is better to master a few types of software than to have a vague familiarity with many software packages without being able to use any one effectively. Finally, ICs with large staffs were found to cause dissatisfaction among end users. Based on these results, it seems that relatively small but well-organized ICs, offering a diversity of services and supporting a limited number of tools, are more likely to satisfy users.
