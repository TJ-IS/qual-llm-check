## Abstract

System performance measurement often is conducted using subjective methods, such as self-reporting of utilization, or surrogate measures, such as user satisfaction. High system utilization or high user satisfaction provides no guarantee of high performance. This study reports initial research relating system utilization, measured as a continuous and independent variable, with an objective measure of system performance. The usage or participation rate of the New Orleans Vessel Traffic Service was correlated with its performance criterion of vessel safety, operationalized as the number of marine accidents. The evaluation model reported in this research employed utilization as an independent variable in the regression equation. This utilization measure was inversely correlated with vessel accidents — increases in DSS utilization were associated with reductions in marine casualties, assuming fixed levels of the other explanatory variables. This research has provided evidence that the system has been very effective in reducing vessel accidents — the objective performance criterion — on the lower Mississippi River. IS managers should consider this method of evaluation as a means of increasing information management credibility.

Keywords: Decision support systems, information system usage, system performance measurement

ACM Categories: H.4.2, K.6.0, K.6.2

## Introduction

Information system (IS) availability alone does not provide an organization any direct value. The IS must be utilized to provide value. Utilization of an IS is the channel through which information technology (IT) can affect performance. According to Trice and Treacy (1988), utilization serves as an important “intervening variable,” between IT and performance. Partially determined by IT variables, utilization is one of several variables that ultimately affect performance. Trice and Treacy (1988) refer to the relationship between IT and utilization as the backward linkage and the relationship between utilization and performance as the forward linkage.

Forward linkages must exist if an IS is to affect performance because IT cannot have an impact on performance if the technology is not used. Increased utilization could be correlated conceptually with either improvement or degradation in performance. Therefore, utilization alone is not sufficient to accurately predict performance. Consequently, system utilization is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for IT to affect performance in a desirable fashion. Figure 1, adapted from Trice and Treacy (1988), illustrates these concepts.

To achieve a more complete and extensive understanding of the linkage between IT and performance, it is advantageous to employ utilization as an independent variable affecting performance rather than as an indicator of achievement itself. This article will exhibit these relationships and apply an improved method of viewing IT performance. Before doing so, we will examine the literature on system usage.

## Research on system usage as a dependent variable

Previous researchers have assumed that usage of an IS is a surrogate measure of its effectiveness or success, but there has not been consensus on this measure. Ein-Dor and Segev (1981) identify several measures employed to determine IS success; they selected system usage as the preferred measure for research on IS effectiveness. However, Keen (1975) has questioned the practice of equating success with use. Robey (1979) demonstrates a strong positive relationship between use of information systems and the attitudes of users toward the same systems. On the other hand, Schewe (1976) did not find any significant relationship between attitudes toward the information system and system usage. Furthermore, Ginzberg (1981) reports that user satisfaction and actual usage have low correlations and in some instances are negatively correlated. For example, using a poorly designed system could lead to user dissatisfaction. Lucas (1978) contends that usage is a good indicator of success only when participation in the system is voluntary. As can be seen, the literature is replete with contradictions on system utilization as a measure of system success.

## Research on system usage as an independent variable

System usage also has been employed as an explanatory variable associated with recognized measures of system performance or success, such as profit or market share. A number of laboratory studies have attempted to relate DSS usage with performance (Aldag and Power, 1986; Benbasat and Dexter, 1982; Benbasat and Schroeder, 1977; Cats-Baril and Huber, 1987; Chakravarti, et al., 1979; Dickmeyer, 1983; Eckel, 1983; Goslar, et al., 1986; King and Rodriguez, 1978; McIntyre, 1982; Sharda, et al., 1988). These studies all varied with respect to the nature of DSS support, types of decisions, and performance measures. However, system usage as an explanatory variable was always operationalized in terms of the availability or absence of a DSS. Field research (Edelman, 1981; Fudge and Lodish, 1977; Gochenouer, 1985) has similarly operationalized system usage as the presence or absence of such assistance.

All of these studies viewed a system in a binary fashion: it was either available or not. The IS literature does not report any prior empirical research studies that employ the rate of system usage as an explanatory variable, a method that would directly relate utilization rate of the IT with the desired changes in enterprise or organizational level performance. This is a serious shortcoming in reporting system performance. This study is an example of how this deficiency can be overcome.

## System utilization and system performance

This study represents an initial research effort that relates a continuous measure of DSS usage (a continuous variable rather than a dichotomous one) with an objective measure of system performance. We call this measure the objective performance criterion. In this field study of an actual working DSS, measurement of system utilization was operationalized by means of an objective, quantitative scale of actual users as a percentage of potential users. This is in contrast to most research studies previously reporting about system utilization (even as a dependent variable), which employ a self-report from users on their usage of an IS. Self-reporting may suffer from validity problems because self-reported usage rates could have little correlation with actual usage (Trice and Treacy, 1988). In comparison to previous studies that used only system utilization as the dependent variable, the research reported here is unique in that it employs DSS usage rate as an independent or explanatory variable. Usage of the IS is related directly with the performance criterion.

Before presenting the study methodology, it is necessary to understand the research environment and the reasons for creating the IS.

## The Research Site

## Need for a vessel traffic service (VTS)

In the early 1970s, the U.S. Coast Guard conducted an exhaustive study of vessel traffic service (VTS) needs for the nation's ports and waterways. According to this report (U.S. Coast Guard, 1973), the port of New Orleans, and the lower Mississippi River area in general, ranked first among the nation's ports in deaths and injuries and second in dollar loss resulting from vessel accidents. In the four years before 1973, there were 211 passing and overtaking collisions in the waterway between the Gulf of Mexico and Baton Rouge, Louisiana. The following events represent examples of the aforementioned types of vessel accidents:

March 16, 1968: The S.S. African Star proceeded downriver from New Orleans; the Midwest Cities, with two volatile oil barges in tow, was headed upriver. Both vessels were equipped with radio-telephones, but their frequencies were mismatched. Confusion in the passing agreement resulted in a collision. Twenty-one people aboard the African Star died in the flames.

April 6, 1969: Near the New Orleans central business district, the freighter S.S. Union Faith collided with the tug Warren J. Doucet, which was pushing oil-laden barges. Twenty-five people died in the resulting explosion and fire.

October 20, 1976: The ferry George Prince was rammed by the Norwegian tanker S.S. Frosta at Destrehan, Louisiana. Seventy-eight people aboard the ferry died in the Mississippi River. The Frosta's pilot later reported that his crew was unable to make radio contact with the doomed ferry.

In all three tragedies, inadequate ship-to-ship communication and lack of knowledge about the position of other vessels played a fatal role. Better information was needed to navigate these waters. This need was not surprising for a traffic-laden waterway beset by blind turns and frequently blanketed by fog. Furthermore, the huge vessels and barge tows using the river respond with agonizing slowness to situations requiring evasive action, and there is scant maneuvering space in relentless currents.

Under the authority of the Ports and Waterways Safety Act of 1972, the U.S. Coast Guard responded to the need for improved vessel safety and established a vessel traffic service for the lower Mississippi River in October 1977. This information system, which was designed to assist vessel operators in safely navigating this treacherous inland waterway, utilized a VHF-FM communications network to distribute information to and from a computer-simulated vessel-tracking system. It also coordinated vessel movements in or approaching any port or waterway. The system attempted to reduce the probability of vessel casualties in order to protect lives, property, and the environment while expediting the movement of vessels.

## Identifying performance criteria

The strategic mission of the New Orleans Vessel Traffic Service (NOLA-VTS) was to improve vessel safety by providing advance information about reported maritime traffic on the lower Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and the Gulf of Mexico. The NOLA-VTS area represented 360 miles of navigable waterway and was divided into four operating sectors. For this type of system, the key performance indicators included various types of vessel accidents, such as collisions and rammings. Other waterways and ports may have different performance criteria. In Europe, for example, port economic development and/or facilitating traffic movement have top priority (Center for Wetland Resources, 1981).

The identification of vessel safety as the primary criterion for a VTS on the lower Mississippi River was the result of interviews and discussions with about 20 members of the Port Safety Council of New Orleans. Members of the Port Safety Council who were interviewed included representatives from the following maritime groups: U.S. Coast Guard (e.g., captain of the port; chief of the Marine Inspection Office; commanding officer of VTS); officers of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers; dock and harbor authorities (Ports of Baton Rouge and New Orleans); steamship companies; barge lines and towing firms; pilot associations; and shippers (Exxon Shipping Co., Texaco, Inc., Chevron U.S.A., etc.). All maritime parites interviewed uniformly stipulated that vessel safety (i.e., absence or reduction of marine accidents) was the most important criterion for a VTS on the lower Mississippi River or any other U.S. port or inland waterway.

## Levels of VTS technology

According to the “framework for DSS” as developed by Sprague (1980), the NOLA-VTS is a specific DSS built from “tools.” The specific DSS is the hardware/software combination that allows a specific decision maker or group of decision makers to deal with a specific set of related problems, such as those encountered by marine navigators on inland waterways. DSS tools are the portfolio of rudimentary hardware and software elements that facilitate the development of specific DSSs. In the case of a VTS, this fundamental level of information technology includes microprocessors, computer languages, graphical display terminals, input devices such as keyboards and radar, and communications equipment such as microwave relays. Fujii and Yamanouchi (1978) and Hill (1973) developed categories for VTSs based on two primary criteria: (1) the complexity and sophistication of the technology; and (2) the degree of the management of vessel movement as measured from passive to active. The U.S. Coast Guard adopted Hill's (1973) descriptive model as the standard classification scheme for VTSs. According to these categories, the NOLA-VTS can be classified as: (1) a vessel movement reporting system; (2) an intermediate level of information technology; and (3) "advisory" in terms of management level.

## System components and general operation

The NOLA-VTS utilized a VHF-FM communications network, which was continuously manned and recorded by Coast Guard personnel and was administered from a Vessel Traffic Center (VTC) in New Orleans. The VTC was staffed by approximately 50 Coast Guardsmen and equipped with radar-like display consoles. The Center processed and disseminated information received from participating vessels operating in the VTS area. As previously mentioned, the goal of the system was to improve vessel transit safety by providing the vessels with advance information of other reported traffic within the VTS area.

## Hardware and Software

Before entering or getting underway in the NOLA-VTS area, the pilot of a vessel radioed to the VTC his position, destination, and estimated time of arrival (in addition to other important information, such as the existence of hazardous cargo). This information was input to a computer simulation program, which tracked the vessel by dead-reckoning (i.e., it updated the vessel's position as a function of direction and speed of movement) and registered it as a “blip” on the VTC display terminals. These terminals were similar to air traffic control displays.

The processing software was custom-developed, using a procedural language, to simulate the movement of vessel traffic. There was a series of four processor and display pairs, one dedicated to each sector of the lower Mississippi River monitored by the NOLA-VTS. When a vessel completed its transit through a sector, the vessel's simulated image was transferred to the next sector's microprocessor and VTC display terminal. Once a vessel exited from the tracking system, there was no computerized record kept of a vessel's transit through the VTS area.

## Communications

A series of VHF-FM transceiver stations were positioned along the Mississippi River, and microwave relays beamed transmissions to and from the VTC via antennas. A separate VHF-FM channel was designated for each of the four VTS operating sectors. The VTC monitored radio transmissions between meeting and overtaking vessels to make sure their navigators had arranged passing agreements. For a limited time, a tape recording was retained of all voice communication between VTS participants and the VTC in New Orleans and among the VTS participants themselves.

## User-System Interface

Watching the blips on their consoles, Coast Guard personnel at the VTC provided vessel navigators with the information relevant to developing situations and the proximity of other vessels. Displayed on each console on command, details were conveyed on request by radio to participating vessels regarding the name, type, cargo, and mile mark for each vessel that the participant in the VTS would encounter as it made headway to its destination. Participants could receive estimates of when and where they would overtake or meet other vessels in the area. Figure 2 shows an example of the VTS output display.

Vessel operators used this information for making decisions regarding the proper navigation strategy in plying the lower Mississippi River. On their own, vessel pilots already have an extensive knowledge base regarding the waterways. As Perrow (1984) notes, however, the information provided by a VTS can effectively enhance the vessel pilots' knowledge of the physical geography and thereby improve navigation safety.

## Information available from the VTS

As part of fulfilling the most critical mission of a VTS — providing information to vessels for planning their transit of the VTS area — NOLA-VTS provided the following additional important information (see U.S. Coast guard, 1985, for a detailed summary).

## Traffic Summaries

Traffic summaries are predictions of traffic to be encountered by the vessel as it moves to the next reporting point. In addition to predictions of vessels to be met, other critical information was relayed by the VTS to vessels. This information included data on “no wake” areas, the existence of dredges in operation, the location of ships anchored out of authorized anchorages, or other hazardous situations that would be passed.

The action of “getting underway” is a most critical maneuver, especially when the vessel must “top-around.” (The top-around maneuver for a ship is a u-turn from a stationary position commencing with a vessel is docked or anchored.) The VTS could provide a prediction of vessels intending to pass an area so that a vessel planning to get underway could choose the best moment to start the maneuver.

## Areas of Low Visibility

NOLA-VTS attempted to keep track of the subjective climatic reports it received from vessels. The VTS could report on visibility as relayed by other watermen, but the VTS itself had no visibility measuring equipment.

## Safety, Speed and Security Zones and Limited Draft Areas

Local restrictions affecting the navigation of traffic are periodically established by the Captain of the Port. Information concerning these restrictions could be obtained from the VTS.

## Aid to Navigation Discrepancies

The VTS received reports and maintained an up-to-date record of all aid to navigation discrepancies within the VTS area. These included buoys adrift, off-station or extinguished, as well as lights damaged, extinguished or destroyed. Once received by the VTS, these reports were included in traffic summaries.

## River Stages and Bridge Clearances

The VTS received information daily from the National Weather Service as to the river stage at the Carrollton, Reserve, Donaldsonville, and Baton Rouge Gauges. By obtaining the appropriate gauge reading and applying it to the vertical clearances for bridges in the VTS area, airdrafts for these bridges could be determined by the user or obtained from the VTS.

## Shoal Areas

During periods of falling river stages, the crossings between New Orleans and Baton Rouge sometimes were subject to shoaling (i.e., the build up of deposits on the river bottom). The VTS relayed the latest information provided by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.

## Speed Checks

A vessel's speed was updated whenever it called in to the VTS giving its location or referencing its position.

## ETA to Destination

By applying the most accurate estimate of a vessel's speed, an estimated time of arrival (ETA) for the destination or any point contained within the VTS area could be calculated for the user.

In an effort to avoid unnecessarily cluttering the VTS channels, none of the information described above was automatically provided to vessels; it had to be specifically requested. While traffic summaries had the highest priority for response, the answer to any question pertinent to the safety of a vessel transit received the VTC's prompt attention.

## Marine decision making with VTS support

Navigating the lower Mississippi River involves decisions that are relatively unstructured and usually relate to the passing and overtaking of vessels. Vessel pilots generally would argue that there is no pre-programmed plan of action for handling most navigation decisions. Their decisions may be unstructured due to several factors, including the uniqueness of the circumstance, the degree of uncertainty regarding the problem, and the lack of time available to adequately prepare a comprehensive solution.

Decisions on how to pass (face-to-face meeting of vessels) or overtake (one vessel passes another going in the same direction) are usually quickly reached but gradually implemented due to the slowness of large ships and barge tows to react to decisions to change course and/or speed. These decisions have severe consequences if they are not made in the best fashion. Poor decisions cannot be reversed quickly because of the nature of vessel steerage and propulsion; bad judgements may result in serious accidents, potentially resulting in significant dollar losses as well as deaths. Carpenter (1988) used historical casualty records for the Port of New Orleans to calculate a savings of \$545,000 for each accident prevented through the use of the VTS.

The decision support offered by a VTS reduces the uncertainty about other vessels' positions as well as their intentions. Advanced warning of critical or hazardous waterway conditions was also included in this central distribution of information by the VTS. In sum, this more accurate and complete information allowed vessel pilots to make better decisions about when and where to pass or overtake other watercraft.

## Research Methodology

A DSS is designed to enhance the effectiveness of decision making. If the rate of DSS utilization can be associated with the desired changes in the criterion variable, such as the frequency of accidents, then decision making and the resulting performance have likely been improved through increased use of the DSS. The purpose of the following analysis is to study the effect of the NOLA-VTS on river safety as measured by the change in accidents resulting from the change in system utilization rate.

To understand the relationship between system utilization and performance, it was necessary to create models that included the factors affecting vessel accident frequency. Correlation analysis (i.e., regression) was selected as the most appropriate technique for constructing models of the factors that contribute to the changes in vessel accident frequency. The methodological approach taken was to incorporate variables in the regression equation that would account for all possible sources of variation in the performance measure. The analysis would identify those factors that contributed at a statistically significant level to changes in the criterion for system performance and success.

## Criterion variable

The NOLA-VTS audited shipping accidents that occurred in its operating area and recorded them on a daily log. The vessel casualty data collected was organized according to two accident types:

collisions and rammings. A collision is defined as any contact between vessels that are underway, anchored, moored, or in the process of docking or undocking. A ramming is the collision of a vessel with a fixed object, such as a wharf, dock, pier, bridge, submerged object, or navigation aid. Collisions and rammings are combined for purposes of this study because they are similar types of accidents and should respond in a similar fashion to external factors. Furthermore, rammings occur infrequently; separating these variables for this study would lead to a noncontinuous series.

The VTS statistics used in this study span nearly three years from April 1979 to December 1981. These 144 weeks represent a continuous time series of accidents on the lower Mississippi River from the moment when NOLA-VTS began recording statistics on utilization of their system until the VTS was temporarily closed in early 1982. (Closure was the result of an interim federal budget reduction.) Over this period, there were 272 collisions and rammings reported to NOLA-VTS. $^{1}$ Table 1 lists various descriptive statistics about the criterion and explanatory variables used in the regression analysis.

## Explanatory variables

Five general categories of explanatory variables were identified to represent potential sources of variation in system performance: (1) length of DSS use; (2) traffic level; (3) DSS utilization; (4) river stage; and (5) weather. The length of DSS use represents the box in Figure 1 labelled “Information Technology,” while the other four variables are represented in Figure 1 by the box labelled “Other Variables Affecting Performance.” The regression equation will evaluate the forward linkage of system utilization and other variables affecting performance with accident rate — the measure of performance. These independent, or explanatory, variables are further described below.

## Length of DSS Use

Trend variables can be employed in statistical models to measure the dynamic impact on the criterion variable resulting from the introduction of new technology. In this modeling effort, a trend effect represented the impact of the NOLA-VTS on navigational safety to the degree that it was independent of other factors. Both linear and curvilinear forms of trend inclusion were evaluated.

## Traffic Level

According to maritime authorities, traffic level is another major element influencing the occurrence of accidents in the study area. Weekly measures of traffic volume include statistics for ocean-going ships, barge tow assemblies, tugs, as well as excursion craft. Weekly observation on traffic levels were constructed by aggregating the daily peak traffic load on the lower Mississippi River as determined by the NOLA-VTS.

## DSS Utilization

DSS utilization is the percentage of total traffic that was communicating with the VTC. This variable was constructed by aggregating the daily usage rate to provide a weekly figure. The U.S. Coast Guard calculated the level of utilization for the New Orleans VTS at the hour of peak daily traffic.

Over the study period, the participation or utilization rate of the NOLA-VTS averaged 74 percent per day (see Table 1). This percentage figure likely underestimates the true utilization rate because it included only participants interacting with the VTS and excluded those navigators and vessels who were only receiving communications from the DSS. Despite a participation rate of less than 100 percent, all vessels within the system's area of coverage were tracked by the VTS computer. Their position, description, direction, and speed were reported by interacting participants.

## River Stage

The stage of the river (height above sea level measured in feet) is a critical element of vessel safety because river stage directly determines the velocity of the current. The hazardous conditions that often accompany the changes in the river stage (hence, river current) precipitate many vessel casualties on the lower Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and the Gulf of Mexico.

## Weather

Meteorological conditions that are hazardous to navigation in the study area include those producing strong winds, heavy rainfall, and fog. Indicator variables were constructed to model seasonal weather patterns.

## Results and Discussion

## Regression model of DSS performance

A regression model was constructed and evaluated to determine the association of DSS usage and other influential variables with system performance (i.e., vessel accidents). Explanatory variables incorporated in this model included those discussed in the last section (measures of length of DSS use, traffic level, DSS utilization, river stage (proxy for river current), weather indicators), along with a lagged endogenous variable.

The best model for the criterion of collisions/rammings explained almost 43 percent of the variation in weekly accidents of this type (see Table

2). $^{2}$ The overall fit of the regression function, $^{3}$ as given by the F-statistic's high level of statistical significance, was quite good.

## Independent variables

## Lagged Accident Rate

A lagged endogenous variable (accident level) of one period represented the existing momentum whereby the level of the criterion was expected to amount to an approximately fixed percentage lower (or higher) than the previous time period (Ostrom, 1978). The analysis suggests that accidents were declining by about 20 percent per week over the study period, independent of the effect of other variables. The lagged accident level was not substantially correlated with the length of DSS use, which suggests differential impacts resulting from these explanatory variables upon the criterion.

## Length of DSS Use

Starting at relatively high levels, vessel accidents were estimated $^{4}$ to have declined at an exponential pace. The accident rate dropped faster initially upon system implementation, and then declined further but at an ever slower pace (see Figure 3). This behavior over time would be anticipated if the DSS/VTS were effective in promoting maritime safety. Given the level of statistical significance, the changes in the accident rate for the lower Mississippi River due to the impact of the DSS/VTS technology had only a 5 percent chance of being a random effect.

At the start of the time series in April 1979, the accident rate was dropping at a rate of 7.65 collisions and rammings per week (see Table 3). As the information technology of the DSS/VTS was used for a greater length of time, the rate of decrease in the accident rate slowed. At the end of the series (December 1981), the decrease in accident rate was negligible, independent of the effect of the other variables in the regression equation. By that time, the learning curve effect of the DDS/VTS had been completed.

## Traffic Level

According to the regression, river traffic had a statistically significant (at the 1% level) effect on the level of collisions and rammings. For every 10-unit increase in vessel traffic, the model predicted a .6-unit increase in these accidents per week.

## DSS Utilization

Change in the usage rate for the DSS also had a statistically significant impact on the performance criterion for the NOLA-VTS. In the regression equation, the DSS usage rate is an independent measure of system utilization. This relationship models the forward linkage of system utilization with system performance, as diagrammed in Figure 1. A 10 percent increase in system utilization rate was associated with a .5-unit reduction in the weekly number of collision/rammings, assuming constant values for all other variables in the equation (i.e., traffic, river current, and weather). The level of statistical significance for the utilization rate variable indicates that this effect of system usage on the measure of system performance had only a 5 percent chance of being a random effect.

This statistically significant relationship between increased DSS utilization and a reduction in accidents supports the hypothesis that an increase in usage rate of a voluntary DSS — a utilization rate that is strongly correlated with the desired impact on the performance criterion variable (i.e., river accidents) — documents a successful DSS implementation in the public sector.

## River Stage

The regression model included a proxy variable for speed of river current, namely river stage, with quadratic effects (see Table 2). Differentiation of the river stage portion of the model, which was significant at the 1% level, indicates that collisions and rammings began to decrease when the river stage reached 3.9 feet.

## Weather

The weather indicator variables for December-January and April-May were statistically significant at the 5% level. The respective coefficients for these weather indicators suggest that one additional collision/ramming would be expected to occur each week during December, January, April, and May for reasons of weather compared to the base months of June through September.

Based on the established system performance criterion (i.e., accident reduction) for the NOLA-VTS, the model reported herein demonstrated that increased system utilization resulted in a significant reduction in accidents — the desired change in system performance. Furthermore, the regression analysis established the link between the IT and performance through the intervening variable of utilization. When utilization of the information technology increased, improved performance was strongly established, with little chance of this relationship being a random phenomenon.

## Summary and Conclusions

Since its inception in late 1977 through its termination in March 1988, the NOLA-VTS monitored and disseminated information about vessel traffic on the lower Mississippi River. The VTS' radio and computer network provided information about marine traffic to ocean-going vessels, large barge tows, and other river craft for improved navigation decisions through one of the world's most difficult areas to navigate.

## VTS closure selection

More than acceptable performance by the NOLA-VTS did not guarantee its continued operation. It was decommissioned in March 1988. In selecting VTSs for closure, the Coast Guard employed factors aimed primarily at resolving its immediate problem of reducing expenses (U.S. General Accounting Office, 1988). Because the Coast Guard limited the closure process to this type of factor, it did not consider any information about the effectiveness of VTSs in enhancing vessel safety — the VTSs' primary goal and the reason they were initially established. The user participation rate was the only stated performance measure the Coast Guard used to determine which VTSs to close. Other factors were based solely on which VTSs could be closed quickly and with the greatest cost savings. These latter criteria did not consider VTS effectiveness in improving vessel safety, including prevention of accidents and fatalities as well as protection of the environment through prevention of oil spills and accidents involving hazardous cargoes (U.S. Coast Guard, 1987).

## DSS evaluation with an objective measure of performance

An early criticism of the NOLA-VTS, based on subjective and likely premature user perceptions, was that the system was not effective in reducing vessel accidents (Center for Wetland Resources, 1981). However, this notion was not consistent with the findings of this study, which are based on objective measures of performance. Srinivasan (1985) reports that perceived measures of system effectiveness are not always positively associated with actual behavioral measures, as appears to be the case with this DSS.

Pagano and Verdin (1987) describe several problems associated with implementing computer technology in transportation (possibly a DSS/VTS), including resistance from users, ineffective use of new technology, and possible short-term loss of productivity. The problem of learning new behaviors and adjusting to new ways of doing old jobs can generate a great deal of resistance to computing technology. The resistance and hesitancy to use computers and the lack of understanding of hardware and software capabilities can result in new technology being used ineffectively. However, none of the objective and quantitative measures employed in this study give much support to the aforementioned thesis as it would possibly relate to the NOLA-VTS.

Over the study period, the average participation rate was 74 percent (see Table 1). This is a very high utilization rate, especially for a voluntary public sector DSS.

As employed in this post-implementation review, objective measures (i.e., numbers of collisions and rammings) of vessel safety determined the effect of the DSS technology on system performance by ascertaining whether this VTS in fact accomplished what it was designed to accomplish. The desired impact, as documented by declining levels of vessel casualties, was associated at a high level of statistical significance with immediate changes in utilization rate of the VTS as well as the long-term usage or adoption of the information technology of the DSS.

An abundance of experiemtnal laboratory studies, as well as field surveys of user perceptions, populate the research literature. However, few quantitative field studies based on actual working systems exist in the IS literature. In the research reported here, a quantitative analysis of an objective criterion for a public sector and voluntary DSS offers several noteworthy findings and implications for DSS development, performance appraisal of IS, and strategy for future research studies in this area.

IS managers should pay particular attention to the exhibited relationship between system usage and the improved level of system performance — the forward linkage. In the performance appraisal of IS, the correlation between objective measures of system utilization and objective measures of performance would clearly establish the linkage between information technology and system performance. If every DSS achieved performance gains as significant as the NOLA-VTS, every IS manager could be perceived as creating organizational impact of heroic proportion.

Creation of models such as the one exhibited in this article should be kept in mind when systems are conceived. Positive performance measurements could be used as a vehicle for promoting other IS activities. Clear measures of success could go a long way toward a positive organizational climate with regard to IS.

In developing DSS, the authors advise IS builders to follow a number of guidelines suggested by this field research: (1) carefully search for performance criteria when the system is being contemplated; (2) obtain agreement from involved users on the performance cirteria; (3) measure performance before the new system is installed (possibly even while the DSS is being built); and (4) measure performance as the system approaches stability. Using a regression model as exhibited in this article would provide a solid justification for DSS work.

Future research designs to evaluate the success of a DSS or any other type of IS should relate an objective and quantitative measure of usage with an objective performance criterion. Objective measures of performance, in our opinion, are superior to subjective measure of user “happiness” or satisfaction. Performance appraisal of an IS should be in terms that are related to organizational goals that objectively guide systems development, operation, and evaluation.
