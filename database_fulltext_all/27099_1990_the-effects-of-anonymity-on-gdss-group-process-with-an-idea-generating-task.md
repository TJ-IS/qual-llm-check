# The Effects of Anonymity on GDSS Group Process With an Idea-Generating Task

## Abstract

This study examines the influence of anonymity on group process in groups using a group decision support system (GDSS) with an idea-generating task. Group members whose contributions were anonymous generated more comments, were more critical and probing, and were more likely to embellish ideas proposed by others than were those whose contributions were identified by name. Implications for group support research are discussed.

Keywords: Group support systems, group process and outcome, anonymity, idea generation and evaluation

ACM Categories: H.1.2, H.4.2, J.4

## Introduction

Irving Janis (1973) analyzed the foreign policy decision making of groups in several presidential administrations. He concluded that these very cohesive and close-knit groups may be prone to “groupthink,” which he defines as deterioration of mental efficiency, reality testing, and moral judgment in the interest of group solidarity. Group members involved in the Kennedy Administration decision to invade Cuba in April, 1961, for example, reported having reservations about the decision, but did not let their differences be known for fear of appearing weak and unintelligent or upsetting the cohesiveness of the group. If the group members involved in the process had employed a mechanism for safely displaying their differing opinions, this potentially disastrous situation might have been avoided.

As Janis's example reminds us, group problem solving is often an inefficient and ineffective process, involving what Steiner (1966, 1972) has characterized as a complex tradeoff between process gains and losses. In an effort to shift the balance toward gains, information systems researchers have developed group decision support systems (GDSSs): interactive computer-based systems that combine communication, computer, and decision technologies to support the formulation and solution of unstructured problems by a groups (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). This study examines one aspect of one such GDSS: the effects of anonymity on idea generation and evaluation.

## GDSS Research

In a review of computer-mediated communication research, Hiltz and Turoff (1978) note that computer-based media produce a sense of impersonality and that this effect is further increased by user anonymity. Research on GDSS use suggests that this impersonality may have a significant effect on group problem solving. For example, DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) hypothesize that anonymity enhances group member participation by reducing inhibitions.

Unfortunately, empirical investigations thus far provide confusing results. Several researchers have investigated the effects of automated group support on group functioning in laboratory and field studies, all comparing automated groups with non-automated groups (see, for example, Gallupe, 1985; Gallupe, et al., 1988; Hiltz, et al., 1986; Jarvenpaa, et al., 1988; Kull, 1982; Siegel, et al., 1986; Steeb and Johnston, 1981; Turoff and Hiltz, 1982; Watson, et al., 1988; Zigurs, et al., 1988). These studies suggest that automated group support can, in some cases, improve group performance and save time. However, the systems were not helpful in all situations.

Findings on how the technology affects member satisfaction were similarly mixed. Further, each investigation compared the work of computer-supported groups with that of non-supported groups (see Dennis, et al., 1988, for a review). In short, though automated and non-automated groups often perform differently, it is not clear how specific aspects of the technology (anonymity, physical proximity, interaction sequencing, group size, response time, graphics capability, and dozens of others) affect specific aspects of the group process. It is time to move beyond what Gutek (1989) refers to as first-generation research strategies and begin to isolate specific components of the technology and observe their effects on process.

The present research explores one such specific GDSS relationship, the relationship between anonymity and group process with an idea-generating task. We chose anonymity because it is professed to be an important aspect of GDSS technology.

Despite two recent investigations of GDSS anonymity (Connolly, et al., 1990; Jessup and Tansik, in press) we are far from understanding the true impacts of anonymous GDSS interaction. Thus, the purpose of this investigation is to determine whether anonymity influences group process. Practically, anonymity is important because it may provide the mechanism group members sometimes need to safely display their ideas and opinions. For this article, anonymity is defined as the identifiability of group member contributions. The next section reviews existing research on the psychology of deindividuation in search of relevant background.

## Deindividuation Research in Social Psychology

Festinger, et al., (1952) suggest that in some situations group members behave as if they are “submerged in the group” (p. 382). Group members do not think of the other members as individuals and do not think that the other members can single them out for their behavior. This “deinviduation” results in a reduction of normal inhibitions, enabling group members to engage in behavior they would not normally display.

Subsequent research in a variety of situations suggests that anonymity contribute to deindividuation. Zimbardo (1969) reports that subjects who were made to feel anonymous by wearing identical white coats and hoods delivered electrical shocks to others for twice as long as did subjects who were visible and wearing large name tags. Diener, et al. (1976) demonstrate that Halloween trick-or-treaters who remained anonymous and were left unattended at the doorway with a candy bowl were twice as likely to steal candy than children who were asked their name and address and then left unattended with the candy. Studies of identifiability effects in social loafing (where individuals loaf and “freeride” on the efforts of others; see, for example, Harkins and Petty, 1982; Kerr and Bruun, 1981; Williams, et al., 1981), and a parallel cognitive loafing paradigm (Weldon and Mustari, 1988), suggest that identified group members exert greater physical or mental effort than those working anonymously.

Though the specific behaviors involved in each of these studies differ, the impact of anonymity was the same: anonymity led to a reduction in behavioral constraints and enabled individuals to engage in behavior they would not engage in when identified. In the context of electronic communication in general, the phenomenon of “flaming” — uninhibited interaction, critical comments, use of strong language, and the like (Kiesler, et al., 1984; Siegel, et al., 1986) — could be similarly traced to the reduction of social constraints. We thus hypothesize that GDSS problem-solving groups working anonymously will generate more comments, and particularly more critical comments, than will similar groups whose members are identified to one another by name. Our study aimed at testing this hypothesis is described below.

## Research Design

The subjects for this study were undergraduate students and were picked from an upper-division organizational behavior course. Those chosen needed to satisfy a course requirement for experimental participation in a laboratory setting. The subjects were drawn from a very large (300 plus) class section, so few of them were acquainted before the experiment. For this experiment, we used the University of Arizona's PlexCenter GDSS, developed by the University's Department of Management Information Systems (for a more detailed description of the system see Dennis, et al., 1988). Using the Dennis, et al. classification, the facility used for this experiment can be classified as a GDSS Decision Room where the computer system is the dominant channel for interaction (rather than verbal interaction). Eighty students participated, working in 20 groups of four members each. The average age of the subjects was 25.4 years. Sixty-six percent of the subjects were male, 81 percent were seniors and 19 percent were juniors. Subjects were randomly assigned to the anonymous or identified conditions, as described below.

The PlexCenter includes 16 interactive workstations in a U-shaped configuration. The system is managed by a group facilitator who is stationed at the head of the room at a separate workstation and file server. For this experiment, the facilitator managed the GDSS and the experimental procedures described below. The system software used included IBM PCNetwork and an electronic brainstorming program. This program displays a theme question to each participant and invites a comment or suggestion of up to five lines. When the participant enters a comment, the file is replaced with another, randomly drawn from those generated by other participants and containing the theme questions and whatever comments it has attracted. The participant appends a further comment and sends the file into the network where it is directed to another randomly selected member of the group. This process of retrieving files from the network, adding comments, and sending the file back to the network is repeated until the end of a predetermined brainstorming period.

Anonymity was manipulated through a program that either attached user names to each of their comments or left them unidentified. Group members could clearly see that their contributions during the problem solving process were either identified or not identified. Further, the group members interacted through the computer system only. Crosstalk (either verbal or non-verbal) was virtually non-existent. As a check of the anonymity manipulation, group members were asked after their session if they thought the panel to review group ideas would be able to trace comments back to individual group members and whether group members would be able to trace ideas back to other group members (measured at the individual level: sample size equals 80, 1 = yes, 2 = don't know, 3 = no).

The anonymity manipulation appears to have been successful: Subjects in the identified condition were more likely to report that the review panel would be able to trace comments back to each group member (F = 70.27, p < .001), and that group members would be able to identify each other's contributions (F = 97.69, p < .001) than were subjects in the anonymous condition. Note, however, that the manipulation was not perfect: Some anonymous subjects were unsure about their anonymity from other group members and from the review panel (review panel can trace: identified condition, mean = 1.15, s.d. = 0.362; anonymous condition, mean = 2.15, s.d. = 0.662. Group members can trace: identified condition, mean = 1.225, s.d. = 0.53; anonymous condition, mean = 2.475, s.d. = 0.599.). It is unclear whether this is simply generalized suspicion or if the physical proximity of the experimental setup may have weakened perceptions of anonymity. In any case, an ANOVA based on the actual anonymity treatment was compared with one based on the subject's (self-report) perceived treatment, and although there was some cross-over of some subjects from one anonymity condition to another, the overall results are the same.

Each group worked alone with the facilitator in the laboratory for approximately one hour. The four subjects working together at the time were thus the only subjects in the laboratory during their session. On arrival at the laboratory, subjects participated in a 10-minute demonstration session that familiarized them with the system and emphasized the identification/anonymity manipulation. They were then presented with the group task: to generate and evaluate ideas to solve the University's parking problem. In

McGrath's (1984) eightfold taxonomy of tasks, this task represents those labeled Type 2, tasks that call for idea generation or creativity.

Parking is a chronic campus problem and appears to have provided a relevant and motivating issue for the student subjects. They were told that their proposals would be reviewed by a panel of traffic management experts who would judge the quality and quantity of ideas proposed. They were then given 30 minutes to produce and evaluate ideas using the electronic brainstorming program. All students were anxious to contribute. They worked hard, and every group was still intensely involved in the task when they were asked to stop at the end of each session. After the 30 minutes, they completed a brief post-experimental questionnaire (the manipulation checks), and were debriefed and released.

The electronic brainstorming program merges and saves all group member comments in a text file. Working from McGrath's (1984) definition of group interaction process (“the patterned behavior of members of a standing group in a particular behavior setting”), we measured group process using the comments group members made through the GDSS using the electronic brainstorming program. After each session, each group’s text file was content-coded using a coding scheme modified from one developed by Gettys, et al. (1987). (A description of the coding scheme and experimental task can be found in Connolly, et al., 1990. These materials are also available from the authors upon request.)

Our scheme coded both proposed solutions to the parking problem and comments addressed to these proposals. A complete listing of potential solutions for campus parking problems was provided by Gettys, et al. (1987). The major branches of that solution space include, “use available parking space more efficiently,” “increase on-campus parking space,” “reduce demand for on-campus parking,” and “change parking priorities and/or policies.” That scheme was used to code ideas. The other comment categories were developed by examining the available text files from previous executive groups. The following categories were determined to represent an exhaustive list of mutually exclusive categories of comments generated during electronic brainstorming sessions (later analysis revealed relatively low correlations among the comment categories, suggesting that they are indeed mutually exclusive):

\- Proposed solution (categorized via the Gettys, et al., scheme and used to determine total proposed solutions)

\- Simple supportive comments (“I like that idea.”)

\- Supportive arguments (“I like that because it will eliminate crowding.”)

\- Solution clarifications (“We could make the remote parking lot in a shopping center,” — adds detail or new features favoring a proposed solution)

\- Problem clarifications (“Another thing we need to worry about is congestion on the local streets,” — adds detail or new features to problem statement)

\- Simple critical comments (“I don’t like that idea.”)

\- Critical arguments (“A drawback to that idea is....”)

\- Questions about solutions (“How would the shuttle deal with handicapped riders?”)

\- Questions about the problem ("Are we trying to deal with student parking or just faculty/staff?")

\- Comments about the computer system (“This system is too slow,” — positive, negative, or neutral comment about the computer network or its operation)

\- Comments about the group process (“Let’s try to agree on something, anyway,” — positive, negative, or neutral comment about the interpersonal processes of the group)

\- Comments off the topic

\- Uncodable text

These categories were used to code group member comments other than ideas. This coding scheme was then used to code each group's text file. Redundant solutions were stripped from each group's list of total proposed solutions to measure the number of original proposed solutions. All comments were used to measure the total number of parsed units of speech (total number of comments). There were typically few, if any, comments about the system, about the group process, off the topic, or uncodable text.

Two independent coders rated each group's files as a check of coding reliability. Each coder first parsed a paper transcript of the output files into individual comments and then coded the comments using the category scheme summarized in Table 1. The coders then met and discussed discrepancies until they reached agreement. This inevitably required some changes in both parsing and coding, but the overall process appears to have been adequately reliable. One coder changed an average of 4.5 parsings and 11.7 codings per file, the other coder 3.6 parsings and 8.1 codings per file. Given an average file size of 63.8 comments per file, the initial parsings were identical for over 87 percent (55.7/63.8) of the comments. Since each parsing change yielded at least one coding change, initial coding reliability was better than 82 percent (52.1/63.8). ANOVA was then used to test for differences between anonymous and identified conditions on each variable in the coding scheme.

## Results

Table 1 presents means and standard deviations for all dependent variables. These measures are counts from the content coding.

The data generally support our hypothesis. Groups working anonymously generated more solution clarifications (F = 4.62, p < .05), more critical comments (F = 8.33, p < .01), more questions about solutions (F = 7.79, p < .02), and more total comments (F = 13.85, p < .01) than did groups working under identified conditions. No statistically significant differences were found for the number of ideas, supportive comments, comments about the system, or comments about the group.

These results suggest that group members working anonymously in an automated group problem-solving environment are more likely to be critical of other's ideas, to ask questions about other's ideas, and to clarify or add to their own and other's ideas. Although we have only scratched the surface of “critical thinking” (see Brookfield, 1987, for a thorough review), our data suggest that anonymous groups are more critical and probing and more likely to embellish an idea.

Further, groups working under anonymity generate more comments. Although anonymity did not significantly influence the quantity of original ideas generated, it did influence the nature of the group session. The GDSS in the anonymous condition appeared to act as a buffer between people, disassociating them from their comments. One user said she liked the system because when someone criticized her comments she felt they were focusing on the content of the comments, not on her personally. She thought this was much less threatening than criticism in a face-to-face, non-automated environment. Similar explanations have been offered for interaction in computer-mediated communication environments in general (Hiltz and Turoff, 1978).

## Discussion

Previous studies of automated group support have shown that the technology can have a substantial effect on group outcome, but may provide little insight into the mechanisms involved. This investigation has shown that a common component of automated group support, anonymity, may be an important variable. By providing anonymity, the GDSS seems to have contributed to what Festinger, et al. (1952) refer to as deindividuation. By disassociating individuals from their comments and buffering group members from one another, anonymity appears to have reduced behavioral constraints on group members and led them to contribute more freely, and less inhibitedly, to the group discussion.

It is significant that anonymity affected some of the variables listed in Table 1 and not others. Both anonymous groups and identified groups made equal numbers of supportive comments and therefore did not differ significantly. One possible explanation is that group members were generally congenial. Anonymous group members, however, felt they could more safely make critical comments or ask questions about proposed solutions than identified group members. Groups in both conditions made few comments about the system and the group, so there was no significant difference. One possible explanation for this is that groups in both conditions were similarly motivated and generally stayed on track. To understand why anonymous and identified groups did not differ on the number of ideas they generated, two other related investigations must be examined.

The results for this experiment mirror those for the Jessup and Tansik (in press) investigation of GDSS anonymity and group member proximity. However, the results of this experiment are different from those obtained in the Connolly, et al. (1990) investigation of GDSS anonymity and evaluative tone. In our experiment and in the study by Jessup and Tansik the anonymity manipulation was identical and had modest effects on process. But the manipulation had no significant effects on output (e.g., ideas generated) compared to those of the Connolly, et al., experiment, where in addition to process effects the manipulation had modest effects on output (measured as the number of original solutions produced). In the Connolly, et al., experiment the researchers used software similar to that used in our experiment and in the Jessup and Tansik experiment, but used it in a larger GDSS facility where multiple groups were run simultaneously (four groups of four). This change of setting apparently allowed anonymity of a more meaningful sort than was achieved in the smaller lab, where only four subjects participated at a time (one four-person group).

Our interpretation of the differences between these experiments is consistent with social presence theory (Reder and Conklin, 1987; Short, et al., 1976), in which social presence is defined as a subjective, cognitive synthesis of all the many factors that reflect the social immediacy or intimacy of a communication medium. Social presence depends on the visual non-verbal cues transmitted, the apparent distance of the person or people that are being communicated with, and the “realness” of those being communicated with (Short, et al., 1976). Similarly, social impact theory (Latane, 1981; Latane and Nida, 1980; Latane and Wolf, 1981) suggests that the degree of social impact — pressure to act — on a group member is a function of the strength (interpersonal power), immediacy, and status of the other group members. Perhaps the relatively “impersonal” larger decision support facility creates an atmosphere that is more conducive to free brainstorming (as originally described by Osborn, 1953) than is the atmosphere created by the smaller, more intimate facility.

## Areas for Further Research

Further research is needed to understand the full impact of anonymity on group process. First, future research might consider the direction of effect to be expected from anonymity. In this case, it appears to have reduced social barriers and inhibitions thereby increasing participation. It it, however, easy to think of settings (such as those in the social loafing literature reviewed earlier) in which participation is aversive and is maintained only by social sanctions. In such cases, clearly, anonymity would be expected to lower participation. A challenge for research in this area is thus to characterize situations in which anonymity might hinder, rather than help, desired group activities.

A second area for potential investigation is the relationship between anonymity and the nature of member relations. For strangers, the labeling of comments with names might produce a general “identifiability” effect (e.g., “That message was generated by person A, who happens to be called John”). For mere acquaintances, this manipulation might have a “recognizability” effect (e.g., “That message was generated by that tall blonde fellow, John, who sat over by the door”). For close associates, this manipulation might have a “familiarity” effect (e.g., “That message was generated by John, my long-time friend and boss”).

The first of these is the pure effect of being identified. The second refers to situations in which members can identify other comments and recognize the authors as people they have met before. The third refers to situations in which members can identify comments, recognize authors, and attach some meaning to the comment via their relationships with the authors. In this study, postexperimental questioning revealed few prior acquaintances among subjects, suggesting that the manipulation was of the first kind, identifiability among strangers. Further research might explore the effects of the other types of anonymity and different ways of embodying the anonymity conditions themselves.

## Limitations

The study is, of course, not without its limitations. Its generalizability is limited by its use of unpaid student subjects, the relatively brief solution period, and the nature of the problem itself. Rank and power, for example, operate more strongly in organizational decision-making groups. Anonymity's influence may be different in such settings. Replication across other populations, tasks, and settings would clearly be valuable. The anonymity achieved in this setting, among small student groups seated within a few feet of one another, may be significantly different from that experienced by an unseen and unsigned participant in a national network of unknown size and duration.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to GDSS research because it suggests that GDSS design features, such as anonymous entry and display of ideas, may be critical in determining how people exchange ideas with one another. We do not claim that anonymity is the sole cause of GDSS effects, only that it is one important cause. We hope that this research spurs other researchers to try to determine which aspects of the technology trigger important changes in the way group members interact with one another. In this way we can begin to understand “why” and “how” technology impacts people.

## About the Authors

Leonard M. Jessup is an assistant professor at California State University, San Marcos. He received his Ph.D. in organizational behavior at the University of Arizona, with a minor in management information systems. He is a member of the Academy of Management and The Institute of Management Sciences. He received his B.A. in information and communication studies and his M.B.A. from California State University, Chico. He has worked as a community relations director in the health care industry and as a sales engineer in the electronics industry. His research interests include small group research, process analysis, and automated group problem solving.

Terry Connolly is professor of management and policy at the University of Arizona, where he also coordinates the Decision Behavior Laboratory. His academic background includes degrees in electrical engineering, sociology, and organizational behavior, and he has held faculty appointments at Georgia Institute of Technology, University of Illinois, and University of Chicago. His primary research interests are in decision behavior in organizational settings and the effects of information processing technologies on them. His publications on these and related topics include two books and over fifty journal papers and book chapters.

Jolene Galegher is an assistant professor of management and policy at the University of Arizona. She earned master's and doctoral degrees in social psychology at Northwestern University and was awarded a post-doctoral fellowship in quantitative methods by the Department of Psychology at the University of Illinois in Urbana-Champaign. Her research is concerned with collaborative intellectual work and with the nature and role of information systems to support such work. She was the organizer of a conference on technology for cooperative work sponsored by the National Science Foundation; an edited book based on the proceedings of that conference will be published shortly.
