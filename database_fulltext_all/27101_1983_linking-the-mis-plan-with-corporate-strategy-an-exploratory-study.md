# Linking the MIS Plan with Corporate Strategy: An Exploratory Study

By: Philip J. Pyburn

## Abstract

In recent years there has been an increasing amount of attention paid to effective strategic planning of the information systems resource in many organizations. The motivation for this attention derives from a number of sources, especially the rapid rate of technological progress in computer and telecommunications facilities and the increasing application of these facilities to problem domains that are critical to organizational success. As a wide range of new opportunities become apparent, it has become more difficult to match the operational, managerial, and strategic needs of the business with appropriate systems activities.

Based upon an exploratory study of eight organizations which involved extensive interviews with IS and senior managers, this article describes the planning practices observed and identifies several factors which seemed particularly important to their planning success, or lack of it. These factors include such things as the style of senior management decision making, the volatility of the business (and by extension, the applications development portfolio), the complexity of the IS organization and management task, and the status and physical location of the IS manager.

Keywords: Planning, strategy
ACM Categories: K.6.0, K.6.4

## Introduction

Until fairly recently, the relationship between the Information Systems (IS) function and corporate strategy was not of much interest to top management in most firms. As one senior executive put it, “the MIS department is like the plumbing; if I pull the chain and the water goes all over my shoes, then I call someone to get it fixed and forget about it.” The feeling in this company, and many like it, was that while IS might provide modest efficiency improvements, the factors critical to strategic success were elsewhere. Therefore, even though the IS manager was sincerely interested in identifying and supporting the basic goals of the firm, top management did not pay much attention because IS was not seen as important to either strategy formulation or implementation.

In the past few years, however, several things have changed to make the relationship between the corporate strategy set and the IS strategy set more important. First, the very issue of efficiency that relegated IS to a back seat is now a central theme in many firms' strategies, especially as productivity improvement becomes critical to survival in some industries. Second, IS has become increasingly important to the successful implementation of corporate strategy, as, for example, when direct customer order entry becomes a vehicle for a manufacturer to penetrate certain large retailers. And third, IS technology has become part and parcel of the products and services of many firms (e.g., banks), to the extent that IS strategy is a major part of corporate strategy. Because of these changes, there is increasing concern on the part of top management and IS management that strategic IS planning succeed.

In spite of this perceived need for better strategic IS planning, however, in several recent studies $[10, 16]$ only a few managers felt that their planning effort was really effective. In one manufacturing firm, for example, strategic IS planning was eliminated, while in a large financial institution the planning process hobbled along, but to no one's real satisfaction. On the other hand, at one bank a senior manager commented that “we would be lost without MIS Planning.”

If, as several authors suggest [6, 7, 12], there is a growing consensus that strategic IS planning has become critical to the success of the overall IS effort, then the obvious question is why so few firms are satisfied with their planning attempts. Could it be that in those firms where strategic IS planning was perceived to be effective, they had found the best general approach? Based on some research done over the past few years [10, 11, 13] it appears not. On virtually every dimension studied, some firms succeeded while others failed. For example, some of the firms that planned on an annual cycle were pleased with their results, but so were some firms that formally planned only when a major change or problem surfaced (perhaps every three or four years). Some firms found IBM's BSP [5] approach effective, while some did not. Some of the best success stories came from situations where there was little formal planning, though in other cases this very informality was seen by some managers to be at the root of planning failures.

And yet, in the strategic IS planning literature one finds few conclusions about these apparently contradictory results. In fact, one is struck most in this literature by the exhortations to plan more effectively and the number of different prescriptions for moving in that direction. Virtually every article on strategic IS planning by either practitioners or academics spends at least a few pages explaining the obvious benefits of such planning. By implication, this suggests that many of the failures of IS planning are the result of not trying hard enough, or at all. Certainly IS management has many reasons for resisting an all out planning effort:

— planning is costly and time consuming, and it requires the attention and commitment of people who are essential to solving today's problems. With backlogs that often stretch beyond two years, it is little wonder that IS managers do not undertake strategic planning, especially given the decidedly mixed success of other firms.

— planning is risky, in that it is both self critical and a process which leads to public, and often written, commitments. In one large bank, for example, a strategic IS plan was developed, with the help of an outside consultant, which led to a complete reassessment of the role of IS. As a result of this reassessment, the senior IS manager was eventually replaced because he was now perceived to be inappropriate for the new IS responsibility.

Perhaps instinctively, or perhaps based on prior experience, many IS managers recognize that a strategic IS planning process that fails often serves only to make matters worse.

Even when convinced to try some form of strategic IS planning, however, the IS manager is still faced with the selection of a planning methodology from the plethora proposed in the literature. Two obvious choices might be IBM's BSP [5] or BICS [17]. Other well known choices might involve a “stage assessment” methodology [18], or the determination of the top managers’ “critical success factors” [19].

In addition, there are numerous planning methodologies that have been proposed which view the development of a plan as analogous to the development of a system. Not surprisingly, these methodologies suggest phases and tasks which are similar to those of a systems development life cycle. For example, Lientz and Chen [7], Schwartz [14], Sporn [15], and Soden and Crandell [20] all propose multi-step planning procedures, with four to fourteen phases. Extending this tradition of “planning cum system development,” Highsmith [4] suggests the use of structured design techniques as part of the planning process.

It is interesting to note that most of these methodologies are reported as having been effective, but only in one or two firms. Therefore, one gets very little comparative description to help determine why the particular scheme worked well in that setting. Given the problems many IS managers seem to have in creating an effective strategic plan, it appears that these methodologies do not transfer consistently well to other environments. Therefore, this study was undertaken to describe some strategic IS planning that was actually underway, to look at the business and technical context within which that planning took place, and to attempt some tentative conclusions about the relative success or failure of the planning activity. Before proceeding to the research, however, a few general points about strategic IS planning will help to define the problem.

## What is strategic IS planning?

Most readers with an interest in strategic planning are probably familiar with Drucker's [2] definition of planning as: “the continuous process of making present entrepreneurial (risk-taking) decisions systematically and with the best possible knowledge of their futurity, organizing systematically the efforts needed to carry out these decisions against the expectations through organized, systematic feedback.” In these terms, planning is thus an attempt to deal with the future effect of current decisions. It is not forecasting, which is an attempt to predict the future. Further, planning is a developmental process rather than an observable outcome or document. Therefore, to describe strategic IS planning one must describe what people do rather than what they produce. To a very large extent, this means describing the communication between managers.

Finally, the focus of this article is on strategic IS planning. It is concerned, therefore, with the relationship between the overall organization's missions, objectives, and strategies, and those for MIS. In part, this focus allows Anthony [1] in addressing the objectives for IS and the resources to be committed toward achieving those objectives. Because IS strategy emanates directly from corporate strategy, however, there is the additional focus on the “linkage” between the two that is absent in organization-wide definitions such as Anthony's. And as King [6] notes, “it is this linkage which is the province of MIS Strategic Planning...”

Stated slightly differently, strategic IS planning, and therefore this study, is concerned primarily with the relationship between IS and the rest of the firm, and the communication between the senior IS manager and the top management team. By this definition, effective strategic IS planning is a process of building some consensus regarding the role of IS vis-a-vis the rest of the firm and the resources that will be committed to achieving that role. Because of the dynamic character of both business conditions and technology, the process of developing such a shared vision is ongoing and more or less continuous.

In this context, the senior IS managers in this study were seeking a strategic planning process which would allow them to develop and communicate to top management:

1. a general hardware and software architecture,

2. a technology assessment program to identify new opportunities made feasible by new technology,

3. a prioritization and evaluation methodology to allocate scarce development resources to the highest payoff areas, and

4. an agreed upon relationship between business strategy and IS strategy to ensure that systems requirements that are most critical to the success of the firm are met.

The difficulty, of course, is that the literature provides the IS manager with almost no guidance on “how” to actually perform the planning tasks. Most authors present block diagrams and discussion of “what” should be done; for example, “determine business objectives,” “assess current applications and new technologies,” etc. While important, these mandates leave open such process questions as who must be involved, what should each participant contribute, which forum is most appropriate, what time horizon is best, how much time and effort should be committed to planning, and how formally should the planning be specified? In this article the initial fragments of a framework to address these questions will be proposed, based upon the apparent impact of certain company characteristics that seemed important in the study.

## The Study

A number of researchers [3, 8, 10, 12] and most practitioners understand that the selection of a particular planning approach, for IS or otherwise, requires the careful consideration of a number of factors specific to the individual firm. At one extreme, several authors report a planning scheme developed and tested for only a single firm [4, 7, 9, 14, 15]. Usually, the planning process is reported because it worked adequately at the firm in question, and by example may work for other firms as well.

The difficulty for the planner, of course, is selecting one method over another. There is little evidence provided as to those factors which influenced the success or failure of a given approach, and indeed, it is even difficult to operationalize most of these planning approaches outside of their original context. In fact, contrary to the “IS implementation” literature, where failures and horror stories provide the grist for numerous articles, there are few reports of planning disasters that can be cited. While there are many normative models which reportedly work for one or two firms, there is little in the way of generalizable guidance. The research reported here thus represents a preliminary attempt to identify a few descriptive measures of strategic IS planning activity that are operational for a wide range of firms, and factors specific to individual firms which influence their selection of one approach over other alternatives.

It should be clear, therefore, that the study was an initial exploration rather than an experimental test of a plausible paradigm. The final result of this effort was the description of characteristics important to strategic IS planning in the few firms studied, not the empirical criticism of one of the many prescriptive models found useful for just a few firms. To this end, a comparative case study approach was undertaken whereby the senior IS executive and the top management team (four to six individuals) were interviewed indepth regarding:

1. their business and IS planning practices,

2. their management "style" and that of other managers in the firm,

3. the nature of their business, including their perception of which factors were most critical to overall success,

4. the extent to which IS was addressing the critical needs of the firm, and

5. the extent to which strategic IS planning was successful in addressing the four objectives of planning noted earlier.

Because planning is such a complex phenomenon, however, it is often difficult to reliably interpret interview data in such a way that generalizable patterns emerge. By emphasizing different areas, interviewees can easily make identical activities seem quite dissimilar. Furthermore, the interviewer can use the volume and complexity of the interview data to engage in “exampling” [21] by selecting only those comments that support preconceived notions.

To overcome these difficulties a simple questionnaire was developed with a few items for each of the five factors listed above. The questionnaire was then administered to the interviewees prior to their first meeting with the researcher. Together, the interviews captured the richness of the planning phenomenon, while the questionnaires helped to ensure a reasonable interpretation. The analysis of the questionnaire data was carried out using several non-parametric techniques, including the Kruskal-Wallis Analysis of Variance, the Mann-Whitney test, and the Kendall Coefficient of Concordance [22]. The “analysis” of the interview data involved two steps: (1) the writing of a single “case” for each organization that was consistent with most of the comments made, and (2) the side-by-side presentation of all comments made by each interviewee for each topic.

## The research sites

Because a comparative case study relies on the fact that the outcomes in the different sites were the result of identified differences in those factors measured for its conclusions, it was important that the sites be as similar as possible. As a result, eight organizations were identified which:

\- were moderate size multi-national process manufacturers (\$75-300 million annual revenue),

\- had formal strategic business planning for at least five years that was perceived by top management as being important,

\- were in the first two years of a formal MIS planning process,

• had corporate headquarters in relatively small cities,

\- were dominated by a “founding family,” and

\- were willing to commit the many hours of top management time necessary for this research.

## The Results

In the analysis of the interview data regarding IS planning practices, three broad approaches seemed to emerge; Personal-Informal, Personal-Formal, and Written-Formal Planning. The Personal-Informal planning style, shown in Figure 1, was characterized by an IS manager whose primary interactions with senior management were verbal and informal. The IS manager used personal, and often social, contacts as an opportunity to discuss issues of importance. This style was described by one manager as “keeping an ear to the ground; being aware of the various events, personalities, and decisions that might have an information systems impact.” To the extent that significant formal documentation of the planning activity was required, usually by corporate planning, it was perceived as something of a “paper shuffling” burden.

In the organizations where the Personal-Informal style worked reasonably well, these informal relationships between senior managers and the IS manager seemed particularly effective in identifying new IS applications. The lack of formality allowed the IS manager to discuss the opportunities in non-threatening ways, and to “sell” new systems efforts. Importantly, the individualized nature of such planning allowed the IS manager the flexibility to choose a time horizon that was appropriate in the given circumstance. Instead of being tied to a formal “schedule” of events and issues, the personal discussions seemed able to look as far forward as necessary. In some sense, the IS manager was better able to define problems in his or her terms rather than in terms specified by a corporate planning department.

In some environments, however, the drawbacks of this informal and personal style were significant. Especially in situations where the systems activity was complex, it was difficult to use the Personal-Informal style to obtain a clear picture of IS requirements and direction. Different managers had different perceptions of what was critical, and the informal approach did not provide an overall framework for evaluating alternatives. As a result, top management often felt that the IS organization was not really addressing the critical needs of the firm.

The Personal-Formal IS planning approach, shown in Figure 1, is something of a mid-ground between highly structured planning and planning which relies on informal relationships. Like the Personal-Informal style, there was heavy reliance on the personal communication of the IS manager. Like formal planning, however, the forums for these discussions were quite well defined. Typical mechanisms included regular steering committee meetings, IS manager involvement in business planning sessions, periodic presentations of IS plans to senior management, and participation in divisional or corporate staff meetings of one sort or another.

It should be noted that only the schedule of meetings, agenda, and the like was formalized. The relationship between top management and the senior IS manager was still fairly informal, depending on verbal face-to-face communication. The plans, documents, and other outputs of the meetings were clearly secondary, and were largely “documentation.” Thus, the production of an IS plan appeared to be a highly perfunctory, “rubber stamp” exercise. As one IS manager put it, “I complete the forms required by Corporate Planning, the steering committee approves, and that’s that.” The real planning occurred much earlier, of course, in the numerous meetings, presentations, and discussions which eventually got reported as “the IS plan.”

Because the Personal-Formal style depends heavily on the ability of the IS manager to integrate numerous discussions and comments into a coherent plan, the link between business objectives and systems activities, including the identification of new opportunities, was sometimes haphazard. Additionally, because many of the forums are not controlled by the IS manager, the planning time horizon was sometimes inappropriate for IS considerations. Thus, with the more formal planning style, the IS manager gained the involvement of senior managers only with some loss of control. The meetings and presentations were usually viewed as general management forums, and the IS manager was expected to conform to their format and focus.

The Written-Formal planning approach (see Figure 1) relied heavily on the schedule, procedures, and documents of a formal, corporate-mandated process as the primary mechanism for managing interaction between the IS manager and senior management. The primary mode of communications was written, and whatever discussion occurred at scheduled meetings and presentations was largely a review of written documents. There was much less reliance on the informal give-and-take which characterized the other styles. In fact, the organizations using this planning approach found that virtually the only real interaction between IS managers and senior management occurred at the annual presentation of the written plan.

On the other hand, the formal planning system of these organizations often provided a “model” of the organization that included the linkages between IS and business objectives and between IS planning and other management systems. The written plan was particularly important in this regard, because it documented these interactions, and thus provided an integrating device for line/IS decision making.

Because the strategic IS planning process was viewed as a component, and often a clone, of corporate strategic planning, however, the IS manager lost control of the interaction. Unlike the personal styles, where the IS manager could more easily identify new opportunities and problems in a timeframe appropriate to IS needs, the Written-Formal planning style was much more closely attuned to the user/senior managers' views. Business objectives and constraints were usually clearly presented to IS management, but often over a time horizon that was too short for an effective IS response.

The existence of these three planning approaches was moderately supported by the analysis of questionnaire responses, as displayed in Figure 2. Of the six factors that seemed to differentiate the various approaches, only four were covered by the questionnaire (as shown in Figure 2). The other two factors were uncovered in the analysis of the interview data alone.

The results presented in Figure 2 seem to suggest the plausibility of the three planning styles, with organizations A, B, and D as Personal-Informal, C and E as Personal-Formal, and F and G as Written-Formal. Because Organization H was included in the study rather late, the questionnaire was not administered to that IS manager. Based on interview data alone, this organization was classified as Personal-Informal.

## The impact of company characteristics

While it is certainly useful to simply describe how strategic IS planning is actually accomplished in different organizations, of more interest is the relative effectiveness of the various approaches. As suggested earlier, however, this effectiveness is probably conditioned by the environment within which they are applied. In other words, the success of the Personal-Informal, Personal-Formal, or Written-Formal planning styles will depend upon certain characteristics of the organization.

To measure the degree of success of the strategic IS planning, the senior operating executives (typically, the president/GM, the vice presidents of marketing, finance, production, and one or two other managers suggested by the president/GM) were asked:

\- the degree to which IS seemed to be addressing what they perceived to be the critical needs of the business (effective),

\- the degree to which the IS organization seemed to be well managed internally (efficient), and

\- the extent to which strategic IS planning made clear the role of IS, the direction that IS was/should be taking, and the resources that were/would be required.

Based upon the analysis of the interview data, it appears that the most important factors influencing the success or failure of the approaches to planning are:

1. The perceived status of the IS manager, as described by the senior managers. High status managers were well respected by their superiors, and were often involved in the discussion of problems and opportunities outside of MIS per se. Low status managers were either viewed negatively or they were considered “part of the woodwork.”

2. The volatility of the business as described by senior managers in comparison to the other organizations studied.

3. The complexity of the IS environment, in the sense captured by Nolan's [20] stage model. For purposes of this study, stages I, II, and III were considered low complexity while stages IV, V, and VI were considered high complexity.

4. The senior managers' personal styles, as characterized by their own description and that of the IS managers, ranging from very informal to very formal.

5. The physical proximity of the IS manager to the senior managers. The same building, or complex, was considered close, within walking distance was considered moderate, and locations requiring a drive were considered distant.

## Conclusions

Especially for those organizations where information systems activity is important to corporate strategy, the “status” of the systems manager dramatically affects planning success. IS is much more likely to be perceived as supporting the critical needs of the business when there are few levels between the senior management and the IS manager.

In reviewing the relationship between success or failure of the IS enterprise and these company characteristics, it becomes clear that the IS manager's importance and impact is often not comparable to others at that level. While several of these organizations have in recent years elevated the position of the IS manager to a higher level of authority, there has quite often not been a concomitant increase in the person's real status. If the IS manager is going to be able to manage the dynamics of a changing business and a changing technology, then the IS manager must participate as a real general management peer.

To some extent the influence of an IS manager's status on the success of IS planning is independent of the style of that planning. The enhanced ability of a high status manager to develop an easy exchange of opinions between technicians and general managers greatly improves the planning process. On the other hand, the more informal the style, the more important this status becomes.

In an organization where many important decisions are made informally in a number of ad-hoc sessions, then it is probably wise to keep the IS staff close to senior managers. Regardless of the IS manager's status, it will be extremely difficult to be an active member of the team if the IS manager is many miles away. In general, as the two groups get closer, the systems activity is perceived to be more useful. When the relationship between senior managers and the IS manager relies on the formal planning process, however, then physical proximity is less important. The formal, written communications largely substitute for an informal give-and-take. Stated another way, this suggests that IS groups which must be located many miles from headquarters will require more formal IS planning procedures than those which are close.

In an environment where the basic management style is characterized by an informal give-and-take and where an informal personal relationship exists between the IS manager and senior management, then formal IS planning does not appear to be a critical determinant of perceived IS effectiveness. Of course, the success of an IS planning effort in such an environment is influenced by the geographic proximity and IS manager status noted above. As an organization becomes more formal in its business practices, the role of formal strategic IS planning disciplines becomes more significant as a countervailing force, even for systems environments which are not highly complex.

As organizations increase in both size and complexity, however, and as their sophistication with information systems technology grows, more formal planning processes help to ensure the kind of broad-based dialogue that is essential to the development of an integrated “vision for IS.” Clearly this phenomena is not unrelated to the dimension of management style, as greater size and complexity often leads to more formal practices. On the other hand, in environments where the IS portfolio is relatively small and simple, formal planning approaches become less critical irrespective of the other factors. Thus, when the systems environment is not terribly complex, strategic IS planning can safely take place in a less formal way. However, as the portfolio of systems work increases in size and integration across user areas, more discipline and formality in the planning process becomes necessary.

On the other hand, in business environments that shift rapidly, reliance on formally specified planning procedures can be too cumbersome. In such situations, the IS manager who has a good informal relationship with senior management can often move quickly. The risk, or course, is that such rapid response to changing requirements can leave a wake of half completed projects and shifts in technology that is very expensive. Though it is not suggested by the data in this study, one might hypothesize that the balance between formality and informality in a planning process could influence the balance between effectiveness and efficiency in the IS organization.

By combining the style of strategic IS planning (shown in Figures 1 and 2) with the relative success of these styles (shown in Figure 3) and the characteristics (shown in Figure 4), one can begin to see a preliminary strategic IS planning framework (Figure 5) emerge. While few organizations are likely to match the archetypal form most appropriate for each style, this framework does provide a set of objectives that managers can use to guide the improvement of IS planning. In some sense, the selection of a planning style is the factor most easily controlled by IS management and the selection of other organizational characteristics are factors most easily controlled by general management.

As can be seen in Figure 5, the success of the Personal-Informal style of strategic IS planning seems to depend in part on both an informal general management style and high IS manager status. Combined with close physical proximity, this ensures that the IS manager can create the sort of regular interchange with senior management necessary for planning to be perceived useful by both senior managers and the IS manager. Thus, the IS manager must be perceived as something of a “general manager” who can initiate a discussion as well as respond to senior management requests.

Additionally, the Personal-Informal style seems most appropriate when the business environment is rapidly shifting and dynamic. Because of the complicated and changing demands placed on the firm to adapt, the information systems group must be highly flexible. Changes in conditions that were unforeseen are likely to have far-reaching impacts on the systems environment, and these impacts must be incorporated into the planning process. By utilizing a number of informal, face-to-face communications channels, the Personal-Informal style allows the IS manager to remain continually appraised of ongoing problems and opportunities.

From the IS manager's perspective inside the department, however, these informal-personal relationships with user and senior managers do not structure the firm's systems requirements sufficiently. Given the long development lead times and resource commitments characteristic of IS technologies, the IS manager needs a coherent "vision" of the information systems structure; a structure around which resources can be deployed. The flexibility of the Personal-Informal style may provide necessary insight into changing business conditions, but it does little to describe the necessary systems response. In the normal situation where a complex business requires complex systems support, this need for structure thus seems to run headlong into the equally strong need for flexibility.

With the Personal-Formal and Written-Formal planning styles, success depends in part on a more formal general management style. If the strategic IS planning depends on personal, face-to-face interaction between IS and general managers (Personal-Formal), then the IS manager's status remains important. On the other hand, if the process relies more on the formal planning mechanism of plans, schedules, and agendas to govern interaction, the IS manager's status seems less critical. Of course, relying on the formal process makes it much more important to keep business and IS planning closely linked, as this linkage is the only vehicle for establishing an IS direction consonant with business objectives.

As with the Personal-Informal style, however, there are important and somewhat mutually exclusive tradeoffs that must be made with the more formal planning styles. The utilization of a number of forms, written documents, and a formal planning structure make it much easier to integrate across complex systems activities. And this is especially helpful when the systems complexity is compounded by some distance between IS and line managers. The patterns which are necessary to allocate resources, select development alternatives, and design the systems organization can be made relatively clear and precise.

The difficulty, of course, is that this well stacked apple-cart can be easily overturned by changes in business direction and systems requirements. As long as the business remains fairly stable, then a more formally constrained relationship between IS and senior management can be quite appropriate. When the business environment is volatile, however, the Personal-Formal and Written-Formal planning approaches simply do not provide the necessary flexibility to be effective.

In the best of all worlds, a firm facing complex and volatile business and systems conditions would respond by the appointment of an IS manager who is widely respected for general management abilities, a person who can be comfortable in the role of a peer with senior management. This IS organization would be located close to senior management, and the IS manager would be intimately involved in business planning activity. Additionally, however, the IS manager would implement a number of quite formal internal planning procedures to ensure that the informal discussions resulted in a coherent, focused “vision of the future.”

The implementation of any IS planning process must, therefore, consider the several possible avenues for developing the mutual understanding so essential in planning. It is not enough to describe the structure of the formal process. One must be concerned with the “quality” of the communication between line and systems managers. In other words, the strategic IS planning designer must look beyond the formal procedures and forms to the way they will be implemented and used. For corporate IS planning managers in divisionalized firms, it is equally important to recognize that divisions within the same firm can be quite different along many of the dimensions relevant to planning. The corporate systems planning process must provide guidance not only as to what should be produced, but how planning should occur.
