# The Effectiveness of External Information Channels in Facilitating Innovation Within Software Development Groups

By: Robert W. Zmud

## Abstract

This study of forty-nine software development groups investigated the effectiveness of ten information channels, linking the software groups to potential information sources about new developments in software methodologies, as a means of facilitating software group innovativeness. While the findings suggest that software group innovativeness can be improved by providing appropriate external information channels, this relationship is contingent on a software group's internal environment. The channels most commonly provided by those organizations participating in the study tended to be those least effective in promoting innovation.

Keywords: Software development, software management, information channels, innovation
ACM Categories: K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

It is generally understood that software expenses are representing an increasing portion of an organization's information-related expenses. It is not unreasonable, thus, to expect that efforts to increase software productivity should be a major concern of most organizations. While little real evidence exists that demonstrates the benefits of methods and tools oriented toward improving software productivity, a vast anecdotal literature vigorously supports their use.

One means of classifying these methods and tools is by the nature of the process by which software is to be developed. Here, a simple categorization might be an “end user” versus a “computer specialist” orientation. Much software today is being developed by or with end users through the use of very high level languages, such as database retrieval systems and decision support systems, and application generators. Other software, however, is being developed within centralized software development groups staffed by computer specialists using more traditional software development tools, i.e., high level language. Generally, these software development groups have responsibility for large transaction processing systems, for applications where control and security concerns are significant, for corporate-wide systems, and for software that must be integrated with systems software. The attention of this article is directed toward the behaviors of these latter software development groups.

Since the early 1970s, a substantial effort has been directed toward developing improved methodologies for developing large software systems and for managing this development process [27, 29]. Studies of the behaviors of software development groups, however, indicate that these methodologies are being only moderately used outside of those organizations marketing software products [10, 11, 21, 25]. A common explanation for the apparent underutilization of these methodologies centers on the reluctance of computer specialists to learn about and use the new methodologies [13]: software development is typically viewed by a computer specialist as a creative, personal endeavor and any attempt to modify or otherwise direct these activities is seen as a threat. An alternate explanation, however, might be that these software development groups are not being provided with the necessary means or motivation to encourage the use of new methodologies.

It seems clear that these new software development methodologies — to be referred to hereon as “modern software practices (MSP)” — typically require computer specialists to change their work behaviors. As such, the introduction of MSP into a software development group might very well be viewed as an organizational process innovation, i.e., the effort to introduce improved means of performing work into an organization. Organizational innovation is known to be influenced by a number of factors, including, but certainly not limited to, recognition of a need for the innovation, top management support for the innovation effort, organizational size, interorganizational relationships, an organization’s receptivity toward change, and individual characteristics of an organization’s members [12, 17, 26, 28, 31, 32]. Additionally, information channels linking organizational members with external information about an innovation have been shown to be an important element in the successful introduction of the innovation [1, 3, 8]. This latter factor is the specific focus of this article, which describes a study examining the effectiveness of a variety of information channels linking software development groups to external information regarding MSP.

## Development of Hypotheses

Organizational innovation is an information intensive endeavor [1, 8, 9, 12, 19, 26]. This point is particularly well stated by Rogers [18]:

An individual or an organization, when considering adopting an innovation to solve some perceived problem, is motivated to acquire information about what the innovation is, how it functions, how much it costs and what its benefits are, how to obtain it, and how it matches with the problem.

A key concern, then, for any organization seeking to promote a particular innovation is the establishment of appropriate information channels assuring that information about the innovation will reach the organization's members.

Three primary strategies are available through which an organization can keep its members abreast of new technological developments [2]: hiring new employees; providing a means to access documents describing new developments; and, encouraging personal contacts, both formal and informal, with individuals outside the organization who are knowledgeable about new developments. Prior research has shown that not all of an organization's members need to be physically linked to external information sources [1, 8, 23]: certain organizational members often emerge to perform an information capture/dissemination role that keeps others informed about new developments. These individuals, however, are not equally effective in all organizational contexts. A finding particularly relevant to this article is that this "gatekeeper" role was not an important factor in the performance of a "technical service" group in an R&D laboratory [2]. As conceptualized by the author, technical service groups serve an organizational role of introducing established technologies. Allowing that the actual mechanisms by which members of these groups are kept informed of new technical developments remains to be explored, it is proposed that an organization's formal hierarchy plays a key role in providing information leading to work group innovations. The similarity between the organization role of a software development group and that of this technical service group is striking [2]:

Cost/performance improvements to existing products, processes or systems. Recombination, modification, and testing of systems using existing knowledge. Opening new markets for existing products.

While it is not at all obvious which type of information channels would be preferred for communicating information regarding MSP to the members of a software development group, the importance of establishing an effective set of information channels seems clear. Numerous authors have suggested various mechanisms for improving the flow of external information $[4, 8, 24]$ ; however, the effectiveness of these prescriptions has not been empirically assessed.

As no basis for discriminating among information channels is available, all are initially conceived as contributing positively to facilitating MSP use. This leads to Hypothesis 1:

H1 — The availability of information channels that possess the potential to provide a software development group with external information regarding MSP should result in the increased use of the MSP.

Prior research, however, does suggest that the effectiveness of particular information channels is contingent on characteristics of the organization making use of the channels [7]. In the referenced study, organizational size and the recency of an organization's technological commitment were both observed to be mediating variables. It is consequently proposed that an organization's internal environment may very well effect the organization's capability to make effective use of a particular information channel. For example, one might expect the following relationships to exist:

1. because of greater resource availability, a larger organization might be expected to provide better information channels than could a smaller organization,

2. because of a greater concern with being technologically current, an organization populated with highly professional members might be expected to make more use of provided channels than an organization populated with less professional members,

3. because of an increase in software development difficulties, an organization developing more complex structure might be more motivated to learn about MSP than one developing less complex software, and

4. because of a general organization-wide receptivity to new technology, a software group in a technologically-progressive organization might be expected to be more innovative than one in a less technologically-progressive organization.

This leads to Hypothesis 2:

H2 — A software group's internal environment (as represented by size, professionalism, task complexity, and context) will mediate the relationship between the availability of a particular information channel and the group's innovativeness.

## Methodology

Forty-nine software development managers, each of whose sole responsibility involved managing an internal software group, responded to a questionnaire. These individuals represented about half of the organizations initially contacted. The groups ranged in size from less than ten computer specialists to over 100. These software groups represented two organizational contexts: non-defense federal agencies (n = 31) and aerospace-related (n = 18). These two contexts were selected such that two quite distinct organizational cultures would be represented with the aerospace organizations believed more receptive to the introduction of new technologies.

Data was collected regarding the use of six MSP: top down development, structured design, structured review, chief programmer team, configuration management, and unit development folder. These MSP were selected as they are among those most familiar to computer specialists and were observed to be among those most used by software groups $[21]$ . Furthermore, prior research on organizational innovation has found that different forces are often observed depending on whether the innovation involved is targeted at an organization's administrative or technical cores $[6, 15, 31, 32]$ . Thus, it was believed desirable to select both MSP directed toward the administrative core (software managers) and MSP directed toward the technical core (analysts and programmers). The first three MSP listed above, as they facilitate the software development process, are categorized as technical MSP. The latter three MSP, as they facilitate the management of the software development process, are classified as administrative MSP. Descriptions of these MSP, as well as others, can be found in $[29]$ .

Each software group's MSP use was ascertained by asking respondents to describe the extent each was applied within their software groups. Six responses were provided: not used at all, a few people/projects are experimenting with it; a few people/projects regularly use it; a number of people/projects regularly use it; most people/projects regularly use it; and, usage has become standard. Three measures were thus derived: the first representing the sum over all six practices (overall use), the second the sum over the three technical practices (technical use), and the third the sum over the three administrative practices (administrative use).

While the availability of an information channel may directly effect MSP use, its influence on MSP use might as well be felt indirectly by simply bringing the practice to the organization's attention, by improving the software manager's attitude toward the practice, or by improving the organization's receptivity toward change. The large amount of literature on MIS and OR/MS implementation consistently demonstrates the strong influence on implementation success of these, and related, factors [30, 31].

MSP recognition was measured by having the software managers simply indicate whether or not they were personally familiar with each of the six practices. These responses were then summed to obtain a measure of overall MSP recognition. Consistent with the procedure regarding MSP usage, subscales were also calculated for technical MSP recognition and for administrative MSP recognition.

Management attitude was measured by having these software managers characterize, along a Likert-like scale, each of the practices according to the following six dimensions: its impact on work performance, its impact on work behaviors, its "state-of-the-art," how successful software groups in general had been in using the practice, how easy it was to experiment with the practice, and how easy it was to observe the benefits of the practice. These characteristics were selected from those typically used to describe innovations [19, 22]. A factor analysis of these items resulted in all loading on a single factor (with all factor loadings between 0.71 and 0.89). The internal consistency of this attitude measure over all six practices was 0.97. Again, three attitude measures were obtained — one for all six practices, one for the three technical practices, and one for the three administrative practices.

Each software development group's general receptivity toward change was measured by having the software managers characterize their own software group. The instrument used was a modification of one developed to assess organizational support for innovation [20]. The four items used ( $\alpha = .80$ ) addressed the degree to which an organization was flexible and continually adapted to change, examined its most basic assumptions, searched for fresh ways of looking at problems, and dealt with new problems and tasks constructively.

In each case, the responding software managers were simply asked whether an information channel was made available to their software group.

While it might have been preferable to ascertain the extent to which each of the channels was actually being used, the potential difficulties in obtaining valid measures from the software managers regarding channel usage by the computer specialists in their software group inhibited the use of such a measure. While validity concerns could as well be raised regarding certain of the study's other variables (particularly technical MSP use), it was expected that a software manager would be cognizant of the methodologies being employed by the staff.

The mediating role of an organization's internal environment was examined through the use of two variables generally observed to positively influence organizational innovation, organizational size [14, 16] and professionalism [5, 17], through the use of a project complexity variable (software groups faced with more complex projects would be expected to exhibit a greater need for MSP), and through the aerospace and federal agency contextual distinction (with the aerospace firms expected, in general, to be more innovative).

Organizational size was measured as the natural logarithm of the number of computer specialists in a software group. Professionalism was measured through four items indicating the number of professionals possessing bachelor degrees, possessing master degrees, belonging to either ACM or IEEE, and actively participated in ACM or IEEE activities ( $\alpha = 0.72$ ). Project complexity was measured by three items ( $\alpha = .80$ ) giving the percentage of projects requiring more than six months for development, more than 25 months of effort, and more than 20,000 in cost. Organizational context was denoted through a dummy variable. Because of the likelihood that variables such as size, professionalism and organizational context might confound other relationships, the correlational analyses, all of which employed two-tailed tests of significance, controlled for their influences.

The relationships between the existence of each information channel and the innovativeness of the software groups are given in Table 3. Here, as with all subsequent tables, only correlation coefficients significant at a 0.10 level or better are shown in order to increase table readability. Two observations stand out. First, the correlations are quite low. Second, a number of the correlations are negatively, rather than positively, related to a software group's innovativeness. Little support for Hypothesis 1 seems evident from these results. While any effort to infer generalizations from such data is unwarranted, it is useful to attempt to assess what is suggested by Table 3. It appears that:

— dues payments, subscriptions, a library, a venture group, internal training as a primary work role and consultant training are positively associated with a software group's innovativeness,

— professional meetings, tuition payments, and internal training as a secondary work role are negatively associated with a software group's innovativeness, and

— no association is present for external workshops.

Generally, the effects of the “positive” channels are with management attitude and a software group’s receptivity toward change. The effects of the “negative” channels, on the other hand, are with MSP use and recognition. Additionally, no distinction seems to exist regarding administrative or technical MSP.

One explanation for the nonsupportive nature of these findings might be that specified as Hypothesis 2: that the effectiveness of an information channel is dependent to some extent on software group's internal environment. Table 4 provides the results regarding the use of organizational size as a mediating variable. The two sets of correlations are quite different, indicating that a mediating effect does exist. When only large organizations are considered, only two of the information channels were significantly associated with software group innovativeness — subscriptions being positively associated with MSP use and MSP recognition, and professional meetings being negatively associated with management attitudes. The finding with the smaller software groups was similar in that only a few information channels possessed significant associations with software group innovativeness. These variables, however, were different than those that emerged with the larger software groups — dues, a library, and consultant training were all positively associated with management attitude; and, internal training, as a primary work role, was positively associated with both MSP usage and management attitude.

Table 5 provides the results regarding the use of professionalism as a mediating variable. Quite different sets of associations were observed for the high professional and low professional software groups. The primary associations seen for the high professional groups are subscriptions, a library, a venture group and consultant training. These were all positively associated with MSP use as well as, to a lesser extent, with management attitude and with receptivity toward change; and, professional meetings and internal training as a secondary work role were negatively associated with MSP use. Interestingly, an administrative/technical innovation difference did arise for these more professional software groups with greater association with the technical MSP. Very little association was observed between the existence of the information channels and software group innovativeness for the low professional groups with only a few of the channels (primarily training related) being associated with receptivity to change and management attitude.

Table 6 provides the results regarding the use of project complexity as a mediating variable. Clear differences were again observed by splitting the software development groups via a mediating variable. The primary associations seen with those software groups facing more complex projects included positive associations involving subscriptions, a venture group and internal training as a primary work role with MSP use, management attitude and receptivity toward change; and, negative associations involving professional meetings and internal training as a secondary work role with MSP use. Far fewer significant associations were seen for those software groups facing less complex projects. Here, the primary significant associations were seen with technical MSP recognition (subscriptions positive but professional meetings negative) and receptivity toward change (library and consultant training positive but tuition payments negative).

Finally, Table 7 provides the results regarding the use of organizational context as a mediating variable. The software groups in aerospace organizations were generally larger, more professional, and engaged in more complex projects than those in the federal agencies. Differences, though not as severe, can again be seen for the two sets of software development groups. The primary findings observed with the aerospace firms included positive associations between subscriptions and technical MSP use as well as management attitude, a positive association between a library and receptivity toward change, negative associations between external workshops, professional meetings, and internal training as a secondary work role and technical MSP use, and a negative association between internal training as a secondary work role and management attitude. The primary findings observed with the federal agencies included positive associations between dues and administrative MSP use, between subscriptions and administrative MSP recognition, and between consultant training and management attitude. Negative associations occurred between professional meetings and technical MSP recognition, between tuition and both administrative MSP use and receptivity toward change, and between training as a secondary work role and administrative MSP recognition. A technical/administrative distinction did arise here with technical MSP dominating with the aerospace firms and management MSP dominating with the federal agencies.

A number of points arise upon examining the results of these analyses involving mediating variables. First, considerable support for Hypothesis 2 does seem to exist as quite different sets of associations were obtained through the use of the mediating variables. Second, the relatively weak associations observed in Table 3 tended to reappear but with much more strength after allowing for the effects of a mediating variable. Subscriptions, a library, a venture group, internal training as a primary work role, and consultant training were generally supportive of software group innovativeness. Professional meetings and internal training as a secondary work role were generally not supportive of software group innovativeness. Inconclusive results were observed for dues, external workshops, and tuition payments. Third, the more conducive the organizational environment for innovation (i.e., higher professionalism and complex projects), the stronger are the associations between the external information channels and software group innovation. This was particularly true regarding MSP use and MSP recognition, neither of which possessed much significance in Table 3. Fourth, for those software groups whose environments were not conducive for innovation, significant associations were mainly observed for management attitude and for receptivity toward change. Finally, few differences were observed regarding the administrative/technical distinction.

## Discussion

Since the effectiveness of external information channels in facilitating innovation within software development groups is apparently contingent on a software group's internal environment, it becomes very difficult to put forth any definitive statements regarding the channel effectiveness. Even though the findings tended to vary, a considerable consistency was observed regarding many of the information channels. This consistency prompts the following comments.

The more effective channels tended to fall into one of two categories — those requiring an active direction and resource commitment by an organization's management (venture group, internal training as a primary work role, and consultant training), and those that are best viewed as reference sources to organizational members (subscriptions and a professional library). Channels directed at increasing the external contacts of individuals (external workshops, professional meetings, and tuition payments) or that disrupt normal work roles (internal training as a secondary work role) were ineffective, at best. These findings support the suggestion by Allen, Tushman, and Lee [2] that innovation within technical support groups occurs through an organization's formal hierarchy, rather than through horizontal information gathering.

Technical support groups, such as those involved in software development, have as a focal concern the enhancement of organizational, operational, and managerial efforts through exploiting existing technologies. The aim is to accomplish assignments in a quick, cost-effective and error-free manner rather than in a necessarily innovative manner. If anything, innovation might tend to be suppressed, to lower the risk of disrupting operations, rather than encouraged. In such an environment, members normally do not have the time or motivation to investigate ways of improving their methods of accomplishing work. In order for innovation to occur in such an environment, the initiative might best come via the formal hierarchy — resources are made available, members are given reduced work loads or are put on special assignment, standards and control systems are modified, etc.

If software development group innovativeness is promoted via an organization's formal hierarchy, then it is not surprising that no major distinctions were observed regarding the distinction between administrative and technical process innovations. That “professionalism” tended to produce a slight administrative/technical distinction is certainly not inconsistent with this argument. Essentially, both administrative and technical innovation is orchestrated via the same organizational mechanisms. Given the results described earlier, one might surmise that this mechanism involves a direct organizational mandate to resolve a particular problem followed by the active use of organizationally provided reference or training sources. This must be treated as pure conjecture, however, as no information regarding software group innovation “strategy” was collected in the study.

Another interesting observation was that there appear to be no “quick fixes” for software groups whose current internal environments are currently not conducive for innovation. With such software groups, the significant associations tended to involve management attitude and receptivity toward change rather than MSP use or MSP recognition. The more effective channels for these purposes were subscriptions, a library, and consultant training. Clearly, a supportive infrastructure must exist before innovation can occur. Initial efforts to increase innovation within “impoverished” software groups, thus, might best be directed at the more longer term objective of improving attitudes and receptivity toward change rather than attempt to immediately introduce new work methodologies.

Finally, the research design employed inhibits any effort to uncover the causal nature of the relationships between these external information channels and software group innovativeness. The negative correlations observed with professional meetings, for example, might be explained in ways other than that hinted at in this article (that attendance at professional meetings might actually inhibit MSP use). Software groups already using MSP might not feel a need to send members to these meetings, or computer specialists from a software group already using MSP might become disenchanted with a meeting and convince their colleagues not to attend future meetings. Any interpretations of these findings should consequently be viewed with caution.

## Conclusion

The results of this study are generally supportive of the notion that the innovativeness of a software development group can be facilitated if appropriate information channels are provided to link the software group with relevant information sources. The relationship between external information channels and software group innovativeness, however, appears quite contingent on a software group's internal environment. If a software group's current internal environment is not conducive to innovation, then any linkage between information channels and innovativeness will lose much of its strength. Also, it must be reiterated that this study has concerned itself with traditional software development activities, i.e., where a staff of computer specialists are assigned generally large and complex software projects, rather than software development activities associated with “end user” computing. Any discussion or interpretation should not be generalized beyond this traditional software development context.

While the data gathered within these 49 software groups indicate that many of these information channels are currently provided, three of the four channels made available by most of the software groups were not positively related to software group innovativeness. It consequently might be worthwhile for those managers wishing to improve the innovativeness of their software groups to seriously assess the information channels currently being provided to their staffs. As the three ineffective channels (external workshops, professional meetings, and tuition payments) consume considerable organizational resources, the gains from such an examination might have a direct impact on a software group's budget as well as its productivity. It should also be recognized, however, that these channels might be provided to staff members for reasons other than to facilitate software group innovativeness.
