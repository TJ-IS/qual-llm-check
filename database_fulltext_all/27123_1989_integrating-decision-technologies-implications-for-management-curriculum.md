# Integrating Decision Technologies: Implications for Management Curriculum

By: Carl R. Adams
Department of Information and Decision Sciences
Carlson School of Management
University of Minnesota
Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455

Jae Hyon Song
College of Business
St. Cloud State University
St. Cloud, Minnesota 56301

## Abstract

The essence of management is decision making. Decision making requires the availability and proper use of data. Three evolving technologies relate to the support of decision making: information processing; decision science methods; and organization of decision makers and decision processes. More and more, because of technological developments and increased understanding of complex decision situations, these technologies must be seen as an integrated whole in order to support efficient and effective decision making. Within organizations, different groups are often charged with each of the three technologies that should support decision making. As a result, we often experience problems where the data is unavailable, is not compatible with the analyses desired, or is not relevant to the decision-making processes that managers wish to use. A substantive integration of these support groups and their expertise would help.

Acting as a change agent, business and management schools can develop a conceptualization of the three technology areas that integrates them with respect to terminology. They can also develop a unified set of general constructs that carry throughout each of the areas. Further, a new series of core courses can be developed that present the integrated technology subjects in a logical sequence.

Management schools have been concerned with teaching concepts and skills related to data acquisition, manipulation and presentation to their students for some time. However, in organizing the delivery of data-handling concepts and skills, these schools have relied on courses from various discipline bases such as decision sciences (DSci), management information systems (MIS), managerial decision making (MDM). These courses in management school curricula are generally redundant, use similar terminology differently, and are not mutually supportive.

This article focuses on the design and delivery of an integrated sequence of core courses that addresses the three technology areas. Drawing on experience at our own schools, we address general course design principles, resolution of issues on content requirements and delivery, implementation issues, and current and future problems related to the effective integration of topics that have traditionally been addressed in DSci, MIS, and MDM courses.

Keywords: Managerial decision making, managerial use of decision technologies, business core courses, MIS education, decision sciences education

## Introduction

Our interest is in the three areas of decision technologies, namely, information processing, decision science methods, and organizational decision making and decision processes. In understanding the need to integrate these decision technologies into the current managerial environment, it is useful to consider an overall decision process as shown in Figure 1.

We recognize that there are other possible descriptions of decision process activities, such as problem finding and intelligence, but the four identified in Figure 1 are sufficient to make our point. It is also important to recognize that these activities occur in both individual decision-making contexts and in group decision processes. The problems in executing the decision activities that occur when managers do not have an integrated view of the decision technologies in an individual process include: (1) inability to acquire and use all relevant data because of communication difficulties with sources of data and because of lack of understanding regarding the form of data that is relevant to analysis techniques, and (2) difficult communication regarding the decision and consequently, a lack of understanding and commitment to the decision on the part of relevant members of the organization. In addition to these problems, the group process could have a lack of shared understanding of the informational inputs to a decision and consequent difficulties in making a decision.

Despite these difficulties, it is not surprising that managers today lack the “right stuff,” i.e., an integrated view, because managerial training is based on a fragmented presentation of these decision technologies. Development of new curricula based on a new conceptual structure that clarifies the relationship among the three decision technologies would go a long way toward reducing the problems noted above. Our view of the movement needed from the current relationship of the three decision technologies to a new integrated concept is indicated in Figure 2.

The guiding principles in moving from the fragmented current position to the more integrated conceptualization include: (1) a unified set of basic terminology and concepts in areas such as definition of data and its characteristics, data sources, and measurement, and (2) common general concepts that are used across the technology areas including: (a) a view of descriptive representation that encompasses charts, graphs, and descriptive statistics; (b) an understanding of formalisms and modeling that views decision analysis, estimation, hypothesis testing, regression, linear programming, and simulation as alternative models and formalisms; and (c) a systems view that allows the discussion of the organizational and technological context in which decision making takes place.

Given the integrated view of the right side of Figure 2, our view of the core course structure needed is indicated in Figure 3.

Utilizing components of each technology, Course 1 would address the basic terminology and concepts. This would be motivated by the intuitive and exploratory use of information in decision making. Course 2 would stress the need for formalism and concepts of modeling supporting decision making. Various decision sciences methods would be addressed in terms of their specific formalisms and models. Course 3 would address the technological and organizational context in which decision making takes place.

## Background

For some time, business schools have been teaching data-handling concepts and skills that are necessary for management information support. The American Assembly of Collegiate School of Business (AACSB) Accreditation Standards and Guidelines for Business Administration Programs recognize the need for

... a basic understanding of the concepts and applications of accounting, or quantitative methods, and management information systems including computer applications (AACSB, 1987, p. 29).

For the general core curriculum, a reflection of the profession's concern for improving the relevant course content and delivery is its appearance as a major topic at several conferences: the Decision Sciences Institute National Conference, Boston, 1987; the Decision Sciences Institute Regional Conference, San Antonio, 1987;

the TIMS/ORSA Conference, St. Louis, 1987; and a meeting of leading statistics teachers, Chicago, 1986 (ASA, 1986).

In the past several years there has been rapid development and wide use of information technology because of the introduction of microcomputers and new concepts in the use of computer software and databases. Thus far, business schools have responded to the changing computing environment by modifying courses from several different disciplines. Statistics, mathematics, and economics are included in the courses generally referred to as decision sciences (DSci). Computing and the development of organizational information resources have been included in management information systems (MIS) courses. Topics from cognitive science, psychology and organizational behavior can be covered in either DSci or MIS or in a separate managerial decision making (MDM) course. A special issue that is discussed in more detail later is the inclusion of both information processing technology and functional area management topics within the typical MIS course.

A number of business schools have introduced a series of courses for computing and information systems majors. Both the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) (Nunamaker, et al., 1982) and the Data Processing Management Association (DPMA) (DPMA, 1986) have proposed curricula for these majors. As part of their recommendations, both the ACM and the DPMA recommend a course in computing to cover such topics as programming and computer concepts, and a course in quantitative methods to cover such topics as optimization and queuing and applications of these methods to scheduling and inventory problems. These courses would be available as prerequisites for computer and information systems majors and as introductory material for students in general. Beyond these limited courses, however, neither organization devotes much attention to recommending core material that addresses the needs of the general business student.

Regular students in management meet the AACSB common body of knowledge requirements in quantitative methods and management information systems by taking one or more introductory courses in decision sciences, management information systems and managerial decision making. Because these courses are related but have been developed by individuals from different discipline bases, they lack an overall clarifying structure or concept. As a result, course delivery has been less than effective and satisfactory. Examples of specific deficiencies are shown in Table 1.

Unfortunately, the major relevant journals (e.g., Decision Sciences, Management Sciences, and MIS Quarterly) have limited their attention primarily to the delivery of specific topics in these disciplines rather than the development of an overall framework that integrates topics across these disciplines.

As mentioned previously, the content of the typical MIS course is an important issue. Our discussion in this article focuses mainly on the information processing technology topics portion of the MIS course, noting that there is substantial rationale to integrate this material alongside the topics in the other decision technologies. Although not discussed in detail in this article, it should be mentioned that the other major component of the typical MIS course addresses the functional management issue, i.e., the management of the information resource of the organization. Major topics in this area include strategic positioning, privacy, systems development, data and systems security, and technical personnel management. While there are differences of opinion on the subject, for example, Dearden (1987) and McLean (1985), our view is that the topics related to functional management of the information resource are rapidly evolving into a cohesive body of material along the lines of marketing, finance and operations management. This material at some point will be seen as justifying a separate course in the management curriculum.

As a result of the argument for integration of the three decision technology areas, we have been involved in redesigning the three undergraduate core courses in the decision technologies area at the University of Minnesota's Carlson School of Management. These courses account for about 4,100 course enrollments per year. The next section describes the content of the integrated course sequence developed at Minnesota and some important issues that have been addressed in the sequence development and delivery. The subsequent section discusses course objectives and principles used in organizing and delivering the course contents, followed by a section that addresses instructional objectives and main topics in each of the three courses of the sequence. The final two sections outline problems encountered in the implementation of the courses, additional problems expected in the further development of this course sequence, and benefits that have been achieved.

## Design Principles for an Integrated Sequence

The orientation of decision science (DSci), management information systems (MIS) and managerial decision making (MDM) as course work areas are related but quite different. Technologies for presenting ideas/facts, processing data, and managing data and information systems are the focuses in MIS. Collecting data, building models, and manipulating data for decision making and problem solving are the prime considerations in DSci, while explaining and describing problem solving and decision-making behavior are the focuses of MDM.

Integrating these areas involves major decisions in selecting teaching materials from among DSci, MIS, and MDM, and in designing the delivery. In order to make these decisions, the overall purposes of the sequence must be established. These purposes can be expressed in terms of the expectation that students should achieve the minimum competency to carry out the following roles (Minnesota Computing, 1988):

\- Locator of relevant data through knowledge of data sources and acquisition technologies.

\- User of data by evaluating and manipulating data through selected models.

\- User-developer of small support systems to meet personal needs.

\- Participant in large application development by specifying and evaluating information requirements.

Considering the competency needed for the different roles, we have made some important assumptions regarding the selection of material to be included in the sequence of the course. These are:

\- Both for communication and personal use reasons, students should be familiar with commonly used formal methods of modeling problems such as decision analysis, statistical inference methods, optimization techniques, and simulation. This familiarity should support the needs of the general manager rather than the needs of a specialist.

\- The scope and depth of materials related to information processing technology should also meet the needs of general users rather than those of specialists.

\- Computer use skills include word processing, spreadsheet, database management, statistics software, graphics package, and modeling software. The emphasis should be on the use of fourth generation languages.

\- It is essential to use microcomputer-based systems.

In addition we have made some significant decisions regarding the delivery of material, i.e., its order and sequence. These decisions include:

1. Using a bottom-up approach to organizing the materials, which proceeds from fairly elementary, intuitive concepts and gradually builds up formalism and complexity over time. Beginning with an elementary understanding of data and organizing/presenting data, the sequence proceeds to formal methods of using data and uses of data in organizational contexts.

2. Starting with individual human information processing systems and viewing information technology as a way to overcome some of the limits in human information processing rather than proceeding from an organizational perspective.

3. Teaching computer access and use skills in problem environments in which students use software to do realistic assignments rather than toy problems.

4. Introducing probability at a conceptual level and without detailed mechanics.

5. Introducing and discussing regression and analysis of variance as ways to explain variation in data. This provides a smooth transition from descriptive statistics to probability and formal statistical tools such as regression and analysis of variance.

6. Viewing various statistical methods as examples of formalisms.

7. Devoting three quarters of the class hours to lectures and the remaining one quarter to microcomputer-based lab sessions. Development of computer skills is spread out over the three courses.

## The Sequence

## Course 1

The first course is called “Fundamentals of Information Development and Use.” It is about understanding and developing data in terms of (1) the needs for data, (2) the sources and characteristics of data, (3) organization of data and various manipulations used to present and summarize data, and (4) variation in data. Probability concepts and operations are introduced as a way to model variation in data. An intuitive form of decision analysis is introduced as a way of showing the possible uses of probability. Upon completion of the course work, students should be able to understand:

\- In broad terms, the use of information in management.

\- Human information processing systems and their limitations.

\- Specific needs for data.

\- Possible sources of data, their limits and strengths.

\- Measurement scales and their operational limits.

\- How to organize and summarize data in terms of location and variation.

\- How to organize multivariate data and understand the elementary concept of association.

\- Regression and analysis of variance as ways to analyze variation in data.

\- Elementary probability operations and the use of probability in a simple decision analysis structure.

\- Common software packages for word processing, spreadsheets, statistics, and graphics.

## Course 2

The second course is called "Formal Decision Making and Data Analysis Methods." It involves formal methods of using data for problem solving and decision making based on the foundation laid in the first course. It builds on concepts, terminology, and intuitively based methods addressed in the first course. This course goes beyond the initial development of data to address formal ways of manipulating and presenting data. In addition, the second course provides a survey of major modeling methods to give a broad perspective to users without overemphasizing specific techniques. Modeling methods include decision analysis, estimation, hypothesis testing, regression, linear programming, and simulation. Upon completion of the second course, students are expected to develop computer skill with use of statistical and modeling software and to understand:

\- Organizational/individual decision making and uses of formal methods to support decision making.

\- Basic concepts and methods of decision analysis.

\- Concepts of statistical inference and its relation to decision making.

\- Specific models and methods of estimation.

\- Regression as a standard model explaining variation in data.

\- Search and optimization techniques as part of decision making.

\- Simulation as a general method of modeling and problem solving.

## Course 3

The third course is called “Information Systems and Use of Information Technology.” It is about understanding uses of data in organizational and technological systems. It focuses on information technology including computers and communication, user roles in systems development, and integrating various sources of data for decision making. Completion of this course provides the ability to understand:

\- The influences of organizational and technical systems on the development and use of information.

\- Technological limits and capabilities that are important factors in providing information support to managers.

\- Participation as an effective user in a large-scale system development effort.

\- Simple models, various databases, and common application software in meeting managerial information needs.

\- The integration of various decision technologies to provide effective development or use of decision support systems.

As with the first two courses, the specific topics in Course 3 are further specified in Appendix A. Appendix B identifies the relationship of topics in traditional DSci (statistics and quantitative methods) and MIS courses to the topics covered in each of the three new courses. While the topic listings alone do not give the full flavor of the integration strived for in the new sequence, Appendices A and B do give substantial indication of the relationships and differences between the new sequence and traditional course work.

## Implementation Problems

Developing and introducing courses of this nature represents a major commitment of resources and requires leadership to overcome implementation barriers. Necessary resources include faculty who develop and teach the courses, and software and hardware for students and instructional staff. Strong support from department leaders was both necessary and sufficient at Minnesota to keep the development proceeding.

Faculty are by their nature specialists and are therefore generally unwilling to recast and reorganize the teaching of fundamental skills and concepts that are related to information support. This is a major barrier to the development of the integrated course sequence described previously. The differing and often narrow frameworks that various individuals bring to the development process are very difficult to integrate.

Hardware and software are expensive to support and difficult to manage. Lab assistants must be provided in student lab sessions and must also be available when students are working on their own.

Textbooks are arranged in a traditional fashion. Thus, the non-existence of appropriate textbooks, the diversity of reading materials, and a lack of good beginning-level, managementoriented cases are all serious implementation barriers.

At Minnesota, development has been carried out by a committee of faculty from both the decision sciences and management information systems areas. Fortunately, these two areas are located within the same department. Thus, there is some reservoir of peer camaraderie to draw on in the development process. As noted, strong leadership has been required to sustain the commitment to the long evolutionary period of development. This process has been ongoing since Fall, 1986. Significant financial support was given to those faculty who participated in the early stages of the development process. The location of relevant areas in different departments would have complicated the development process.

Hardware and software needs were met through a combination of two strategies. University support of the expansion of computing labs and computing facilities was accomplished by creating substantial excess demand — students and faculty complained enough that the University had to speed up the provision of needed facilities. This resulted in the addition of two classrooms with a total of 70 personal computers and double (to 40) the non-classroom machines available to students. In addition, software and hardware needed for faculty and graduate assistants was supported by outside grants.

Text material was arranged through a combination of standard texts from the areas of business research, statistics, and MIS. These were augmented by a substantial amount of specially prepared handouts that the students were required to purchase. These materials were gathered and prepared by a group of faculty with the substantial support of doctoral student teaching assistants. Continual improvement of the materials is expected, with the eventual preparation or acquisition of new standard text material.

In addition to the implementation barriers (faculty, facilities and texts), there are many continuing problems to be considered. These include:

\- Incorporation of new developments in software and hardware.

\- Coordinating software skill development with the needs of other courses in the curriculum.

● Hardware and software access.

Thus, the group of faculty and doctoral student assistants is expected to continually update the materials for some time to come.

## Conclusion

The problem of providing managers with an integrated set of decision technology capabilities is being addressed by a revised sequence of undergraduate core courses at Minnesota. There have been and will continue to be implementation difficulties. However, we believe that the effort will produce benefits that far outweigh the costs. While many faculty were initially reluctant to reorient their thinking, they are now comfortable with the new alignment. Student reactions were initially somewhat negative, reflecting the logistics of text and computer support as well as uncertainty in faculty presentation. Now the student reaction is very positive and they feel they are receiving valuable and distinctive instruction in an important topical area. While it is difficult to demonstrate, we believe that students completing the new sequence of courses will be much better prepared to support managerial decision making than those receiving the traditional treatment. In addition, this new arrangement clarifies the division of material related to information processing and material related to the functional management of the information resource. This paves the way for more focused development of an information management course that should be made available to all management students.
