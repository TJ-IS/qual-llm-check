# Planning, Critical Success Factors, and Management's Information Requirements

By: Malcolm C. Munro
Basil R. Wheeler

## Abstract

Focusing on a manager's goals and critical success factors has been advocated as an approach to defining senior and middle managers' information requirements. In this article a field study is described in which the planning processes in a corporation were used as a mechanism for identifying goals, critical success factors, and performance measures and standards, i.e., information requirements for managerial control. A general approach generated from the field study is described and the advantages and disadvantages of the approach are analyzed.

Keywords: Information requirements, information analysis, critical success factors, systems analysis, management control, planning

ACM Categories: 3.5

## Introduction

In an article in Harvard Business Review entitled “Chief Executives Define Their Own Data Needs,” John F. Rockart describes a new approach to assist managers, and in particular chief executive officers, define their significant information needs [17]. Rockart discusses the strengths and weaknesses of several approaches, and indicates that the new approach being applied at MIT focuses on an executive’s “critical success factors” (CSF’s).

CSF's are those factors which determine success for a company or business unit, those tasks which must be done well to ensure success. As such it is these “areas of activity that should receive constant and careful attention from management. The current status of performance in each area should be continually measured, and that information should be made available” [17, p. 85]. In essence, Rockart indicates that ensuring the attainment of an organization’s goals necessitates good performance in these areas, and it is therefore imperative that management receive constant feedback regarding them. $^{1}$

Use of the Critical Success Factors approach involves two or three interviews in which the executive's goals and the CSF's that underlie the goals are discussed. In addition, the analyst and executive examine interrelationships among the CSF's and the goals to determine which CSF's should be combined, eliminated, or restated.

The successful application of this approach could be a significant matter since the designers of information systems have historically been least successful in developing information systems for senior and middle level managers. The works of Henry Mintzberg are useful in penetrating the maze confronting the information analyst concerned with this problem $[13, 14]$ .

In this article the CSF's approach as described by Rockart utilizes the corporate planning process to aid in the identification of CSF's, and later, appropriate performance measures. In effect, the analyst becomes involved in the ongoing planning and control processes of the organization. The products of this approach are critical success factors, performance measures, performance standards, and the information and decisions associated with the above, i.e., information necessary to achieve organizational control.

A primary goal of this article is to help managers and analysts add structure and understanding to activities directed toward defining the information needs of managers with control responsibilities whether at senior or middle levels. The approach to be described was derived from a field study of the planning activities of senior middle level managers in a large resource based company. The approach emphasizes the link between a manager's information needs on the one hand, and decision responsibilities and the organization's objectives on the other. It also requires the manager to play an active role in the process, an ingredient often cited as essential to successful systems design and implementation.

## Background

Empirical research and analytical efforts in information requirements analysis (IRA) are characterized by an inadequate theoretical base. $^{2}$ At present, no generally accepted framework is available, hence most work is based upon a variety of axioms. Furthermore, due to the complexity of the problem and the nature of organizations, IRA experimental studies tend to be narrow, focusing on some particular aspect of interest.

Much of the published work begins with the premise that a manager's information needs are best defined by exploring the manager's decision making responsibilities [1, 7, 10, 11, 18] and cognitive style [3, 4], the most prominent example being that of the widespread interest in Decision Support Systems [8]. Related studies in IRA which center on decision making include Kennedy and Mahapatra [9], King and Cleland [11], Munro and Davis [16], and Rockart [17].

Since this article is particularly concerned with the requirements of senior and middle level managers for control information, it is useful to study the works of Robert Anthony [2] and Gorry and Scott Morton [7]. Anthony divides managerial activities into operational control, management control, and strategic planning. As Gorry and Scott Morton have indicated, the nature of the information required varies for each activity. For example, for strategic planning, information is often from external sources and does not require a high degree of accuracy. By contrast, for operational control activities, the information is almost always from internal sources and must be more precise. Management control activities lie between these two, and management control information is focused on here.

Management control is the process of ensuring that resources are obtained and used effectively toward the attainment of corporate goals $[2]$ . Therefore, it is necessary that managers be able to determine where they and their business units stand in relation to such goals. However, in practice corporate goals do not make very useful operational yardsticks, primarily because of their unquantified nature. For example, one corporate goal of an oil company might be “to increase the supply of conventional reserves of crude oil.” It would be infeasible for the responsible manager to determine the progress of a business unit against this standard as the measure is not specific enough. Consequently, there is a requirement for more detailed, intermediate targets—targets which constitute a translation of the relevant corporate goals into operationally useful performance measures for each business unit. The identification of these targets or performance measures and their related information is generated by the manager’s planning activities. In the section which follows, a field study is described in which managers involved in planning participated in the development of performance measures, and in doing so defined their information needs. From the field study, a method is derived for defining information for management control through involvement with corporate and business planning.

## The Field Study

Prior to and during the field study, one of the authors was engaged as a seminar leader in a corporate training seminar for senior middle level managers of a large natural resources company. As the seminar was concerned with improving the participants' skills and executive performance, several on-the-job assignments were contrived in cooperation with one of the corporation's vice presidents. Briefly, the information systems assignment required each manager to do the following:

\- derive the key objectives for the business unit by analysis of the corporate strategy statement, and

•develop appropriate performance measures for the business unit, including standards for comparison.

Several weeks after the seminar was completed, the authors undertook to individually interview some of the participants. The purpose of the interviews was to review and assist with the assignment, assess progress, and in particular, to attempt to discover the strategies used to execute the assignment. From observation and analysis of the strategies employed, the authors sought to develop a general strategy which might be useful to other managers faced with the ubiquitous problem of defining information which supports organizational control functions.

By fortunate circumstance, two of the executives had already progressed to advanced stages in completion of the assignment. Interviews with these two permitted the development of a strategy which was then applied in assisting the other managers with developing the means for specifying their information needs.

Interviews lasted up to two and one half hours depending on the nature of each manager's responsibilities. Those with relatively unstructured decision responsibilities necessitated a longer interview than did those managers whose decision responsibilities were less complex and more highly structured. Time committed to direct completion of the assignment by the participants, exclusive of the interviews, ranged from as much as two full days with extensive subordinate participation in the case of a senior executive, to as little as three or four hours where the executive acted independently. The latter situation was most common.

A significant environmental factor in this field study was the corporation's high level of commitment to short and long range planning. Planning was a serious activity for all involved, and individual performance was assessed in relation to the successful completion of developed plans. Consequently, the participants approached the assignment aggressively, which greatly assisted the authors in deriving the approach.

## The Approach

The process of determining information requirements for control purposes consists of five major activities:

\- understand business unit objectives,

\- identify Critical Success Factors,

\- identify specific performance measures and standards,

\- identify data required to measure performance, and

\- identify decisions and information required to implement the plan.

Understanding this process is enhanced by an examination of conventional corporate planning processes and the manager's role in these processes [18].

In a typical case, the corporation will distinguish between a business plan and a corporate plan. The business plan is usually concerned with the strategies for particular products, groups of products, functional activities, etc. For example, an oil company might choose to develop distinct business plans for exploration, production, and marketing activities respectively. By contrast, the corporate plan seeks to coordinate all of the activities of a company so that superordinate goals are achieved. The business plan is normally concerned with a time frame as short as one to five years, whereas the corporate plan may look ahead as far as twenty years, though ten years is typical.

The process described below is an example of a general approach to top down corporate planning in a decentralized firm [18, p.53]. In this example, the corporate plan or strategy statement is produced by a top management council consisting of the chief executive officer and vice presidents. The vice presidents in turn work with their respective subordinates to identify those aspects of the corporate plan which are relevant to the business units, divisions, or departments for which they are responsible. The products of this activity are business unit strategy statements which specify key objectives, strategies, specific objectives, etc., for each business unit. Each of the business unit strategy statements developed are in turn presented for approval to the planning council, or top management, prior to implementation.

Once the various business unit strategy statements have been approved, senior managers in charge of each business unit work with their subordinates to develop specific business unit plans which are as quantified as possible. The figures generated for each business unit plan are in turn provided to a staff group which combines all business unit plans to generate a business plan for the corporation as a whole. Once again the business plan is presented to the planning council for approval. From this point on, it becomes the responsibility of the executive in charge of each business unit to achieve the objectives specified and implied in each business unit plan. This is accomplished by developing detailed programs which implement the strategies indicated in their business unit plan. The corporate planning process as described is portrayed in Figure 1 and is summarized in Table 1.

## Understand business unit objectives

The first activity in defining required information is begun following approval of the corporate business plan and the development of programs to implement the plan. It is at this stage that the information analyst becomes active. This activity involves ensuring that the manager and the analyst both understand the overall corporate plan, its goals, objectives, and general thrust. Even more important to the task at hand, both must have a full understanding of the objectives of the manager's business unit in relation to the corporate plan. If the manager was an active participant in the formulation of the business unit plan, an understanding of objectives will already be present. Otherwise careful discussion of the plan is required.

Developing this understanding will be relatively straightforward if an explicit up-to-date corporate plan is available; this is increasingly the case as long range planning becomes more in vogue. On the other hand, where such a plan is not in existence, there may be a requirement for research, discussion, and analysis of the business. In general, the manager's perception and judgement of corporate directions and business unit objectives should prevail.

## Identify critical success factors

For each of the objectives identified for the manager's business unit, the manager and analyst must now identify the critical success factors which the manager must address if each business unit objective is to be achieved. These CSF's can be identified during the space of one or two manager/analyst discussions. CSF's are best described by a short label or expression, as opposed to a narrative statement, which effectively communicates the area of the activity. For example, a CSF identified by all managers in the field study was "human resources." Ensuring that sufficiently qualified staff is available appeared critical to the success of each business unit. In Rockart's article, some CSF's mentioned were technological reputation, company morale, and liquidity position [17, p.89].

## Identify specific performance measures and standards

Having identified critical success factors, the manager and analyst attempt to identify specific performance measures for each CSF. To be useful, the measures should be quantitative as opposed to qualitative in nature. In the field study, the dimensions of quantity, quality, cost, and time were used as general performance measures by three of the managers interviewed. There was no requirement to choose these dimensions but they served well in many areas, and proved useful as a starting point for specific performance measure generation. For example, performance measures identified for the “human resources” area were “percentage of staff turnover” (quantity), “number of staff qualified for promotion” (quality), and “amount of money to be spent on personnel training” (cost).

However, in some cases quantification may not be easy. At such times the use of indirect surrogate measures which infer progress toward an objective may be considered. For example, part of the responsibilities of one manager involved developing and maintaining a favorable image for the corporation in certain sensitive areas. His activities in this regard were directed toward influencing the opinions of individuals such as legislators, government administrators, and research personnel—persons normally sought out by the media when incidents or accidents associated with oil operations occurred. Keeping opinion leaders informed of company efforts to avoid accidents, maintain standards, etc., leads to a more balanced public view of the company. The obvious measure of performance in this delicate area would be the changing condition of the corporate image; yet such a measurement is neither regularly available, nor useful for assessing the performance of a single manager. Instead the manager chose the surrogate measure “number of visits,” reasoning that his talents at persuasion and interpersonal communication were the reasons why he held his position. Therefore if his information exchange plans were being executed, it would result in an improved corporate image, or at least a better protected one. The indirect surrogate measure “number of visits” was used to infer progress in improving the corporate image.

Once performance measures have been identified, the actual performance standards to be met can be set. Frequently, performance standards will be derived directly from the business plan. In other cases a manager may develop performance standards in consultation with his superior or subordinates or may wish to set the performance standards on his own. Obviously, where past performance figures are available, such figures may provide some assistance in setting realistic standards.

## Identify data required to measure performance

Generally, once the performance measures and standards are determined, the data required to evaluate progress in relation to these standards is easily identified. For example, if “percentage staff turnover” is chosen as a performance measure, it is obvious that the information needed is the total number of staff and the number of staff who have departed during a given period. Similarly, assessments of quantity, quality, cost, or time required with respect to performance in other areas are not difficult to generate, collect, and record. Taken together, business unit objectives, critical success factors, performance measures, and performance standards constitute a “work plan” for the managers in question. To ensure that both the managers and their superiors agree on the work plan developed, a document should be prepared which specifies the critical factors identified, the performance measures developed, and their desired levels. This document should be regarded as a contract between the managers and their superiors which will guide the managers in the performance of their responsibilities over the designated period, and facilitate more objective evaluation of their performance. This does not preclude making adjustments to the work plan, but contributes to a better understanding of what is expected of the managers by both themselves and their superiors.

As an aside, once performance measures and standards have been decided upon, it is important for the corporation to establish a performance review process. If such a process is not established, the performance measurement exercise will be of little value. When the concept of performance measurement is first implemented, all concerned are likely to be conscientious about evaluating performance. However, as time passes there is a natural tendency for such systems to be forgotten and no longer used. Preventing this is not an easy task. Procedures are required that are active rather than passive in nature. That is, procedures must be designed to require superiors to take some action or make some decision with respect to individual managerial performance. For example, managers might be required to submit copies of their subordinates' work plans to their superiors, along with periodically written evidence that progress in relation to these work plans has been reviewed.

## Identify decisions and information required to implement the plan

Though the field study did not extend beyond identifying the data required to measure performance, it is clear that managers must also identify the decisions which must be made to implement the plan developed. These decisions can be identified by the manager and analyst considering each CSF identified earlier. This examination and discussion will result in the identification of a set of decision areas in which managers must act to achieve business unit objectives. For novel or complex decision situations, information to assist decision making may simply be unavailable. However, many decision situations lend themselves to analysis. Such analysis takes the form of the development of a model of the decision process involved, and the specification of the information needed at each step in the process. $^{3}$

It is also important to recognize that decisions are not made in isolation. The decisions of any decision maker affect, and in turn are affected by, decisions made in other managerial areas. Ackoff [1] suggested that the relationships between major decisions in an organization should be determined and flowcharted. This implies that the analyst should spend some time with each manager determining not only what that manager's decisions are, but how they affect and are affected by decisions made by other managers. Ackoff indicated that this decision flow analysis may reveal that important decisions are being made by default, or that interdependent decisions are being made independently. The need for a reassignment of managerial responsibilities or changes in organizational structure may also be identified.

By utilizing the corporate planning process, the information analyst designs the information system from the top down, and is therefore able to determine the information most critical to the manager's decision making.

Thus this approach provides information which is tailored to the manager's needs. As a result, the manager is not only more effective, but also more efficient. Since only information directly associated with the manager's control processes is provided, the volume of information which the manager must absorb and analyze is significantly reduced, saving time. In the field study, the information required by one manager monitoring the progress of several different projects was reduced from several pages to a single page. Furthermore, the time spent analyzing the information was reduced from about three hours to less than a half hour. Brevity, fragmentation, and verbal communication characterize a manager's work according to Henry Mintzberg [13, p. 54]. An information reporting system which ignores this will not be used. Information about a manager's critical success factors, permitting rapid monitoring of performance in the key areas, would seem to better accommodate the time pressures of the senior executive.

The process of engaging in a detailed analysis of business unit objectives, critical success factors, and performance standards with the information analyst will aid the manager in understanding the role that both the manager and the business unit play in achieving corporate objectives. This in turn should lead to better performance by the manager and better direction for the manager's subordinates. If the manager in question sees the wisdom of having subordinates participate in the process, improved performance, communication, and productivity of the entire business unit should result. In addition, the more explicit the measures of performance, the more able the manager will be in appraising subordinates' performance.

This approach may also provide structure to some managerial jobs previously thought to be relatively "free-form." For example, in the field study, one of the managers involved was a director of environmental affairs. His responsibilities involved assessing the environmental impact of the corporation's exploration and production activities, and maintaining constant liaison with environmental groups. This manager had been of the opinion that the qualitative nature of his work would not lend itself to critical success factors analysis. However, he found the approach rewarding as it forced him to analyze his responsibilities in detail, and to identify the key tasks on which to focus his efforts. He came to view his activities as being relatively structured through involvement in this activity.

While more structure may be welcomed by some managers, it is likely that others may view such job structuring negatively. Those executives whose managerial style is highly entrepreneurial, and whose successes are based on heuristic talents, may dislike the constraints implied by having to specify “critical success factors” and “work plans.” Such individuals may well possess executive talent of the greatest value to the corporation. Their ability to perform successfully in highly unstructured environments may mean that they are more suitable than others for top management positions where major decisions must often be based on fragments of seemingly unrelated data. For this kind of manager, imposing a structure which runs counter to his or her cognitive style may be dysfunctional. If the situation becomes too uncomfortable, or the manager’s productivity declines, the company and the manager may part ways. There is also the risk that the “maverick” is passed over at promotion time because of these real but obscure difficulties engendered by the planning process. A solution to this dilemma might be to develop critical success factors and performance measures for the executive’s use only. In other words, such information might be used by the executive to monitor performance of the business unit, but not by the company to measure the performance of the executive.

## The analyst's viewpoint

The normally difficult challenge of determining the manager's information needs becomes much more manageable from the viewpoint of the information analyst. Natural guidelines as to relevance, accuracy, timeliness, and other information characteristics are inherent in the process by operating within the planning context. Moreover, the organizational pressure on the manager to meet specific objectives ensures a serious and enthusiastic participation in an activity which will directly affect the achievement of objectives. This overcomes the usual problem of asking a manager to spend time doing something which may not ordinarily be perceived as directly relevant to the manager's job.

Utilizing the planning processes of the organization also provides structure to the analyst's interviews, a structure which is often absent. An unstructured interview usually leads to educated guesswork as to what information to provide the manager, and the product is frequently unsatisfactory. In the absence of structure the analyst becomes dependent on the manager to articulate the information required, an ability which the manager, often may not have. For example, most managers have a real need for both "hard" and "soft" (qualitative) information. Hard information, the quantitative sort most often derived from the organization's financial reporting systems, is the easiest to articulate and obtain. Yet, the ready availability of such information often leads to overlooking the need for identifying critical soft information. This problem can be overcome by an approach which is driven and structured by the requirement to identify information for critical success factors, whether associated with hard or soft areas of the manager's responsibilities.

Identifying CSF's and information for management control purposes in the context of an organization's planning processes seems to overcome some potential difficulties noted by Gordon B. Davis [6]. Davis suggests that human information processing limitations might cause managers to reduce the number of critical success factors they actively consider to a range of five to nine, whereas the number of CSF's in the actual situation may be greater. In other words, managers might inadvertently overlook or fail to recall some CSF's. This is a real possibility when the manager/analyst interviews are unstructured. However, using the approach in this article, the manager/analyst discussion is structured by the presence of goals and objectives identified earlier by the planning process. As a consequence, CSF's are generated in response to stimuli, i.e., goals and objectives, as opposed to the analyst relying solely on the individual manager's limited information processing capacities. Since the number of CSF's for a particular objective seldom exceeds three or four, the structure provided by the goals and objectives identified in planning should ensure that no CSF's are overlooked.

Other phenomena noted by Davis which might impair the effectiveness of the critical success factors approach are bounded rationality, human ability to evaluate probabilities and to evaluate causality, and the biasing effect of availability of data. In general, Davis points out that “what is needed is an analytical model of the business unit that the analyst can use in eliciting executive responses and with which to evaluate the critical success factors obtained from executives for relevance, correctness, and completeness” [6, p.58]. $^{4}$ Unfortunately a valid analytical model of a complex business unit is seldom available, however useful it might be. When the analyst is working with a manager whose cognitive style may be characterized as “systematic,” to use McKenney and Keen’s terminology [12, p. 81], the analyst may be able to have the manager articulate an analytical model of the business unit without great difficulty. On the other hand, if the manager is more of an intuitive thinker, developing such a model may prove too difficult or time consuming. However, applying the CSF’s approach in a planning context may overcome the difficulties noted by Davis. Just as the CSF’s approach provides natural guidelines with respect to information characteristics, it also promotes a higher level of relevance, correctness, and completeness of the critical success factors elicited from executives. The key to this outcome is the explicit linkage between goals and objectives on the one hand, and critical success factors on the other. Since the margin for error on these dimensions is minimized by this linkage, an enhanced level of relevance, correctness, and completeness is inherent in the product.

A difficulty exists with respect to the problem of generating performance measures in “soft” areas. The use of surrogate measures from which performance might be inferred in these areas was referred to earlier. Yet surrogate measures are obviously not completely satisfactory. If performance measures are to assist the manager in achieving some measure of control over assigned responsibility areas, the more direct must be the connection between the performance being measured and the organizational component being controlled.

An approach to determining information requirements which is dependent on a commitment to planning may be difficult to apply in a corporation in which organized planning does not occur. The greatest problem in this regard will be the absence of corporate strategy from which to derive the necessary goals and objectives which apply to the individual business units. Still, the information requirements may be defined reasonably well if the manager in question holds a clear understanding of the unit's organizational mandate.

Finally, as the organization and its environment changes, so too will corporate goals, objectives, and management's critical success factors. Adoption of the approach described in this article will ensure that through the mechanism of the planning cycle critical success factors, performance measures, performance standards, and related information are regularly updated.

## Conclusions

Attending to those factors critical to the achievement of the organization's goals results in more effective management. Senior and middle management's information needs for control are defined by identifying critical success factors within the context of corporate planning processes. The critical success factors method seems to be an answer to the criticism that management information systems activities can be of little assistance to executives at senior organizational levels.
