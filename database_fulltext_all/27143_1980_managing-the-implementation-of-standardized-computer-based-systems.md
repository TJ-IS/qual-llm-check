# MANAGING THE IMPLEMENTATION OF STANDARDIZED COMPUTER BASED SYSTEMS

By: Lee L. Gremillion

## Abstract

Systems development and implementation are traditionally approached as a process of designing and fitting a tool to its user's needs. Cases arise, however, due to economic constraints or the desire to standardize, in which an organization directs its subunits to implement a system "as is." In such a case, organizational change is mandated and must be managed as such.

Certain units of the U. S. Forest Service have been successful in developing a process to manage the implementation of a standardized program planning system across multiple national forests. The process includes a systematic evaluation of the knowledge, skills, and/or attitude changes indicated for specific individuals in the implementing units. This in turn allows the selection of the most appropriate techniques to foster the changes. The results of these Forest Service efforts have been encouraging and offer some insights into the process of managing system implementation.

Keywords: Systems implementation, governmental systems, user behavior, implementation management, organizational change

ACM Categories: 1.9, 3.59

## Introduction

The computer industry, from its beginnings, has been characterized by continuing technological advance which has dramatically lowered the cost of computing hardware. As a result computer technology is becoming available to an ever-widening circle of applications within organizations. An application which could not be justified fifteen years ago because it would require a half million dollar machine is possible today on a forty thousand dollar mini. However, widespread use is limited by the high personnel related costs of designing and programming the systems to run on the relatively inexpensive hardware. Systems developers face a significant challenge to overcome these cost constraints. This might be the single greatest problem in applying new developments in technology to the needs of organizations [3].

One approach to solving the problem is to use generalized, packaged programs and systems which are developed at one site, and can be installed at other sites with little or no modification. In many cases, such packages assist in dealing with the problem of cost, and also with the growing organizational concerns for standardization and control. In some cases, it may not be a question of whether the organization can afford to let subunits have individualized systems, but rather that it does not want them individualizing their systems. For example, a decentralized corporation may develop a project evaluation system to help make capital investment decisions across subunits. In such a case standardization itself is perhaps the major benefit of the system.

Using generalized, packaged systems, however, calls for a new focus in research on systems implementation. The existing systems development (e.g., McFarlan, Nolan, and Norton [4]), and OR/MS implementation literature (Schultz and Slevin [6]), largely concentrate on development and implementation as a process of tailoring a system for its potential user. The primary implementation task is one of understanding and defining the particular user's needs, and then of designing the system to fit those needs.

When installing a pre-written package “as is” we forego the opportunity to tailor beyond the flexibility built into the package. The primary activities are no longer information needs analysis and system design, but instead center on user training, education, and motivation. The user must be taught to adapt and change his behavior in order to meet the requirements of the system.

This is not to say that user needs are ignored as not important. Rather, we make an assumption that, at this point, management has determined that installing this particular system is the “best” way to meet the organization’s needs. Presumably a number of alternatives has been weighed, and an administrative decision has been made that the system in question is the preferred one. Perhaps even a pilot study or prototype system has been completed and should be operationalized in multiple units. Whatever the case, the user must learn to fit the system if the implementation is to succeed.

Documentation is limited on how this process of “plugging in” systems is accomplished. This article reports the efforts of one organization to directly and systematically manage the implementation of standardized computer based systems.

## Two Cases of Standardized Systems Implementation

## The implementing organization

The National Forest System (NFS) is a major component of the United States Forest Service, an agency of the Department of Agriculture. The NFS is responsible for the management of the 187 million acres of land contained within the nation's national forests. These forests include lands of great value for producing commercial timber, for grazing livestock, for outdoor recreation, and for a number of other uses.

A “typical” National Forest is a sizeable administrative and geographic unit, covering over one million acres, employing several hundred persons, and managing an annual budget of 10 million dollars. The NFS has a long tradition of decentralized management because of the complexity of managing such a land area. Policy direction, however, must be set from the top of the organization since the basic resource allocation decisions are made on a political basis. These two factors necessitate the use of elaborate formal planning systems as integrating mechanisms.

One of these is program planning, the annual process by which the organization formulates its budget requests to the Congress. This planning is described in detail by McKenney, et al. [5], but the important feature of this article is that it is a process in which all units take part.

In 1977, Forest Service top management decided that within the next four years, all the forests in the system would begin using a standardized computer based system, known as ADVENT, in preparing and submitting program plans. Management felt that having all forests use ADVENT would improve program planning by (1) increasing standardization to facilitate comparisons across units, and (2) integrating the program planning process more closely with other forest management activities. The explicit decision had been made that no tailoring would be done to suit the individual forest's desires for reasons of cost and standardization.

## The standardized system

ADVENT itself is a series of programs for building, maintaining, combining, and analyzing work projects. Work projects for a national forest include such things as timber sales, campground construction and maintenance, and wildlife habitat improvements. Forest personnel define possible projects in terms of costs, manpower, and outputs, in the program planning process, which they would like to propose for funding. ADVENT includes linear and goal programming as well as less sophisticated methods of selecting groupings of projects required to meet forest targets and objectives. The system runs on a large central timesharing computer to which the forests have remote access.

Responsibility for scheduling and directing individual forests' ADVENT implementation is delegated to the nine Regional Foresters in the system; forests are grouped geographically and administratively into regions—Figure 1 shows the regional organization. Top management has merely specified that the forests are to be “on the system” by the time the planning cycle for Fiscal Year 1983, i.e., winter 1980-81, comes around. It has been up to the regions to define exactly what this means for their forests in terms of both activities and schedules.

During the course of these implementations, the author was part of a Cooperative Research Project with the United States Forest Service (USFS), studying information systems use. This made possible the detailed study of how several regions went about putting their forests on ADVENT.

## Implementation case one

Management of one large western region, referred to here as Region A, decided to have all nineteen of its forests implement the system during the Fiscal Year 1980 planning effort, i.e., winter 1977-78. The strategy it employed was a simple and commonly encountered one: forests were told to “do it” and technical training was made available on voluntary bases. First, the Regional Forester sent a letter to all the forests directing them to “use ADVENT” in their upcoming program planning. Next, each forest was sent copies of the system reference documentation, which specified formats and procedures. Finally, the regional program planning staff held a two day training session for any staff members that each forest wished to send.

These implementations were the subject of a study conducted by several members of the Cooperative Research team, relating organizational characteristics to implementation results. As a dependent variable in this study, the team used “implementation success” for each forest as defined by regional management. In ordering the forests to use ADVENT, regional management had only required that the forests submit their program plans in ADVENT data format. What they envisioned as successful use, on the other hand, involved much more than mere data format conversions. Successful use entailed generation of project proposals by ranger district personnel, the building of an ADVENT project database, and a formal decision making process at the Forest Supervisor’s office level. This decision making process involved using the ADVENT analytical tools to select projects for inclusion in program proposals. At the end of the program planning cycle, the regional program planning staff was able, with some objectivity, to place each forest on a scale ranging from minimum use to successful use. The variability was considerable, ranging from “total success”—i.e., extensive use of the system, including sophisticated analyses—to “total failure”—i.e., inability to comply even with the basic data requirements.

This study, reported in [1], identified four forest characteristics which correlated closely with implementation success. These characteristics were the attitude of the forest line management, the forest staff's computer related skills, the competence of the forest program planning officer, and the forest's prior experience using project data in program planning. These characteristics were identified through a factor analysis of several dozen more specific measurements of forest personnel, obtained through structured interviews. The correlation of each of these four factors with implementation success, measured on a five point scale, was significant at the .02 level.

The study group also concluded that the “implementation strategy” used in Region A almost guaranteed uneven success. When examined closely it was clear that certain assumptions were implicit in it:

1. that the management and staffs on all the various factors in the region were at the same level of knowledge, skills, and attitudes concerning ADVENT;

2. that the addition of only technical knowledge of the system was needed to induce the behavior identified with successful system use;

3. that the individuals on the forests would be able to identify whether they had sufficient technical knowledge; and

4. that if not, they would be willing to seek out the necessary technical training.

These would appear to be highly questionable assumptions in any case, and analysis of the implementation results indicate that they were wrong. They are, however, inherent in a strategy as passive or nondirective as that employed by Region A.

## Implementation case two

As a result of this study, members of the research team were invited to work with the program planning staff of another NFS region, Region B, as this region began to implement ADVENT on its forests. The objective was to apply the lessons learned in Region A in order to improve the implementation strategy and, ultimately, the likelihood of success in Region B.

Region B planned to begin forest level ADVENT use during the Fiscal Year 1981 program planning cycle, winter 1978-79. Management had already decided on a phased approach, with about one third of the region's forests implementing the system each year over the next three years. Both the Regional Forester and his program planning staff were quite receptive to the idea of pursuing an active, even aggressive, strategy in order to maximize the likelihood of successful system use by the forests. Beginning in spring 1978, the regional staff began developing their plan with the assistance of the research team.

## A Conceptual Model

Strategy formulation was guided by a simple conceptual scheme developed by the research team as shown in Figure 2. Successful system use can be identified with a certain behavior exhibited by the implementing unit, i.e., a set of events, activities, and outputs which are to occur on, or flow from, the unit. This, in turn, implies specific job behaviors on the part of the individuals within the unit if these events, activities, and outputs are to be forthcoming.

Specific individual job behavior implies specific skills, knowledge, and attitudes to enable that behavior. These attributes may be quite different from those currently held by the individuals in question. It is the role of implementation activity, then, to move individuals in the implementing units from their current states of knowledge, skill, and attitude to these appropriate states.

The model implies that one “backs into” a determination of implementation strategy, determining first the desired behavior of the organization as a whole, and then of individuals within the organization. Implementation activities can be correctly specified only after the desired states of skills, knowledge, and attitudes of individuals in the implementing units have been determined and compared with the current states. One must first determine where one is and where one wishes to go before going about selecting a vehicle.

## Using the Model

Region B proceeded in just the fashion described. Its “strategy formulation process” consisted of five distinct steps.

1. Definition of Successful Implementation. In the first step the regional program planning staff had several meetings with the Regional Forester to discuss forest level ADVENT use, and the specific goals for the implementation. A basic goal was to comply with the Washington Office's directives concerning the system. In Region 3, however, top management felt that a certain level of ADVENT use could help meet some regional objectives as well. Specifically, management sought a forest level program planning process which was (1) consistent across all forests, (2) relatively stable from year to year, (3) efficient in terms of the manpower required to produce the plans, and (4) integrated with other aspects of forest management (e.g., monitoring and controlling ongoing activities).

The program planning staff then defined a specific set of outputs, activities, and events, including a relevant time frame and sequence, which should occur within the implementing forests to achieve these objectives. The flow of events through a program planning cycle on a forest begins with the supervisor's office staff issuing instructions and directions for the ranger district personnel as to the parameters of the project data they should prepare. Ranger district staffs then generate site specific projects—that is, work projects which represent proposed activities to be performed in the district during the target year. These projects are submitted to the supervisor's office staff for editing and entry into the ADVENT system. Using ADVENT, the forest staffs, with supervisor and ranger participation, select and aggregate project data into forest program proposals. Both direction setting and program formulation involve negotiations between supervisor and rangers, and between the various resource areas. Finally, the forest submits the program proposals to the regional office. The Regional Forester reviews and approves these specifications for ADVENT use.

2. Identification of Required Individual Performance. Once the desired behavior of the organization as a whole was defined, its implications for the various individuals on the forest were identified. This was primarily the job of the regional program planning staff, with line management's concurrence. Briefly, they determined that if a forest were to use ADVENT successfully, the roles of individual personnel would be the following.

Forest Supervisor—The supervisor should agree to use the system on the forest and must issue instructions to personnel that they use it. Subordinates must also realize that the Forest Supervisor intends to use the system as a decision making aid.

District Rangers—The rangers must guide the use of the system on their district as their functional staff people put together site specific projects. They should coordinate the process among their people so that viable district programs are formed from the various projects submitted by the rangers.

Ranger District Staff—The staffs in the districts and the rangers must actually generate the bulk of the specific projects—including the number of people, resources, dollars, and equipment needed to accomplish a project—and record the information in the proper format.

Supervisor's Office Staff—The resource staff members in the supervisor's office have several responsibilities. They should advise the forest supervisor of the objectives and directions issued to the districts for programming. They should advise the ranger district personnel in their functional area when the program data is being generated. They should edit and validate the projects submitted by the districts in their particular area. Finally, they should advise the forest supervisor during the process of forming forest programs from the projects.

Program Planning Leader—Someone on the supervisor's staff manages the process, ensuring that deadlines are met, proper formats are used, technical assistance is made available, etc. This person will also be responsible for editing and ensuring that the projects submitted by the districts are in the proper format. In addition, this person is responsible for helping the forest personnel in using and interpreting the outputs from ADVENT.

Computer Specialist—This person will actually operate the computer facilities or intelligent terminals, in running the ADVENT programs. The Computer Specialist will be responsible for problem solving and technical support in the areas of ADVENT itself, and the computer system in general.

3. Knowledge, Skills, and Attitudes Identification. The next step was to identify what knowledge, skills, and attitudes these individuals would need to perform as desired once the desired individual behaviors were specified. The program planning staff compiled a list of these for each of the individuals or groups mentioned above. As might be expected there was a considerable amount of overlap, and a brief summary across individuals and groups as follows.

Attitude is a critical attribute in the line management personnel who should commit to actually using the system as a management tool. The Forest Service is a fairly authoritarian organization. There is a strong tradition that “what the boss wants” is the right thing to do. Accordingly, the “bosses,” i.e., line managers—forest supervisors and rangers, should have and show some motivation for using the system. Otherwise, their subordinates are likely to perceive it as unimportant.

All personnel involved should have a basic knowledge of how the system works, but at varying levels of detail. For line management, knowledge of the capabilities of ADVENT and how it will be used in the management process should be at a conceptual rather than detailed level. The staffs who will be generating and validating project data should be familiar with the formats and procedures, and skilled enough with their use to be able to enter data, and request and utilize the reports. The program planning leader should be familiar with the technical details of the system, possess interpersonal skills for working with a variety of individuals on a new process, and have enough administrative skill to understand how to relate this process and system to the management process. The computer specialist should be knowledgeable in the basics of computer based systems as well. In short, there should be on the forest (1) management commitment to the process, (2) a general knowledge among the users of what they are doing and why, and (3) varying levels of technical, interpersonal, and administrative skills. An estimation was made, based on the regional staffs' knowledge of ADVENT and on other regions' experiences as to what level of technical system detail each individual or group of individuals would need.

4. Evaluation of Need for Knowledge/Skill/Attitude/Change. Having made an estimate of what knowledge, skills, and attitudes forest personnel ought to have, the regional program planning staff next evaluated the implementing units to determine what the current state of these attributes actually were in the forest personnel. This was done formally in a series of interviews with key personnel—forest supervisor, primary staff officers, and District Rangers—taking two days on each forest. Some of the techniques used earlier in the forest study in Region A proved useful as measurement tools in these evaluations [2]. By comparing the desired and actual knowledge, skills, and attitudes for the forests, the regional staff was able to develop a list of who needed to be taught what or be influenced in what direction.

5. Selection of Change Methods. Having established where personnel were (current knowledge, etc, on the forests) and where they wanted to go (desired knowledge, etc.) the regional staff was now ready to take action. Specific implementation activities were designed and executed to make the forests ready to use the ADVENT system. The staff was able to use a variety of conventional organizational change techniques as appropriate to the needs identified in designing these implementation activities. These included:

Top Management Support—The Regional Forester agreed to stress, both in writing and in meetings, his interest in the forests' ADVENT use.

Peer Evaluation and Interaction—Implementing units visited a forest in another region, which had served as a pilot for ADVENT use, to have that forest's personnel demonstrate and explain the system.

Job Redesign—Forests were directed to formally establish a “Program Planning Leader” role, and were given guidelines as to how it should be filled.

Technical Education—Technical ADVENT training, both in system use and project design, was given to forest staff members.

Structural Change—Forests were directed by regional management to create a computer specialist position in their organization, and to hire a qualified individual to fill it.

Participative Design—Forest supervisors and key staff members were called into a meeting in the regional office during which they jointly discussed and decided on many features of how the system would be used.

All of these activities were directed and scheduled by the regional staff. They were carried out by the forests mostly in the summer of 1978, and actual ADVENT use for program planning commenced that fall.

The results in Region B differed significantly from those in Region A. The three pilot forests were successful in using the system as prescribed for Fiscal Year 1981 program planning. Three additional forests implemented the system for Fiscal Year 1982 planning. The remaining forests in Region B will implement for Fiscal Year 1983 planning.

The ADVENT implementation method is no longer an experiment but is now official policy in Region B. NFS top management has incorporated much of it into systemwide ADVENT policy [7].

## Conclusion

The primary value of the approach taken in Region B was to provide a framework for considering the variables which affect implementation success. It is likely that the regional staff would have selected many of the same implementation activities no matter how they went about formulating strategy. This particular approach seems to have been helpful in two ways.

First, it gave an orderly structure in which the lessons learned in Region A could be applied. The Region A experience indicated that certain characteristics of individuals within the implementing units (knowledge, skills, and attitudes) correlated with implementation success. The Region A results could be explained in a way that suggested action by focusing on required individual behaviors and knowledge, skills, and attitudes appropriate to those behaviors. For example, familiarity with site-specific project design correlated highly with success in Region A. Region B interpreted this as lending support to their judgement as to the knowledge district and supervisor's office staffs needed to build a project database. Accordingly, project design was made part of the training provided.

A second benefit of this approach was that it helped structure the problem so that the appropriate tools could be applied to solving it. This was done by identifying specific skills to be taught, knowledge to be imparted, and attitudes to be fostered. For example, evaluation revealed that there should be an individual on the forest with detailed knowledge of computer systems in general, and ADVENT and the NFS's computer system in particular. Examination of the forests' current staffing showed that none of them possessed anyone with skills like this. This prompted the decision that a structural change—i.e., hiring someone to a new position—was necessary to implementation success.

As we saw in Region A, a unit which has been directed to implement a standardized system may not be able to evaluate its needs for change in knowledge, skills, and attitudes. Thus, it is unable to select for itself the appropriate implementation activities. The responsibility for making that evaluation and designing implementation strategy falls on the manager who orders the implementation. If that manager fails to understand and accept the responsibility, as in Region A, the implementation results are likely to suffer.

This implies that a manager should, before ordering such an implementation, ascertain whether the expertise to perform the evaluation and design strategy is available, and be prepared to expend resources in doing these activities. The Region B experience suggests that the results of “managed” implementations justify this expenditure.
