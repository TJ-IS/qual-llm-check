## Abstract

This paper describes the processes used by Digital Equipment Corporation in planning and managing its worldwide networks. As a corporate communications utility that has evolved into a strategic and tactical business tool, it is a critical success factor in today's business environment. It is designed with an open architecture for easy adaptability to growth and change. Descriptions of the network planning and management strategies and techniques demonstrate how DIGITAL meets its voice, data, and video requirements. This paper makes recommendations on how to plan for and manage a network utility with no disruption of day-to-day business operations.

Keywords: Data communications, distributed networks, network management, distributed applications, pricing and resource allocation, centralization/decentralization

## Introduction

Digital Equipment Corporation's (DIGITAL) worldwide internal networks are essential to the way the corporation does business. Employees across the globe work more effectively because they have easy and ready access to the information they need, when they need it. DIGITAL, a multinational Fortune 50 company, believes that the most successful corporations in the future will be those that view the network as a critical tool in seizing new business opportunities, improving productivity and the quality of work life, and gaining a competitive edge in the marketplace. In order to achieve these goals, network resources must be as pervasive, reliable and easy to use as electricity and running water.

Cost-effective decisions, faster time to market, increased sales and customer satisfaction are some of the benefits derived from the proper planning and management of a network that is sophisticated in its technology and design, yet is easy to use. Large corporations may spend anywhere from 1.5% to more than 2.5% of their total revenue on communications. With expenditures of that magnitude, careful planning and management are clearly required in justifying costs and reaping maximum benefits.

## Networking Experience at DIGITAL

DIGITAL's fifteen years of experience in using networking technology began with point-to-point transmissions over dial-up facilities. The first networks solved very specific business needs. DIGITAL's manufacturing operations implemented a world-wide network in the late 1970s to support information systems which controlled the movement of inventory between manufacturing plants. Engineering implemented a network in mid to late 1970 for developing and testing network products, and enabling electronic communications among project teams that were separated geographically. Specifications, computer-aided design (CAD) information, and project status reports were sent instantaneously over the network to engineers who needed the information. Over time, these business-specific networks not only proved their worth but also demonstrated the need to communicate across organizational boundaries.

For example, not only does manufacturing use the network today to meet its need for easy access to engineering's product designs, but sales organizations collect order status and scheduling information from manufacturing systems. This use of the network eliminates costly time delays that these organizations used to experience. DIGITAL's business units now depend on its networks to achieve business goals. Networking has clearly become as integral a part of the business as other utilities (heat, electricity, water). Initially, we provided limited gateways between the existing networks. However, these gateways did not provide full, transparent networking because they were difficult to use and manage, particularly in an environment of rapid growth and change.

## Today's Network Environment

DIGITAL's internal network environment today consists of:

1. EASYnet, the name used internally for its worldwide data network, links more than 21,000 computer systems—from desktop to data center. It is used to transfer information instantly where it is needed.

2. A separate private packet network that supplements EASYnet by providing a highly cost-effective alternative for wide area terminal access.

3. Extended local area networks (using Ethernet and bridge technology) and statistical multiplexing networks are used extensively to link users in smaller offices to computers in their respective district and regional headquarters.

4. The Digital Telephone Network, a large, private telephone network that supports more than two hundred locations in North America and Europe.

5. The Digital Video Network, a direct-broadcast satellite network (one-way video with two-way audio) for delivering training and product announcements, state-of-the-company messages, and allowing employees in the field to interact directly with senior management during the broadcasts.

## Business Dependency on the Network Environment

DIGITAL's day-to-day business operations depend heavily on this network environment. (See Appendix.) Development projects, which often involve groups located in multiple facilities around the world, use the network to capitalize on the time value of product information. For example, engineers working in California can, in seconds, send product design changes to their counterparts in England.

Every customer order flows through the corporate network from the initial entry point in the local sales office to the corporate sales headquarters and on to manufacturing. Based on manufacturing schedules for the required line items, a confirmation and committed shipment date are transmitted back to the salesperson for immediate communication to the customer. Order changes and status inquiries are also sent back and forth quickly over the network. Upon shipment of a product, billing information is immediately forwarded to the appropriate financial departments. ## The Network as a Utility

DIGITAL believes that the organizations with the most successful internal networks in the future will be those that view the network as a utility. In order to derive the maximum benefit and be able to use the network for seizing new business opportunities, network resources must be pervasive, reliable and easy to use. When employees move into new offices, they should be able to expect that behind a plug in the wall they will have everything they need to meet their voice, data and video communications requirements. What is actually behind that plug should be of no concern to anyone but the internal communications organization.

What does it really mean to be a utility in this context? First, it must be easy to use. Second, it must be easy for users to report problems and get them fixed or bypassed. Third, and most important, the utility must be “bullet proof” in the eyes of the end user, regardless of whether that user is a major business or an engineer at a terminal. This last point deserves some expansion.

For a network to be truly bulletproof, it must have the following characteristics:

1. Very few problems

2. Very few single points of failure

3. Instantaneous, and preferably automatic recovery/failover

4. Somebody to talk to (not an answering machine) at all times who can get the user back "on the air," repair the problem, keep the user informed, and handle the problem until it is fixed

5. Simple problem escalation procedures to reach people who can provide solutions

Clearly, a good deal of planning is needed to move toward the concept of the network as a utility.

## The Planning Process

Before setting out on the development of a network plan, whether tactical or strategic, it is important to keep in mind the major goals of the planning process:

1. Document the business requirements

2. Document any environmental constraints

3. Document all key assumptions and risks

4. Document the communications/network architecture

5. Identify major projects and/or tasks

6. Determine resources needed and their costs

7. Gain senior management support and commitment

8. Establish or enhance the credibility of the telecommunications organization and of the utility

Each individual organization undoubtedly needs a planning process that is customized to its particular environment and needs. ## Business plans

It is crucial to understand the service level requirements of the organization's business functions. For example, what is the business goal for responding to a customer's order-status inquiry? This is often difficult information to pin down, but both network plans and MIS application plans must be driven by the goals of the business. However, telecommunications managers are obligated to caution business managers and to propose other alternatives if the achievement of a particular business goal is either impossible or is extremely cost-prohibitive from a communications standpoint.

## MIS plans

In many organizations, the behavioral norm is to run around, usually at the last minute, "tweaking" the network in order to meet the needs of a new application that has just appeared. This is no longer necessary or appropriate if the utility providers are doing their jobs properly. For the most part, this means that the responsibility is on network managers to work with the MIS community to help them design (or procure) applications that are compatible with the network utility.

In addition, tightly coupling communications and MIS processes requires developing guidelines and getting directly involved with the application development teams to ensure that proper tradeoffs are made among:

-performance

-user-friendliness

\- security

-data integrity

-data storage

— location of systems

DIGITAL has gone a step further by defining an overall internal computing architecture and the necessary supporting development tools. The company intends to develop most future applications in conformance with this common architectural approach. The architecture addresses such areas as data management, system management and operations, hardware and system software configurations, and communications. The separation of production transaction processing from reporting and ad hoc inquiry is a central concept of the architecture.

## Network Management

DIGITAL's network architecture and management structure have consciously been planned to reflect and reinforce its corporate culture, organizational structure, and computing style. Like the networks themselves, network management is built according to a distributed model. The staff believes that it does not make sense to attempt to manage a network of more than 21,000 computer systems from a single, central point in the company environment.

The model shown in Figure 5 represents the proper mix of responsible central control and responsive local autonomy. Strategic direction generally comes from the upper two levels (shaded area), with much of the operational responsibility residing in the lower layers. The operations staff at the corporate level is fairly small.

As corporations become more involved with computer networking, and as their businesses begin to rely on the ability to electronically move information around the corporation, network management takes on increasing importance. In a large and complex network such as DIGITAL's, it is imperative to subdivide the network into manageable segments.

## Styles of Network Management

Network management is defined broadly to include the processes of:

— Setting service level objectives that will meet the business needs

—Monitoring service levels

—Restoring service in the case of a failure

—Fault isolation and resolution —Ensuring security of information by controlling access

—Planning for the evolution and growth of the network

—Budgeting, negotiating, and justifying adequate funding

For a small network, an informal approach to network management is often justified. However, for a large corporate network utility, a formal approach and commitment to network management is required. The choice among varying degrees of centralized and decentralized network management depends on the scope of the network, the culture of the organization, and MIS plans and strategies.

In terms of the network management and support organization, DIGITAL has established separate organizations and metrics for the physical transmission network and the utility itself, including many of the applications running on it. The company has also structured a geographical hierarchy of support and operations personnel who tie together in a matrix-management model. (Refer to Figure 5.)

This geographically decentralized support organization is particularly helpful in dealing with unique issues that arise in international telecommunications. By using local personnel who are familiar with the tariff and regulatory environment in each country, staff can work more effectively with the local carriers to implement the most appropriate communications services. The central network management organization provides some direction and consistency across the different geographies.

## Media Used at Transmission Level

Various media are used at the transmission level. Within a building or a campus setting, Ethernet is the technology that is used to tie systems and terminals into a Local Area Network (LAN). Ethernet typically uses coaxial cable as its physical media. Between sites, a variety of wide-area transmission facilities are used. These facilities include point-to-point leased data circuits, leased satellite circuits, private DIGITAL-owned fiber optic cable (which interconnects various New England area locations), as well as public switched services such as X.25 and ACCUNET.

## Worldwide Backbone

Topologically, DIGITAL has a worldwide backbone subnetwork that provides a high-capacity and high-reliability transport mechanism to interconnect EASYnet areas (Figure 6).

Each level of the topology is designed to conform to specific interconnect design rules that have been developed to provide consistent, predictable performance levels. Every DIGITAL location has been classified based on its requirements for network access and its level of support resources and expertise. The topology rules are intended to ensure minimum service levels for each class of site.

-Cost

## Network Management Tools

To support the decentralized operations structure, DIGITAL developed a comprehensive set of tools that assists network operations and support personnel. These tools take advantage of standard DECnet "hooks" and callable interface provided by the DECnet Network Management Layer. The tools fall into three categories:

Status Monitoring: Operational tools provide alarms and logs of network events, particularly unsuspected events and outages. The goal is to detect a failure and begin to isolate and repair it before end users are affected.

Troubleshooting: In many cases, network failures are caused by complex interactions or by problems that are not obvious. Often, problems cannot be reproduced because of changing loads and configurations. A set of troubleshooting tools is provided to assist in network fault isolation.

Traffic Data Collection: The Digital Network Architecture (DNA) is a fully distributed, peer-to-peer networking architecture. As such, data describing network activity, circuit loading, and circuit quality resides on each system across the network. A set of tools has been developed to correlate and roll up this data to provide the basis for the metrics described above.

## Network Security

With such universal access, security of information is a key consideration in the operation of a network. Efforts are focused on three major areas:

Access to the Network: In general, the network is intended for employee use only. Thus, access to the network from a particular system and access to the systems (by asynchronous dial-up, Ethernet terminal servers, public packet-switched networks) is controlled.

Security of the Transmission Paths Across the Network: In cases where sensitive data is being transmitted over the network, software encryption is used to protect the information from eavesdroppers. Other encryption methods will be employed selectively in the future.

Security of Specific Information on the Network: Access to each system on the network can be controlled. Individual file protection is provided by the various operating systems. This control is based on local, legal and business requirements. The degree of security control implemented on each system is a matter left to the discretion of the local system manager, within the requirements of certain corporate-wide policies and standards. In addition, some layered software products are used for assisting in the analysis of security risks and incidents.

## Accounting and Funding

Operating a network utility can involve considerable expense. Funding must be negotiated between the network manager and the user community and/or business manager. The corporate network utility is not generally viewed as a profit-making enterprise. In many cases, there is a requirement instead to recover costs from the user base. Various levels of detail of accounting information may be used as the basis for a charge-back algorithm. In public and voice networks, detailed usage-based accounting and billing is traditionally used as a means of most equitably recovering the costs of network operation.

At DIGITAL it was decided that the administrative overhead of collecting massive amounts of network usage data and processing it to allocate costs out to the individual user or session level would outweigh the benefits. Instead, the shared corporate network resources are funded as an overhead cost to the major functional organizations within DIGITAL. In this way, significant administrative costs associated with detailed usage billing are avoided, and at the same time, the notion of the network as a utility is fostered. Each individual user does not have to worry about justifying and paying for each use of the network.

DIGITAL works with major MIS and related groups in the implementation of network applications in order to ensure effective use of the network, and may adjust cost allocations periodically based on changes in the degree to which corporate organizations make demands on the network.

## Future Plans

The network growth is expected to continue with DIGITAL's business growth, both in the number of connected systems and in the amount of traffic carried. There are plans for substantial capacity upgrades and a refinement of the topology of the backbone layer. Over the next 12 to 18 months, there will be a significant emphasis on carrying both voice and data transmissions over common wideband media (a combination of leased T1, satellite, and DIGITAL-owned fiber optics) between many of DIGITAL's larger locations. This will:

—reduce “unit” transmission costs —provide better quality —give increased flexibility to respond to changing requirements

DIGITAL expects to provide a standardized service for the electronic transfer of information to and from systems outside of DIGITAL—systems owned by customers and suppliers.

## Conclusion

Increasingly, a company's communications networks are becoming the heartbeat that keeps the business growing and prospering. Communications must flow freely through the network so that all business functions can share accurate information. Groups such as manufacturing, sales, customer services, finance, order administration, materials management, and design engineering all need to work with timely and accurate information regardless of where in the world they are physically located. Time is a valuable asset in all organizations. Using it wisely results in getting products to market days, weeks, even months ahead of the competition, and results in increased market share. A delay could constitute failure to successfully enter the marketplace at all. A properly planned and managed corporate network utility can make the difference between success and failure. The processes described in this article, while tailored to DIGITAL's own environment, should be generally applicable to many large, forward-thinking organizations.
