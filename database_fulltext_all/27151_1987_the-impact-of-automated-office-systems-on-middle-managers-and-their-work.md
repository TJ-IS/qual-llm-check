# The Impact of Automated Office Systems on Middle Managers and Their Work

By: Zeeva Millman
McGill University
Montreal, Canada H3A IG5

By: Jon Hartwick
Associate Professor of
Organizational Behavior
Faculty of Management
McGill University
Montreal Canada H3A IG5

## Abstract

A survey of seventy-five Montreal middle managers was conducted, investigating their perceptions of the impact of automated office systems on their jobs and work. Two key findings emerged in the results. First, middle managers perceived that office automation had led to a variety of changes that, almost without exception, made their jobs and work more enriching and satisfying. Second, middle managers with first-hand experience with various systems, either through the presence of such systems in their organization or through their own personal use of such systems, were even more positive than managers without this exposure. The importance of these findings is discussed in the context of related work drawn from the fields of psychology and organizational behavior.

Keywords: Office automation, job and work design, job satisfaction

ACM Categories: A.1, C.4, K.4, K.6

## Introduction

In recent years, radical changes have been taking place in the office as various automated office systems and technologies have been introduced. Virtually every function has been affected. Clerical and secretarial operations have been altered as word processing and electronic information storage and retrieval systems have replaced the typewriter and filing cabinet. Management information and decision support systems have been implemented to aid in the decision-making and control processes of the organization. Even the normal modes of communication have been affected with the advent of such technologies as electronic mail and teleconferencing.

## Literature Review

## A priori claims and prophecies

A sizeable literature looks at the impact of such systems on individuals and their work. Much of this literature consists of a priori claims and prophecies, based on the personal experiences of experts and concerned parties. Interestingly, the experts seem to disagree about the effects of such systems on workers and their jobs $[3, 10]$ . At one extreme are those who see automation as having a negative impact on the workplace $[9, 27, 30]$ . They claim that automation robs work of its enriching elements. Skilled jobs are stripped of their conceptual content, tasks are fragmented, and work becomes routine. Power and decision making become centralized, leading to greater supervisory control and an accompanying loss of individual freedom and responsibility for one's work. In addition, the workplace becomes dehumanized as electronic communication systems replace face-to-face contact. Due to these changes, workers become dissatisfied, alienated and less motivated to perform.

At the other extreme are those who see automation as having various positive effect on the workplace $[1, 6, 16]$ . They claim that automation leads to the enrichment of jobs and work. Because routinized tasks are those that are typically automated, much of the drudgery and monotony in work is eliminated, allowing the individual greater time and ability to concentrate on complex conceptual and decision-making tasks. Micro and personal computers lead to the decentralization of information and decision making throughout the organization, thus allowing for greater personal freedom, responsibility and control. Finally, the workplace becomes more humanized as electronic communication systems make it possible to communicate with others more often and with those who are farther away. As a consequence, workers become more satisfied, motivated and productive.

## Research literature

Following a comprehensive review of the research literature, Attewell and Rule $[3]$ recently concluded that the evidence in support of one or the other of these extremes is fragmentary and mixed. (See $[2, 13, 30]$ for evidence of the negative impact of automated office systems and $[8, 22, 25]$ for evidence of the positive impact of such systems.) According to Attewell and Rule, the most plausible explanation for this is that both processes are occurring. In some organizations, for certain individuals and jobs, and with certain systems and technologies, automation is likely to have negative effects. For others, automation is likely to have positive effects. One goal for future research is to determine which of the two tendencies predominates. Moreover, since the occurrence of each process is likely to be influenced by a variety of individual, organizational and system variables, a second goal is to determine when and where each tendency is more apt to occur.

## Survey Considerations

This paper reports the results of a survey that was designed to answer two general questions.

What effects do workers see automated office systems having on their jobs and work?

Specifically, how do workers see such systems changing the nature of their jobs and work, their satisfaction, and their personal and organizational effectiveness? As noted earlier, past research provided fragmentary and mixed evidence concerning this question. However, as preliminary interviews were conducted in preparation for our survey, we were struck by the number of positive responses we encountered. As was expected, the workers in our survey reported that office automation had generally led to a variety of positive changes in their jobs and work, satisfaction, and personal and organizational effectiveness.

How does first-hand experience with various systems affect workers' perceptions?

Numerous studies suggest that personal experience or use of various systems and technologies is positively related to perceptions and evaluations of those systems and technologies $[4, 24, 28, 29, 32, 33, 36, 38]$ . Much less research relates personal experience or use to workers' perceptions of their jobs and work, satisfaction or perceptions of personal effectiveness on the job. However, we expected to find such perceptions to be positive for those workers who have had first-hand experience, either through the presence of various systems in their organization or through their own personal use of such systems.

The major considerations in designing the survey were who to poll, what systems to investigate, and what aspects of jobs and work to investigate. Each of these considerations will now be briefly discussed.

## Middle management perceptions

Since middle managers have often been identified as the workers likely to be most influenced by office automation $[13, 18, 25, 27]$ , they were chosen as targets for the survey. Because a major function of middle managers is to gather and analyze information, their work is apt to be strongly affected by the introduction of management information and decision support systems. Middle managers supervise and are supervised, so they are likely to experience the full impact of office automation on the communication and control processes of the organization. They also occupy a central position in the organizational hierarchy and are likely to be well aware of the impact of various changes on the productivity of the organization. Middle managers should therefore be able to give a clear picture of the changes resulting from the introduction of various automated office systems.

## Five automated office systems

Five different systems were chosen for investigation—the main frame computer, personal computers, word processing, electronic mail and teleconferencing. These systems are representative of the major systems being implemented in organizations today, spanning a wide range of different organizational functions. Main frame and personal computers can be used by middle managers to store and retrieve information, and to support and make decisions. There may, however, be substantial differences in the way each of these systems are utilized toward this end. For example, it is claimed that the main frame facilitates the centralization of information and decision making within the organization, while personal computers are likely to promote a decentralization of information and decision making. Other systems, such as electronic mail and teleconferencing, are likely to affect the ability and ease of communication, both within and outside of the organization. All of these systems may affect many different aspects of middle managers' work, but the biggest impact is likely to be on the supervisory and interpersonal dimensions of the job. Finally, word processing is also likely to have a substantial impact on the organization, but many middle managers choose not to personally use word processing systems, delegating their use to secretaries and other subordinates. As a consequence, they may see few changes in their own work as a result of the introduction of these systems.

## Perceptions of jobs and work

The introduction of automated office systems is likely to affect many different aspects of the middle manager's job and work. To determine the full range of potential changes, the job satisfaction [26, 35] and work design [19, 20, 21] literature within the field of organizational behavior were consulted. Research on job satisfaction has identified a number of key concerns for most workers—supervisory style and competence, pay and job security, promotional opportunities, relationships with coworkers, and the nature of the work itself. The work design literature elaborates on the latter concern, and identifies the major motivational characteristics of work—skill variety (including the number, variety and intrinsic interest of needed skills), task significance (the importance of one's job for the organization or for other individuals), autonomy (encompassing both freedom in when and how to do one's job and the responsibility for the results of one's work) and feedback (knowledge of how one is performing on the job). In the design of the questionnaire, questions were created to tap each of these concerns.

Finally, a number of questions were included to tap perceptions of personal and organizational effectiveness. In sum, the survey questions covered a wide range of perceptions. This being the case, it was hoped that a picture of the full impact of office automation on middle managers' jobs and work could be discerned from the results.

## Research Methodology Subjects and procedure

Questionnaires were sent to 151 middle managers from fourteen diverse organizations, all located in Montreal or the immediately surrounding areas. Included were the head offices of a conglomerate involved in the transportation industry (rail and air), a major airline, the local offices of an automobile manufacturer, a national railway, an oil company and several smaller local organizations. Contacts within each organization distributed the questionnaires. The experimenters emphasized to the contacts that they were to distribute questionnaires to all qualifying middle managers, defined as operational level managers with supervisory responsibility who have direct access to computers. From 5 to 50 questionnaires were distributed by each contact (depending on the size of the organization) though in four of the small organizations, questionnaires were mailed directly to the respondents.

Each respondent was guaranteed anonymity and provided with a stamped, pre-addressed envelope. Seventy-six of the questionnaires were returned for analysis (50.3% of those distributed)—a reasonable return rate for a survey of this type. All but one of the returned questionnaires was usable. Thus, a final sample of 75 middle managers was obtained and used in the analysis. An analysis of the stated characteristics of the respondents' organizations suggested that a reasonable dispersion of organizations was achieved.

The respondents ranged in age from 23 to 61 with a mean age of 35.6 years. 95% were male. 26% had a high school education; 49% had some university education or an undergraduate degree; a further 26% had at least some graduate training. Respondents had been in their organizations from 1 to 41 years with a mean tenure of 14.2 years. They came from a variety of functional areas—marketing and sales, 20%; budget, accounting and finance, 17%; systems services, 15%; business services, 9%; engineering, 9%; administrative services 8%; human resources, 5%; research and development, 4%, and others, 12%. 80% held staff positions and 20% held line positions. Finally, 21% indicated that they had no computer experience; 40% stated that they had less than five years experience, and 39% indicated more than five years experience with various systems. On the whole a diverse and representative sample of organizations and individuals seems to be included. Moreover, the sample includes a substantial number of individuals whose computer experience is recent or limited.

## Measures

Participants were given a questionnaire asking for their perceptions of various automated office systems and the effects of such systems on their jobs and work. They were first asked a number of questions about each of the five different systems—the main frame computer, personal computers, word processing, electronic mail and teleconferencing. They were first asked to indicate which of the systems were currently being used (a) in their company or organization, and (b) in their own department. Participants were then asked to indicate how often they used each system in their own department and answer on a three-point response scale (never, occasionally, frequently).

Participants were also asked to indicate how office automation had influenced them personally. Specifically, they were asked to state whether automation had increased, decreased, or had no effect on fifteen different aspects of their jobs and work. Eight of these questions were derived from Hackman and

Oldham's research [19, 20, 21] and dealt with various aspects of an individual's work (importance of the job, amount of work required on the job, accuracy demanded by the job, skill needed on the job, how interesting the work is, amount of freedom in how to do the job, degree of responsibility for the results of one's work, and knowledge of performance on the job). Four additional questions dealt with other job concerns detailed within the job satisfaction literature [26, 35]. The questions asked about the amount of supervision received on the job, job security and opportunities for advancement, and relationships with fellow employees. Finally, three questions asked about the perceived impact of automation on personal, departmental and organizational effectiveness.

## Results of the Survey

## System presence and use

Respondents were first asked to indicate which systems were currently being used in their organization and department. As can be seen in the first column of Table 1, each of our respondent's organizations was automated, at least to some degree. Virtually all of the organizations possessed main frames, personal computers and word processing. Electronic mail was less prevalent, but still present in most organizations, and teleconferencing was the least common system—only a third of our respondents reported that their organization used teleconferencing. As a second column of Table 1 shows, each of the systems was less likely to be found in a respondent's own department. However, a sizeable presence for each of the systems still remained. Because many of our respondents came from the same departments and organizations, the data in Table 1 should be viewed with some caution. Still, the data suggest that automated office systems have become the norm, not the exception, in today's organizations.

Respondents were also asked to indicate whether they were using each of the systems. Table 2 presents the percentage of our respondents indicating that they are using a particular system (either occasionally or frequently). However, as noted above, many of our respondents do not have one or more of the systems in their department or organization. To see how this affects usage, percentages were calculated using only those respondents who have a particular system in their department. These data are presented in the second column of Table 2. As Table 2 shows, there seems to be a good deal of usage of each of the systems. More than half of the sample indicated that they are personally using the main frame and personal computers. 40% use electronic mail. Reported use of each of these three systems jumps to more than 75% when such systems are present in the respondent's department, thus providing both greater opportunity and more convenient access. In contrast to these results, far fewer middle managers are using word processing and teleconferencing. Even when these systems are present in their departments, less than half of our respondents report using them. Overall, two factors seem to be influencing system usage.

## Perceived changes in jobs and work

In the second part of the questionnaire, respondents were asked to indicate whether automation had increased, decreased, or had no effect on fifteen different aspects of their own jobs and work. These responses are summarized in Table 3. The middle managers in the sample, if they see any changes at all, perceive that automation results in favorable changes to their jobs and work.

Work seems to have become more demanding — there is more of it and it seems to require greater skill and accuracy. However, middle managers seem to like this change, stating that their work has become more interesting and important. These results are consistent with those of previous surveys $[8, 22, 25]$ . The respondents also claim that they have more freedom in performing their jobs and greater responsibility for the results of their work. Finally, feedback seems to have improved, since they claim to have greater knowledge of their job performance. In virtually every respect, the respondents seem to be stating that automation has improved the nature of their work.

## Influence of first-hand experience

To a great extent, perceptions are likely the result of first-hand experience with different automated office systems. A number of multivariate analyses of variance were conducted to determine the impact of first-hand experience. As a statistical tool, the MANOVA tests whether one (or more) independent variable has a significant impact on a set of dependent variables. Responses to the fifteen questions in Table 3 were placed on a three-point scale (with a decrease designated “-1”, no change “0”, and an increase designated “+1”). These were used as the dependent variables in the analyses. Each MANOVA was tested to see whether or not a given independent variable had a significant overall effect on the middle managers’ perceptions of change. When a significant overall effect was found, separate univariate F-tests were conducted to determine what specific aspects of their jobs and work were most affected.

## Effects of system presence

It was expected that middle managers who had various systems in their organization would see the general benefits of systems and, as a consequence, be more likely to claim that automation led to a number of positive changes in their jobs and work. This prediction was only partially supported. As expected, the presence of personal computers and word processing resulted in changes in the middle managers' perceptions (multivariate Fs (15,57) = 2.41 and 3.83, respectively; ps < .01 and .001). Mean differences and univariate Fs for these effects are shown on Table 4. As can be seen, the middle managers from organizations with personal computers and word processing were more likely to claim that office automation had increased the importance of their job, their job security and opportunities for advancement, and their personal and departmental effectiveness. For the most part these are general, evaluative concerns. It is interesting to note, however, that the middle managers' perceptions of more specific aspects of their work, supervision and relationships do not seem to be significantly affected by the introduction of these systems. This suggests that the managers were pleased with the introduction of these systems to their organization and perhaps became more satisfied with their jobs as a result, yet few saw concrete changes resulting from their introduction, either to themselves or their work.

## Effects of personal use

Direct contact through personal use of the system is likely to have a greater impact. As was expected, middle managers who used the main frame and personal computer reported a number of positive changes resulting from the introduction of automated office systems (multivariate $F_{s}(30.74) = 1.66$ and 1.82, respectively; ps < .05). These changes are detailed in Table 5. Middle managers who personally use both the main frame and the personal computer were more likely to claim that automation had increased their personal effectiveness, made their work more interesting, and given them more job security. Beyond this, however, the claims diverge. Those who used the main frame were more likely to report that automation had increased the demands for skill and accuracy on the job and made them more personally responsible for the results of their work. In contrast, those who used the personal computer were more likely to claim that automation had increased both their personal freedom and the importance of their job. While some of the details vary, the users of each system seem to be impressed with the changes resulting from office automation.

## Discussion

There are two key findings in the present results. First, middle managers in the survey perceive that office automation has led to a variety of changes in their jobs and work. Almost without exception these changes are seen to be in the direction of making their jobs and work more enriching and satisfying. Second, middle managers with first-hand experience with various systems, either through the presence of systems in their organizations or through their own personal use of systems, were more likely to report these changes than those without first-hand experience. Each of these findings will now be discussed in greater detail.

## The enrichment of jobs and work

Office automation has made work more demanding—there is more of it and it requires greater individual skill and accuracy. However, respondents are pleased with these changes, for they claim that their jobs have become more important and interesting. In addition, our middle managers believe that office automation has given them increased autonomy. They state that they now have more freedom in how to do their job and greater responsibility for the results of their work. They also claim that they now have greater knowledge of the results of their work, which suggests that feedback has improved. As a whole, these changes suggest an enrichment of their jobs and work. Each of the three psychological components of Hackman and Oldham's theory of work design seem to be positively affected. These perceptions are likely to be beneficial to the organization. Evidence suggests that such perceptions are associated with increases in individual motivation and satisfaction, in addition to decreases in absenteeism and turnover [5, 11, 12, 17, 20, 21].

The middle managers claim that they now have more job security and increased promotional opportunities. While they do not perceive any great change in the amount of supervision they receive, the perceived increases in personal autonomy and feedback could lead to an increase in the perceived quality of their supervision. Finally, they claim that relationships with fellow workers are unchanged, or even slightly improved. These changes and those found in the nature of their work should result in an increase in middle managers' job satisfaction, an increase that they attribute to the introduction of various automated office systems. There is likely to be a decrease in absenteeism and turnover [26, 34, 37] and an improvement in productivity [23] as a result of this increase in satisfaction. Consistent with this argument, our middle managers claim that automation has indeed led to increases in productivity, both in their own work, and in the work of their individual departments and organizations.

These findings concur with experts [1, 6, 16] and researchers [8, 22, 25] who have suggested that automation leads to the enrichment of jobs and work. In their review of the relevant research literature, Attwell and Rule concluded that surveys “generally contradict the deskilling/job degradation hypothesis. Most workers . . . regard the new technologies in a positive light” [3, p. 1187]. Much of the negative evidence concerning the impact of automation is in the form of personal experience and individual case studies [2, 9, 13, 27, 30, 39]. The sheer number of such reports suggests that automation can indeed have a negative impact on jobs and work, but these would seem to be exceptions, occurring only for a minority of jobs or individuals.

## First-hand experience and perceptions of change

It was found that middle managers who have first-hand experience with various systems, either through the presence of systems in their organization or through their own personal use of the systems, were more likely to report positive changes in their jobs and work. This was especially true for those with a main frame, personal computer, or word processing. The middle managers who have these systems in their organizations were more likely to report that automation had increased the importance of their jobs, job security and opportunities for advancement, and personal and departmental effectiveness. These reports probably reflect true changes that they see occurring in their organizations, both to themselves and to others, and they reinforce claims that automation leads to general improvements in the workplace. Such changes are likely to lead to an increase in the middle managers' general satisfaction with their jobs. The mere presence of systems does not appear to change the middle managers' perceptions of the work they do, but the personal use of systems was associated with a number of perceived changes in the nature of the middle managers' work. Users were more likely to report that automation had improved their personal productivity and made their work more interesting. As one might expect, there were also a number of specific changes associated with the use of different systems. Users of the main frame were more likely to report that automation had led to increased demands in their work, and that their jobs now require more skill and greater accuracy. Because they also state that their work is more interesting, we suspect that they do not mind this increased demand. While counterintuitive, such findings have been reported in the past $[8, 22, 25]$ . In addition, the middle managers claim that they are now held more personally responsible for the results of their work. This perception is consistent with the greater centralization of information and control that is often believed to accompany automation $[2, 27, 39]$ . In contrast, users of the personal computer were more likely to report that automation had given them greater freedom in how to do their jobs and made their jobs seem more important.

Compared to non-users, the users of various systems were more likely to claim that automation has made their work more enriching and satisfying. However, this relationship between individual perceptions and use is correlated. Consequently, a number of different causal mechanisms may potentially explain the data.

It is possible that use leads to such perceptions. Perhaps middle managers who use different systems see the effects of this use, both on themselves and their jobs, and develop beliefs about the impact of automation as a result of personal experiences. This sort of process underlies a good deal of the work explaining how individuals initially form beliefs and attitudes toward different objects and events $[15]$ . If this process is valid it suggests that the middle managers' perceptions are likely to reflect reality, and thus provides evidence for there being positive effects from the introduction of automated office systems. There may be another explanation, however, as both the cognitive dissonance and self-perception theories of attitudes suggest that individuals form attitudes and beliefs in order to rationalize or explain their behavior $[7, 14]$ . The users of different systems may therefore develop perceptions to explain or justify this use, both to themselves and to others. If this is true, it is possible that their perceptions do not actually reflect reality. In practice, it may not be important to know which of these processes provides the best explanation. Assuming that, for either reason, usage leads to the development of perceptions, the key is to introduce and encourage the use of various systems. As systems are used, one is likely to develop a variety of positive perceptions of the changes in one's job and work. In turn, these perceptions are likely to lead to increased satisfaction and productivity in the workplace.

Another general possibility is that the perceptions lead to use. Most attitude and expectancy theories of individual motivation [15, 37] suggest that an individual's beliefs and attitudes are a major causal determinant of his or her behavior. Middle managers who believe that automation has positively affected their jobs and work may, for this reason, choose and begin to use the different automated office systems that are available. This possibility has an implication for management practice. It suggests that one should make use of the present survey results, and those of others [8, 22, 25] to convince new and potential users of the many positive changes that they can expect in their jobs and work when they use the various systems made available to them. Once convinced, these individuals may personally choose to use the systems.

Current evidence in the psychological literature suggests that all of these processes are apt to be present [15]. This suggests that the process of introducing and using various automated office systems is likely to have a variety of direct effects—due to the greater efficiency and effectiveness of the systems themselves—and indirect effects—due to the greater enrichment and satisfaction of jobs and work—on middle managers and their jobs and work.
