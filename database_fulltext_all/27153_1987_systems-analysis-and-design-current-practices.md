## Abstract

The process to develop computer-based information systems has changed significantly within the last ten years. Research was conducted to determine how organizations currently perform systems analysis and design. Responding organizations provided evaluations of their current approaches and tools. The results should provide insights for information systems practitioners and have implications for educational efforts and research in the information systems field.

Keywords: systems development methods, systems development life cycle, traditional/classical approach, structured methods, automated tools, prototyping, information centers.

ACM Categories: D.2.1, D.2.2, K.3.2, K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

Organizations have been using computers for over thirty years to perform data processing tasks and to develop information to assist with decision making. Many different tools, techniques, and approaches have been used to develop computer-based information systems (CBIS) during this period of time. There has been a continuing need to improve the process for developing these systems. The steady, dramatic improvements in hardware technology in terms of improved capabilities and lower costs have served to heighten the need to improve the software development process.

One of the first major studies of the use of computers in business organizations was performed by the McKinsey consulting firm in 1968. The major conclusion of this study was that two-thirds of the organizations were considered to be unsuccessful computer users because of the lack of diversified computer applications throughout their organizations and because the upper management in these organizations considered the use of the computer to not be cost-justified [27]. This early study seemed to indicate that the development of CBIS in organizations could be fraught with problems.

Furthermore, the management information systems (MIS) literature of the past twenty year contains many examples of projects that were unsuccessful in developing CBIS in organizations for reasons such as:

1. The system developed did not meet the user's requirements.

2. The system developed exceeded the estimated time schedule.

3. The cost of the system developed was too great compared to the value of the benefits realized.

4. The system developed was excessively difficult and costly to maintain [8, 11, 28].

Therefore, a research study was considered necessary to attempt to determine how organizations are currently developing CBIS and to describe their relative satisfaction with the use of the available tools, techniques, and approaches. A questionnaire was constructed to collect data about how systems analysis and design is being performed in organizations and to learn what types of results are being achieved. This paper was written to present the findings of this research effort to MIS practitioners and MIS academics.

The paper begins with an overview of what is believed to be the major approaches available today to develop CBIS. Next, the research that was performed is described. Findings are presented that describe the extent and the manner in which the various approaches to develop CBIS are being used by organizations. Evaluations express the degree of satisfaction with the tools and/or documentation aids considered appropriate for use in a specific approach, and interpret the overall results being achieved from the approaches currently used to develop CBIS. The paper concludes with recommendations for ways to improve the processes for developing CBIS in organizations in the future.

## Overview of CBIS Approaches

## General

Many approaches have been used over the years to develop CBIS in organizations. Early efforts followed the systems development life cycle and the traditional/classical approach to systems analysis and design. Within the last ten years, the structured approach to systems analysis and design has emerged to address CBIS problems. The traditional/classical approach and the structured approach have also been commonly combined with the systems development life cycle approach $[4, 25]$ . Additionally, automated aids, prototyping, and information centers have been used to complement these other approaches for developing CBIS in organizations. The following section presents a brief overview of each of these approaches.

## Traditional/classical approach

The traditional/classical approach to systems analysis and design can be described in terms of how the products of the phases of the systems development life cycle are documented. The primary traditional systems documentation tools are narrative descriptions, system flowcharts, program flowcharts, file layouts, and input/output layouts. Colter has categorized these tools as belonging to first generation analysis techniques [6], and Couger has described the diagramming techniques in terms of their evolution and major characteristics [8].

Prominent textbooks in the MIS field that were published in the early 1970s advocated the use of these documentation tools [4, 10, 21]. Some information systems textbooks that were published in the early 1980s continued to provide many examples of systems documentation which utilized these traditional tools [7, 26]. DeMarco published a text that could be used in systems analysis and design classes that emphasized the use of structured methods [11].

## Systems development life cycle approach

The systems development life cycle (SDLC) approach can be described in general terms as a way to break a systems project into phases, activities, and tasks to accomplish the overall work in the project. SDLC enables systems projects to be planned and controlled in a business-like manner to ensure that users and managers get the systems they want in a reasonable period of time and at acceptable costs. It is believed that the SDLC approach is widely used, but that a standard way to break systems projects into phases does not prevail. Murdick explored this variability in the phases of the systems development life cycle in an early paper which listed seventeen different systems development life cycles published by various information systems authors $[23]$ .

Current books which describe the process of systems analysis and design continue to advocate their own systems development life cycle phases $[8, 9, 25]$ . Although a standard systems development life cycle may not exist, it is believed that this approach is well suited for use in the development of CBIS and that the specific phases, activities, and tasks should depend on the types of systems project and the information systems environment in the organization. Standards do not exist for the amount of project time and resources that should be applied to the various project phases. However, it is generally stated that when structured methods are used in systems analysis and design, the front-end phases of the systems development life cycle should consume more time and resources than when the traditional/classical approach is used [30].

The need to manage the process for building CBIS prompted some organizations in the 1970s to market products termed systems development methodologies (SDM). These packages generally prescribe the use of a specific set of phases in the systems development life cycle. The SDM product includes a description of the tasks to be performed in each activity within a phase, the content of end products to be delivered at the end of an activity and/or phase, and the formats for all the program, system, and user documentation considered necessary [22]. The early SDM packages (also known as project management systems) could be used to complement the traditional/classical approach to develop CBIS [3]. More recently, some SDM products have been developed which integrate the use of the structured methods with the management of the systems development life cycle [5].

## Structured approach

The structured approach to systems analysis and design can be described in terms of concepts and tools. The main concepts in the structured approach are to decompose system complexity and to describe systems in terms of data flow diagrams, structure charts, data dictionaries, and process descriptions. Systems are analyzed and designed from the abstract level to the detailed level. Data flow diagrams and structure charts are developed for each level into which a system is partitioned. These diagrams are supported by a data dictionary which describes data stores, data flows, and data elements and by process descriptions written primarily in structured English or pseudocode. The use of traditional systems documentation tools is minimized.

Some of the early books which advocated the structured approach to systems analysis and design were written by Yourdon, DeMarco, Gane and Sarson, and Orr [11, 17, 24, 30]. These books were supplemented with seminars and training sessions for organizations that desired to use the structured approach. By the mid-1980s, textbooks had appeared that presented the structured approach to systems analysis and design $[15, 25, 29]$ . Thus, it is believed that in the last five to seven years some organizations have been developing CBIS using structured analysis and structured design techniques. It has been difficult to determine how many organizations have moved from the traditional/classical approach to systems analysis and design to the structured approach.

## Automated approaches

Automated approaches to systems analysis and design have been developed to support the work performed by systems analysts in the development of CBIS. Since many analysis and design tasks are done manually in the traditional/classical approach, and the structured approach the need for computer-assisted analysis and design tools has long been recognized. Couger describes some of these early tools, such as decision table processors and techniques that supported system definition and specification $[8]$ .

Concurrent with the development of structured methods, tools have been developed to support many of the tasks performed in systems analysis and design. Software packages such as accelerator claim to offer the capabilities required to fully support the design and documentation of a software system. The package supports the development and maintenance of data flow diagrams, structure charts, and other graphical techniques. It includes the development of a related data dictionary and various analysis tools to assist in ensuring that such documentation is correct, complete, and consistent [1]. Packages of this nature support the concept of a systems analyst workbench that is computer-based to support systems analysis and design tasks, and which provides systems documentation in narrative and graphic form that is computer-accessible.

Many of the tools that have been developed to provide computer assistance to a systems analyst are geared to a specific type of task or activity. However, broader-based techniques have also been developed, such as program generators and system generators. Jenkins identifies and describes many software products of this type that are available today [20]. Systems such as ISDOS and PLEXSYS, which have been under development for several years at the University of Michigan and the University of Arizona respectively, are attempting to integrate and automate all the phases of the systems development life cycle [8]. In addition, the Nastec Corporation currently markets the CASE system, which purportedly consists of the hardware and software necessary to support a systems analyst throughout the systems development life cycle. The CASE system is described as being capable of supporting the implementation of the principles of software engineering. Through the use of CASE, the overall process for the development of computer software is better managed, resulting in improved productivity for software developers and a higher quality software product [5].

## Prototyping approach

The prototyping approach to systems analysis and design is characterized by the development of an initial model of the desired CBIS early in the systems development life cycle. Edelman described the prototyping approach in terms of the following four steps: users provide a general specification, system designers develop a prototype, users try it out, and system designers modify the system to meet the users' detailed specifications $[14]$ . As Jenkins states, the prototyping approach is built upon the simple proposition that users can tell you what they want changed in an existing information system more easily than they can tell you what they want to be developed in an information system $[19]$ . The prototyping approach primarily addresses the problems of defining user requirements completely and correctly, reducing the time required to develop information systems, and reducing the maintenance associated with information systems because of changing user requirements. The main issues surrounding the prototyping approach to systems analysis and design appear to be how to relate it to the systems development life cycle, how to tell the types of systems for which the approach is most applicable, and the types of results and potential problems when the approach is used.

## Information center approach

The information center approach to the development of CBIS in organizations by the users of these systems is reputed to have been originated by IBM Canada to address the application development and maintenance backlogs which exist in many computer-using organizations [13]. They recommended that a staff of data processing specialists be created to assist users with developing their own computer applications to satisfy their unmet information needs. The main technologies supporting this concept are fourth generation/end-user languages and personal computers.

Information centers can complement the efforts of the traditional data processing department, reduce the systems development and maintenance effort, and control the proliferation of personal computers in organizations [3]. The principal issues in the use of information centers in organizations seem to be: the relationship of the information center to the traditional data processing department, the types of applications appropriate for user development, and the responsibilities of the information center staff and the users for the developed systems.

## The Research

## Purpose of the research

Over the last ten years, the content of systems analysis and design courses in colleges and universities has changed significantly. For example, Burch and Strater's 1974 text, Information Systems: Theory and Practice, emphasizes the traditional/classical approach to systems analysis and design using the systems development life cycle [4]. But in the fourth edition of this text, published in 1986, these concepts are supplemented with presentations on structured analysis and design, automated aids, prototyping, and information centers [3].

Instructors of systems analysis and design courses have had to change their approaches to teaching these courses to remain current. Instructors of these types of courses, as well as MIS practitioners, should be concerned with the relevance of the material presented in these courses. There are various ways to ensure that what you are teaching is still relevant; staying abreast of the current literature, consulting with organizations, and performing appropriate research. Therefore, the primary purpose of this research effort was to determine how organizations currently are performing systems analysis and design.

## Research methodology

It was decided to develop an extensive questionnaire (20 pages) to survey computer users about their approaches to systems analysis and design. The primary purpose of the research was to collect data about the approach used, the tools used in the approach, the results being achieved, and the key factors to develop improved CBIS. Questionnaires were sent to computer users throughout the United States. The Directory of Top Computer Executives, Fall 1984 was used primarily to select the organizations to receive the questionnaires.[12].

Ninety-seven usable questionnaires were returned and provided the data which are presented and interpreted in this paper. Table 1 provides a summary profile of the diverse organizations which responded to the survey. Because of the volume of data collected, only a summary of the more important results will be presented in this paper.

## The Findings

## General

As expected, the traditional/classical approach to systems analysis and design and the systems development life cycle was reported to be widely used by the respondents. It was somewhat surprising to learn that the structured approach to systems analysis and design was used as frequently as was reported. The relatively newer approaches were reported as being less widely used, except for information centers. Table 2 provides a summary of the six approaches to develop CBIS that were identified on the questionnaire. It is interesting to note that nine respondents reported using approaches not among these six but upon analysis, each of their approaches can be considered a variation of one of the six approaches.

The following section of the paper presents the major results of the research. Summarized data are presented for each of the six approaches to develop CBIS. Summarized data are also provided which pertain to the results being achieved from the use of these approaches and the key factors for the development of improved CBIS. A brief discussion and interpretation of the more significant results is also provided. It should be noted that some respondents did not complete all the appropriate sections of the questionnaire.

Some of the interesting observations based on this data relate to the amount of time allocated to the various project phases. Phase 3, detailed design, which includes program design, coding, and testing still accounts for a significant portion of project time. Authors such as Couger, Colter, and Knapp have suggested that the phases prior to programming should be increasing in costs and that the costs associated with programming and testing would be lower [8]. It appears that the organizations that participated in this survey have not yet begun to place the proper emphasis on the front-end phases of the systems development life cycle because the time being allocated to them is relatively low. It is also discouraging to note that the project review phase is used by a low number of the respondents. Many organizations may not be using the review phase as a means to improve the overall process for developing CBIS in their organizations.

## Automated approaches

Organizations that use automated approaches to support systems analysis and design (29%) were requested to evaluate specific automated techniques based on their experiences. Table 8 presents a summary of the results of this evaluation. The respondents were, in general, not familiar with such comprehensive techniques as the Problem Statement Language/Problem Statement Analyzer (PSL/PSA) or the Software Requirements Engineering Methodology (SREM).

Application generators and/or program generators have been used with good results by about one-fifth of the respondents and a comparable number are considering using them. Over one-fourth of the respondents use a package to support the development of data flow diagrams and/or structure charts. These results provide a clear indication that the previously manual process of systems analysis and design is changing. It appears that the systems analysis and design of future CBIS will be increasingly automated.

Furthermore, many respondents are using other automated tools to support the development of CBIS in their organizations. Packages that facilitate the development of data dictionaries, screen designs, and report designs are being very widely used and with favorable results. Moreover, packages that support documentation and the overall process of project management are becoming increasingly widely used.

## Key factors to develop improved CBIS

The organizations participating in the survey were asked what they considered to be the most important key factors for them to develop improved CBIS in the future. Table 13 presents a summary of what the respondents believe these key factors to be. It is revealing that the factors which are considered to be the most important are improved management support and involvement, improved user involvement, and better trained user and management personnel. These factors have long been considered essential to the successful development of CBIS in organizations [2, 18]. Some of the more recent literature continues to emphasize the importance of these management and user-oriented considerations [16, 22]. It is important to recognize that even though the approaches to develop CBIS are becoming more sophisticated, the quality of management and user participation continues to be a very important factor for successful development of CBIS in organizations.

The technical factors that are considered to be the most important to develop improved CBIS are: techniques to shorten the systems development life cycle and systems development methods which integrate the available techniques and tools. Since the pressure to reduce the time necessary to develop CBIS has existed for many years, the goal is to shorten the development life cycle, but still develop systems that are useful and maintainable.

Apparently, many respondents have recognized that a major need exists to use a systems development method which integrates the available techniques and tools. Couger suggests that fifth generation development techniques for CBIS will achieve greater linkage and more automation of the systems development life cycle [8]. This seems to be a goal of many organizations who are developing CBIS today.

## Conclusions

## Current practices

The results of the research strongly suggest that organizations today are using a combination of approaches to develop their CBIS. Within these approaches they are also using a mixture of techniques and tools. The clear trends appear to be that:

1. Organizations will continue to develop their CBIS within the framework of a systems development life cycle.

2. Structured approaches will increasingly supplement traditional/classical approaches to develop CBIS.

## What is needed

What is needed to improve the processes for developing CBIS in organizations in the future can be described in terms of what MIS practitioners need to do and what MIS academics/researchers need to do.

MIS practitioners should view the process to develop CBIS in their organizations as they would any other systems project. Therefore, there is a need to formally describe the current process to develop CBIS in an organization, identify and evaluate alternative approaches to develop CBIS, plan for the improvement of the process to develop CBIS in the organization, and review the results being achieved to modify the plan accordingly. Unfortunately, the development of CBIS in many organizations today appears to be more of an art than a science, despite the emergence of the principles of software engineering, integrated SDM packages, and automated aids for systems analysis and design. However, organizations can evolve toward improved procedures to develop CBIS by following the guidelines suggested below.

MIS practitioners can describe the current process to develop CBIS in their organization by developing appropriate procedure/standards manuals, and by ensuring that the review phase for each CBIS development project is formally conducted. Alternative approaches, techniques, and tools to use to develop CBIS can be identified by the usual ways to stay current in the MIS field, such as reviewing the current literature and attending relevant conferences and seminars. The alternative approaches, techniques, and tools identified need to be evaluated formally in terms of their relative advantages and disadvantages and other appropriate cost-justification techniques.

The plan that an MIS practitioner develops to improve the process to develop CBIS in their organization should include pilot projects when new approaches, techniques, or tools are to be used in their organization. Guidelines should be developed for the systems analysts in the organization to provide direction for the use and integration of the various approaches, techniques, and tools which the organization selects for its systems development process. The formal review of pilot projects and the ongoing review phase for each

CBIS development project should provide the evaluations necessary to modify the plan to improve the process to develop CBIS in the organization. In the near future, MIS practitioners will need to continue to choose from the available approaches, techniques, and tools which support the development of CBIS. They will need to select the combination that best suits the needs of their organization and then to continually evaluate new developments in light of their current process for systems development.

MIS researchers need to direct their research efforts to support the major needs of MIS practitioners who are seeking to improve the process for developing CBIS in their organizations such as those identified in this research: the need for techniques to shorten the systems development life cycle and the need for systems development methods which integrate the available techniques and tools. Automated approaches, the prototyping approach, and the information center approach appear to have the potential to shorten the systems development life cycle. Therefore, MIS research should continue to be directed to these approaches and their relevant techniques and tools. MIS practitioners need guidance on when to use these approaches, techniques, and tools and how to best integrate them into their process to develop CBIS.

MIS academics that teach a systems analysis and design course should recognize that all six of the approaches to develop CBIS that are described in this paper are currently being used and have their own set of relative advantages and disadvantages. Therefore, systems analysis and design courses should include appropriate material about all of these approaches. Furthermore, these courses should continue to emphasize the importance of meaningful management support and user involvement in the development of CBIS.
