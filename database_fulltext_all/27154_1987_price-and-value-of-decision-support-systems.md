# Price and Value of Decision Support Systems

By: Dan R. Pieptea
Evan Anderson

University of Texas at Dallas
School of Management

## Abstract

A two-dimensional framework for Decision Support Systems (DSS) cost-benefit analysis is proposed. One dimension reflects the degree to which the supported decision is structured, the level of managerial activity, the level of uncertainty and the source of information used, while the second dimension classifies DSS based on the phase of the supported decision according to Simon's model for the decision-making process. The review of the current literature reconciles some of the contradictory findings in the DSS cost-benefit literature, and shows that the adequacy of the valuation method depends on the attributes of the system. The main DSS valuation issues are identified, and the paper discusses the potential gap between price (determined by estimated costs or the market) and the value (which is subjective). The magnitude of this gap is found to be correlated with the extent to which the supported decisions are structured.

Keywords: Decision support systems, selection, pricing, valuation, cost-benefit analysis

ACM Categories: K.6.2, K.6.3

## Introduction

The literature on cost-benefit analysis of DSS is in a state of controversy. At one end of the spectrum, authors such as Hennen et al. [16], Lembersky and Chi [24], and Boykin and Levary [8] find traditional cost-benefit analysis a useful method for justification of DSS projects, while other authors, such as Keen [21], propose alternative methods, such as value-based techniques. McLean and Riesing [28] take an even more extreme view and note that DSS are discretionary in character and have no justification or right to exist beyond the user's ability and desire to use them. This paper analyzes the main factors that make valuation of DSS different from the rest of MIS, develops a unitary structure of reference, and reconciles opposing views in the literature by showing that DSS evaluation techniques are context dependent.

The importance of Decision Support Systems (DSS) evaluation can not be overemphasized. Managers and policy makers are confronted with a wide range of choices of DSS or tools to develop DSS. Choosing the right DSS software can make an important impact on the overall decision-making process of the organization. DSS has evolved naturally from traditional Management Information Systems (MIS). There is a standard practice across the industry to employ traditional cost-benefit analysis techniques for justification and undertaking of DSS development projects. [38]. However, DSS has peculiarities that make it unique among the rest of MIS, and that have compelled some authors to adopt alternative evaluation techniques that differ from traditional cost-benefit analysis. Keen [21] proposes value analysis as an alternative to cost-benefit analysis for DSS project estimation. In a study of eighteen implemented decision support systems, Hogue and Watson [17] found that in only 23% of the studied cases had any kind of cost-benefit analysis been performed as a basis for evaluation of DSS project desirability. Keen found that only 13% of the cases performed traditional cost-benefit analysis. This paper reconciles contrasting valuation methods for DSS, which have been described in the literature, and asserts that the adequacy of a valuation method can be assessed only by taking into consideration the attributes of the system.

A necessary condition for selection of a DSS is that the value exceeds the price. However, because the value of a DSS is often subjective, the value-price relationship becomes hard to assess. Little attention has been devoted so far in the literature to this relationship. The present paper fills this gap in the literature by focusing on those attributes that impact the evaluation process.

Traditional cost-benefit analysis has two components: 1) cost analysis and 2) value estimation, which are based upon a capital budgeting technique, such as Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Accounting Rate of Return (ARR), or Payback Period Method. Costs and benefits are compared mostly in monetary terms. However, in the case of DSS this approach is not always well-suited. Benefits provided by DSS are often qualitative, rather than quantitative.

The costs must be assessed within the framework of the system development life cycle. The DSS life cycle is fundamentally different from that of other classes of computerized management information systems, and management participation in the early stages of DSS design has an increased importance. Interviews conducted by the analyst often force user management to reflect on the mechanics of the decision-making process. The analyst-manager dialogue, motivated by the design of a new DSS, can add insight to the decision-making process. Testing the system with live data can bring more insight into the decision-making process and even influence it. Thus, benefits can be derived somewhat earlier than expected, and implementation can begin while still in the development phase. On the other hand, sometimes the DSS is continuously upgraded throughout its life cycle, in which case development does not end prior to termination of the system. Typically, the discontinuance of DSS is not accompanied by replacement of the system with another one, while in the case of other MIS not only are old systems replaced, but there is also a transition period during which the old system is phased out.

These are aspects which differentiate the DSS development life cycle and increase the uncertainty of the evaluation process. While costs are predictable with a higher degree of certainty and are more quantifiable than value, there is a potential gap between price and the value of DSS. We assert that the price-value gap is larger for systems aiding highly unstructured decisions in the choice phase. The literature review brings empirical evidence to support our assertions.

## Structure of Reference

To evaluate the relationship between value and price of a DSS we adopt as structure of reference a two-dimensional space in which DSS can be represented. One dimension represents the phase of the decision-making process aided by the system (i.e., intelligence, design or choice), while the second dimension introduces a scale on which distinctive qualities of the supported decision are measured (i.e., degree of structure, managerial level, level of uncertainty and source of information). Grading a particular system on a continuous scale for each of these characteristics is a subjective matter. For this reason the second dimension has robust categories that aggregate a set of attributes into three classes.

## Dimension I: the supported phase of the decision-making process

In developing the first dimension of the framework, we apply to DSS the decision-making Model proposed by Simon [35] and Newell and Simon [31]. The phase of the decision-making process in which a DSS intervenes is an important system attribute, and we adopt it as the first dimension of the proposed classification space. Human decision-making is depicted as a three-stage process: intelligence, design and choice.

Intelligence corresponds to identification of problems and conditions calling for decisions. This phase refers to detecting anything that is deviant, that is, anything that does not match a predetermined plan, norm or standard. For example, a manager of a warehouse identifies a stock that drops below the order point. The basic purpose of the intelligence stage is to acquire knowledge of what is going on in the environment. DSS for this stage provides the decision maker with integrated, well-analyzed and formatted data. To this category belong DSS applications that create trend reports, exception reports and ad-hoc inquiries. Although some of these applications can sometimes be classified as Transaction Processing Systems (TPS), they belong to the DSS class to the extent to which they support the decision-making process. While not all of the exception or trend reports are intended to support the decision-making process, the initiation of some decisions, such as action against delinquent accounts or decisions to audit IRS returns, are inseparable from the computer-aided method of identifying the exception [38]. Expert systems, such as MYCIN (used for diagnosis in medicine), help identify the nature of a problem and thus aid the decision-making process in its intelligence phase.

The Design activity invents solutions to the problem. During this phase, quantitative techniques and design tools are frequently used. Each prospective solution is examined, and the decision maker is provided with the pros and cons for each. In this state it is assumed that all the relevant data are accessible for further analysis, and DSS will typically incorporate planning or forecasting models. Artificial intelligence applications and optimization techniques, such as linear, large scale and dynamic programming, and networks and statistical models, are used to aid this phase of the decision-making process. More computer support is now being developed for this phase in the form of expert systems. Expert systems avoid blind search by using rules to reason that make use of expert knowledge stored in the computer, and by constructing inference paths from this reasoning to generate problem solutions. Successful expert systems, such as SMP, ART, and MACSYMA, have been built for mathematics, along with PROSPECTOR for mineral exploration, DENDRAL for chemistry, and PUFF for medicine.

Choice involves the selection of a particular course of action. This phase is more complex due to difficulties such as multipreference, uncertainty, conflict of interest, and control. DSS, serving as a tool to support this phase of the decision-making process, will help the decision maker, but will not replace human choice. DSS provides three types of information in this stage: 1) highlights of the various proposed solutions, 2) possible scenarios of outcomes if the solution is adopted—the so called “what-if” systems, and 3) feedback information to help implementation of the adopted alternative.

## Dimension II: decision classes

Classification criteria for the decision-making process has been developed in the literature independently from the study of DSS. Among the most relevant categories are those defined by: 1) degree of decision structure, 2) level of managerial activity, 3) degree of uncertainty, and 4) source of information used.

Degree of decision structure was first formally defined by Simon [35] where he distinguishes between two types of decisions: programmed and unprogrammed. In later literature these concepts were extended to include a continuous spectrum of decisions, from structured to unstructured. A structured decision is today regarded as one which can be described by the steps of a highly quantitative procedure, while an unstructured decision is based on heuristic methods—experience and intuition.

Level of managerial activity was introduced by Anthony [4], as a classification criterion for decision activities. He identified three levels of managerial activity: 1) Strategic planning (SP) requires deciding on the objectives of the organization, the resources used to attain these objectives, and the policies for governing the use of these resources, 2) Management control (MC) assures that resources are used effectively to attain the organization's objectives, and 3) Organizational control (OC) assures that specific tasks are carried out properly. The three managerial levels SP, MC and OC correspond to top management, middle management and supervisory level, respectively.

Degree of uncertainty is another classification of decisions. Ahituv and Neumann [2] find three relevant categories. The first is deterministic decisions (usually structured) made with all data known with certainty, and the second is probabilistic decisions (usually unstructured) where conditions and outcomes can be stated in terms of probabilities, and thus the problem can be transposed into a programmable one. The final category is a random one where events and their occur rence probabilities cannot be specified and the problems usually take a highly unstructured format.

Source of information used is defined by Vierck [39] as involving two categories of decision-making processes based on the information source: internal and external. Although little attention has been paid so far to this aspect, the information source is an important item in the DSS analysis and design phase, and has considerable implications in the valuation and pricing of DSS.

The four classification criteria discussed in this section of the paper (degree of structure, level of managerial activity, degree of uncertainty, and source of information) are correlated. In constructing a second dimension of the classification space, we extend earlier research that identifies partial correlations. A deductive argument is now invoked. If quality A is associated with quality B and quality B is associated with quality C, then A and C are not independent. Regarding correlation as degree of dependency in a probabilistic sense, correlation becomes transitive. The association of attributes has motivated the construction of dimension II as a set of distinct classes.

Adopting Simon's terms for description of the degree of structure and Anthony's model for structure of the managerial activity, Ahituv and Neumann [2] find a relationship between these two types of decision classification criteria. They present evidence to suggest that the degree of structure of problems in an organization changes systematically as we move from lower to higher levels of the hierarchical model of the organization. At supervisory levels, decisions are highly structured, while middle level management make both structured and unstructured decisions. Highly unstructured decisions are typical for top management. Lerch and Mantei [26] found a correlation between the degree of structure and level of uncertainty. Recognizing the importance of the information source, Vierck [39] finds a correlation between this attribute, the level of structure, and the managerial level. He asserts that internal sources of information are typically used at the supervisory level in structured decisions, while on higher hierarchical levels where decisions are more unstructured, external sources are used more extensively. In addition to simple repetitive investment analysis decisions, top management face complex and unique decisions, such as acquisitions or mergers, for which external information is vital.

Using the correlation displayed by the attributes of the decision-making process, the four dimensions—degree of structure, managerial level, degree of uncertainty, and source of information—are projected into one decision class. Based on the relationships among the discussed attributes, three classes are defined. Table 1 represents how characteristics of decisions change as a function of the management level, and summarizes the definition of the three classes described below.

Class 1: These are highly structured decisions made with certainty, typically at supervisory levels using internal information. Example decisions may be related to accounts payable and payroll preparation, accounts receivable and purchasing, customer waiting lines, and situations involving group behavior.

Class 2: These are semi-structured decisions made by middle and top management. Usually decisions in this category use both internal and external sources of information and are made in environments of both certainty and uncertainty. Examples of decisions in this category are budget and cost analysis, forecasting, productivity increase, and sales promotion.

Class 3: These are highly unstructured decisions made by top management in an environment of uncertainty, using both internal and external information. Examples of decisions in this category are choice of plant and warehouse locations, mergers and acquisitions, future product lines, and new policies.

We have developed the framework with which we proceed to study DSS valuation and pricing. For the classification of DSS a two-dimensional space is used. One dimension refers to the segment or phase of the decision-making process—intelligence, design or choice—while the second dimension refers to the decision class—class 1, 2 or 3. This structure of reference will be used later in the paper to classify a set of DSS. The appendix briefly describes the DSS considered for exemplification and their classification. ## DSS Specific Valuation Factors

## Tangible benefits

Most researchers and practitioners agree on the importance of tangible monetary benefits, but note the difficulty of assigning a dollar value to benefits, which are most often intangible. Keen [21], Wagner [40], Green [15], and Hogue and Watson [18] find that monetary assessments are performed infrequently. They suggest that the DSS evaluation process should be based on value assessment and definition of cost thresholds that can be justified given the perceived benefit [21]. This approach contrasts the traditional cost-benefit analysis for DSS evaluation, does work by Tierauf [38]. We maintain that there is no common recipe for valuation, and the appropriate approach depends on the attributes of the DSS in question.

Traditional cost-benefit analysis can be performed successfully for DSS that display a high degree of structure, aim at decisions that are made in a fairly certain environment, and primarily address the intelligence phase. For example, Hennen, et al. [16] report a return on investment of 40% for the MDS and COMPASS systems. Another typical decision support system which is highly structured and serves primarily the decision-making process in its intelligence phase is that used by the Office of Tax Analysis at the U.S. Treasury Department for estimating the impact of proposed changes to the U.S. tax code. The system relies on three independent, large-scale optimization models. Their forecasted distribution of potential benefits and losses on various subpopulations plays a significant role in Congressional tax policy debates $[29]$ . Also, Lembersky and Chi $[24]$ report that the VISION decision simulator implemented at Weyerhaeuser used a dynamic programming approach for improving timber processing. Their operational results were tracked at a number of field locations, and the measured benefits from the use of this decision support system was 7 million per year.

An interactive decision support system was implemented to schedule a ship for Monsanto, where several different voyage alternatives had to be evaluated periodically. Traditional cost-benefit analysis has estimated their savings to be \$20,000 per year [10]. Adelman [1] reports an extremely interesting approach and use of DSS cost-benefit analysis, where multiattribute weighting techniques have been used to examine the costs and benefits for a range of design configurations in order to identify an “efficient frontier” for determining the best set of candidates at different cost levels. The actual choice of the system is conditioned by the price/value tradeoff preference of the firm, as indicated by the shape of its indifference curve.

## Intangible benefits

The intangible benefits play an important role in the evaluation of DSS, and many decision support systems are primarily justified by such benefits. The managers (users of DSS) perceive the main intangible benefits of DSS to be: facilitation of thoughts, improvement of communication, and a sense of success and capability to perform sensitivity analysis. Evaluation of intangible benefits is subjective, sometimes impossible, and as the weight of such benefits increases in the valuation process, so does the potential gap between the value and the price of the system.

DSS facilitates thoughts by allowing decision makers to use normative approaches to decision making. For example, in the implementation process of the BONDS system, one of the most important benefits derived from its use was that management was able to focus on forecasts of future economic scenarios, rather than concentrate on investment strategies with no quantitative justification $[10]$ . An important intangible benefit provided by DSS is its ability to perform sensitivity analysis. In a “what-if” approach, the model is rerun for successive scenarios. Optimization models provide insight on marginal untility based on “shadow-prices,” resulting as optimal solutions of the dual problem. The construction of the underlying models and the decision support system design often requires a careful analysis of the decision-making process, which by itself brings more insight into the process.

A long line of research discusses the high potential of DSS for improving the quality of communication among the individuals involved in collective decision-making processes. DSS can improve communication either directly, in the form of group decision support systems, or indirectly, by allowing the decision makers to focus on qualitative models instead of on each other. Group decision support systems (GDSS) were identified by Huber $[19]$ as a separate group of systems among the DSS that specialize in information sharing. General GDSS has capabilities that go far beyond slide projections or other traditional group communication means. For example, the group can decide during the meeting if it wants to see the data or even use the system in a “what-if” mode. In this way, a GDSS can prevent the deferment of a solution and can even improve consensus. This capability can considerably enhance the contribution of decision groups to the organizational decision-making process.

Due to its particular usage, pricing of GDSS has a special place within the DSS group. In a more recent paper, Huber [20] examined the design and implementation of GDSS and concludes that it is most likely to be rented rather than bought. Due to the high specialization of GDSS, users find it more appealing to use it on a rental basis, either “on-call” where both system and operating staff are provided at the customer’s location for a rental fee, or vendor-site location, where the hardware, software, and support staff are all provided for a fee at a location where the customer brings the decision group.

Extensive use of DSS can have drawbacks, too. Naylor [30] expresses concern that too much use of the decision support system might lead to spending too much time behind the terminal, leading to the isolation of the manager and ultimately to reduced communication. We believe that the use of a model imbedded in a DSS will improve communication among the members of a decision group only if all (or at least most of) the participants use the system themselves. In this case, input parameters and output results of the DSS become part of the user language that is understood by all participants. A homogeneous usage of the system among the members of the decision-making team is a key element in the successful contribution of a system to improved communication.

Last, but not least, of the perceived intangible benefits of DSS is a sense that the system has been a success, and probably the most natural criterion for measuring DSS success is the extent to which it is being used. In a field study involving 124 organizations and 378 DSS, Sanders and Courtney [33] find a substantial positive correlation between DSS success and the degree to which the user's decision context is unstructured. They reject the hypothesis according to which DSS is more successful in semistructured environments than in unstructured environments. This suggests that as a transition is made from DSS for structured decisions to DSS for unstructured decisions (and consequently from Class 1 to Class 3 in the proposed framework), the probability of success increases and the sense of success becomes a more important factor in the system evaluation. Being a subjective and nonquantifiable factor, it contributes to the increase in the potential gap between the real value of the system and its price.

DSS does not replace managers in the decision-making process, but helps managers gather the facts, develop solutions, and choose among alternatives. It is the human mind that makes the final decision. While it is hard to objectively estimate a priori the intangible benefits, the a posteriori benefit estimation is even more difficult. It is almost impossible to infer what the manager's decision would have been if the decision support system were not used. Moreover, the impact of the decision support system is hard to test experimentally, since by using the system, management gains knowledge that makes the experiment irreproducible.

## DSS Specific Cost Factors

The life cycle and personnel structure are two major factors that make the cost side of the cost-benefit analysis of DSS different from that of the traditional MIS.

## System life cycle

Many different data processing life cycles have been proposed. Davis [12] developed his based on the decision-making process postulated by Simon [32]. DeMarco [14] presented a structured system life cycle based on the concepts of structured analysis and design proposed by Yourdon [43]. For our purposes, the system life cycle developed by Powers, et al. [32] is valuable, as it can be applied to DSS and thus permits comparisons between traditional MIS and DSS. Their work distinguishes the following 5 stages as a basis of cost-benefit analysis: recognition of need, system development, installation, operation, and system obsolescence.

Recognition of need. In this phase, management, aware of the value of MIS outputs, may ask for DSS-type support. In a study of a large number of DSS, Hogue and Watson[17] found that in $67\%$ of the cases, the need for accurate information was the motivating factor for decision support system development.

System development. This phase contains the following subphases: initial investigation, analysis and general design, actual development, and implementation. User participation in early development stages has generated considerable discussion in the literature. In the instances in which management is involved in the design process, benefits are derived from the earliest stages onward. While early literature indicates a high degree of correlation between user satisfaction and user involvement, more recent studies by Anderson [3], DeBrabander and Thiers [13], and Mann and Watson [27] argue that user involvement in DSS development is a complex variable which can and should be managed.

Installation. In this phase, transition takes place from development to ongoing operation. In the case of DSS, implementation and installation will overlap to a great extent because there are no major implications from "going live," as is the case with other management information systems. The cost of a detected problem in the implementation-installation phase of a decision support system is much smaller than the cost of a similar error in a transaction processing system because the information provided by the DSS error can be easily disregarded, while the results of the transaction processing error might later lead to severe data integrity violation.

System operation. In this phase of a computerized system, the programs and procedures that comprise the system are static. DSS operating costs are lower than similar costs for other MIS. In fact, often DSS uses databases and computing resources which are common to those of transaction processing systems, which the organization has to provide independently of the existence of the decision support system. An important component of the operating cost of DSS is the amount of time management uses the system. However, this component is subjective and conditioned by the degree of satisfaction derived from using the system. While many DSS that support the intelligence phase of the decision-making process and address highly structured problems are run on a periodical basis, DSS that address the design and choice phase and support unstructured decisions are run more irregularly.

System obsolescence and termination. While other MIS are terminated only after they are replaced by other systems that support the day-to-day data processing needs, DSS are often terminated by complete abandonment. A typical case is the one reported by Balbirer and Shaw in a sequence of papers [5, 6] on the evolution of a financial planning system at Central Carolina Bank. Before discontinuance, the system had been widely used and the parameters of the model had become a means of communication among the people who structured the bank's balance sheet. The extensive use of the model led to the development of a kind of expertise that enabled the users to predict the reaction of the model to input parameters before running the computer programs. This made the actual use of the system unnecessary. In addition, the increasingly volatile interest rates rendered the underlying linear model obsolete.

DSS life cycles can vary widely from one project to another. In a comprehensive discussion of planning and organizing for DSS, Sprague and Carlson [36] identify three possible developmental approaches: the “quick-hit,” the “staged-development” and the “complete DSS.” The relatively large number of “quick-hit” DSS that are in use can be explained partially by the use of prototypes in the design and development of DSS. If the application is suited to end-user languages, the prototype is often cheaper than conventional systems development.

The role of information centers in facilitating the construction of DSS in the user departments has been increasing in recent years $[32]$ , and their use appears to be an inexpensive alternative to development of the so-called “quick-hit” DSS. Information centers can have an important impact on the system development life cycle and on the total cost of the system.

## Personnel

A major factor in differentiating the cost analysis of DSS from that of other MIS is the structure of personnel involved in the design, development, implementation, and operation of the system. Studies of the personnel structure differ to some extent. Wagner [41], in a study of Interactive Financial Planning System (IFPS) users, states that out of the selected systems, most DSS were built by planning analysts. Locander, et al. [25] find that Computer Based Information Systems (CBIS) professionals are needed during the development of a decision support system. In contrast, Blumenthal [7] states that the DSS and CBIS functions “simply do not mix” in terms of organizational resources. We reconcile the two views by asserting that both findings are correct in the context defined by the systems included for analysis in these studies.

DSS systems set forth in the framework of this paper are classified as supporting highly structured decisions and addressing the intelligence phase of the decision-making process, are sometimes described as TPS and are viewed as part of traditional MIS, as both the personnel structure and cost analysis are similar to traditional MIS. For systems that plot in the Choice/Class 1 cell of the diagram in Table 2, the structure of the personnel involved is characterized by more direct user development and participation. However, user involvement is also conditioned by the magnitude of the project. Short, easy to develop projects are typically developed by the user with the aid of high-level programming tools, while large projects that require more sophisticated programming languages and techniques make use of specialized, technically-oriented professionals.

## Diversity of DSS and Impact on Valuation

In this section, the diversity of the existing DSS is stressed by illustrating the alternative categories with representative systems, and a subpopulation of DSS is classified in the proposed framework. The set considered for exemplification is comprised of DSS for which valuation analysis has either been described in the literature, or of which we know from experience or personal contacts. Following the principles proposed earlier, each case is classified in the two-dimensional space. The appendix briefly explains the functions of the selected systems and their classifications. Where systems had multiple and diverse functions (e.g., BONDS, MUB), we were able to classify a system in multiple cells. In this way a system with several diverse functions received greater weight in the empirical study. Table 2 graphically represents how the systems plot in the two-dimensional space.

An empirical frequency function has been constructed based on the number of systems that plot in each cell of the reference structure. Table 3 summarizes the empirical findings.

The numbers in Table 3 suggest that the DSS population displays a large variability, and studies must be interpreted in the context of the considered systems. Systems in the Intelligence/Class 3 cell can sometimes be associated with Transaction Processing Systems (TPS). Only a few TPS have characteristics qualifying them to be a DSS. A low density of representatives will be displayed at all the borders of Table 2. Most DSS fall in the DESIGN/CLASS-2 cell. While the relative frequencies indicated in Table 3 do not represent the exact distribution for the entire DSS population, the numbers suggest on one hand that DSS are diverse and on the other that the systems are not evenly distributed. It was our experience in collecting the sample that DSS that plot in the central cells are more frequent than those that plot in the peripheral categories.

As one moves from nonstructured to highly structured decisions and from INTELLIGENCE to CHOICE, the intangible benefits become the more important motivating factors for system selection. In this context, while the price is set by tangible and measurable costs, the value becomes a subjective matter and the premise is created for a potential gap between value and price of DSS. Because this gap is conditioned by the subjectivity of value perception, we conjecture that this gap is related to the intangibility of benefits that increases as we move towards the CHOICE/CLASS-3 cell of the diagram. This situation is represented graphically in Figure 1.

We reconcile contrasting views with respect to the applicability of traditional cost-benefit analysis and assert that cost-benefit analysis can and should be employed for systems in the Intelligence/Class 3 cell of Table 2, while value-based methods are more appropriate for systems in the Choice/Class 1 cell. Valuation of systems addressing highly unstructured decisions in the Choice stage are subjective, making it impossible to set equality between cost and value.

## Conclusion

In this paper we have approached the valuation and pricing of DSS in a two-dimensional framework. One dimension reflects the degree to which the supported decision is structured, the level of managerial activity, the level of uncertainty, and source of information used, while the second dimension classifies the DSS based on the phase of the aided decision according to Simon's model for decision-making. Value and cost factors for DSS have been addressed, and the following summarizes our conclusions:

1. Any value-cost analysis for DSS should take into consideration the type of the particular system to be analyzed and there is no common recipe that can be applied successfully to all DSS.

The systems in the lower-left corner of the classification diagram can sometimes be associated with TPS, and the number of systems that have the characteristics that make them eligible as DSS is small. Based on the systems under study in the empirical part of this paper, we conjecture that a low density of representatives will be displayed at all the borders of the classification diagram. Most often DSS belongs to the category defined by Class 2 and the solution design area.

3. While benefits and cost are considered in Class 1, intelligence case can be assessed with traditional cost-benefit analysis. As we move towards the upper-right corner of the classification diagram (Table 2), the intangible benefits become more important factors for justifying DSS, and valuation becomes more subjective. By the same token, the change in personnel structure and the cost of management time introduces a degree of subjectivity and intangibility in the cost side of the analysis. However, overall cost factors are more predictable than the benefits. While costs are based on resources used and market prices, the value becomes more subjective, and traditional cost-benefit analysis techniques are rendered less meaningful.

This paper has identified a potential value-price gap that is shown to be conditioned by subjectivity in the perception of value. This research puts the value-cost analysis of DSS in proper perspective and provides a framework that can be used by researchers and practitioners as a means of communication.

# Description and Classification of DSS Subpopulation

AAIMS (An Analytical Information System)—supports planning, finance marketing, and operations functions for American Airlines. (Classification: Intelligence, Class 2) [23]

BONDS—Derives bond investment strategies and performs sensitivity analysis to determine the impact of policy constraints on portfolio value. (Classification: Design, Class 2; Choice, Class 3) [10]

BRANDAID—Supports market planning. (Classification: Design, Class 2) [2]

CAS (Claims Administration System)—Helps insurance adjuster decide how claims ought to be processed. Developed by System Development Corporation. (Classification: Design, Class 1) [11]

COMPASS—Helps mill management determine the effects of the decision system's logic and explore alternative merchandising strategies. (Classification: Design, Class 3) [16]

DIF (Discriminant Function)—Secret computer coding process that assigns a score to IRS returns based on which returns are selected for audit. (Classification: Choice, Class 1) [34]

GADS (Geodata Analysis and Display System)—Aids geographic allocation of resources and people. (Classification: Design, Class 2) [2]

MDS (Merchandising Decision System)—Manufacturing decision system used to maximize the return achieved by a forest product mill. (Classification: Intelligence, Class 2) [16]

MUB (Management Union Bargaining)—Makes use of a decision tree model to develop bargaining positions acceptable to both management and union. (Classification: Choice, Class 3; Design, Class 3) [42]

MYCIN—Medical expert system. Helps physician diagnose infectious diseases. Given an initial set of symptoms, the system acts as a consultant by requesting pertinent information, such as results from a laboratory test. Possible diagnoses are suggested with a probability factor. (Classification: Intelligence, Class 2) [8]

PAMS (Plan Analysis and Modelling System)—Interrogates and analyzes historical data. Has access to models for generating future plans. (Classification: Intelligence, Class 2) [38]

PMS (Portfolio Management System)—Supports a portfolio manager by interrogating large databases. Has access to simple models. (Classification: Design, Class 3; Intelligence, Class 3) [2]

PROSPECTOR—Mineral exploration expert system. Helps choose the place where mineral exploration has the highest expected payoff. (Classification: Choice, Class 2) [26]

PUFF—Medical Expert System. Helps physician design a treatment after the disease has been diagnosed. (Classification: Design, Class 2) [26]

SMP (Symbolic Manipulation Program)—Uses artificial intelligence techniques to manipulate algebraic expressions, and analytically takes derivatives of complex mathematical functions. (Classification: Design, Class 1) [37]

STROBE—Gathers and interprets information regarding organizational decision making. (Classification: Intelligence, Class 1) [22]

VISION (Video Interactive Stem Inspection and Optimization)—Makes use of dynamic programming model to improve timber processing. (Classification: Design, Class 1) [24]
