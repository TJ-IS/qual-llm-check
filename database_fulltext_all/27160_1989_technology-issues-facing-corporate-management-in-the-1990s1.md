## Abstract

This article discusses major issues raised by the authors and several senior information technology executives in large corporations that affect the way technology is introduced and used in major corporations.

These issues and “drivers” (primary causes of change) are likely to have the most significant impact on the management of information technology within corporations now and in the future. If these drivers are acknowledged as real (or validated by research as real), then the understanding of their impacts on both corporate organization and technology management would very much benefit by research. This article suggests some of the directions such research could take.

This article is essentially a preview from the top of the intertwining of business, industrial operations, and technology management for the next five to ten years. Most of the issues, and their consequences, are subject to continuing research by the President's Council of the Society for Information Management.

Keywords: Information technology, strategic planning, management directions, organizational paradigms

## Introduction

The organizational and macrofinancial “drivers” and their consequences impact how we need to think about the future management of information technology. For the purposes of this article, “driver” means a primary cause of change: a concept or activity that, once it is deployed, almost inevitably results in substantial change in the way we manage, relate, communicate, and work. The issues and drivers discussed below have been “discovered” in discussions with many IS executives from large corporations. Many of the drivers, each taken in isolation, are not new in any real sense. Some of them are almost “old hat.” What is new, however, is the gestalt impact of the drivers interacting with each other and their combined effect on the corporation, its effectiveness, operations, and success in the marketplace. Similarly, the far-reaching impact of microelectronic chips required the combination and synergistic blending of knowledge and specific scientific and technical solutions from physics, chemistry, electronics, mathematics, logic, engineering, systems programming, and application of management sciences. The impact of the interaction of the individual elements of knowledge is enormously greater than that of each element by itself.

There is no intention of falling into the fallacy of proposing that the management of information technology itself will become (or is) the most important element of corporate structure and corporate management. We do assert, however, that effective deployment of the well-integrated technologies in a corporation acts as the “enabler,” or catalyst, for innovative and more effective management of the various principal functions and departments of the corporation and the enterprise as a whole.

# Organization and Macrofinancial Drivers in the Management of Information Technology

The key issues and drivers are set out below in summary form. Their impact, interaction, and the direction in which they may be leading us are discussed later in this article. We label these issues and drivers “key” because individually and interacting together they produce a major impact on how corporations will function and organize themselves, and manage technology within such organizations.

It is important to remember that the world is not homogeneous, even though the issues and drivers discussed are thought to have a potentially wide (if not universal) impact. The mix of drivers in different industries, in companies at different levels of managerial competence and sophistication and in companies of different sizes will result in quite a wide range of organizational approaches. The level of competence of the CEO and his or her direct reports is especially significant.

The following are some of the key issues and drivers bringing about major shifts in directing the management of technology into the future. They are organized under the following categories:

1. Management and organization;

2. The hybrid partnerships;

3. Managing the infrastructure;

4. Macrofinancial and planning issues.

## Management and organization issues

The deep penetration of technology into the planning and operation of the enterprise, at all organizational levels, demands the evolution of new paradigms for managing the use of technology throughout the business. The issues surrounding such change and the developments driving it are:

\- The evolving integration and interaction of many technologies and their gestalt impact on the management organization and direction of the business are beginning to be visible.

\- The missions of line management are being directly affected by technology.

\- The defensive, as well as offensive, use of technology for competitive purposes will be explicitly managed by those with line responsibility for competitive strategies and decisions.

\- The macrofinancial and competitive effects of the use of all the technologies interacting synergistically together throughout the corporation lead to the growing involvement of the CEO and his or her direct reports in steering the use of technology.

\- The role of functional and departmental line managers in determining and managing the use of technology is expanding.

\- The management of technology and the management of the use of technology are being segregated. The devolution of planning and use of information processing “intelligence” into individual business units and departments is changing the information technology mission. The key problem is that while separating the management of the use of technology from management of the technology itself appears to be inevitable, their complete segregation would be detrimental to the enterprise.

\- The increasing automation of the value chain, including internal and external linkages, is beginning to affect the organization of the business, its power structures, and required skill mix.

\- There is a need to bridge the gap between the management of physical automation and the management of administrative and knowledge processes. The requirement is emerging for the complete integration of the use of physical automation and the use of information technology into the missions and accountabilities of line management.,

\- The integration of the use of technology into functions and departments and the overall macrofinancial impact on the business are driving the management of the use of technology toward line management at all levels of the organization.

## The hybrid partnership

The transfer of management of the use of technology to line management requires the creation of a partnership between line and information technology management. The issues driving this transformation are:

• Information technology management will have to transfer the leadership for use of technology to line management and transform itself into an expert support, knowledge-based function.

\- In larger, multi-divisional, or multi-SBU companies, the focus of IS management is likely to shift to the line management of the strategic business units or divisions. The corporate role of IS management, other than support of the corporate functions themselves, is again being opened for discussion and research.

\- Senior information technology executives face a major task of distributing the management of the uses of technology to line management, while at the same time becoming a strategic focus of knowledge on how it should all hang together for maximum competitiveness of the organization as a whole.

\- For the shifts to be executed smoothly, approaches to information management within the enterprise have to evolve substantially.

\- A new hybrid concept of line/staff information technology management has to be developed and integrated into the management of the enterprise.

\- This hybrid IS management concept needs to be integrated via the partnership paradigm with the line management of the use of technology throughout the enterprise.

\- The partnership concept has to be translated into sharing at all levels of the organization. It needs to be anchored in cooperative sharing and constructive, mutually supportive relationships.

\- In order for companies to shift successfully into the partnership mode, we must rethink and redevelop what the appropriate skills and operational/management behavior are for both sides of the partnership.

\- This rethinking is likely to lead to a definition of a corporate development process that trains high-potential professionals in the full range of management skills required by line and IS management.

\- This corporate development process should be conducive to a flow of high-potential managers through many IS positions. Succession for both the senior IT executive and line management (user executive) can become a natural by-product of the process.

\- Resistance by MIS departments that are slow to shift to the partnership paradigm will need to be overcome.

\- Many aspects of the shifts taking place in all areas of IS management are not yet well understood. This could be studied in future research.

## Managing the infrastructure

The management of the technology infrastructure portion of the partnership paradigm needs to recognize that today, and in the future, their task requires the orchestration of many technologies in response to the needs identified by business strategies:

\- Data processing is becoming just one of many technology-intensive functions in an organization. Understanding is needed on how to deploy individual elements of the technology resources, as well as on how to manage the architecture. They no longer fit into a homogeneous organizational approach.

\- It has become necessary to distinguish between information storage, retrieval, switching, and communications on the one hand, and management processing and the use of the information by client (user) departments on the other.

\- A new hybrid concept of line/staff information technology management has to be developed and integrated into the management of the enterprise that:

\- Contains staff elements that interface with the management of the use of technology by line management.

\- Plans and “sells” technology and systems strategies and architectures within which local (functional) decisions can be made freely.

\- Contains a line element that operates “utility” services, e.g., computing and telecommunications networks, and provides technology infrastructure wherever the use of those technologies cuts across major organizational segments of the enterprise.

\- Concerns itself with excellence of:

• Utility-type production operations.

\- Service level agreements and support of multiple users.

\- Reliability of technology owned by line users within the overall corporation technology architecture.

## Macrofinancial and planning issues

In a partnership paradigm, line management determines the business use of technology and is accountable for its business and operational impact. In a strategic approach to technology deployment, the impact on the company is macrofinancial and needs to be expressed in business profitability terms, not in “value of information” terms:

\- There is growing recognition of the macrofinancial impact of automation on the company as a whole, whether it be the automation of processes, discrete manufacturing, administration, or knowledge worker tasks — particularly those impacts that eliminate, or substantially reduce, major company cost drivers.

\- Management and planning of the use of technology will shift to being based on macrofinancial business-wide measurement concepts and considerations, rather than cost-benefit analyses of individual applications and application enhancements.

\- The integration of the use of technology into the primary missions and accountabilities of line management is bringing about the need to measure the macrofinancial impact of automation on the business as a whole.

\- The macrofinancial impact of the use of technology is driving the need to include technology planning and use in the strategic planning process of the company. The absence of an effective strategic planning process often further complicates the situation.

\- There is a need to integrate the planning of the use of existing and evolving technologies into a view, or vision, of their combined macrofinancial, competitive, organization structure, and power impacts.

\- The macrofinancial impact of the use of information technology on the company is still not well-understood — and therefore neither well-measured nor reported. Yet, the macrofinancial impact on the business is the most significant measure of the effectiveness of the deployment of technology.

## Discussion

The combined effects of these drivers are manyfold, transcending the impact of any one or a small group of drivers alone. Their “enabling” characteristics — i.e., in-depth automation of all administrative and physical elements of the value chain, extending from the company’s suppliers to the end users of its services or products — are already transforming the way we manage automation-oriented technologies. They will also transform the way we organize our businesses.

## Managing the use of technology

There is a need to recognize that managing the use of technology must lead the management of the technology itself and that the two aspects need to be separate but not segregated. Complete segregation of the management of IT technology from the management of its use may appear to offer a simple and attractive organizational solution to senior line management. This is not the case. Doing so has a number of undesirable consequences:

1. The direction and vision of technology infrastructure are not driven by that of the enterprise.

2. Partnerships to accomplish major corporate goals and the people relationships necessary to sustain them are hindered rather than encouraged.

3. The direction of a major corporate resource becomes dispersed and less effective.

The dispersion of the management of information technology as a by-product of its deep penetration throughout the corporation is today a major subject of discussion.

Such dispersion is a symptom of a situation where senior management has not recognized that:

\- The management of the use of technology, for major macrofinancial and competitive benefits, needs to be an integral part of corporate vision and strategic direction.

\- The technology infrastructure must be based on and evolve from this vision and direction.

\- A partnership between the two is essential to produce results.

Dispersion and the potential of costly chaos detrimental to the profitability of the company are symptoms of a lack of understanding by senior managers of their role in leading and managing the business use and impact of technology.

Identifying line management's role in directing the use of the technology infrastructure has become essential to building infrastructures relevant to the direction and structure of the company. Line accountability today is not well-defined nor anchored in a well-understood organizational structure paradigm. Several issues need to be researched and resolved:

\- A process for evolving to an organization where line management is accountable for planning and managing the use of technology, while IS is accountable for planning and managing the technology infrastructure itself.

\- Some leading-edge companies are evolving the partnership concept as the relationship and organizational form for building effective bridges between managing the use of technology and managing technology itself. How should such partnerships work at all levels of the company?

\- What skills are required of senior executives and professionals involved in such a partnership, both on the line management side and on the technology management side, and what are their critical success factors?

\- Organizations do not change and evolve easily, particularly where shifts in accountabilities and internal power structure may be concerned. None of the above issues can be researched effectively without examining the role of the chief executive officer in all of them.

## Line management missions are impacted by technology

The impact of the interaction of the different aspects of automation needs to be recognized and deliberately planned, whether it be the automation of continuous processes, discrete manufacturing, distribution (physical and administrative) administration and finance, or knowledge worker tasks. It is such interaction that has macrofinancial impact on the company through significantly reducing or eliminating cost drivers. New directions in cost accounting and cost management (Kaplan, 1988; Kaplan and Johnson, 1987) are bringing visibility in these areas at last.

Information technology and its use has evolved too far and has too deep an impact on the business to continue being managed through a relatively simple organizational structure by a single conceptual paradigm. Below is a (partial) list of what needs to be managed:

\- Personal computing

\- Individual contributor networking

• Departmental computing

• Departmental networking

\- Internal value-added linkage automation

\- External value-added linkage automation

• Voice, data, and process control communication

• Physical processes automation

\- Administrative workflow automation

\- Expert systems processes

• Business strategies support

• Competitive advantage management

• Time-to-market acceleration

\- User-led prototyping management

\- Technology innovation

\- Technology architecture

\- Internal systems management consulting

\- Management of automation impact on organization

\- Traditional data center management and operation

Managing all of these leads us to conclude that we are no longer managing a single IT function. The senior IT executive is facing the need to manage a network of technology resources, supporting a network of uses determined by line management.

In order to accomplish this, the corporation is demanding unprecedented flexibility, which requires vision, comprehensive understanding of the business, as well as complex distributed technology.

Successful organizational partnerships need to exhibit complete flexibility in being able to cope with — or even lead — the response to challenges such as:

\- Impact of divestments/acquisitions

\- Business unit combination/separation/restructuring

\- Manufacturing/distribution contraction/expansion

• Changes in strategic directions

• Changes in organizational structures and relationships

• Changes in external linkages and business alliances

\- Adaptation to the impacts of major business cycles

• Changes in the management style of the CEO and senior executives

\- New markets

\- New products

\- New technology

In order to be capable of formulating partnerships and organizational approaches that recognize and exploit the deepening interpenetration of technology and line management prime missions, the senior IT executive must significantly broaden his or her conceptual horizons and vision of the role of technology management in the company. There is a need to plan and manage:

\- Organizational changes and transformations triggered by significant deployment of technology;

\- The impact of deploying technology on established value-added linkages — restructuring of their organizational relationships and substantial simplification of work/information flow;

• Accountabilities and power structures involved in managing technology versus managing the use of technology;

\- Major macrofinancial impacts of technology deployment and related productivity actions and organizational structure changes.

These are some of the senior management issues the senior IT executive must focus on in the future. Delivering a computer system with documentation and user training is becoming a middle management task. A principal critical success factor in the senior IT executive's mission has become the ability to identify and communicate the line management roles in managing the business use of the technology infrastructure and to relate these to the direction and structure of the company. The executives who manage the technology and its deployment (use) must be ready to deal with the political and organizational consequences.

As technology begins to impact directly on the effectiveness with which line management's primary missions are carried out, measuring its effectiveness in the company by cost-benefit analysis of individual applications becomes largely irrelevant and may well be misleading.

## Macrofinancial effects of technology

The automation of value-added chains in companies is bringing about macrofinancial impacts on the business as a whole. Numerous examples can be cited: $^{2}$

\- Planned automation of warehousing, design, and the ordering cycle, and above all the total impact of their interaction, is the foundation of the successful business strategy of Benneton, the international sweater chain (Istvan, 1988).

\- The automation of product design and all the steps to reduce the “time to market” on new models and upgrades has enabled Honda to out-perform Yamaha in the marketplace (Istvan, 1988).

\- Frito-Lay has automated all the steps from monitoring customer shelf space, replenishing stocks, factory scheduling, to distribution.

• Airline reservations systems have expanded to control the whole chain, from traveller enquiry and ticketing to fleet scheduling.

These examples illustrate a planned strategic approach to managing a network of technologies, deployed through a network of line management-determined uses, to achieve one overall macrofinancial impact on the company and enhance its ability to compete. The future senior IT executive must be concerned with understanding and planning the macrofinancial impact on the business in partnership with his or her senior management colleagues. The relevant questions here deal with understanding, planning, and reporting the interacting impacts of a number of technologies on:

\- Cost of products

\- Time to market

\- Market share

\- Gross margin

• Productivity per employee

• Number of employees per revenue dollar

• Assets employed per revenue dollar

• Product and service quality

In the partnership, the plans that result in the macrofinancial effects must be “owned” by line management accountable for business performance. The macrofinancial impacts themselves must be owned and committed by the line organization itself as part of delivering its primary commitments to the CEO and the board of directors.

## Characteristics of the senior IT executive

In this strange new world for the senior IT executive, fraught with unprecedented opportunity to affect the well being of the company, what is the senior IT executive's role? What is his or her background? How do we guide and teach aspiring middle managers, and possibly senior executives from other functional areas, who need to pass through the senior IT executive role successfully on the way to the top? Unfortunately, there are no pat answers to these questions at this time.

There is a need for research and industry studies to gain better understanding of how the management of technology and the management of its use can be effectively carried out by a partnership mechanism of line management and technology management. The content and structure of the technology infrastructure will tend to vary from company to company, driven by the strategy of the company and the supporting strategy for the deployment of technology. Is there a “technology infrastructure minimum” and a paradigm for structuring its management and organization at all levels of the company, without which the partnership simply cannot be workable and effective?

In manufacturing and material handling companies, there is an evolving need to manage the integration of physical automation with information and communication automation. The roles of line management and technology management needed for success have to be researched and defined more substantially than they are today. Currently, too often there is turf conflict unless the CEO firmly leads. A rethinking of major organizational accountabilities is required.

From the discussion in this article, some of the tasks and characteristics of the senior IT executive are emerging.

The senior IT executive now has to integrate line management and technology strategies and their combined, planned, macrofinancial impacts. Today, centralization vs. decentralization of IT has become the wrong, simplistic issue. Instead, the senior IT executive needs to ask:

\- Am I in tune with the company business strategy (or the lack of it)?

\- Am I in tune with the current organizational direction?

\- Am I in tune with my company's and our industry's business cycle?

\- Am I inward-looking (focused on managing the technology), or externally oriented (helping colleagues plan the use of technology)?

In support of the partnership with line management, the future senior IT executive will simultaneously manage a mix of line and staff functions and must possess the mix of managerial skills required by both. The partnership will also require that both sides be ready to deal with — in fact, carefully plan — the political and organizational consequences of the all-pervading deployment of technology at all levels of the corporation.

In order to support this process, the senior IT executive needs to understand and communicate the future gestalt impact of many interacting technologies and their synergisms, as well as anticipate the strategic and operational effects of their use.

The key directional issue that appears to be emerging for the senior IT executive, and which needs further debate and research, can be stated as follows:

The senior IT executive should focus on ownership and sharing of knowledge to implement others' (peers) strategic initiatives and contribute to them. He or she should not focus on the ownership of a large organization and technical facilities structure as his or her prime mission.

The information technology function needs to become recognized as a knowledge function, integrating knowledge of business and technology directions; it should lead through knowledge rather than hierarchical authority.

The line management of the technology infrastructure, and of possible technical utility services, should be viewed as important but secondary to the senior IT executive's primary mission.

The organizational structure and functioning of the senior IT executive's own domain become critical if the emerging concepts of partnership are to have a chance of working. The senior IT executive now must be a consultant, head of research and development, and manager of a complex technical infrastructure. Neither a pure line nor a pure staff approach is likely to work. A new, hybrid staff-line organizational concept is emerging.

## Conclusion

In automation-related technologies, whether information or physical processes automation, business and our society indeed have a tiger by the tail. In order to master the tiger and reap its full benefits, new paradigms for managing the deployment of technology are required. These will be based on concepts of partnership and cooperation rather than managed conflict and interdepartmental competition. In such a paradigm, the accountability for the effectiveness of the use of technology will lie squarely with line management.

The senior IT executive's role will be one of partner consultant, strategy planner, and manager of technology infrastructure in a hybrid structure. The line manager will be accountable for planning and managing the business use of technology and delivering the macrofinancial and competitive benefits committed by him.

The old-style EDP or IT executive requires tremendous personal and organizational growth to maintain a constructive leadership position in the organizational evolution — indeed revolution — of the 1990s. His or her principal tasks in the 1990s will be much more those of a senior executive close to the CEO/COO, including the following: $^{3}$

\- Build a partnership structure and process with line management at all levels of the organization.

\- Build and manage a hybrid staff/line organization to manage the technology infrastructure and utility services.

\- Consult with senior line executives and furnish guidance on opportunities opened up by technology evolution.

\- Manage through knowledge and influence rather than formal power.

\- Realign the mindset and skill set in the IS organization.

\- Utilize “soft” skills (i.e., listening and negotiating, helping).

\- Manage line management expectations of technology use.

\- Think of the job from a strategic and enterprise operational perspective.

\- Possess creativity and vision, willingness to take risks and tolerance for ambiguity.

\- Juggle contradictory and scarce resources.

\- Delegate infrastructure and utility services planning and operation to his or her direct reports.

\- Be a contributor to the management and direction of the enterprise more so than technology management internal matters.

\- Be on top of the macrofinancial and competitive contribution of technology use committed by line management.

\- Support the CEO/COO in creating the enterprise vision and strategies.
