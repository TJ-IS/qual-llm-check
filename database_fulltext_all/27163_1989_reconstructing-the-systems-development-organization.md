## Abstract

Today's typical information systems (IS) organization clings to the belief that it is primarily in the new system development business, a notion based increasingly on substantial self-deception. In reality, many IS development staffs now devote the majority of their efforts to the repair and enhancement of currently installed systems. This “maintenance” task, as it is commonly termed, is widely misunderstood, misrepresented, and undervalued. A reconstructed view of the systems development organization is necessary, one in which the repair and ongoing development of installed systems is given proper emphasis and strategic recognition.

Keywords: Information systems organization, systems development, application software maintenance

ACM Categories: D.2.7, D.2.9, K.6.1, K.6.3

## Introduction

The emergence, rapid growth, and institutionalization of the information systems (IS) function within the modern business organization is a relatively recent phenomenon, having transpired only within the last three decades. Today, however, the future of the IS function is in some doubt. The dramatic diffusion of microcomputers within the business raises in some minds (e.g., Dearden, 1987) the question of whether the traditional, centralized IS function will persist, or whether it will wither away, displaced by the current rapid spread of end-user computing.

The present do-it-yourself movement among traditional users of IS services is generally understood to have been fueled not only by the new access to microcomputing, but by a substantial unfulfilled demand for information services (Alloway and Quillard, 1983; Sprague and McNurlin, 1986). The visible portion of this demand is represented in the typical IS organization by an extensive backlog of user requests, which, unfortunately, has not shrunk with the growth of end-user computing (Martin and McClure, 1983; Carr, 1987). Instead, in many businesses, the backlog has grown still further, much to the consternation of both users and general management.

What makes up this burgeoning backlog and workload? A substantial portion, research has shown, is the “maintenance” of previously developed and installed systems (Lientz and Swanson, 1980; Helms and Weiss, 1985; Jones, 1986). Tending to these systems is, of course, both necessary and good. Few would quarrel with this. Unfortunately, however, the term “maintenance” suggests to many general managers the need to repair system defects or shortcomings that would not exist had the original development been properly conducted. The IS manager thus wins few points for his or her maintenance efforts. General management wants to know, “When are we going to stop spending so much on maintenance?”

To begin to understand why this question is so hard to answer, 12 field-study cases were recently developed as part of an ongoing research project. These cases, vignettes of which are presented in the Appendix, focus on how IS organizations structure their maintenance work. In particular, the cases identify and describe alternative approaches to organization design, task definition and assignment, resource allocation, and policies for coordination and control. The cases appear in full in Swanson and Beath (forthcoming), and our research methodology is further described in Swanson and Beath (1988b).

In our study, we found most IS organizations struggling to find a structure within which application system maintenance could be performed effectively and efficiently. We found further that organizational contexts were quite diverse and that specific problems and attempted solutions varied accordingly, but that most were based, at least in part, on a common view of systems development and maintenance that is, in our judgment, now largely obsolete. We have concluded, therefore, that a reconstructed view of the task of systems development and maintenance is needed. This conclusion follows not only from our analysis of the twelve cases, but also from our larger program of research and involvement with IS practitioners over almost two decades.

We present here our proposed reconstructed view. Because our purposes are prescriptive, and because our view is not narrowly derived from the data of the twelve cases, we will speak in very broad terms on occasion. The reader is cautioned accordingly. We trust that each manager will think hard about the validity of our basic argument in the context of his or her own special situation. Those wishing to compare their own situations to those of the cases in depth may consult Swanson and Beath (forthcoming).

We begin our discussion with a look at current approaches to structuring the systems development activity. We then consider the conventional distinction between “development” and “maintenance,” which we find to be arbitrarily drawn. This motivates in turn our reconstructed view. Implementation guidance follows. In our conclusion, we draw implications and return to the question of the “withering away” of the IS organization.

## The Systems Development Department

Today's systems development department forms the technical core of the traditional IS function. As shown in Figure 1, the IS function also typically houses a data processing or operations unit and a planning group. Frequently, additional small units (e.g., a technical support staff, a data administration group, or an end-user computing function) are further included. The staff of the systems development department consists of systems analysts and programmers; their task is to develop and maintain the application systems that serve the business.

Within systems development, the most typical departmentalization is based upon groups of systems in support of a business function of the host organization, such as finance, production, or marketing, as shown in Figure 1 (Zmud, 1984; Swanson and Beath, 1988a). Such IS organization charts often mirror the organization charts of their host organizations, in terminology if not in layout, with the advantage of demonstrating to general management that IS is focusing on and being responsive to its clients.

Within the client-focused departments, subdivisions generally take one of two forms. Either programmers and analysts share the development and maintenance of systems for a client sub-function, or else the developers are separated from the maintainers. For example, the Financial Systems group might be further divided into an Accounts Receivable group and a Financial Statements group. Or it might be departmentalized as the Accounts Receivable Development Project and the Financial Systems Maintenance Group.

In discussing their rationale for choosing between these two approaches, IS managers often reveal their preference for development work rather than maintenance work. That is, where the client sub-function form is selected, the “onerous” maintenance task is said to be spread among individuals so that none will be forced to do more than his or her fair share. Maintenance is often used as a training ground for unskilled recruits in this type of organization. The separation of developers and maintainers, on the other hand, is usually explained as being more productive for the development teams, which are freed from maintenance interruptions. In these organizations, maintenance is sometimes seen as a dumping ground for older employees unwilling to master new technology. Not surprisingly, given these circumstances, maintenance is seen by career-oriented staff as de-motivating, low-level work (Couger and Colter, 1985).

Maintenance has therefore not been well received by the traditional IS organization. It is most often regarded as a form of overhead, to be contained wherever possible by using improved development techniques, charging users for changes to systems (even the correction of IS errors), and, most recently, promoting end-user computing. It continues to be assumed that the principal organizational mission is development, preferably new systems development.

In our research we noted that most of the organizing techniques in use reflect this focus on development. For example, by far the bulk of the planning effort goes to determining the slate of projects for new systems to be developed, while maintenance is simply projected from prior expenditures. Management control reports, by extension, are largely devoted to tracking new development projects. With limited planning and control, it is little wonder that accountability for maintenance is difficult to trace.

Resource allocation similarly focuses predominantly on development. Task assignments all too frequently reveal a career path that starts in main-

The fragmentation of the maintenance function across the IS department makes it difficult to justify the purchase of code analyzers and sophisticated test support packages in any but the largest installations. At the same time, organizations build elaborate systems of development forms and procedures that are not easily adapted to maintenance activities. Thus, while our understanding and control of development improves annually, maintenance remains a black art.

Three negative consequences of the emphasis on development are apparent: (1) accountability for maintenance is difficult at best; (2) the high reciprocal dependence between system maintenance and proper business functioning is ignored; and (3) the potential of information systems to integrate the enterprise by linking its installed systems is concealed.

Nevertheless, the typical IS organization clings to the belief that it is in the new system development business, a belief reinforced by the structure of the IS organization and based on substantial self-deception. This self-deception centers, in particular, around the operational definitions of “development” and “maintenance.”

## What is Meant by "Development" and "Maintenance"?

Thus far we have used the terms development and maintenance without precise definition. Development is generally understood in the IS literature to refer to the analysis, design, and implementation of new applications. Maintenance, on the other hand, has been defined to include corrective, adaptive, and perfective modifications to installed systems (Swanson, 1976). In practice, many organizations combine the adaptive and perfective categories, and distinguish simply between “fix-it” or repair work, and “enhancements” (Couger and Colter, 1985).

In any case, the development of new systems produces more systems to be maintained. And these systems grow (as measured in lines of code) after installation at a rather alarming rate, adding further to the maintenance burden, as illustrated in Table 1. Most IS organizations report that they allocate at least half their effort to “maintenance” (Lientz and Swanson, 1980; Parikh and Zvegintzov, 1983). We might assume, therefore, that the balance of their effort is allocated to the development of new systems. However, upon closer observation, this does not seem to be the case.

In practice, the IS organization has traditionally distinguished between development and maintenance along the lines illustrated in Figure 2. Here, the systems and programming task associated with a hypothetical portfolio of 12 application systems is portrayed. Within this portfolio, eight systems are assumed to be installed and operational, and four are assumed to be under new development, yet to be installed. For ease of illustration, the tasks associated with the systems are assumed to be equal in magnitude.

Traditionally, as shown in Figure 2, maintenance is defined to include minor enhancement of operational systems, as well as other “keep it up and running” work. Development is defined to encompass major enhancement of operational systems, as well as work on new systems yet to be installed. Overall, development is thus typically thought of as larger than maintenance, even though the majority of the systems are being repaired or enhanced.

If the technical task is small, e.g., under 100 estimated hours, it is “maintenance;” if it is large, it is “development.”

This partitioning of enhancement work muddies our understanding of both the new system development and repair components of the systems and programming task, often making it impossible to know how much of which activity is being carried out. It simultaneously preserves the myth of IS-is-development and conceals the amount of repair work required. And clients with operations dependent on computing tend not to quarrel with this arrangement, as it results in a lump sum of money for “maintenance,” money that can be used to keep their systems functioning with a minimum of red-tape.

Categorizing enhancement work on the basis of size also makes apparent sense when we consider the differences between repair, enhancement, and new system development work. Repair work is small and new system development tasks are much larger, on average. Enhancements run the gamut in between. New systems and some enhancements (particularly those enhancements driven by new technological opportunities) encounter new technology much more frequently than do repairs. This may contribute to the lower motivating potential of repair work. Repair is also the most constrained work, in terms of design, in that it must fit within the existing system. Enhancements are often similarly constrained, while new systems are the least constrained. It is probably in consideration of characteristics such as these that organizations justify the categorization of small enhancements as repair and large enhancements as new development. That is, the notion is that small enhancements are most like repair work, and larger enhancements are most like new system development.

If we look beyond carrying out the technical task itself, however, and consider more carefully the management of the entire effort, including interacting with business operations, the categorization of enhancements on the basis of size makes much less sense. All enhancements, large or small, differ fundamentally from new systems development in that enhancements are not buffered from business operations, as is new systems development. The justification of new systems is often done by IS on behalf of the user, in answer to the question, “Why should we spend all this money to change our business processes?” Justification of repairs and enhancement, on the other hand, is usually done by users themselves, based on their experiences with the installed system. The user’s question is, “Why shouldn’t we improve this system? We need this now!” Whether such improvement involves a repair or an enhancement is rather uninteresting for the user. Installed systems represent significant investments, both in terms of systems development expenditures and in the intangible costs of organizational change, borne heavily by users. Repair and enhancement are the means for salvaging the return on these investments.

In summary, it is little wonder that the general manager feels that the important issues of development and maintenance are largely uninterpretable. Is too much effort being spent on maintenance? It depends on what kind of maintenance you are referring to. What is meant by “managing the IS portfolio as an asset”? It is difficult to respond when the emphasis is simply on adding to the portfolio. “Why don’t we purchase more of our software?” asks the manager. “Nobody does it better than we do,” comes the answer. “Then why do we spend so much on maintenance?”

It would seem timely to ask whether the conventional distinction between development and maintenance work is part of the organizational problem more than it is part of the organizational solution.

## Toward a Reconstructed View

Our reconstructed view proposes that the fundamental distinction in the systems development department should be between work on installed systems and work on future systems, yet to be installed. Further, work on installed systems should be recognized to constitute the substantial heart of the total task. Figure 3 illustrates.

The proposed distinction emphasizes the relationship of each system to business operations, rather than the size of the technical portion of the task. When business operations are dependent on a system, the analysis and programming work may be a minor part of the larger task of getting the business to run properly. When business operations are not yet dependent on a system, the technical portion may in contrast constitute the bulk of the task. In the reconstructed view, systems are classified as “installed” or “future.” Whether the work on installed systems is repair or enhancement is de-emphasized (as reflected in Figure 3). The proposed distinction is thus a sharp one, though it possesses some complexities of its own.

The basic premise of the reconstructed view is that work on installed systems differs from that on future systems more than development work differs from maintenance work, as traditionally distinguished. The crucial difference is hypothesized to be in the working relationships required between the IS organization and its user organizations.

As suggested above, installed systems are imbedded in the host organization, which is likely to be dependent on them. Significant opportunity costs in lost productivity are incurred by not repairing and enhancing these systems. Work on these systems operates necessarily within a tight feedback loop in which changes are initiated by the user and directed toward continued reliable use. Problems arising from use provide the impetus for change, the urgency of which may be substantial. Requirements for changes are grounded in users' accumulated experience with the system. Design is incremental, user participation is highly motivated, and technology is, more often than not, familiar to users and systems staff. Service is the essential objective and the appropriate basis of performance assessment.

In contrast, future systems are buffered from the day-to-day operations of user organizations, which function without these systems in the short run. Their costs are for the most part yet to be incurred, and resources can usually be shifted to other investments should further development cease, thus minimizing opportunity costs. A few other comments about the scope of the installed and future systems groups can be made. Replacement systems, or new systems being developed or purchased to replace installed systems in the portfolio, constitute, by our estimate, more than half of all new systems developed by the mature IS organization (Swanson and Beath, 1988a). As these new systems do not yet form the basis of current business operations, they would be the responsibility of the future systems group. However, as illustrated in Figure 4, a lateral integrating mechanism would be used to facilitate involvement by the installed systems group. The use of the task force concept is proposed for this purpose.

Similarly, a task force would guide any phased implementation of a future system. During this period, responsibility for the system would thus be shared between the installed and future systems groups.

As proposed, the future systems organization would therefore constitute an umbrella organization for the temporary housing of future systems projects and their staffs. The staff responsible for developing a future system could be regarded as “on loan” from the installed systems organization. When a future system is accepted as installed, certain of its staff might therefore rejoin the installed systems organization to maintain and enhance it. The installed systems group might also logically be extended to include responsibility for production control of operational systems and for the support of end-user computing.

The reconstructed organization thus clearly has its advantages. What might be the disadvantages or concerns? Paramount might be a concern that the future system unit would be elitist and isolate itself from users even more than is the case today. Also of concern might be the costs of coordinating between the installed and future systems units. Users might also resist working with two units where formerly they worked with one. These concerns are significant ones. However, we believe they are outweighed by the disadvantages of clinging to the illusion of being in the new system development business.

In summary, the reconstructed view emphasizes the role that installed systems play in business operations over the traditional role of providing future systems. The old issue of whether IS should have a “separate maintenance organization” is reformulated to the new issue of temporary housing for future system development. The IS organization is no longer young, expansionary, and primarily in the future systems business. Rather it is mature, responsible, and in the business of tending to its family of installed systems.

## Implementing the Reconstructed View

The proposed reconstruction of the systems development organization is of course only a guiding view, requiring adaptation to local circumstances where implemented. As an organizing concept, it serves primarily to focus attention on the support of business operations in the larger, mature IS organization. In its implementation, it implies a number of other organizational changes as well. Several such changes are suggested, based in part upon our case studies. They are by no means exhaustive of the creative possibilities.

## The well-defined portfolio

A well-defined, commonly understood application system portfolio should be recognized as fundamental to the reconstructed view. It is the principal basis for the organization's design. In general, applications staff should be grouped to work in units corresponding to major systems (or families of systems) within the portfolio (Swanson, 1984). At first, these groups will probably correspond to the same user areas as in the previous departmentalization. But as time goes by, departmentalizing around subject databases or information streams may prove to be more beneficial to the organization.

Surprisingly, all the IS organizations we studied had some difficulty articulating their current portfolios. Commonly understood systems definitions of the portfolios were lacking. Instead, lists of installed data processing jobs often prevailed. Significantly, the failure to organize around an application system portfolio obscures both the products and services provided users and, ultimately, the IS staff effort that must be allocated to these tasks. It thus undermines the reconstructed view.

## Acceptance tests

Given the new emphasis on distinguishing between systems on the basis of installation status, an acceptance test procedure should be established for the formal transfer of system responsibility between the future and installed system work units. Such a procedure may be more or less formal, we found from our studies, but must not be trivialized. Otherwise, the fundamental distinction between installed and future systems is compromised.

## Monitoring system evolution

Because installed systems in the portfolio age tend to grow as described above and also require more maintenance, it is recommended that IS organizations systematically monitor the age, size, and growth of their portfolios. Among our cases, few were taking steps in this direction. Types of repairs and enhancements should also be tracked to provide a proper foundation for planning and control. With adequate data on the evolution of a system, replacement decisions are better informed. The true burden imposed upon the installed system work unit can only be assessed if information about the individual systems is tracked over time.

## Life cycle audits

The ongoing maintenance and continued development of the installed system portion of the portfolio should also be supported by means of a life cycle audit process. Such a process is needed to manage the portfolio on a proactive, rather than reactive, basis.

As conceived here, life cycle audits are intended to monitor a system's health to extend its productive life. Their philosophy is thus forward-looking and contrasts sharply with the classical "post-implementation" audit, which is typically one-time and backward-looking (confirming that the delivered system corresponds to the promised system).

Unfortunately, we find that the typical IS organization does not make use of a life cycle audit process, nor does it track the growth and evolution of systems. Rather, the typical IS organization manages (usually reactively, though sometimes creatively) its “applications backlog.” This posture is regarded here as incompatible with the service orientation of the reconstructed view.

## Assessing staff demographics

The demographics of the IS staff should be assessed on a regular basis in much the same way that the system portfolio is monitored. Among the important staff member characteristics are: length of service, immediate prior job experience, and levels of education and training (Swanson and Beath, 1986). Problems in managing the system development organization may sometimes be traced to mismatches between systems and staff (e.g., as with an aging systems portfolio that must be maintained by a staff with little service experience and, hence, a lack of system familiarity). Attending to staff demographics may assist in the signaling and diagnosis of such difficulties: Among the cases we studied, most had as much difficulty describing their staff characteristics as they did describing their application system portfolios.

## Career path planning

Enhanced career path alternatives for IS staff should also be a focus of management attention. The turnover and morale problems of any IS organization are closely related to the career paths established for its professionals (Bartol and Martin, 1982).

A principal finding of our research is the striking diversity of career paths among IS organizations. This diversity is seen in widely varying policies for personnel selection, placement, and advancement, not only within the IS organization but within the host organization as well.

In particular we note that responsibility for the successful management of strategic systems should be increasingly emphasized in career advancement. Within any application system portfolio, certain systems may be identified as fundamentally strategic. These systems are those that are crucial to the success of the host organization in terms of the smooth functioning of daily operations or competitive position within the industry (McFarlan, et al., 1983).

Such systems may be either installed or future, of course. But responsibility for installed strategic systems should be understood to be the major professional achievement of a systems professional.

## Education of staff

Finally, the future applications staff professional should be better educated in the basic business supported. As users become better educated in computer technology through, for example, the use of microcomputers, they can be expected to assume a larger role in system development. Their expectations will rise as they gain experience with user-friendly off-the-shelf software. In short, future users will be better educated in the business of the IS department.

IS professionals thus face a two-fold challenge. First, they must retain their claim to computing expertise. Familiarity with the traditional mainframe environment will probably not suffice for this claim; older professionals who are not well educated in microcomputing will find it increasingly difficult to work with clients.

Second, users who are increasingly knowledgeable about computing will expect, in turn, that IS professionals become better educated in the basic business supported. Business systems analysis, rather than computer programming skills, should therefore be the focus of professional development.

This emphasis should also enhance the career path opportunities for the IS professional wishing to assume management positions in the host organization. The present research confirms that a number of IS organizations are moving in this direction.

## Conclusion

What then are the implications for the future of the IS organization? As argued here, IS will no longer see itself as being primarily in the business of new application development. Rather, IS attention and responsibility will shift to currently installed systems. The origins of these systems will be increasingly diverse; some systems will have been developed by IS, but others will have been purchased or developed elsewhere in the host organization.

In general, it is agreed, the future environment of the IS organization will be one of an increased infusion and diffusion of information technology throughout the business (Sullivan, 1985). Without doubt, one feature of this diffusion will be widespread new system development activity beyond the confines of the IS organization itself. The result of this activity will be numerous user-developed systems, many of which will compete among themselves for ongoing organizational acceptance beyond the local confines in which they originate (Henderson and Tracy, 1986).

The reconstructed systems development department provides an important means for this evaluation and acceptance. The installed systems work unit may on occasion assume responsibility for user-developed systems, particularly where these systems are judged to be of strategic organizational importance. Beyond this, however, IS may provide the professional expertise needed to support a diversified process of computing application. Specifically, IS may promulgate and enforce software standards, serve as the registrar for systems developed throughout the business, and provide needed reference assistance and consultation in system acquisition and implementation at all levels.

In the long run, there is little danger that the IS organization will, in general, wither away. Whether centralized or decentralized, the acquisition, maintenance and enhancement of the major application system portfolio will constitute an essential business service. Not all of today's centralized IS organizations may fully grasp this, of course. Some may fancy themselves as still in the new system development business. Perhaps, these organizations may reason, maintenance of IS's newly developed systems might simply be turned over to their users: We develop, they maintain. The best of all worlds! Until, perhaps, the users secure the right to obtain IS services elsewhere. Woe to the one that gives away the store in order to avoid providing service!

## About the Authors

E. Burton Swanson is associate professor of information systems at the Anderson Graduate School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles. He received his Ph.D. in business administration from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1971. His previous working associations include 10 years with IBM, primarily in computer application system development. His research interests are in the organizational uses of computer-based information systems and in organizational approaches to system development, implementation, and maintenance. He currently serves as editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research, a new publication of the Institute of Management Sciences (TIMS).

Cynthia M. Beath is assistant professor in the Information and Decision Sciences Department at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. She received her Ph.D. and MBA in management from the University of California, Los Angeles. Prior to her academic career, she worked for more than a dozen years in industry as an information systems professional. Her research interests center on the partnership between the information systems department and its clients.

# Appendix Twelve Cases in Information Systems Maintenance (Reference: Swanson and Beath, forthcoming)

1. Westcoast Refining and Marketing employs an applications staff of 148 analysts and programmers organized into six departments, four of which provide development and maintenance for major functional areas, another of which is engaged in the development of a major new system, while the sixth provides technical support. Turnover among the largely college-educated staff is high, with a median length of service of only two years. H.B., manager of Systems and Programming, considers maintenance to be one of five key problem areas and has organized a Maintenance Task Force, which has in turn enumerated 22 specific problems in need of attention.

2. Nationwide Soft Drink employs 108 applications analysts and programmers in four departments doing development and maintenance. One department maintains systems developed by two of the others. Its staff tends to be less educated in comparison, and a principal motivation is to move “up” to the development groups. F.H., its manager, considers the “stigma of maintenance” to be his most pressing problem.

3. West Coast High Tech Manufacturing employs an applications staff of seven individuals responsible for maintaining a portfolio of 33 systems. Eleven new systems, currently under development elsewhere in the parent organization, are soon to be implemented. These will replace most of the current local manufacturing and material control system. Lack of documentation for existing systems, 24 of which are six years of age or older, is a significant problem. User requests for service are acted on as time permits; no prioritization or review of the requests is made.

4. Diablo National Laboratories employs an applications staff of 67 individuals organized into four departments, each of which develops and maintains a group of related systems. Fourteen major systems comprise the current installed portfolio; three new systems are under development. System users are generally highly educated; so too is the IS staff, with 34 members having masters-level college degrees. In support of a planned move to a distributed computing environment in which users will develop and maintain many of their own systems, user computing skills are currently being developed by rotating job assignments between IS and user departments.

5. National Foods employs an applications staff of 46 individuals. Two departments are engaged in systems development and a third provides database support. Within each of the development departments, a separate staff is assigned to maintenance, termed “support.” Management believes that development and support work appeal to different types of people, and hires accordingly. There is little movement of staff across these work lines. According to one manager, support types like to solve lots of small problems, with quick feedback. “They like the big in-basket, big out-basket,” he says.

6. Integrated Information Technologies employs a development staff of 266 individuals at its East-coast manufacturing location. Organized into 18 work units within 5 departments, all staff members work as programmers. Systems analyst positions are reserved for user departments. Eighty-nine major installed systems comprise the current portfolio; 21 new systems are under development. Approximately 750 user requests comprise the substantial applications backlog, which a recent survey has identified as a major management concern.

7. Big City State University has a staff of 45 systems analysts and programmers organized into two major groups responsible for 28 installed systems serving a dozen user departments. Five new systems, three of which are replacement systems, are also under development. Management makes no formal distinction between development and maintenance work, emphasizing instead “production systems,” which meet certain “Stable Base System” criteria. Responsibility for a major production system is a stepping stone to further advancement within the organization.

8. United Food Stores employs an applications staff of 19 analysts and programmers. Most were recruited from IS units in other companies. United provides little formal training. Thirty-three major installed systems are maintained; two-thirds of these were developed in-house, while the others were procured via custom-built contracts. Several of the installed systems are critical to store operations, and responsibility for their maintenance is reserved for senior people. Overall, because more effort is spent on maintenance than development, turnover among younger staff members, who prefer development work, is high.

9. Small City Manufacturing has a newly created Manufacturing Information Services (MIS) department, which is a spin-off from the “Mother IS” unit from which it originated. Employing a staff of 117 programmer/analysts organized into nine departments, MIS maintains a portfolio of 22 applications providing manufacturing support. No new development work is being undertaken during the present transition period. The staff includes 36 persons who formerly worked as systems analysts for user departments, in addition to those who formerly worked as programmers in IS. All now work in programmer/analyst positions defined to integrate the skills of both groups.

10. Advanced Technologies Manufacturing has an applications staff of 102 organized into two major groups of departments responsible for system development and maintenance. Of 30 major installed systems comprising the applications portfolio, 14 are more than 10 years old. Several have been adopted at multiple plant locations. None of the IS staff has previous IS work experience outside the parent company; most have been hired directly out of school. Company turnover is relatively low; staff who leave IS tend to assume other positions at the plant.

11. Metropolitan Gas Company employs an applications staff of 113 analysts and programmers working in four development units and one technical support unit. Twenty-five major systems are currently maintained; ten are under new development. Sixty-nine staff members spend two-thirds or more of their time on maintenance. Nevertheless, current practice is for all staff members to have both development and maintenance responsibilities. Maintenance assignments are spread around to avoid the “second class citizen” syndrome. No major changes are envisioned. “It’s just a necessary evil,” concludes one manager.

12. Western Aeronautics has an applications staff of 108 analysts and programmers, down from 130 the previous year. With new development work currently frozen while the company seeks a major new defense contract, the staff works exclusively on the maintenance and enhancement of 171 installed systems. A new approach to maintenance is soon to be implemented; a staff of 20 individuals will be responsible for the production software library and will provide the first line of operational support in the event of all production difficulties.
