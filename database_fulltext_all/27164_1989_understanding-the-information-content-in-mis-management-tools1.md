# Understanding the Information Content in MIS Management Tools $^{1}$

By: Christine T. Kydd
Department of Business
Administration
University of Delaware
Newark, Delaware 19716

## Abstract

This article suggests that failure to address the uncertainty and equivocality that exist during the development and implementation of a new management information system is a major reason why projects fail. Uncertainty and equivocality occur naturally at different intensities in the various stages of the systems development life cycle. They can also be introduced into the situation based on the level of technology of the new system. Specific management tools that can be effective for reducing uncertainty, resolving equivocality, or both, are presented and evaluated according to the capability of each for eliminating such conditions. General guidelines concerning application of the framework are provided for managers.

Keywords: MIS failures, information processing, communication, uncertainty, equivocality, communication tools

ACM Categories: K.6.1, C.5.m

## Introduction

At a time when many of us thought that computers had become more user-friendly and individuals more computer-oriented than ever before, statistics still indicate that between one-third and one-half of all information systems projects never reach the implementation stage (Turner, 1982). Numerous other reports of incomplete or unsuccessful implementation efforts continue to appear in the academic literature and business press. Bank of America recently announced the cancellation of a new automated trust accounting system. The announcement stated that the bank had set aside \$80,000,000 to cover the loss incurred up to the point that the project was cancelled (for additional examples see Jones and Kydd, 1988; Lucas, 1975; Markus, 1984; Schmitt and Kozar, 1978). Clearly, such failures are still a major problem faced by systems specialists, managers, and organizations today.

Problems associated with the development and implementation of new management information systems have been attributed to technical issues, social issues, and various interactions between the technology and the individuals intended to use the technology (Lucas, 1975; Markus, 1983; 1984). On the technical side, there can be recurring problems or setbacks based on the chosen technology. It is easy to understand how such problems lead to frustrated systems people and disillusioned users regarding the new system, particularly as due dates pass by with work still incomplete. Such problems may be attributed to either hardware or software. They may depend, for example, on the way in which a new system was designed to link with previous organizational computer facilities or on the way the programs were designed and written. The difficulty stems primarily from the technology rather than from the user.

Many studies suggest that there are also serious barriers to success associated with the social or behavioral side of information systems development and implementation (Alter, 1976; Edstrom, 1977; Ginzberg, 1981a; Lucas, 1976; Nichols, 1981; Schmitt, 1978; Zmud and Cox, 1979). This attention to behavioral and organizational issues has been incorporated into recent implementation theories (Kling, 1980; Markus, 1983; 1984; McFarlan, 1983). Several specific reasons for failure of information systems at the implementation stage, as suggested by these theories, include lack of an appropriate systems champion, change agent or integrator (Ackoff, 1960; Argyris, 1970; 1971; Curley, 1983; Kolb and Frohman, 1970; Lewin, 1947; Nichols, 1981; Schein, 1961; Turner, 1982), poor user attitudes/perceptions/commitment levels (Ginzberg, 1981a; 1981b; Lucas, 1978; 1979), lack of management support (Bean, et al., 1975; Ginzberg, 1981a; Lucas, 1978; Schmitt, 1978), low user initiation and/or participation (Alter, 1978; Boland, 1978; Schmitt, 1978; Zmud and Cox, 1979), and low involvement in project definition and planning by users and management (Ginzberg, 1981a).

There are also broader, organizational situations that contribute to development and implementation problems. Information systems have been strongly linked to an organization's power structure (Kling, 1980; Markus, 1984). Gaining or losing access to information based on the way a new system distributes information often means gaining or losing power as well. Therefore, since implementation of an MIS can significantly change a company's power structure, those on the losing side may not want to accept the system. Other organizational structures such as standard communication patterns, performance evaluation methods, work flow coordination, and organizational culture may also be affected by the introduction of a new system (Markus, 1984).

Although technical features or individual or organizational characteristics alone may contribute to development and implementation problems regarding a new system, many studies ascribe the difficulties encountered to an interaction between the technical and the social aspects (Curley, 1983; Keen, 1981; Kling, 1980; Lucas, 1975; Markus, 1984; Nichols, 1981). A number of important issues in this discussion include (1) the fit between the technology and the individuals who will use it (e.g., have the individuals been prepared technically? Are they ready emotionally and socially for the change?); (2) the fit between the features of the new system and various organizational features (will the system support the current organizational structure and patterns, or will it change them radically?); (3) perceptions of how jobs will be changed by the introduction of the new system; and (4) perceptions of the level of commitment on the part of upper management concerning actual use of the system. This last issue is particularly important, as evidenced by several studies that underline the critical role that upper management's commitment plays in determining the extent to which a new system is accepted (Jones and Kydd, 1987; 1988; Markus, 1984). If the commitment level is perceived to be low, users often "pretend" to use a new system, while still relying primarily on old methods to carry out tasks.

Although problem areas have been identified and suggestions made on how to increase the likelihood of MIS project success, these suggestions vary by specificity and applicability to particular organizational situations. Nichols (1981), for example, conducted a behavioral analysis and suggested a plan of action whereby the systems analyst steps into the user's shoes to gain a true understanding of the job and effects of the new system on that job. Others suggest more general ideas such as the assignment of project responsibility to the user, intensive user education programs, top management involvement, and use of the systems analyst as a technical expert and catalyst for user involvement (see Zmud and Cox, 1979, for example). These and several other researchers have hinted at the need for good communication on both technical and organizational issues among the relevant parties, including systems analysts, users, managers of systems and user personnel, and top management. Ein-Dor and Segev (1977) suggest that top management should create an environment that fosters interaction among participants; Zmud and Cox (1979), Dickson and Powers (1973), Turner (1982), and Boland (1978) all endorse a problem-solving team approach where a sense of mutual trust and commitment among the participants exists to encourage a free exchange of beliefs and opinions; and Boland (1978), Nichols (1981), and Zmud and Cox (1979) emphasize the importance of good interpersonal skills on the part of the systems analyst for the resulting quality of the interaction between the analyst and user.

Communication, therefore, is a critical key to the successful development and implementation of an MIS project. The purpose of this article is to discuss specifically what mechanisms facilitate communication at various stages in the systems development life cycle and in various information systems situations. Situations are defined in terms of the levels of uncertainty and equivocality that are present based on the level of technology used. This characterization uses the information processing framework that suggests that organizations process information to reduce uncertainty and resolve equivocality (Daft and Lengel, 1986; Weick, 1969; 1979). The framework is a useful one for organizing many of the reasons (reviewed above) for failure of development and implementation efforts. It also leads to suggestions for the types of management tools appropriate for various types of projects at various development stages.

In the next section, management tools that are typically used during the systems development life cycle are discussed. Then each tool is evaluated for its usefulness in reducing uncertainty, resolving equivocality, or both. This evaluation is done with respect to both stage in the life cycle and level of technology used in the development environment. Guidelines for appropriate management tools to use in each stage and for each situation are offered.

## MIS Management Tools

There are a number of management tools that are typically used during the planning, development, and implementation of a new information system. Several of these tools are described below.

Systems standards documents generally provide instructions on how information systems should be designed and implemented. Such documents often include a systems development methodology and associated deliverable standards. They provide a great amount of objective information regarding how system work is to be done within a given organization and how standard problems should be dealt with.

A systems integrator, or champion, is a person who acts as a liaison among the various stakeholders of a new information system. This person is able to move such a group of analysts, users, and managers, toward a collective decision regarding the planning, design, and implementation of the system. Such a person provides the strong and unfailing support often needed to complete a systems implementation effort. If he or she is at a high enough management level, an integrator acts to coordinate top management involvement with lower levels of managers (Curley, 1983).

Systems analyst/user meetings for requirements specification are planning meetings. The purpose of such meetings is to determine what user needs are and what the new system will do to meet those needs. Users are often unclear about their needs. Open discussions with the systems analyst helps a user to clarify what the requirements of a new system should be in order to be of maximum benefit to the user.

Preliminary written specifications (a systems requirements document) provide users with a great deal of objective information about the system's capabilities. Such a document informs potential users about the system's major functions before the user has committed to the final system and before the system has been built. Often preliminary specifications serve to provide a written summary of the discussion between the analyst and the users concerning requirements of the system (Gane and Sarson, 1979).

A structured walk-through of design specifications is a useful management tool among analysts, managers and users. This type of walk-through allows the analyst to help the users experience the way a system will work when completed by taking them through the system one step at a time. It is a detailed description of how the system uses data and what the relevant inputs and outputs are at each stage. A walk-through can be useful in clarifying what the system will and will not do and what the final use of the system will be. At this point in development, the user and analyst can still discuss viewpoints that differ and resolve ambiguous issues that arise from the user's lack of understanding of the system to date. The advantage is that the user has something concrete to focus on and can determine what still remains ambiguous or unclear about the system.

A structured walk-through of code is similar to the above but is conducted for the purpose of detecting errors in code during the development process. This tool is very useful to analysts and programmers in determining where the code is incomplete or inconsistent. It also points out the outputs of the system, which can then be compared to specifications.

Prototyping goes one step beyond a structured walk-through. It allows the integration and synthesis of different perceptions of what a system will look like when completed. A prototype is a model of the finished system, which the user can utilize on a limited basis. It is more than just a description; it is an actual working system (Alavi, 1984). Thus, the user can readily identify aspects of the system that are unclear or that the user would like to change. If several different potential users of the same system have vastly divergent views about the system's design and capability, the availability of a prototype allows each user to see where his or her view differs from the actual system. Through several iterations, a consensus can be reached on the final specifications of the system.

User training sessions can serve a variety of needs. They may be used to quickly transmit large amounts of objective data about the use of a system, or they may focus primarily on users' concerns with implementing the system in their respective positions. In the first case, such a session may be used primarily to educate users on the system's use. In the latter case, the participants may discuss various viewpoints and perceptions, working to integrate them into one cohesive and unified view of the new system. Through such discussions, users and analysts can resolve ambiguous issues.

Formal system documentation includes comprehensive information about a particular information system, including how it works, what the relevant input and output files are, whether any databases are involved, and who has responsibility for running, updating, and maintaining the system. For a given system, this documentation should help to answer virtually any question regarding the workings of the system. Since the documentation is specific to a particular system, it may or may not include general systems standards. In some cases, the systems analyst may include in the documentation notes on recurring and unexpected bugs in the system or on changes in responsibility assignment over time.

The planning, development, and implementation of a new information system can be the cause of great uncertainty and ambiguity in an organization. Each of the tools described above has a certain capacity for reducing this uncertainty and/or ambiguity if used in the proper stage of the development life cycle and under appropriate conditions. Following a discussion of the uncertainty/equivocality framework, the tools are evaluated for their potential contribution to each stage and condition.

## The Uncertainty / Equivocality Framework

It has been proposed that organizations process information both to reduce uncertainty and to resolve equivocality (Daft and Lengel, 1986; Perrow, 1967; Weick, 1969; 1979). Uncertainty is defined as the absence of information (Downey and Slocum, 1975; Tushman and Nadler, 1978). Therefore, in order to reduce uncertainty, questions must be asked and additional information gained, usually through the collection of objective data, to learn the answers. The questions in this case are known; it is just a matter of obtaining the information necessary to answer those questions. This information may be in the form of raw data or may be the output of a sophisticated modeling and analysis effort.

Equivocality, on the other hand, is defined as ambiguity and the existence of multiple, conflicting interpretations about a given organizational situation (Daft and Macintosh, 1981; Weick, 1969; 1979). It is assumed that there is confusion and a lack of understanding about what appropriate questions need to be asked. One of the most difficult aspects of equivocality is that it is not feasible to ask a “yes or no” question. In an equivocal situation, the questions themselves must be defined or created; they are not already known. To resolve ambiguity, there must be an exchange of existing views in order to define what the relevant problems are. There is a need for social interaction so that intuition, judgment, and beliefs can contribute to the enactment of a shared interpretation.

Figure 1 shows the framework that emerges when combinations of the uncertainty and equivocality factors are formed. The horizontal axis represents organizational uncertainty. The vertical axis represents equivocality. Each cell describes a general situation for various levels of uncertainty and equivocality. Examples of events falling into each cell are provided in the next section (from Daft and Lengel, 1986).

When both uncertainty and equivocality are low (cell 1), little additional information is necessary, and since a common interpretation already exists, there is little need for social interaction. An example is the use of a routine, familiar technology in a stable and predictable environment. When uncertainty is high but equivocality is low (cell 2), there is a need for additional objective data to provide answers to predefined questions. Examples include a situation of sudden high turnover or an unanticipated customer reaction to a new product feature. Equivocality resolution requires the sharing of opinions and viewpoints in order to lead to a common interpretation that can direct future activities. To support these different behavioral responses, different types of organizational communication mechanisms should be put into place to support the reduction of uncertainty or the resolution of equivocality, as needed.

Following Daft and Lengel's (1986) framework, the particular organizational mechanism required to reduce uncertainty or to resolve equivocality depends largely on the amount and richness of the information needed for a given situation. Amount of information refers to the quantity of data that will be sufficient to answer the questions that arise from the situation at hand. Richness of information, on the other hand, refers to how well a particular communication transaction is able to overcome differing points of view or clarify ambiguous issues in a timely manner. Rich information generally enables debate and discussion to take place so that a consensus can be reached. Communication media that are best at providing rich information are those that allow “face-to-face” meetings, or at least voice contact. In order from best to worst, examples of media that transmit rich information are faceto-face meetings, telephone contact, personal letter or memo, impersonal letter or memo (written), and numeric documents (Daft and Lengel, 1986; Daft and Wiginton, 1979; Galbraith, 1973; 1977; Lengel and Daft, 1984).

For example, underlying goals or targets must be agreed upon before any further activity can be carried out. Rich information processing mechanisms such as face-to-face meetings, group negotiations, and exchanges of opinions help resolve equivocality. Once initial goals are set, then planning also encompasses uncertainty reduction by gathering objective data through less rich media such as written reports and documents.

## Evaluation of MIS Management Tools

The information systems management tools described earlier vary in their capacity to process rich information. Thus, they also vary in their capability for reducing uncertainty and/or equivocality. Figure 3 suggests how the tools might be placed along a continuum with respect to this capacity.

It should be noted that uncertainty and equivocality are not mutually exclusive conditions and often exist together. In fact, one may argue that if equivocality exists, then there must be some uncertainty as well. An argument supporting this view is: If the questions have not yet been defined, how can the involved parties already know the answers? On the other hand, one might argue that equivocality can be high while uncertainty is low: i.e., the questions have not yet been defined; but once they are determined, the answers are readily forthcoming. This article supports the view that uncertainty and equivocality can exist together simultaneously in a given situation in any combination (for example, high uncertainty and low equivocality, low uncertainty and high equivocality, high both, low both). Figure 3 shows these combinations in two dimensions only to suggest where the strengths of each tool lies. This frame is expanded in the next section.

Tools that are best suited for uncertainty reduction include formal systems documentation, systems standards documents, and systems requirements documents. Each of these tools is a written document and therefore has limited capacity for processing rich information. However, each conveys a large amount of objective data and provides answers to questions about a given system or the method to be used in the development of the system. Thus, the primary but not exclusive use of such tools is to reduce uncertainty. A systems development methodology (often part of systems standards) could be considered useful in resolving equivocality also because it provides guidelines for the process to be used in systems development. Also, a systems requirements document could serve as a catalyst for further clarification of system deliverables, if the document does not meet prior expectations of user or analyst. In this way, it contributes to equivocality resolution.

They also can help to process rich information through their social interaction component, enabling participants to resolve equivocality.

Tools that are better at resolving equivocality than uncertainty include a structured walk-through of design specifications, analyst/user meetings for requirements specification and prototyping. In each case, the tool primarily enhances open discussion and the processing of rich information through multiple cues and channels. Thus, each allows divergent views to be expressed and discussed, and enables the development of a common interpretation. These tools may provide objective information as well, thereby aiding uncertainty reduction; but they are seen as contributing primarily to equivocality resolution.

## Tools for Stages of the Systems Development Life Cycle

Based on the continuum just described, it is useful to think about what conditions generally exist during the various stages of a systems development life cycle and what tools would be most useful in reducing either uncertainty or equivocality or both during each stage. The four stages considered including planning, analysis and design, implementation, and operation (McLeod, 1986).

## Planning

The initial planning stage of an information system generally represents a situation of high uncertainty and high equivocality. Neither user nor analyst has a clear understanding from the start about requirements and desired capabilities of the new system. Therefore, a variety of communications tools should be employed during the planning stage.

Since equivocality needs to be addressed first, tools such as a development methodology (part of systems standards), analyst/user meetings for requirements specifications, and use of an integrator are most appropriate at the early stages of planning. A development methodology clarifies for the analyst what the development process should be. It also provides information regarding expected milestones and associated delivery dates to the user. Thus, it helps both analyst and user to focus on what the relevant questions are. A user/analyst meeting for requirements specification is another critical tool through which users and analysts together determine what the system will do. Through a face-to-face meeting, the analyst and user can discuss, debate, and eventually come to an agreement on the system specifications. Such a meeting allows for rich information processing, which is necessary to resolve existing equivocality concerning the new system. Use of a system champion or integrator also contributes to reducing ambiguity at the planning stage. An integrator can serve as a liaison between various user groups and the systems department, or between divergent groups of users of the same system. Through summarization and integration of the various viewpoints, an integrator helps to define a common frame of reference.

Later in the planning phase, such tools as a structured walk-through of design specifications, a written systems requirements document, or a prototype model of the system are useful in reducing ambiguity. The structured walk-through allows both the user and analyst to see how the system is designed to work to date. A written requirements document, although a less rich communications tool, can make a user aware of misspecified or unspecified issues regarding the system. Both tools can be followed up with discussion of potential additions, deletions, or modifications. A prototype model is an excellent tool for reducing equivocality because the user can see exactly how the system works and what it does and does not do. However, a prototype would be appropriate at later stages of planning only because at the very least, some user/analyst discussions would have to precede the building of a prototype system.

## Analysis and design

If the equivocality at the planning stage has been dealt with successfully, then all that should remain at the analysis and design stage of an information system is uncertainty. Thus, this stage is characterized as a high uncertainty, low equivocality situation. Most of the questions regarding the new system have already been determined; however, the answers to those questions are not yet known.

At this stage, tools that should be employed to reduce uncertainty include systems standards documents, preliminary written specifications, and structured walk-throughs of code. Each of these tools helps the analyst primarily to determine how the system should be built. The standards document, possibly including a development methodology, provides very concrete and specific details as to the development process. Preliminary written specifications force the analyst to put down in writing exactly what the system will do and how it will work. Such specifications can be passed along to the user at this stage as well to get early feedback on the validity of the specifications. Written specifications also provide objective information to the user concerning how far the analyst's thinking has come to date regarding the system's development. A structured walk-through of code allows the analyst and programmer to determine whether the code is complete and consistent. All of these tools can be used in the analysis and design stage to reduce uncertainty, i.e., providing objective information that helps the individual answer predefined questions.

During the analysis and design stage of any information system, the analyst can learn that certain desired features agreed upon during the planning phase are infeasible. If this occurs, then both analyst and user need to rethink the design specifications. This, in effect, means that they must return to the planning stage and use the tools indicated there to reduce both uncertainty and equivocality.

## Implementation

The implementation stage of the development life cycle should ideally be one of low uncertainty and low equivocality. However, that is rarely the case. More often, when a system is finally implemented, numerous unforeseen problems occur, both along technical as well as social interaction lines. Thus, this stage is characterized as one of moderate uncertainty and moderate equivocality.

It is appropriate in this stage to utilize tools across the entire spectrum shown in Figure 3. If technical problems arise, they most likely represent uncertainty; i.e., why was some other system affected when the new system was implemented? Or why didn't the new system work the way in which the analyst intended it to work? To answer this sort of question, the analyst needs to collect additional objective data using such tools as systems documentation, standards documents, written specifications or a walkthrough of code. If problems linked to the social network of the company arise, such as an unanticipated shift in perceived power or an intended user group refusing to take responsibility for the system, they are generally problems of equivocality. Thus, such tools as group meetings, use of an integrator, and possibly user education sessions are appropriate.

## Operation

The operation stage of an information system should be one of low uncertainty and low equivocality. By the time the system is in full operation, most of the bugs, both technical and other, have been worked out. This is not always the case, of course. Consider the situation where one firm had a long-standing forecasting system in place, but whose users and analysts went to great lengths to manually force the system to give the desired output. In most situations, however, there is little uncertainty or equivocality associated with a fully implemented, operable system. Events that might create new uncertainty or ambiguity concerning a working system are the effect of a new system on a working system (for example, a bug suddenly develops in a working system that has been accurate for years), or a change in the personnel who control the use of the working system (for example, the system is no longer being used, or is being used incorrectly). Tools that are good at addressing uncertainty in the first case and equivocality in the second case should be utilized to correct these conditions.

Changes in the environment could also occur that would require a complete reassessment of a working system. For example, industry deregulation could nullify most of the underlying assumptions of a given system, in which case users and analysts might be faced once again with conditions of high uncertainty and high equivocality. Thus, tools appropriate for the planning stage would again be appropriate here.

## Tools for Different Levels of Technology

In this day and age computer technology is moving too fast for any given individual to be able to keep up with it. It is important, therefore, to consider the nature of the interaction between potential user groups and the level of the technology they are being asked to embrace. Over and above the individual stages of the systems development life cycle, this interaction can be a source of uncertainty and equivocality throughout the stages of development. In this section, technology is defined in terms of the uncertainty/equivocality framework. Examples of systems that fit into each combination of uncertainty and equivocality are provided along with suggested tools for reducing each.

The level of technology of a new system compared to that of the organization or relevant subset of individuals has been cited as being related to the level of risk associated with implementing the new system (McFarlan, 1981). If the level of technology used in a new system is higher (i.e., more sophisticated) than that which the analysts and users are familiar with, then the risk associated with the project is high. Previous experience with the technology, however, reduces the project's risk of failure.

In terms of the uncertainty/equivocality framework, technology can be broken down into two factors — variety and analyzability — as shown in Figure 4 (Daft and Lengel, 1986; Perrow, 1967; Van de Ven, 1980). Uncertainty is represented by the amount of variety inherent in the set of tasks that the technology performs. When there is little task variety, then the level of uncertainty is low, because little information is necessary to describe the few types of tasks that the system carries out. Similarly, when task variety is high, then uncertainty is also high, since much more information is required to inform users about all of the different types of tasks the system is able to perform.

Equivocality is represented by task analyzability; that is, how easily each task the system performs can be analyzed or understood by the user. When several of the tasks are very difficult to understand (unanalyzable), then equivocality is high. Users faced with using such a system may have problems based on their shallow comprehension of the system. When most of the tasks are easy to understand, then equivocality is low. In such a case, even though the users may still have numerous questions regarding the system, at least they know enough about the various tasks to be able to ask relevant questions. System comprehension is related to situational and personal factors that have been found to be strongly related to successful implementations (Lucas, 1978). This suggests that users are more likely to support systems development and implementation when they understand what the system does. This high comprehension can exist only if equivocality is low.

In Figure 4, each cell represents a combination of task variety (uncertainty) and task analyzability (equivocality). For each category, an example of an information system type is given, along with the general types of communication mechanisms appropriate for addressing uncertainty and/or equivocality.

Cell 1: A well-focused, routine system is representative of this cell, where there is little variety regarding the system technology, and all tasks performed are easily analyzed. An example is an accounting system where tasks are standard and repetitive. In such a case, there is low uncertainty and low equivocality. For most potential users, implementing such a system would create few problems from a technical standpoint since they would understand what the system does. Therefore, they would require little objective information concerning how to implement and subsequently use the system.

Since uncertainty and equivocality are both low, a small amount of information is required, and communication media of low richness are sufficient. Therefore, written specifications, systems documentation from the analyst, and possibly a few training sessions should be satisfactory to the users regarding such a system.

Cell 2: In this cell, there is great variety in the tasks the new system performs, but each of those tasks is easily understood. Therefore, the result is moderate to high uncertainty and relatively low equivocality. An example of this type of system is a production planning system. From a technical standpoint, what the potential user needs in this case is a large amount of information concerning what the system does and how it is used correctly. Questions concerning the system are well known and easily answered with additional data. In general, there are no ambiguous issues concerning understanding of the system.

The appropriate management tools include such media as formal systems documentation (written), user manuals, and user training sessions where the emphasis is on communicating a quantity of information in a short period of time. Tools that foster discussion and debate (provide rich information) are not required in this case, since equivocality is low.

Cell 3: There is little task variety for these systems, although the tasks that are performed are difficult to understand, and therefore, the system falls into the unanalyzable category. This corresponds to low uncertainty and high equivocality. An example could be an expert system. A naive user who is faced with using a new expert system would not necessarily need large amounts of information to learn how to use the system, but may need extensive training to understand what an expert system is all about. Since expert systems are generally based on one individual expert's intuitive decision process, a user may see this process as highly ambiguous. From the user's viewpoint, formulating questions may not be possible. This may hamper willingness to try to use such a system.

To alleviate the equivocality in such a case means making the tasks seem more analyzable, or understandable, to the user. This can best be accomplished through the use of rich communication media such as face-to-face user/expert and user/analyst meetings, a structured walk-through of design specifications, and use of a prototype system. It is easier and less stressful to try to get clarity on a subject through personal, face-to-face attention than through written memos such as formal documentation. In this example, a meeting and/or walk-through between the user and original expert, or a prototype may help the user to better understand the decision process being modelled in the system.

Cell 4: In this cell, there is great task variety, and the tasks are primarily unanalyzable (high uncertainty and high equivocality). An example of a system falling into this category is one of the leading-edge, artificial intelligence or robotics systems. Particularly for the naive AI user, these systems would certainly appear to be very sophisticated and complex. For such systems, there is not only a large amount of information that needs to be transmitted to the potential user, but there are also many ambiguous issues that need to be resolved.

For systems falling into this cell, rich information mechanisms are necessary as well as mechanisms that are able to communicate a great quantity of data. For reducing uncertainty, appropriate mechanisms include formal systems documentation, systems requirements documents, and group user training sessions, all mechanisms where large amounts of information can be provided in a clear and unambiguous manner. Where ambiguous issues exist, differing perceptions regarding the system can be aired and consolidated through discussion and debate in analyst/user meetings or during a structured walk-through of design specifications. An integrator is also useful in this situation as a person who can talk to both the technical personnel involved and the non-technical user, and help to resolve unclear issues between the parties, thus reducing equivocality. An early prototype of the system would further help to resolve ambiguity for the user.

## Conclusions

The framework presented in this article and its application to the information system development and implementation setting is important because it provides an integrative framework concerning the failure of information systems. Many of the factors associated with information system failures in the literature, such as lack of management support, lack of a systems champion, and low user involvement in project definition and planning, result in conditions of uncertainty and/or equivocality and are therefore subsumed under the uncertainty/equivocality framework. Based on this framework, the article suggests that many systems fail because the uncertainty and equivocality that exist at the start of any IS project are not adequately addressed. Uncertainty and equivocality occur naturally (at varying intensities and combinations) in the different stages of the system's life cycle. They also can be created based on the level of technology used in a given system. Thus, it is imperative that analysts, managers, and users work together through all phases of planning, analysis and design, implementation, and operation to reduce existing uncertainty and to resolve ambiguous issues. To accomplish this, the appropriate communication tools must be selected and applied based on each tool's capacity for eliminating uncertainty and/or equivocality.

In order to apply this framework to a specific situation, the following general guidelines are provided for MIS designers, developers, implementors, and managers.

1. Systems people and managers of MIS groups need to be more aware of the fact that uncertainty and equivocality exist at various stages in the systems development life cycle and can lead to problems in completing the system successfully if not adequately addressed. MIS personnel also need to examine the level of technology of each new system for potential sources of uncertainty and/or equivocality.

2. Systems people and MIS managers need to better understand and act to implement the appropriate communication mechanisms that can reduce the particular existing levels of uncertainty and equivocality. This refers to the determination of whether some type of social interaction is required (e.g., use of prototyping, walk-throughs, or meetings) or whether more formal and impersonal management mechanisms (e.g., systems documentation, preliminary specifications, or written reports and documents) are sufficient to resolve the problems created by the uncertainty and equivocality.

These guidelines should not be particularly difficult nor time consuming to implement since all the tools are already in widespread use. The key issue, however, is recognition of the uncertainty and equivocality that may exist at various stages of a systems project and appropriate application of the correct tools to resolve resulting problems.

Future research should address methods or procedures that would help managers to be better at recognizing when uncertainty or equivocality exists. It could also focus on the evaluation of the electronic communication tools that are currently on the market. Such tools as video conferencing or group decision support systems have been designed to replace or enhance more traditional communication methods such as a face-to-face group meeting. Whether these electronic tools can address equivocal situations as effectively remains to be seen, however. Similar issues exist regarding uncertainty. This should be a fruitful area for further research.
