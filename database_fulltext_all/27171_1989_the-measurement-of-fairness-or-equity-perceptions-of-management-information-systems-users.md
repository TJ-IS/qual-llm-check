By: Kailash Joshi
School of Business Administration

## Abstract

This article reports the development of an instrument to measure the perceptions of fairness or equity experienced by users in the context of allocation of information systems resources by the centralized MIS function. Literature in the area of equity and social justice is presented to identify the dimensions of equity relevant to the MIS environment. The article presents the results of data analysis on a cross-sectional survey conducted to refine and evaluate the instrument. Support for reliability and validity of the instrument is presented. Possible applications of the instrument are discussed in the concluding section.

Keywords: Political perspective of MIS, management of MIS, instrument development, user information satisfaction, validity and reliability assessment, procedural fairness, distributive fairness, reciprocal fairness, equity, factor analysis

## Introduction

The existence of power and conflict in the management information systems (MIS) design, implementation, and operation has been well documented in the MIS literature (Danziger, et al., 1982; Keen, 1981; Kling, 1980; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Markus, 1981; 1983; Martin, 1984; Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975; Pfeffer, 1981; Robey and Markus, 1984). User groups compete for information resources and attempt to control the direction of systems development and implementation to their own advantage (Kling, 1980). The use of power and influence in the MIS environment may result in unfair distribution of resources and unfair design choices for some user groups. When users find the process for distribution of information systems resources to be unfair and their own outcomes to be less than what they feel they deserve, they will experience feelings of inequity or unfair treatment.

While conflict among users and maldistribution of information systems resources have been identified in the MIS literature, the consequences of conflict and maldistribution of resources have not been systematically examined. Literature from the equity and social justice area indicates that perceptions of fairness or equity are likely to influence individuals' behavior and attitudes (Ajzen, 1982; Cohen and Greenberg, 1982). Equity theory identifies individuals' basic need for fairness in social exchanges and views changes in human attitudes and behavior as an attempt to restore fairness or equity. Some researchers consider equity theory as a first step in the development of a general theory of social behavior (Walster, et al., 1978).

In view of the nature of the resource allocation process and the existence of power and influence, equity and fairness are likely to be important in the MIS environment as well. Equity and social justice literature can provide a sound theoretical framework for understanding user behavior and attitudes. The inclusion of concepts of equity and social justice can enrich the existing models of user behavior and attitudes and improve our understanding of these phenomena.

While MIS literature has identified organizational behavioral issues associated with users as the major source of problems in the design and implementation of information systems (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977; Demaio, 1980; Ginzberg, 1979; Hawgood, et al., 1978; Lucas, 1975, 1976;

Swanson, 1974; Welke, 1979; Zmud, 1983), it has yet to develop a suitable theoretical framework to explain user behavior. Equity theory has the potential to provide explanations for user attitudes and behaviors. Some user behaviors such as non-usage of systems, resistance to the introduction of new systems, sabotage, user complaints, preference for distributed computing, and user application development (end-user computing) can be examined within an equity framework. Attitudes such as user information satisfaction can also be better explained by considering predictions from equity theory and social justice literature.

Measurement of equity or fairness in the MIS context should provide the means for conducting empirical research in this area and should provide a solid foundation upon which better theories and frameworks of user attitudes and behavior may be developed.

This article examines the relevance of the concepts of equity and social justice to the MIS environment and reports on the development of an instrument to measure information systems users' perceptions of equity or fairness. In the next section, literature in the area of equity and social justice is examined to explore the concepts of equity and fairness and to identify the relevant determinants of equity or fairness in the MIS context. The following section presents a framework for the development of an equity instrument and defines the domain of equity or fairness in the MIS context by including literature from the political perspective of MIS in the analysis. The development of the instrument and the results of data analysis from a cross-sectional survey are presented in subsequent sections. Reliability and validity of the instrument are discussed before conclusions are presented in the final section.

## Theoretical Foundations

The concepts of equity, $^{1}$ fairness, and justice have been recognized and discussed in the social psychology literature since the days of Aristotle. The terms “equity,” “fairness,” and “justice” have been used interchangeably in the social psychology literature for the same concepts. Equity theory, proposed by Adams (1963, 1965), has viewed equity (or fairness) in terms of proportionate equality (Cohen and Greenberg, 1982). Equity theory addresses human beings' eternal quest for fairness or equity in social exchange. It attempts to integrate the insights of reinforcement theory, cognitive consistency theory, psychoanalytic theory, and exchange theory (Walster, et al., 1978).

Walster, et al. (1973, 1978) have extended the propositions of Adams (1965) in the hope of developing the outlines of a general theory of social behavior. The main propositions are:

Proposition I: Individuals will try to maximize their outcomes (where outcomes equal rewards minus costs).

Proposition IIA: Groups can maximize collective reward by evolving accepted systems for equitably apportioning resources among members. Thus, groups will evolve such systems of equity and will attempt to induce members to accept and adhere to these systems.

Proposition IIB: Groups will generally reward members who treat others equitably and generally punish (increase the costs for) members who treat others inequitably.

Proposition III: When individuals find themselves participating in inequitable relationships, they will become distressed. The more inequitable the relationship, the more distress individuals will feel.

Proposition IV: Individuals who discover they are in an inequitable relationship will attempt to eliminate their distress by restoring equity. The greater the inequity that exists, the more distress they will feel and the harder they will try to restore equity.

In assessing equity, individuals compare their outcomes with their inputs to assess whether they are getting a fair return for the efforts or resources they bring to the exchange. In addition, individuals compare their outcome to input ratio with that of others in their reference group, and if individuals find disparity, they find the interaction inequitable (Walster, et al., 1978).

Walster, et al. (1978) have analyzed the impact of power on social justice. Individuals or groups of individuals are expected to differ in terms of their power and influence. The powerful individuals can capture a lion's share of resources, which will result in uneven (i.e., unfair) distribution of resources. The findings of equity research and the analysis of power in social settings as reported by Walster, et al. (1978) can be summarized as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c c c c} \text {Power} & \dashrightarrow & \text {Ability to} & \dashrightarrow \\ & & \text {capture lion's} & \text {distribution} \\ & & \text {share of} & \text {of resources} \\ & & \text {resources} & \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {Perceptions} & \dashrightarrow \text {Dissatisfaction} \\ \text {of inequity} & \text {with the agents} \\ & \text {responsible for} \\ & \text {resource allocation} \end{array}
$$

Initial formulations of equity as defined by Adams (1963, 1965) have considered only proportionate equality. This point of view has been challenged by many researchers who argue that merit, equality, and need are also important considerations in the determination of justice (Cohen and Greenberg, 1982).

Eckhoff (1974) has made a strong distinction between two major classes of social exchange: reciprocation and allocation. Reciprocation exists when one person is directly exchanging resources with another and there is “give and take.” The other major class of social exchange is the allocation of resources. Allocation occurs when an allocator divides resources between two or more groups. An individual who perceives himself/herself to be unjustly treated — that is, receiving less than deserved — will feel anger and display aggressive behavior toward the allocator (Cohen and Greenberg, 1982).

Leventhal (1976b) provides a very thorough analysis of the allocation model and the criteria that play a major role in the assessment of fair allocations. Like Leventhal's formulation, in assessing fairness, individuals evaluate their current levels of receipts by comparing them to the standard defined as deserved. The deserved outcomes are viewed as a weighted sum of deservingness based on different criteria such as contributions (equity), need, equality, or other criteria. Each individual forms his or her deserved expectations based on the weight or importance he or she assigned to these criteria. Therefore, Leventhal's formulation takes into account all criteria that the concerned individual views as relevant. When individuals are asked to assess fairness, they are viewed as comparing their current outcomes to what they consider to be their deserved outcomes.

In addition to equity in the final outcomes, the absence of fair procedures for distribution of resources and resolution of conflicts can also give rise to perceptions of inequity. Leventhal (1976a) has stressed the importance of both distributive and procedural fairness in assessing an individual's perceptions of fairness. "Even when receivers' outcomes seem generally consistent with their deservingness, persons may doubt the fairness of their outcome if procedural fairness has been violated" (Leventhal, 1976a, p. 232).

Researchers in the area of equity and social justice have also examined the response of individuals to perceptions of unfairness or inequity. "Responses may include altered expectations, comparison standards, beliefs or attitudes, or overt behavior" (Cohen and Greenberg, 1982, p. 16). In the context of groups, behavioral reactions to inequity have appeared in the form of reduced output, lower quality, and employee turnover (Walster, et al., 1978). Inequity is also expected to influence attitudes toward the person who is viewed as responsible for inequitable allocation of resources (Ajzen, 1982).

While the initial formulation of equity theory was in terms of individual's rewards and contributions, the social psychology literature has also examined the issue of justice at different levels of analysis. Several researchers have pointed to the importance of the nature and size of the recipient class as a variable in its own right (Brickman, et al., 1981; Cohen, 1979; Cohen and Greenberg, 1982; Eckhoff, 1974).

The equity literature suggests that in the case of exchange between two or a small number of individuals, perceptions of their own receipts and comparison with each individual may be relevant, but in the context of an allocation model where the recipient size is large and individuals belong to different groups, an individual's awareness of outcomes for his or her own group is likely to provide a basis for the individual's assessment of fairness (Wegner, 1982; Cohen and Greenberg, 1982). In addition, the comparison should not be made with a specific individual, but with the outcomes of other generalized groups or units, particularly if the recipients can be viewed as belonging to groups or units (Berger, et al., 1972).

Equity theory has also found application in consumer satisfaction and job satisfaction research, both of which have strong parallels with the concept of user information satisfaction. Equity theory serves as one of the major paradigms in explaining consumer satisfaction/dissatisfaction behavior (Fisk and Coney, 1981; Huppertz, et al., 1978; Swan and Mercer, 1981). Liechty and Churchill (1979) have examined the relevance of ideal, expected, minimum-tolerable, and deserved expectations for consumer satisfaction. Liechty and Churchill (1979) argue that for services, deserved expectations (based on equity considerations) are more important due to the personal commitment and participation in the production process from the consumer.

Researchers in organizational behavior have also identified equity as one of the causal factors influencing job satisfaction (Greenberg, 1982; Locke, 1976; Pritchard, et al., 1972; Walster, et al., 1978). Walster, et al. (1978), in their review of the field studies and experimental research on the applicability of equity theory to employer/employee relationships, conclude that the research has generally provided support for the propositions of equity theory. In addition to pay, rewards such as status and workspace have also been found to operate within an equity framework.

## Defining domain of fairness or equity in the MIS context

The first step in the development of the equity instrument was to clearly specify the domain of the construct and to identify its main dimensions. While equity or fairness issues have not been explicitly discussed in the MIS literature, organizational literature on equity and MIS literature focusing on the political perspective provide guidelines for identifying equity related issues in an MIS context.

Pfeffer (1981) has presented an excellent overview of power and conflict in organizations. In the context of information systems, Pfeffer notes that “the design and location of information systems will be entwined with the struggle for power which occurs in many organizations” (p. 275).

In the MIS literature a number of authors including Bostrom (1980), Danziger, et al. (1982), Keen (1981), Kling (1980), Kling and Iacono (1984), Lucas (1975), Markus (1981, 1983), Martin (1984), and Robey and Markus (1984) have argued in favor of the political perspective of systems implementation. Various consequences and manifestations of power and influence in the systems context have been identified in the MIS literature. These include: control over information resources (Danziger, et al., 1982; Kling, 1980; Pfeffer, 1981), resource politics (Danziger, et al., 1982; Kling, 1980; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Markus, 1981; Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975), conflict in task allocation (Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975), maldistribution of computer resources (Danziger, et al., 1982), and conflicting user priorities (Markus, 1983).

A review of MIS literature on the political perspective suggests that the politics of MIS resource allocation exists mainly at the departmental level rather than at the individual level. Studies by Danziger, et al. (1982), Kling and Iacono (1984), and Markus (1983) demonstrate the intense competition among user departments and the use of power and influence for gaining control over computing resources.

Unlike rewards such as pay and promotions, information systems services are structured at a departmental level rather than at an individual level. For example, consider computing budgets, assignment of systems staff, priorities for development of systems, procedures for determining priorities, etc. These issues are generally applicable to a departmental unit rather than specific individuals.

The issue of fairness or justice is also strongly linked to the use of power and influence in an organizational contest. Therefore, the nature of political coalitions in the organizational context should have an important bearing on the politics of resource allocation and its perception by individuals. Pfeffer's (1981) analysis suggests the importance of the departmental unit in organizational politics and its influence on the frame of reference and perceptions of individual participants.

The MIS environment is characterized by the existence of various user groups or departments that interact and compete for information systems resources. User groups possess different levels of power and attempt to influence the information systems processes to obtain a lion's share of these resources. User groups also interact directly with information systems and provide inputs to develop and maintain the systems. The MIS environment is characterized by competition at the departmental level rather than at the individual level. In the MIS context, the recipient unit is a department and individual users are likely to identify closely with their departmental units. Therefore, in the MIS context, the social psychology literature reviewed earlier (Berger, et al., 1972; Cohen and Greenberg, 1982; Wegner, 1982) and Pfeffer's (1981) analysis clearly suggest that an individual user's awareness of outcomes for his or her department is likely to provide a basis for the user's assessment of fairness, and that comparison should be made with other user groups rather than individuals.

The above view of the MIS environment suggests that the allocation model proposed by Leventhal (1976a, 1976b) may be appropriate for identifying and measuring fairness in the MIS context. The allocation model suggests procedural fairness and distributive fairness to be two dimensions of equity or fairness relevant for the MIS environment. In addition, user groups also participate in an exchange relationship with information systems, where they provide inputs for the development and maintenance of information systems. Therefore, reciprocal fairness is also a dimension of fairness in the MIS context.

Based on the above discussion, three dimensions of fairness that are relevant to the MIS context can be identified:

1. Procedural fairness

2. Distributive fairness

3. Reciprocal fairness

This leads to the definition of the domain of fairness or equity. Equity or fairness in the MIS context may be defined as an information system user's perceptions of fair treatment for his or her group in the allocation of information systems products and services for the development, operation, and maintenance of information systems when compared to other similar user groups in the organization. A user's assessment of fairness will be influenced by his or her perceptions of what his or her group received compared to what it deserved. The assessment of fairness will also be influenced by considerations of procedures that exist for carrying out various processes in the development and operation of information systems. Considerations of inputs provided for designing and operating information systems and benefits received from information systems should also be relevant in the assessment of fairness.

While the dimensions identified in this article represent somewhat different contexts of equity domain, where inequity may be experienced, these dimensions are likely to be interrelated. The perceptions of fairness in the distribution of resources are likely to be related to procedural fairness issues as suggested by Leventhal (1976a). Similarly, design and distribution choices such as priorities and task allocations (distributive fairness) are also likely to influence users' outcomes and inputs, i.e., reciprocal fairness. Therefore, the three dimensions of equity may not be factorially independent facets of equity.

## Development and refinement of measures

The next step in the development of the instrument was to develop measures or items for measuring fairness. The definition of domain of equity in the MIS context and identification of procedural fairness, distributive fairness, and reciprocal fairness as the main dimensions of equity in the MIS context provide guidelines for developing suitable measures for equity in the MIS context. MIS literature facilitates the identification of suitable measures for the dimensions of equity identified in this article.

A number of procedures may exist to guide the development process of an information system. These include: procedures for determining priorities for systems development and procedures for conflict resolution among users (Markus, 1983; Robey and Markus, 1984). If these procedures are not perceived to be fair by users, then users are likely to experience inequity.

Distributive fairness, or fairness in final outcomes, may be judged by users based on the allocation of computer resources (Danziger, et al., 1982), design choices made (Markus, 1983), allocation of priorities (Danziger, et al., 1982), resolution of conflicts (Keen, 1981), and meeting user requirements for normal operations and maintenance (Davis and Olson, 1985). The computer resources may include hardware (Danziger, et al., 1982), computer budget (Nolan, 1979; Wetherbe and Dickson, 1979), systems staff (Danziger, et al., 1982). Design choices made during the design of systems may include consideration of user requirements (Robey and Markus, 1984), permission to access information (Mason, 1986), and system monitoring of user activities (Mason, 1986). In addition, priorities may be assigned by systems staff for development of new systems and modifications to existing systems (Robey and Markus, 1984). Systems staff may also have to resolve the conflicts among user groups (Keen, 1981; Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975) and may be viewed as favoring some user group(s). There may be conflict in task allocation among user groups (Mumford and Pettigrew, 1975), leading some users to question the fairness of task allocations.

Reciprocal fairness, or fairness in exchange with information systems, may be judged based on the inputs that users provide to design and operate the systems relative to the benefits that users obtain from the systems.

The following steps were taken to develop items for the measurement of equity and to refine them through evaluations and field trials involving users, systems professionals, and MIS academics. The validity and reliability of an instrument can only be ensured through a systematic development and refinement process. The steps listed below were designed to achieve these objectives.

1. As previously discussed, literature from the area of equity and social justice as well as MIS was reviewed to identify the dimensions of equity in the MIS context and to identify the conditions under which users may perceive inequity. The domain of MIS-equity was defined with a focus on the issues of resource allocation implied in the context of products and services provided by the centralized information systems function, as viewed from the perspective of non-clerical users. A rough list of items was developed for measuring MIS-equity based on the MIS literature for each of the three dimensions of MIS-equity identified above.

2. The list of items and instructions were evaluated by a team of three MIS academicians, three MIS managers, and four systems analysts. The MIS team was asked to assess the clarity of instructions and the items included in the measure and to suggest instances or circumstances under which users may experience inequity, if these were not already covered in the instrument. The process of review by academicians was repeated a number of times and included a review by two management academicians from the job satisfaction stream of research, in the final stages.

Based on the feedback received, the following improvements were made. Items that referred to more than concept were split into two or more items. The language of the items was refined to make the statements clear and unambiguous. An introduction to equity and detailed instructions for filling in the questionnaire were added to the instrument (see Appendix). Each user was required to respond to the items with reference to his or her organizational work unit. A definition of the term “department” was included in the instructions.

3. The instrument was then pretested by administering it to six experienced MBA students and six users of information systems in the payroll and personnel division of a large organization. Each user was asked to fill in the questionnaire. While filling in the questionnaire, they were asked to point out any items that were unclear, confusing, or ambiguous. After the users had filled in the questionnaire, they were asked to point out any other MIS situations not covered in the questionnaire in which they experienced inequity. The users were also asked to briefly describe how they interpreted each item and what determined their response.

Based on the feedback received, the following changes were made (see the Appendix for the final list of items). The item related to computing time was dropped since most of the users felt that the computing budget was also referring to the same concept. The item on access to information was split into two items: ease of access (physical access) and permission to access information. The anchor “compared to other similar user groups" was added at the beginning of each item. However, for some items this anchor did not make sense because this comparison was already implied — for example, "conflicts with other users about the design of systems have been handled on a fair basis."

At this point the questionnaire was ready for further testing in the field. The items associated with the dimensions of fairness in the instrument are summarized below. Numbers within parentheses represent the item numbers from the equity instrument (see Appendix).

## Method

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to obtain additional data for evaluating the instrument. A total of seven organizations from the Midwest region participated in the study. The organizations included five medium-sized engineering and chemical manufacturing organizations, a government agency, and a major university. Additional users were contacted through short-term executive development programs and evening business courses at a large university. The additional users who were contacted through the classroom were employed in various organizations in regular full-time jobs. A total of 226 questionnaire responses were received, consisting of 165 from participating organizations and 61 from individuals in evening and executive development courses. Nearly 520 questionnaires were distributed, of which 163 were distributed in the classroom. The overall response rate (43%) and the number of respondents (226) appear to be satisfactory for the purposes of this study. Nunnally (1978) suggests that for factor analysis there should be about 10 responses for each item included in a scale. Considering that there are 17 items in the proposed equity scale, 226 responses seem to be adequate.

The survey was restricted to the users of information products and services provided by the centralized MIS function. Clerical users, such as data entry clerks, were excluded. During interviews with users and MIS experts it was pointed out that many clerical users are unlikely to have the necessary inputs to comment on the items included in the equity instrument. For example, items on procedures for allocation of resources, benefits from the system, and conflicts in design choices may not be relevant to many clerical users. Therefore, clerical users were excluded from the survey.

An examination of demographic data (see Table 1) confirmed that up to 93 percent of the users included in the study were making some direct access to reports and screens, and only 7 percent of the users were using information systems exclusively through their subordinates. The average experience of users in using MIS was 8.5 years. The users were also heavily dependent on their MIS. The average dependence reported was close to 6 out of a maximum possible score of 7 in the range 1 to 7. The responses also indicate that users at all non-clerical levels were fairly represented in the survey. An examination of 20 responses where the “Other” response was marked indicated that these users represented staff positions. Therefore, this group of users appears to be a reasonable sample from the targeted population for further data analysis.

## Analysis of Data

Nunnally (1978) and Churchill (1979) have suggested that the first step in the analysis for the development of a measure should be to examine the item-to-total correlations to identify items that may exhibit measurement error or not share the core of the construct. Churchill recommends that factor analysis should be conducted only after purifying the measure by eliminating the suspect items.

On examination of the item-to-total correlations, item number E12 (items are listed in the Appendix) was found to have low negative correlation with the total score. Item E12 was the only negatively stated item; it was reverse-coded before analysis. The negative correlation indicates that respondents may not have noticed the negative wording of this item, resulting in possible response error. Therefore, item E12 was dropped from all further analysis. After dropping E12, the item-to-total correlations were recalculated.

The correlations obtained were all above 0.46, therefore, it was not considered necessary to drop items from the equity scale at this stage. However, an examination of this item-to-total correlation data suggested that item number E10 may not share the core of the construct. Item E10 was identified as having the lowest item-to-total correlation of 0.466. With the exception of E11, the item-to-total correlations of all other items were in the range of 0.572 to 0.758. Therefore, E10 was removed from the item-to-total analysis. On subsequent analysis of item-to-total correlations, E11 was found to have the lowest correlation of 0.488. The next three lowest correlations were 0.568, 0.582, and 0.590, respectively. In view of the relatively large jump in item-to-total correlations between E11 and other items, E11 also appeared to be an item not belonging to the common core. The item-to-total correlation data, after excluding E10, E11, and E12, is presented in Table 2.

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the 226 responses received to examine the underlying groupings of items, with a view to identify the factors that can parsimoniously describe the data. The first factor analysis was conducted on all the items included in the questionnaire, with the exception of item E12, which was dropped from the analysis due to the measurement error mentioned earlier.

The results of factor analysis are reported in Table 3. Three factors were extracted with the eigenvalues of 7.64, 1.40, and 1.17, respectively. All the items load on the first factor at a level above 0.525, and all the items have a greater loading on the first factor, with the exception of item E10. The factor loadings suggest that only the first factor is substantive. Some variance is being pooled on the second factor probably because of the wording of items E10 and E11 referring to “access to information.”

Results of factor analysis suggest that equity construct is essentially represented by the first factor on which all the 16 items load. Considering the results of the Scree-Test (Norusis, 1985), the size of eigenvalues, the proportion of variance explained by the factors, and factor loadings, it can be concluded that the second and third factors are not substantive. Moreover, the second and third factors also do not correspond to the dimensions conceptualized in the theoretical analysis.

Recall that results of the item-to-total and item-to-item correlation analysis suggested that items E10 and E11 do not fit well in the core of the construct being measured. Therefore, it was decided to drop E10 and E11 from the equity instrument. A second factor analysis confirmed the essentially single factor nature of the equity construct, represented by 14 items, E1 to E9 and E13 to E17. The correlation between the 14-item scale selected and the 16-item total scale (excluding E12) was calculated to be 0.990 at a significance level below 0.001. The high correlation obtained suggests that the 14-item scale is almost as good as the 16-item scale. The reliability of both the scales is the same at 0.926. Therefore, in the interest of parsimony, it was decided that the 14-item scale can provide a fair representation of the equity construct. Equity should be measured by averaging responses on items E1 to E9 and E13 to E17.

It should be noted that items belonging to all three dimensions are represented in the 14- item scale. The domain sampling model of measurement suggests that it is not necessary to include all the items that can be conceptualized for a given construct. In the interest of parsimony, it is preferable to identify a minimum set of items that can represent the domain and achieve a high reliability. In instrument development, the objective is not the development of a perfect representation, but the development of a valid representation of the construct that has an acceptable reliability for its intended use. Progress on meeting these objectives is discussed in the next section.

## Validity

In assessing the validity of an instrument, the focus is on determining the extent to which the instrument measures what it is intended to measure. Nunnally (1978) has identified content validity, predictive validity, and construct validity as central to any construct measurement.

## Content validity

Content validity depends on the extent to which the items making up the measure are a representative sample of the domain of items associated with the variable being measured (Nunnally, 1978). If items that are outside the construct are included in the instrument, construct validity will suffer.

The support for content validity is provided by the theoretical basis for the development of items and through evaluation of the content by MIS experts and users. In this study, the dimensions of construct were identified based on equity and social justice literature. Specific items in each dimension were identified based on support from MIS literature, as well as through interviews with users and MIS experts. The items were further refined based on feedback from users in the field and MIS experts. In the final field trials, users were very positive about the content validity. Ten MIS experts also gave very positive feedback on the content validity of the instrument and unanimously agreed that the scales seem to be a fair representation of the domain and that none of the scales appear to be outside the domain.

## Construct validity

Construct validity is related to what the instrument is in fact measuring. It is established by showing that it is an appropriate operational definition of the construct it intends to measure.

Construct validity of a measure is supported if two different measures of the same construct demonstrate high correlation. In the first 28 responses, users were also asked to respond to a single item assessing the overall perceptions of equity. The item is reproduced here:

Compared to other similar user groups, overall, my department has been treated fairly in the development and implementation of information systems.

$$
\begin{array}{c c c c c c c} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \end{array}
$$

The total equity scores obtained from the average of 14 items included in the equity scale were correlated with equity scores obtained from the single item scale. A correlation of .82 was obtained with a significance of below 0.001. The high correlation between two somewhat different measures of the same construct also supports the construct validity of the equity instrument, though it should be acknowledged that while the measures may be somewhat different, the method is the same.

The results of factor analysis also provide support for the construct validity of the instrument. One major aspect of construct validation is in determining to what extent hypothesized measures of a construct measure the same thing or break up into clusters of variables that measure different things. It has been demonstrated that items in the equity scale are best represented by a single factor on which all the items in the scale load. Nunnally (1978) notes that, “... the clustering of variables as done in factor analysis constitutes a very important aspect of construct validation.” Therefore, it appears that the instrument has achieved reasonable construct validity.

## Conclusion

This article presents a first attempt at developing an instrument to measure fairness or equity in the MIS context. The development of the instrument has been grounded in the equity and social justice literature and the MIS literature. The instrument has demonstrated a high reliability (.93). Discussion and evidence to support the validity of the instrument have been presented. While the validity of an instrument can not be fully established by a single study, the equity instrument appears to be suitable for use and evaluation in future studies.

Before considering the possible uses of the instrument, it is necessary to point out some limitations in what has been achieved. The study has focused on the equity concerns of non-clerical users. The instrument has been developed and tested through non-clerical users. Therefore, it is appropriate for non-clerical users. However, clerical users are also an important segment of the user population. Excluding clerical users has limited the scope of this study. Future research should also include clerical users in the investigation of equity concerns. Clerical users are also likely to have perceptions of how equitably their departments have been treated. While the awareness of resource allocation issues and equity concerns of clerical users could be somewhat different, the three dimensions of equity and many items developed for these dimensions are likely to be applicable for clerical users as well.

The instrument has been developed in the context of centrally managed information systems products and services that are used by multiple user departments who share common resources and resource constraints. The measure does not apply to user-developed systems or very small organizations with basically one department or only few employees.

The instrument could be useful for conducting empirical work in the area of the political perspective of MIS, which is based primarily on case studies and theoretical analyses. The influence of power and maldistribution of computing resources could be empirically examined through the instrument.

The instrument may also be useful for researchers and professionals in the area of management of MIS. The instrument may be used to evaluate the impact of various resource allocation mechanisms, such as steering committees, budgeting, chargeback systems, and various user involvement and conflict resolution mechanisms. A major concern of MIS executives is how to enhance the “customer satisfaction” of their users (Halbrecht, 1978). This instrument can be used along with other user information satisfaction measures to identify the major source and location of user discontent, so that appropriate actions can be taken.

The equity and social psychology literature suggests that inequity is likely to influence attitudes and behavior. Therefore, consideration of equity perceptions in the MIS context could provide additional relevant information to the researchers of user attitudes and behavior. Measurement and analysis of equity issues could potentially lead to better models of user attitudes and behavior.
