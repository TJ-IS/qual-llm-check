## Abstract

Telemarketing is a growth industry that falls within the realm of information management. The telemarketing services provided by AT&T American Transtech grew from non-existence to a position of leadership in the industry in just four years. The organization was able to accomplish this feat with a total systems approach using innovative methods for approaching and satisfying new markets. Hardware innovations, including the use of ISDN, along with a humanized implementation have been combined in this successful effort. This article describes the tele-marketing service effort of American Transtech and the lessons learned that could be applied to other information management organizations.

Keywords: Telemarketing, ISDN, strategic advantage, organizational impacts, human factors

## Background

American Transtech was originally formed in 1983 as a separate subsidiary providing financial services for over 22 million AT&T and regional holding companies' shareholder accounts. Since divestiture, American Transtech has recognized a new business opportunity and has provided additional services to other organizations in the rapidly growing field of direct marketing. This growth and diversification was essential to continue to exist as a successful business entity.

In its new direct marketing endeavor, Transtech offers inbound and outbound telemarketing, direct mail and fulfillment, list management, and database management services. This is no small endeavor. In one sample year, Transtech managed more than 22 million inbound and outbound telephone calls, including 6.5 million direct marketing calls, 140 million pieces of mail, and 150 million computer transactions. In less than four years, American Transtech has rapidly grown into a leading telemarketing center, providing a full range of services to a wide variety of customers. There is much to be learned on how this was done and why this growth was possible. The information executive cannot ignore this area.

There has been a plethora of literature related to gaining a competitive advantage through the use of information technology (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Parsons, 1983; Rackoff, et al., 1985; and Wiseman, 1985). The literature has been expanded to the use of telecommunications (Keen, 1986) and has even focused on telemarketing (Stone and Wyman, 1985). Combining all of these related areas will be a necessity for the information executive. This article focuses on the use of information management in the telemarketing effort and the lessons learned from this experience.

## The Telemarketing Industry

Telemarketing is a growth industry. It is used by over 80,000 companies to enter new markets, improve sales productivity, provide superior customer service, research markets, and be more accessible to customers. Four hundred of these companies are service bureaus, providing telemarketing capabilities to companies that chose not to set up telemarketing systems themselves. American Transtech is the fifth largest telemarketing service bureau in the country.

Some people may look down on telemarketing. A small portion of telemarketing includes the people who disturb you in the privacy of your home and try to sell you a magazine subscription. But telemarketing also includes catalog sales and order taking, seasonal peak selling, renewals, such as for magazine subscriptions via telephone, customer product or service help information, sales lead qualification, dealer location information, and “800” numbers that provide potential customers with information. In fact, many information management groups may already perform a form of telemarketing with user “help lines” or vendor “hot lines” that provide assistance for current information systems users.

The focus of telemarketing is on revenue enhancement rather than cost reduction. This may be a new view for IS managers who have classically focused on cost reduction. The revenue enhancement is done through one-on-one personal contact from an agent who is there to serve a person at the end of the phone line. The telemarketing agents are consultants and information providers to the potential buyers, who may be seen as the decision makers. An individual can talk to someone else perceived as an expert by dialing a telephone from the convenience of his or her home or office.

However, there may be opportunities for cost reductions, such as warranty cost reductions, where the customer may be able to fix minor problems with a product. The GE Answer Center, for example, has handled many customer appliance problems by having a service agent help a customer over the phone. This center currently handles over two million calls per year (Stone and Wyman, 1985), calls that save travel costs and mailing costs. Other cost reductions could come from encouraging customers to call for more information after viewing a brief advertisement thereby reducing the size and amount of advertising needed. Details then could be provided to interested customers by telephone.

Telemarketing services can fill many organizational needs. Services can be either inbound or outbound. Inbound services allow handling requests from existing or potential customers on products, services, promotions, or dealer locations. Outbound services involve contacting potential customers to attempt to sell a good or service or to conduct market research surveys.

Telemarketing can be used by very small to very large organizations in a variety of industries. The telemarketing effort must be seen by the information executive as a part of information management. Critical data, often giving an organization a competitive advantage, can be collected or disbursed through a telemarketing system.

Telemarketing requires a complex combination of computing and telecommunications hardware and software; a carefully planned and managed set of procedures; a motivated work force; and a unified business strategy linking information technology to the organization's mission. (See Figure 1.)

Needed hardware includes: a call distributor or switch to send calls to appropriate agents; processors and stored databases to record incoming data and to access data for inquiries; local area networks to distribute incoming calls; and various input voice units to record messages to be sent. Software includes: a call management system to collect data from the switch; report generation to measure and control the telemarketing process; and telemarketing software that cues agents with screens containing scripts allowing order taking, branching based on customer responses, and access of appropriate databases to provide data for the scripts. All of these components must be carefully integrated.

The proper integration of hardware, software, databases, procedures, and people will lead to desired productivity. Measures of telemarketing productivity differ from other information processing activities. Two critical elements are speed of answer and abandonment rates. Speedy answers will allow more calls to be handled. Abandonment rates are connections not made when the incoming caller hangs up because he or she feels that too much time was spent waiting for an agent.

With the above environment, you can make an agent of your organization available anyplace at any time without your customers being aware that they are not being served from your home office with your employees. Providing effective telemarketing is and will be a real challenge for information managers. Productive telemarketing agents with supporting hardware/software are needed to work effectively and efficiently. American Transtech faced and met such a challenge.

## The Business Challenge

In 1984, American Transtech began its direct marketing line of business with a single inbound telemarketing project. The project involved a major hotel that was running a “free trip” promotion that turned out to be more popular than expected. American Transtech was asked to assist with customer inquiries. The staff and facilities were put in place within four days to handle the project, including trained personnel working with a tested script. The evolution of this type of service from a series of special projects to an established line of business has required both a highly motivated, customer-focused staff and the use of integrated networking and computer technologies.

A careful analysis was done to determine the critical success factors (Rockart, 1979) in the tele-marketing area. American Transtech realized that its success would be linked to its ability to efficiently provide:

\- A flexible full-development cycle for telemarketing services, including the design, development, implementation, and management of telemarketing projects. A methodology for handling and satisfying requests, much like an information systems development life cycle, would be needed. Each request for service should be seen as a project to be planned, analyzed, designed, and implemented.

\- Innovative, integrated solutions to meet customers' needs. Reducing time per incoming or outgoing call is a big factor in terms of revenue enhancement and cost reduction for the telemarketing service. For example, quickly identifying a caller's geographic area on an inbound request for product service would allow the call handler to access a database that would allow answering the caller's question. The agent then could move quickly to the next question or caller.

\- Cost-effective project management of custom services. A particular market niche is to provide smaller clients with custom services. The dilemma is to provide these custom services at a low enough cost so that a small client will still see the service as economically feasible. Of course, generating custom scripts and maintaining the caller's perception of being serviced by a dedicated call handler would have to be economically feasible.

\- Proven responsiveness and quality customer service. This could be done through providing a superior working environment for both permanent and temporary staff. Telemarketing operations could turn out to be a “sweatshop” operation where turnover and associated expenses are very high. Human resource expenditures account for nearly 50 percent of the cost of operation. A superior working environment enables an organization to attract and keep a work force of skilled temporary employees who can be activated and released as work loads change.

The importance of handling calls quickly cannot be ignored. American Transtech currently handles six and a half million direct marketing calls annually. A savings of 3 seconds per call results in a capacity increase of 106,000 more calls that could produce more revenue at no cost.

## The Information Systems Challenge

In order to support the objectives stated above, a unique approach to integrate both voice and data technology in a humanized setting was required. In addition, management information was needed to successfully reap the benefits of the telemarketing approach. With integrated networking, switching, and voice capabilities, the system needed the flexibility to:

\- Handle sudden shifts in call volume by reassigning agents. Call volume is not constant and often has sudden and severe shifts and spikes. Some agents could be overloaded, while other agents handling different accounts could be sitting idle. Often there is not enough knowledge about incoming calls to predict the needed capacity.

\- React quickly to new customer requirements. For example, unpredicted initial response to a marketing promotion may require an increase in agents, a change in scripts, added hours of service availability, or additional switching capacity.

\- Provide personalized service by routing calls to agents specifically assigned to a project. This allows incoming calls to be handled by knowledgeable agents who can provide the most intelligent responses.

\- Generate information for detailed management reporting on cost efficiency. Managing with real-time, up-to-date information allows for better control and resource allocation. Schedules can then be adjusted based on an analysis of the information received.

The job of business is to be ready with the product or service when the customer needs or wants it. The task is to make it as painless as possible for the customer to buy or get further information about a produce or service.

The job of IS is to get the right information to the right agent at the right time to match the product or service with the needy customer. For example, a financial services firm would provide services to people at retirement age or provide an equity loan when a mortgage is paid off. IS people provide more precise and accurate information that allows an organization to better focus its selling resources.

An organization must know the buying history of customers so it can be there to sell the product or service when the customer needs it. This must occur before a competitor “just happens to show up” when a need is generated. An example is to follow a customer’s pizza-buying patterns and then call to suggest pizza when the pizza seller is running a special.

## An Innovative Solution

Through the use of a state-of-the-art voice and data switching system, American Transtech has the ability to monitor, distribute, and analyze incoming and outgoing calls. This technology is currently available and would fit in any other organization's information management plan. The technology has been combined with the following activities to give American Transtech a competitive edge.

## Improved managerial information generation

To address the need for improved managerial information, a digital voice/data switch was selected as the hardware foundation for Transtech's solution. Working in conjunction with the switch is a sophisticated call management software system that enables managers to monitor, in real time, contractual criteria (i.e., speed of pickup, length of call, etc.). This may be done for individual projects or groups of projects, and it allows for the easy redirection of calls to make maximum use of available resources. This capability is one of the most important in meeting productivity criteria, since it allows managers to keep agents and available equipment assigned in the most efficient way.

## Vectoring

A critical question that managers must answer is, “Once a call comes in, what do you do with it?” American Transtech’s answer to this question comes in the form of “vectoring.” Vectoring is a critical component of managing inbound calls. It is a simple-to-program language that allows transfer of control when handling an incoming call. (See Figure 2.) The vectoring language in the example specifies what to do based on various date and time parameters for the project (see steps 1 through 4 where a call is either accepted and placed in a queue in steps 7 through 10 or the caller hears a prepared message and is disconnected in steps 5 and 6), what to do when all agents are busy (see step 11 where the caller is put on hold with music for 30 seconds), and what to do if too many calls stack up (see steps 7 and 14 where the caller receives a busy signal from the switch so they will hang up and call back). Through this language, all incoming calls for a given project can be distributed appropriately; project overflow conditions can be efficiently handled; and contracted customer service levels can be maintained. The vector specifications can be changed easily by an authorized manager.

Once a call is accepted and placed in a queue, it must be handled by an agent or “communicator.” Telemarketing is an extremely labor-intensive business. In order to effectively manage this human resource component, sophisticated management reporting and control capabilities must exist. At American Transtech, these capabilities exist at several levels. The first level, as mentioned above, contains the ability to monitor and change the disposition of incoming calls through the call vectoring facility. This allows for the possible redistribution of overflow calls from a particularly busy project during a peak period to idle agents who may have reached a lull of activity in their projects.

## Real-Time Monitoring

The second level of control exists with the real-time monitoring and reporting of all telemarketing work group activity. While call vectoring handles the distribution of the calls, this second level tracks the disposition of the calls once they have arrived into the work center. (See Figure 3.) Information at this monitoring level is updated every 30 seconds for all work groups. An individual work group may be dedicated to a single client or shared by multiple clients and may be working on a mixture of outbound or inbound applications.

The data captured and updated, as shown from left to right in Figure 3, includes: group identification, the number of idle agents available for a call, the number of agents on the automatic call distribution system, number of calls handled, average speed of answer (secs.), average length of call (secs.), the number of callers that have hung up while waiting in queue (abandonment rate), the number of callers waiting in queue, how long (secs.) the oldest call has been waiting in queue, the number of calls that are overflowing into or out of a back-up work group, and the percent of time agents are spending on-line talking to a caller. In addition, a special exception reporting area is displayed at the bottom of the screen that tracks various customer-contracted performance measures such as speed of answer, talk time, number of calls in queue, and on-hold time. An audible tone will sound to alert a monitoring manager to an instance outside of any specified parameters. Utilizing this information, vectors can be changed to redistribute calls to take the best advantage of the available human resources; percent occupancy times can be tracked to avoid agent burn-out amongst the work groups; and potential work-flow problems in projects can be avoided before they impact customer-contracted performance measures.

## Project Manager Control

The third level of quality control exists with the project manager who can monitor detail data on individual agent performance for their projects. The project manager has real-time data similar to that shown in Figure 3 for the work groups but also will receive historical data on individual agent and work group performance. Through analysis of this data, calling trends can be tracked, and scheduling and forecasting requirements can be adjusted to prepare for anticipated peak periods.

Coupled with the ability to monitor and handle shifts in call volume, the technology allows Transtech to effectively manage its most important resource, its people. The sophisticated switch is supported by an improved technological capability that many information managers will be evaluating in the future.

## Improved technological capability

Additionally, American Transtech is utilizing ISDN (Integrated Services Digital Network) to reduce costs and provide unique capabilities for its customers. One such capability is its dealer locator services. Through ISDN, three simultaneous activities occur: when a caller desires to know the closest dealer offering a product or service, the caller's area code and telephone number can be captured and instantly sent to a customer database; the process uses the 800 number that was dialed to identify and display for the agent the client and project, allowing the agent, who may be assigned to several projects, to answer the phone already knowing the client's name and customer information. An additional database is searched to retrieve the name and phone number of the dealer that is geographically closest to the caller's telephone number. The dealer information is automatically displayed on a terminal as an agent answers the phone. (See Figure 4.)

This automatic retrieval and display of information to an agent dramatically reduces the contact time of each call, while both capturing and providing up-to-date information for both the calling party and American Transtech's telemarketing client. This capability also allows an agent to focus his or her energies exclusively on the client rather than on the technology necessary to access needed information from a database.

Through this one application of ISDN, American Transtech has been able to save an average of 12 seconds per call. This equates to a 20 hours per week savings for an eight-agent pool of workers. In addition, telecommunications costs have decreased 3.6 cents per call. This significantly impacts the bottom line of Transtech when one considers the total volume of calls processed over a year. Along with the above savings in time and dollars, a previously measured 10 percent inaccuracy rate in the gathering of customer phone number information has disappeared through the use of ISDN.

American Transtech is also utilizing voice response technology to provide its customers with an enhanced level of service for applications such as market research studies and capturing of customer feedback data. This technology also helps solve one of Transtech's most pressing human resources issues, that is, a diminishing labor pool to handle client accounts. This application of voice response technology could lead in the future to a combination of ISDN data capture capabilities and automated voice prompts. This would further reduce the number of inbound telemarketing applications that need to be handled by the customer service agents. In essence, the communicator could be taken out of the

ISDN loop in Figure 4 to have a completely automated application. All telemarketing applications could not be handled in this manner, and American Transtech is very sensitive to the types of applications for which this solution may be appropriate.

## Improved script software development

Another vital part of American Transtech's direct marketing solution is its specially developed telemarketing software to generate communicator scripts that can be easily tailored to meet individual project needs. Prior to development of the software, Transtech could not efficiently service smaller telemarketing clients. This unique software handles the database needs of small-scale projects quickly and at a reasonable cost. It provides flexible, results-oriented control of all American Transtech projects. This language could be considered a new type of generator, a 4GL for script generation. This is an example of utilizing automation to off-load activities that humans may find either tedious or difficult.

Through the use of this software, an entire tele-marketing project can be created in as little as two hours. This allows for tremendous flexibility in meeting rapidly changing customer requirements and provides American Transtech with a significant competitive advantage in the small project market. American Transtech was responsive to customers' needs by providing a custom service in a non-custom time frame. This may be a future standard for all IS groups.

## Organizational/behavioral aspects

Not all the changes have been of a hardware/software nature. Other changes have focused on the organizational and behavioral aspects. This exemplifies the need to take a total systems approach, not just add technology and hope for the best. First, organization structure of American Transtech has changed. The structure has become much more flattened, with decision making moved further down the organizational hierarchy. (See Drucker, 1988.) With regard to the flattened organization structure, someone at Transtech commented that “the word comes from the mountain, but the mountain is not very high!” Second, the attitude of the employees has changed. The people in the organization at all levels are much more customer-based than product-based. This has included a focus on customer needs before any solutions are proposed. People at Transtech do not approach clients with solutions and hope to find (or even create) a problem that they can solve.

Training is taken very seriously at American Transtech. Every agent receives extensive training on handling of customers on the telephone. The skills are not just assumed but are tested. The agents receive cross training so they can perform for many different clients. This cross training allows better load balancing and scheduling flexibility by allowing the agent to handle different accounts when there are demand fluctuations. Role plays and dress rehearsals are used to assure that an agent can handle different caller types. Since inbound call handling is not scripted as with an outbound call, the agent must be trained to think and react quickly to handle unusual customer calls. The caller even may present threats or harm, so the agent may have to react as quickly as someone handling a "911" line. On complex projects, a trainee may be required to apprentice under an experienced agent.

An important aspect of training is maintaining motivation. Handling rejection on outbound calls is difficult for agents. Likewise, increased calling rates made possible by current technology have given communicators the chance to be rejected faster and more often on outbound calls than previously possible. Management is aware of this issue and uses information to provide feedback to agents on their performance.

If the agent is working with a client company providing product information, the agent may go through training on the client's products. American Transtech agents have even been trained by clients who have included plant tours, details about quality assurance programs, and information on competitors. Once the agent answers a call for a client, it is nearly impossible for the caller to detect that he or she is talking to an American Transtech agent and not an expert at the client company. The team spirit of the calling teams is openly shown at American Transtech by the display of clients' promotional materials in the work areas.

With regard to performance, many clients contractually require certain quality monitoring. Throughout the day, agent calls are monitored by supervisors who do written evaluations that not only detect and mention weaknesses but look for superb performance as well. Areas that are carefully monitored include identification and addressing of customer needs, conveying information correctly, courtesy and professionalism, and collection and verification of customer data that must be collected for the client. Remote monitoring, done by clients, is also included in performance evaluations and serves to strengthen the client/agent relationship.

Important components in American Transtech's solution include:

\- Redesigned jobs — more freedom and flexibility

\- Redesigned organization — less management layers to get the job done

\- Redesigned work units — teams that focus on the customer

\- Redesigned hardware / software environments

While these efforts may appear to be both costly and time consuming, the bottom-line benefits to American Transtech have offset these costs.

## Business Benefits

American Transtech has applied and managed new technology to make quantum leaps in cost savings, quality of service, and improved work environment. It should be remembered that telemarketing applications at Transtech have grown from non-existence four years ago to a level that positions Transtech as one of the top five service bureaus in the country. Much of this was due to having all the pieces in place to produce and implement a telemarketing application in a short period of time. The use of advanced, integrated data processing and communications systems has enabled AT&T American Transtech to:

\- Reduce average call response time from 20 seconds to less than eight seconds. This was accomplished solely through the transition to a new digital switch that would recognize idle agents more quickly. The older analog switch took more time to reset itself after recognizing that an agent had terminated a call.

\- Reduce the length of dealer locator calls an average of 12 seconds (this equates to 3.6 cents savings per call). This was due to the use of ISDN to capture caller data and query online databases.

\- Increase the accuracy of data collected on callers by 10 percent. This is due to machine capture of data and is another example of using the machine for its strengths and humans for their strengths. Having this capability has allowed American Transtech to garner new clients and increase business from existing clients.

\- Respond promptly and thoroughly to client needs. At American Transtech you will not hear agents laughing as they ask their clients such questions as, "You want it WHEN?!" The response is one of quiet confidence that the project can be implemented and operated to please the client.

\- Increase the quality of customer service (i.e., avoiding the loss of current customers, increasing revenues from current customers, and adding new customers). For example, some customers who are too small to do their own telemarketing use service bureaus. Yet these types of projects have not been practical for service bureaus due to the high programming expenses and non-productive time of dedicated agents. Transtech's solution allows for rapid script generation and for small clients to share an agent, making this market niche a possibility.

\- Make maximum use of its human resources to benefit both the customer and the bottom line. Transtech's 4 percent turnover rate of permanent employees is extremely low for the telemarketing industry. This is occurring even though several large competitors have relocated into American Transtech's geographic area.

\- Pursue market leadership in a highly competitive field. Transtech's advanced technology and commitment to excellence earned it a rating in Telemarketing magazine as one of the top five service bureaus in the country.

The synergy of network equipment, hardware, software, personnel, and project management has created a significant competitive advantage for both American Transtech and its customers. AT&T American Transtech has applied innovative approaches to information management in a modern-day production environment to achieve its success. New applications where American Transtech is doing pioneering work with technologies such as ISDN and voice generation to conduct market research are positive directions that could allow future growth.

## Lessons Learned for Information Executives

Telemarketing provides a personal one-on-one high-touch experience by using high-tech communications and database technology. This is a rare case of allowing agents of your organization to be more humanized by using technology. Communication and database technology allow the agent to “remember” and know things of interest to the caller. This allows the agent to be more thoughtful by holding a personal conversation with the customer and assuring satisfaction with products and services. Studies have shown that for catalog firms, the average phone order is 20 percent greater than the average mail order (Stone and Wyman, 1985). Information is the key, since the order taker turns into a consultative seller offering additional products that may be of interest to the caller. IS plays a critical role in providing the information that is needed to perform consultative sales and cross selling. This is especially true if there is a special sale or bargain that may be offered to the customer.

Since the telemarketing activity is so dependent on technology, IS should play a leadership role in it. But to do this, the IS executive must make a special effort to understand the telemarketing effort and where information management can provide support. Figure 5 presents a view of some of the important support roles. IS managers should team up with marketing managers to explore the possibilities within their organization. With regard to generation of leads, it should be remembered that it is likely that 80 percent of your business comes from 20 percent of your customers. This 20 percent must be identified and specially treated. The other 80 percent also must be identified and approached differently. Each group is a different target that the organization must attempt to hit. With regard to scheduling of sales calls, an excellent customer profile with good history can predict when a customer is ready to buy again. Ease of buying when a need presents itself is all some customers need. IS should provide a telemarketing agent with information that could personalize a phone conversation. Customers who perceive that the organization is treating them in a special manner will become loyal to a product line.

An activity like checking of credit should be done automatically so the activity is transparent to the customer. With regard to inventory, managers must realize that it is difficult to sell a product that is not in stock. Once a product is sold, an agent can be provided with prompts and scripts to contact the customer to assure satisfaction and at the same time be there with other products to sell. An example is L.L. Bean, which brings up past orders when a customer places an order and checks to see that the customer is satisfied with past purchases. This aligns with L.L. Bean's corporate strategy of replacing any product that a customer is not happy with.

IS managers must not ignore pioneering technology. American Transtech has access to leading technology through its “mother” company, but Transtech management still believes that they must be risk takers to achieve the biggest benefits. Often it is not just the technology that could lead to success or failure, but attention must be given to the complete package of tele-marketing components.

Managers must not be complacent in these applications but must question the obvious and improve applications even if they are not broken. Managers must be proactive rather than reactive in a changing environment. Managers must realize that they cannot do it the way they have always done it — there likely is a better solution. A stodgy data processing approach will not work.

Managers constantly must be aware of human strengths and technology strengths. For example, it is wise to let the technology handle things like time and date recording to free the agents to make more calls. The caution is that each situation in each environment is unique. The tele-marketing approach from one organization cannot merely be copied and used in another organization. American Transtech's experience has shown that each project and case must be analyzed, planned, designed, and implemented as a unique situation.

Planners must be cognizant of the organizational culture and ensure that proposed systems fit within the culture. Of course, there is some carryover in learning from one project to another.

The human and organizational element has been found to be critical. Communication must be clear between all levels of management and workers, between clients and telemarketing personnel, between team members, and between callers and the agents. This communication can only exist if trust, empathy, mutual respect, and responsiveness are seen as important foundations for work that must be accomplished.

No area has been ignored at American Transtech. Physical design of facilities is important. It is impressive when visiting American Transtech to see where thousands of calls are coming in every hour; yet one hears no phones ringing and no chatter of conversation. It is important to use acoustical damping and “white noise” to reduce distractions. An empathetic designer will understand what is needed to allow people to get their work done.

American Transtech realized that it must look beyond technology for synergistic solutions. The work had to be done differently, but the managers never lost their focus on the alignment of information technology with the strategic plan to meet business objectives. And the managers also believe they have merely scratched the surface in the use of telemarketing technology and accompanying human components.

## Conclusion

Information management executives cannot ignore the area of telemarketing. It includes the islands of data processing technology, telecommunications, and human/organizational aspects. Telemarketing can be used to generate new information to gain a competitive advantage. The area of telemarketing should be seen by an information executive as an opportunity, not a problem. If the information executive ignores the area, the information management unit may lose it to other groups within the organization. Telemarketing applications can allow information managers to take an active role in the mainstream of the business and aid the information manager in playing a critical role in understanding, formulating, and implementing the organizational mission. The opportunity can aid in entering new markets and serve current markets even better. But the information executive must remember that to achieve success, there is a need to look beyond just a technological solution and take a total systems approach.
