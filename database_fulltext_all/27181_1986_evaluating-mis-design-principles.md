# Evaluating MIS Design Principles

By: Paul C. Nutt

The Ohio State University

1583 Perry Street

Columbus, Ohio

## Abstract

MIS design approaches call for managers to specify their information needs to be used as requirements in the design process. To explore the merits of this practice, managers from organizations across the United States were interviewed. The managers were asked to indicate their preference for personal, interactive, report, and analytic types of information for various stages of decision making and decision familiarity. These preferences were assessed to determine if they were influenced by managers' personal characteristics (such as psychological makeup and experience), by the decision task, or both. Analysis found that preferences were not influenced by manager characteristics but were influenced by the decision task. The managers demonstrated an understanding of information uses consistent with the decision support literature, providing support for user specification of MIS information requirements.

Keywords: Design, information requirements
ACM Categories: C.4

## Introduction

Management information systems (MIS) are coming under increasing criticism. Most executives believe that an MIS is essential, but many contend that its performance has not measured up to their expectations. Disillusionment has led to MIS system redesign, replacement, and, in extreme instances, the disbanding of entire departments. Much of the difficulty with the MIS has been laid at the designer's doorstep, suggesting that MIS design practices merit careful study to determine their value.

The practice of information needs specification by users is employed by many MIS designers, making user specification a crucial ingredient in the design process. For example, information requirements in the key indicator approach described by Rockart [44], calls for managers to specify needed information for exception reports and the like via interviews. The “total system” approach, advocated by Anthony [4] among others, also queries managers about their information needs, comparing these needs to available information to define requirements. Keen and Scott Morton [27] call for the design of decision support systems (DSS), contending that information must have a decisional purpose. The DSS approach identifies information requirements from normative and descriptive models of decision making required by the organization. Decision makers are asked to list critical decisions and the information required to support each decision. These procedures suggest two crucial steps in MIS/DSS design: identifying key activities and then specifying the information required to support these activities. As a result, most MIS designs rely on managers and others to specify the information that the system is to provide.

The pivotal role of user specification of information needs in MIS design calls for careful assessment of this practice. One way to establish the merit of user specification is to evaluate the MIS produced by these specifications. However, evaluations of information systems have not established a clear link between organizational performance and the information provided by the MIS [11]. MIS evaluation has been limited by the laborious task of tracing information use by an organization and the difficulty of linking this information to improved organizational effectiveness [24, 46]. These difficulties have led to measuring success by the extent to which a user's perceived needs have been met [11, 13, 27, 42, 45]. The information system designer asks managers to specify their information needs and then validates by measuring whether the MIS provided the required information, which may create a tautology. Managers whose information needs have been met seem likely to indicate that they were satisfied. To claim otherwise would imply shortcomings in one's ability to know and articulate one's own needs.

As Ives and Olson [24] point out in their review paper, the merits of user involvement in improving MIS have not been demonstrated. This MIS design practice has been based on intuition, experience, and unsubstantiated prescription and has at least two potential pitfalls. First, one must assume that users have in-depth knowledge of information and its role in their business so they can articulate their information needs. Second, users are assumed to know the types of information that are best for their important and frequently occurring decisions. Mason and Mitroff [31], Alavi and Henderson [2], and others contend that information preferences will be distorted by a manager's cognitive makeup as well as other user specific characteristics, such as experience and background. A healthy skepticism regarding a manager's ability to correctly diagnose their information needs, let alone articulate those needs has emerged [24].

The purpose of this article is to explore two questions. First, are a manager's information preferences influenced by intrinsic factors, such as cognitive makeup and user specific characteristics? Second, can managers articulate defensible information needs for various stages of decision making? To answer these questions a survey of managers in a wide variety of organizations was carried out. By surveying practicing managers real concerns in MIS design were captured and the artificiality of a laboratory with naive subjects, such as business students, was avoided. This survey, developed to be comparable to those used by MIS designers, was used to elicit preferences from managers to see if their preferences were influenced by cognitive factors (decision style, risk preference, and tolerance for ambiguity), personal characteristics (e.g., experience), or by MIS design considerations (design criteria, information sources, and the stages of decision making). If these preferences are influenced by design considerations, and not by cognitive and personal factors, the practice of user information specification can be supported.

## The Survey

The survey used to elicit managers information preferences was based on the PIRA model developed by Henderson and Nutt [18]. The PIRA model provides a normative definition of information requirements. PIRA stands for the types of information that can be handled by an MIS: personal, interactive, report, and analysis. Two additional factors are considered by the PIRA model: stage of decision making and situation, as shown in Table 1. Finally the survey questioned managers about their views of the importance of criteria to judge the merits of a MIS.

## Information Types

The PIRA model suggests that an MIS can be designed to handle personal and interactive information as well as the more traditional report and analytical information. Personal information stems from an individual's store of experience [22]. However, personal information is often colored by the individual's background, education, etc., making this information subject to a variety of biases that limit accurate recall, classification, and inference [48]. For example, vivid cases cater to the sensory and real time biases of managers [33, 34] and create an emotional interest that cause managers to weigh these cases out of proportion to their evidential value [38]. To cope with these biases an MIS could provide expert systems that store what Janis and Mann [25] call representational heuristics. These devices help the manager to qualify, organize, and store information, and facilitate distortion-free recall. To ferret out personal information the users engages in a dialogue with the model, such as that undertaken by a cardiologist when consulting a computerized EKG interpreter before selecting a diagnosis.

Interactive information stems from talking to an individual or a group. Managers rely on a network of friends and colleagues as well as subordinates, peers, and superiors to provide information. An MIS could be designed to support interactive data acquisition by making provisions for the interactive use of data and source files. Interactive use of an MIS database can be facilitated by providing features such as flexible formats and rapid retrieval tailored to the specific needs of group use. Designs of this type allow approaches such as teleconferencing and group process to efficiently use databases (e.g. [32]).

Tichey, et al., [47] call for executives to draw on information pertinent to strategic management by making provisions for the rapid detection of signals in turbulent environments and by sharing this information with friendly organizations. The individual contacts or personal networks of executives provide a significant part of this information [3]. The MIS could store these contacts, organized by information source, type, and features such as expertise. The composite of these sources becomes a network that is broader than the network of any single executive. Executives with the necessary authorization could access these sources by information type or some other pertinent characteristic. Using an MIS to support personal and interactive information sources in this manner calls for a non-traditional approach to information management.

Information generation via data files, such as books, reports, memos, and accounting summaries, takes the report form. This information must be searched to find diagnostic relationships. Finally, information can be generated through analysis by techniques such as break-even statistics, cost-benefit, simulation, mathematical programming, etc. Analysis aggregates and condenses information to draw inferences, as compared to reports which often provide the same information in a broader, less specific fashion. The distinction between reports and analysis lies in the explicitness of the inference: for reports the inference is implicit and must be teased out by the manager, whereas for analysis the inference is explicit. This distinction does not make analysis superior to reports because the inference may not address the priority issue. Reports and analysis are traditional forms of an MIS. In each case hard copy that summarizes key indicators, aggregated to various degrees, is provided.

## Decision Stages

The PIRA model treats decision making as a process to be supported by one or more of the PIRA information sources. Such an approach is recommended by Zmud [53] who points out that an MIS should support decision making throughout the decision process, including the stages of problem or issue finding, problem structuring, solution generation, and choice. Similar requirements have been suggested by Ackoff [1], Gluck [14], Hofer and Schendel [20], and Churchman [18]. The PIRA model adds implementation to these stages and, like Mintzberg, et al., [35], defines decision making as a process of systematic inquiry. The stages in a decision making process are defined here as determining needs, option identification, development, evaluation, and implementation. To determine needs, the manager explores various ways to stipulate the problem or issue to be addressed, and defines objectives. Next, a structure is created which allows the manager to focus inquiry by identifying an arena of action and selecting options relevant to priority issues. In development, potentially useful courses of action are shaped and fine tuned. Next, the manager uses evaluation to select a course of action or to compare a proposed action with current practices. Finally, information is solicited to determine how the choice can be implemented. Gaining acceptance for the preferred course of action is stressed. In each stage information is required to illuminate issues, options, procedures, evaluations and acceptance.

## Decision Familiarity

The final component in the PIRA model is situation. For simplicity, two categories are used: familiar and unfamiliar. The familiar situation calls for decision making in situations that the executive has faced before. The familiar situation has three attributes. First, a relevant procedure has been used previously (such as ways to allocate salary increases among employees). Second, alternatives are well defined so the likelihood of a desirable payoff can be estimated. Third, key organizations or individuals with whom the decision maker traditionally deals are involved and are affected in familiar ways. An example might be selecting a person for a line management position. The familiar situation is highly structured because past practices are available and will be used as a template. A familiar or high structure situation is defined as having attributes of known procedure, estimable payoffs, and a known environment.

The unfamiliar situation calls for decision making in new or unique situations. There are no obvious procedures to follow, alternatives are obscure so payoffs cannot be estimated, and new or different organizations and individuals are involved that will be affected in unpredictable ways. The decision has no precedent that can be applied so the unfamiliar situation has low structure. Thus the unfamiliar situation, for the purposes of this study, is defined as having no clear procedure to follow, obscure alternatives and an unknown environment.

## Preferred Information Sets

Henderson and Nutt [19] use the MIS design literature to identify preferred information sets for each decision stage and situation familiarity (Table 1). In the familiar situation personal sources may be used initially because the decision making can be memory-based. Decisions made in familiar situations concentrate on the option development, evaluation, and implementation stages [41]. Report and interactive information sources (e.g., a description of past practices, validated by talking to others) can be used to surface options; personal and analytic to evaluate those options; and report sources for implementation feedback. Needs surfaced through performance audits and accounting reports stress the report source.

Decisions in an unfamiliar situation require considerable problem solving, calling for all stages of decision making to be formally activated. Literature pertinent to decision making [12, 30, 35] calls for personal and interactive information sources to be used to surface issues; interaction and analytic to identify options; interaction, personal and analytic to refine and to evaluate them; and interactive and personal to manage their implementation process using change agents and user participation. To support the practice of user articulated information needs, the managers should be able to recognize these requirements in their information preferences.

## Information Criteria

Managers are often asked by the MIS designer to select and weigh criteria to judge an MIS, in addition to specifying its features. The following criteria have been suggested [27, 52]:

1. Precision — The accuracy of information provided by the MIS.

2. Creativity — Information that gives a fresh or novel perspective.

3. Consistency — Information known to be reliable, remaining unchanged when collection modes and other events change.

4. Judgement — Making practical experience available through an MIS.

5. Clear Steps — Information that stems from data compiled by processing steps that are known and understood.

6. Several Sources — The ability to cross check information using parallel sources to verify its accuracy.

The weights assigned to these criteria can be analyzed to reveal additional insights into the information preferences of managers. The extent of agreement on criteria weights (importance) has three implications. First, managers may agree but find none of the criteria to be important, giving all a low rating. Such a finding would suggest that users are not able to judge MIS performance because these criteria are widely recognized as important determinants of an MIS. Second, a failure to agree on which criteria are important suggests that user's views are capricious, making the process of user specification of criteria merely an exercise in cooptation [24]. Third, if managers find the criteria to be important, the notion that MIS users can specify MIS systems features would be supported, and the implication of the weights assigned can be assessed. The importance accorded each criterion is given by its weight and suggests the rationale to be applied in information system specifications, which can be generalized to the user community.

## Other Influences On User Specification

Many believe that users perceptions of their information needs will be influenced by factors such as cognitive makeup and personal characteristics of the user [5, 9, 19, 27, 29, 31, 37]. These factors may distort a manager's views and thereby color his or her preferences.

## Cognitive Factors

Three aspects of cognitive makeup were considered: style, risk preferences, and attitudes toward ambiguity. Cognitive styles have been derived from Jung's [26] theory of psychological types. Jung contended that people have distinctive differences in the way they gather and process data. Some people take in information by sensing, stressing facts and details (what is), and others by intuition, stressing possibilities (what might be) as well as environmental and contextual factors. Jung finds that one mode of data gathering tends to dominate.

Information can be processed by thinking or feeling approaches and, again, one mode tends to dominate. Thinking uses impersonal logic-based procedures. The feeling mode is concerned with the personal stakes of people who are affected, hence values are the key consideration.

Four styles of decision making are delineated by these modes of information gathering and processing: sensation-thinking (ST), sensation-feeling (SF), intuition-thinking (NT), and intuition-feeling (NF). The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, or MBTI, has been used to specify an individual's cognitive style [37]. The reliability of the MBTI as a measure of cognitive style is widely verified [6, 52]. Further, considerable research has been carried out which finds that cognitive style (also called decision style) alters how managers deal with a variety of situations [17, 19, 36, 39]. For example, Hoy and Hellriegel [21] found that the cognitive style of executives caused them to see organizational effectiveness measures differently, suggesting that a manager's view of the best set of critical success indicators may depend on the manager's decision style. Churchman [9] contends that cognitive style must be incorporated into the design of an MIS, tailoring the system to the needs of its user. Buttressing this view, Blaylock and Ress [5] found that information preferences during decision making, were related to cognitive style. These findings suggest that MIS designs should be constructed to cater to the preferences of ST, SF, NT, and NF users.

Risk preference can be measured by the Kogan and Wallach [28] instrument. The scale is made up of twelve questions that examine risk in work and personal situations. The respondent chooses between two options such as jobs, stocks, and the like. One of the options is more attractive, but less likely to be realized. Respondents are asked to indicate the likelihood they will adopt either a “low risk-low payoff” or a “high risk-high payoff” option. The scores of the choice dilemmas are averaged to create a scale which runs from 1 (risk tolerant) to 9 (risk averse). In this study the risk scale will be used to determine if a manager’s views of risk influence his/her information preferences. For example, a risk averse manager may prefer analysis regardless of other considerations, such as decision making stage or decision familiarity. A relationship between risk and information preferences suggests that MIS design should account for a manager’s views of risk, which complicates it design.

Ambiguity tolerance can be measured by the Bunder scale. Bunder [7] uses sixteen questions about person- and work-oriented situations that have ambiguity. Each situation is rated on a one (tolerant) to seven (intolerant) scale. A perfectly tolerant person would score 16 and a perfectly intolerant person would score 112. Scores typically range from 20 to 80, with a mean score of 45. In this study, the Bunder scale will be used to indicate if manager's with different tolerances for ambiguity have different perceptions about their information needs. For example, an ambiguity tolerant manager may prefer person centered information (personal and interactive) whereas the ambiguity intolerant manager might prefer reports and analyses. A relationship between ambiguity and information preferences calls for MIS design approaches to compensate for a manager's inability to deal with ambiguity so all sources will be considered during decision making.

## Individual Characteristics

A variety of individual characteristics may also influence perceptions. For instance, people higher up in an organization may see different needs than the department heads. Research on information use by managers suggests that age, experience, educational background, general level of experience, and organizational level may be important factors [16, 29]. Individual characteristics are important if they color perceptions leading to systematic differences in using particular types of information during decision making.

## The Research

## Hypotheses

The hypotheses considered in the study are listed below. They are stated to reflect the expected direction of each relationship which links the information preferences of MIS users with cognitive factors, personal characteristics, and MIS design considerations.

## Design Hypotheses: Information Preferences Stem From MIS Design Considerations

H1: Managers information source preferences will vary according to situation familiarity and decision stage.

The design hypotheses can be tested by whether interactions between the design factors are observed. A two-way, “stage-source” or “situation-source” interaction occurs if managers treat information as stage dependent or as situation dependent. A three-way interaction makes information preferences dependent on both stage and familiarity. Either a three-way or two-way interaction must be observed if the design hypotheses is to be supported. The existence of main effects, a preference for an information source without a stage or situation contingency, would invalidate H1. Such an outcome suggests that a particular type of information is preferred regardless of the decision making demands or situation.

## Cognitive Hypotheses: Information Preferences Arise from Differences in Cognitive Makeup

H2: Preference for information sources will vary with cognitive style. NFs will prefer personal and interactive information in all stages. STs will prefer report and analysis information in all stages. SF and NT will have different preferences and will shift from personal/interactive to report/analysis.

H3: Preferences for information sources will vary by the individual's tolerance for ambiguity and risk preference.

H4: The weights assigned to criteria to judge an MIS will depend on the individual's cognitive makeup.

Cognitive hypotheses 2, 3, and 4 can be tested by whether the cognitive factors interact with preferences for information sources or weights assigned to the MIS evaluation criteria. A “source-style” interaction would support H2, and a “source-ambiguity” or “source-risk” interaction H3. If perferences are influenced by decision style, ambiguity, and risk, an MIS must be contingently designed to take into account these factors. Interactions of criteria weights with decision style, ambiguity, and risk would support H4. If the cognitive hypotheses are not supported, an MIS can be designed without specific consideration to individual differences, making its design less expensive than that called for in the literature [24, 31].

## User Characteristics: Information Preferences Arise from Individual Differences

H5: Preferences for design factors (information sources, decision stage, and decision familiarity) will be independent of age, experience, time on the job, and background.

The user hypothesis contends that an individual's personal makeup does not influence preferences. If this hypotheses is supported, an MIS need not be tailored to the individual. Interactions between user characteristics (e.g., age and the like) and design factors suggest specific contingencies that the design process must consider.

## Study Design

Several study approaches were considered. A laboratory investigation with students was ruled out because such a study would lack external validity [10]. Students do not have the necessary experience to establish information needs for decision making in organizations. The participation of experienced managers seemed essential. However, no single organization, or organizations locally available would provide a sufficiently rich cross section of views. A random sample of organizations was infeasible because there was no way to insure that the selected organizations could be enticed to participate. Non-respondents in such studies are high, creating threats to internal validity. A survey, administered under controlled conditions, seemed the only feasible way to tap the views of practicing managers.

To capture a wide variety of organizations and points of view, managers attending executive training programs were selected as participants. This approach had two virtues. First, it insured that diverse organizations and a broad cross section of viewpoints would be represented. Data for the study were drawn from five classes, or cohorts, taken over a five year time period from managers who worked in 75 service and product providing organizations across the United States. Approximately 40% of the attendees paid their own tuition, suggesting that both upwardly mobile managers and the typical managers were represented in the data. Second, by having the managers fill out the survey in a classroom environment, experimental control could be exercised. Experimental control was needed to, among other things, insure that respondents followed instructions and did not consult with others as they completed the survey. These precautions allowed the managers' responses to be treated as independent observations. Finally, a survey of managers under controlled conditions insured that questions would be clarified in a consistent manner, and allowed for debriefing.

The managers were given the MIS Preference Survey constructed for this study. (The survey can be obtained by writing to the author.) The survey asked questions about criteria and information sources. The managers were told that the purpose of the study was to learn about their preferences for information types. They were asked to treat the survey as if it were being used by MIS consultants for the initial phase of an information system design or redesign. To control for social desirability and related effects, no lectures or handouts describing ideal information types were provided until after the survey was completed.

The managers were asked to fill out the survey by checking anywhere along the scale to reflect their views. Anchored rating scales were used so perceptions of scale increments and zero points had comparable definitions. The anchors and increments create interval scale properties in the responses so parametric statistical methods can be used. The scale had the following increments and descriptors:

Value Description
0 Not important
1 Somewhat important
2 Important
3 Very important
4 Critically important
5 Dominating all concerns

The survey asked each manager to indicate his/her preferences for personal, interactive report, or analysis information in each stage of a decision process, for familiar and unfamiliar situations. The contrasts were defined by the questions posed in the survey following the format shown in Table 1. The managers were also asked to indicate their view of the importance criteria to evaluate the information sources provided by an MIS. After completing the survey, each manager was given the MBTI instrument, the Kogan and Wallach Risk Preference Scale, and the Tolerance for Ambiguity Scale to measure his or her cognitive makeup.

The reliability of the survey instrument was tested in two ways. To determine consistency, ten respondents were asked to fill out the same survey at a later point in time. Congruence was determined by changing the examples in the survey and having another ten respondents complete the altered survey later in the same day. In each case reliability was measured by a factor with two levels (first and second completion of the survey). A one-way, fixed effects ANOVA was used with a block for questions in the survey to test the reliability factor. The error variance in the ANOVA captured individual differences (e.g., capriciousness) and measurement error, such as questions and scale ambiguity. F tests, with consistency and congruence used to define reliability, were not significant suggesting that the survey form could be regarded as reliable.

Data describing the respondent's age, educational background, years on the job, and level in the organization were collected from program application forms. Managers with less than four years experience, typically recent college graduates, seemed unlikely to be asked by their organization to participate in MIS design. This group was dropped, leaving a database of 48 experienced managers for the study. These managers held mid to upper level positions in their organization (e.g., operating officer, CFO, or middle level executive) and were in a position to see the ramifications of an information system and, therefore, were likely to participate in its design.

## Study Variables

Dependent variables in the study were the ratings of each information source, for each decision stage, in a familiar or an unfamiliar situation, and ratings of each criteria. The independent variables were MIS features, cognitive variables, and individual characteristics. The MIS design features were information source (personal, interactive, report, analysis), stage of decision making (needs, options, refinement, evaluation, and implementation), and situation (familiar, unfamiliar). Cognitive variables were style (ST, SF, NT, and NF), risk preference, and ambiguity. Individual characteristics considered in the study were: age, experience (time in the industry), and educational background. The latter factor has three levels: accounting, general business, and content specialist (e.g., health degree for hospitals or chemist for an oil company). All but mid to upper level positions were screened out so this factor was controlled rather than studied.

## Relationships Under Study

Two relationships can be proposed. The first deals with design issues:

Rating = Information source (I) + Decision stage (D) + Situation (S) + Cognitive style (CS) + Risk Preference (RP) + Ambiguity Tolerance (AT) + Age (A) +

Experience (E) + Education (Ed) + Interactions.

The main effects can be segregated into design factors (I, D, S), cognitive factors (CS, RP, and AT), and personal characteristics (A, E, and Ed).

For the design factors, a three-way and two-way interaction is expected: information source preferences should be linked to design stage and/or situation. The existence of an interaction would suggest that information, in the mind of the executives, should be tailored to fit stages of decision making and/or the situation. If an interaction term is not found, the value of surveying executives to determine their needs can be questioned. Interactions between the CS variable with the design factors may also occur. Specifically, the interactions CS \* I, CS \* D \* I, and CS \* D \* I \* S are expected to be significant.

Previous studies indicate that cognitive factors may be important as main effects, suggesting that preferences cannot be collected without reference to the cognitive makeup of the respondent [2, 5, 19]. Two implications can be drawn if user information preferences are related to cognitive style. First, surveys asking the executive to specify MIS features cannot be understood independent of the individual's cognitive makeup. Second, the MIS designer may have to tailor information according to cognitive makeup of the user [27, 32].

Individual characteristics must not be related to preferences for MIS features if current MIS design approaches are to have validity. Should such a relationship exist, the user, when asked for his or her preferences, would provide views colored by personal characteristics that may have little bearing on the information needs of the organization. Interactions of personal characteristics with the design factors (e.g., Ed \* I of Ed \* I \* D) would also suggest that preferences cannot be reliably collected. Such a finding would tend to discredit the MIS design approaches currently in use.

The second relationship deals with evaluation issues and is shown below:

Rating = MIS evaluation criteria + CS
+ RP + AT + A + E + Ed
+ Interactions.

An interaction of criteria weights with the cognitive factors, or user characteristics, would suggest that the weights assigned by managers to judge an MIS are influenced by the manager's cognitive makeup or by their personal circumstances. Such a finding makes it impossible to understand criteria weightings without reference to these factors and would tend to invalidate the practice of user information requirements specification.

## Results

The standard ANOVA format is an inappropriate mode of analysis to use here because it assumes that the ratings of information sources for various decision stages and task familiarity are independent. A Repeated Measures ANOVA was required to analyze the data because respondents filling out the survey compared their ratings of information sources between stages and situations. Each rating calls for a hierarchical set of considerations (information preferences given a particular stage and task familiarity) which makes the ratings dependent. The Repeated Measures ANOVA deals with dependencies by blocking for the respondent and computing the residual to account for the dependent ratings. In analyses of this type, the respondent serves as a block and experimental units (information source, decision stages, and familiarity) within a block are different instances of treatment.

## Hypothesis I. The Design Factors

The results of the analyses, shown in Table 2, support the design hypothesis. Information sources, the stage-information interaction, and the stage-information/familiarity interaction all proved to be significant. The mean values for the variables are shown in Tables 3 and 4.

Managers were found to have preferences for interactive information. This finding suggests that MIS designers should provide interactive use of both reports and analytic information. Formal arrangements for group process in the interpretation of the data seem desirable. For example, an MIS can provide ways to use information by individuals or in a dyad, triad, or group mode. A variety of group discussion formats could be envisioned. For instance, a brainwriting [41] or a nominal group [12] could receive information as an input and be asked to act on it. Personal sources (e.g., anecdotes) were rated as more important than reports and just as important as analysis. This suggests that managers weight their own experiences very highly. This finding suggests that the next generation of MIS designers may be asked to aid the manager in systematically storing this experience so it can be extracted without distortion for future use.

These preferences are sensible because the early stages of decision making call for search. Reports and analysis offer less guidance in this area because they reflect what has been, rather than what is or might be. Creativity in identifying options is more likely to occur when relying on person-centered sources of information [15]. These findings suggest that MIS users understand which information is best for need identification and option surfacing.

Managers involved in implementation were found to prefer interactive (I) information. This type of information may be preferred because acceptance is inferred by managers through informal and off-the-record conversations. Introspection (P) can be used to isolate incentives and inducements to move key people to accept a decision. In this context interaction is required to bargain. The ability of the respondents to recognize that person-centered sources (P and I) are essential for implementation supports the notion that managers can specify desirable MIS features to guide the design process.

The “information-stage/familiarity” interaction was also highly significant. The ratings are shown in Table 4. The new distinction offered by the three-way interaction is the contrast between the familiar and the unfamiliar situation and its influence on information preferences.

The shifts in preference from the familiar to the unfamiliar decision was greatest in the early decision stages. Managers considering need for an unfamiliar decision continue to prefer interaction, but want to substitute personal information for analytical and report information. Managers appear to understand that reports and analysis have limited value in determining needs in new and unique situations. Analysis was seen as somewhat more desirable for option development when faced with an unfamiliar decision. Managers prefer to use introspection and then analysis to identify options when faced with an unfamiliar task. Person centered sources (P and I) are preferred when the task of option identification is carried out for a familiar decision.

These findings have several interpretations. Managers prefer not to use analytic information for option surfacing when they can impose the template of past experience. For example, last year's approach to allocate the money budgeted for raises among employees can be surfaced from memory and validated by talking to others, because the task of deciding on raises seems familiar.

Analysis information is preferred to interaction when the task is changed from familiar to unfamiliar. For instance, if the market for some professionals (e.g., accountants or engineers) becomes murky, the decision rules used to assign pay increases in the past no longer apply and the decision shifts from a familiar to an unfamiliar one. Analysis and introspection (A and P) are preferred to create information to set the raise policy under these conditions. These differing preferences are consistent with normative views of decision making and suggest an understanding of the need to alter information requirements when a situation changes from familiar to unfamiliar.

## Hypotheses 2, 3, 4: The Cognitive Factors

## Cognitive Makeup

To explore the cognitive hypotheses H2 and H3, measures of each manager's cognitive makeup were correlated with the manager's information preferences. Cognitive makeup was measured by the MBTI, Tolerance for Ambiguity, and Risk Preference scales. Cognitive factors (ambiguity tolerance and risk preference) were included in the Repeated Measures ANOVA analysis as a co-variate with the seven design factors. Cognitive style was included as a multi-level factor (Table 2). These analyses found no support for the cognitive hypotheses, H2 and H3. This suggests that an MIS need not be designed to accommodate the cognitive makeup of an individual user, greatly simplifying the task of information system design.

When Mason and Mitroff [31] and Alavi and Henderson [2] call for information systems to cater to the cognitive preferences of the MIS user, they also call for a vastly more complex set of considerations to be introduced into MIS designs. First, human interface issues must be incorporated into MIS design considerations. Second, a mechanism that allows the user to state his/her information preferences must be designed. And, third, data must be segregated by cognitive preferences. For example, “what is” information (S) must be augmented by possibilities (N), and logical inferences (T) by network considerations which detail how stakeholders feel (F) about an impending decision.

This study found that selective information provision, based on cognitive style, has little value. Cognitive style fails to account for much of the explained variance in the study. The task, defined by decision stage and familiarity, seems to be the dominant consideration in this study. Huber's [23] review of several surveys and simulation studies, similar to the research reported here, drew similar conclusions. It appears that MIS design efforts can be directed toward handling a variety of data types (e.g., PIRA) rather than investing in a contingent mechanism that allows the user to access a particular source. However, a more balanced approach to decision making can be created when the range of information suggested by cognitive style considerations is made available [9]. Coaching and training can be used to help managers use information they are inclined to reject.

## MIS Evaluation Criteria

The cognitive hypothesis pertaining to criteria (H4) was not supported. The criteria weightings were not dependent on the manager's cognitive style. However, tests of statistical significance found that the managers tend to agree on the relative importance of the criteria (Table 5). Accuracy and consistency were rated high in importance and subjectivity was rated low.

The preference for accuracy and consistency may stem from evaluation needs in organizations that call for an MIS to support assessment processes. These preferences may be linked to the dominant use of information from an MIS for control purposes. The intermediate importance assigned to clear steps and parallel sources also seem consistent with using an MIS for evaluative and control activities.

Judgement was seen as having little importance. This finding is surprising in light of the recognition that personal information is valuable for diagnostic and implementation tasks. Apparently, managers do not see an MIS as supporting the task of stimulating introspection, implying a traditional view of MIS uses. Creativity was given an intermediate level of importance. This suggests that using an MIS to provide information to stimulate innovation may be a new thought to the typical MIS user. Again, a traditional view of MIS applications is implied.

The cognitive factors had no influence on the criteria assigned by potential MIS users. This suggests that cognitive style, ambiguity tolerance, and risk preference do not influence how a manager looks at the product of an MIS. The practice of user information specification, and the ability of users to articulate consistent MIS performance expectations is supported by this finding.

## Hypothesis 5: User Characteristics

The user characteristic hypotheses was supported. Personal characteristics had no influence on the ratings. When age and experience were introduced as covariants into the Repeated Measure ANOVA neither influenced information preferences in a given decision stage or familiarity. This suggests that a manager's preferences can be measured independent of his or her background. Other personnel characteristics considered were educational background and organizational level. Educational background had no effect on the results and organizational level was controlled by limiting participants to middle and upper level managers.

## Summary and Conclusions

This study examined a key premise of MIS design approaches that calls for users to indicate their information preferences. User specification assumes that managers are able to recognize and articulate their information needs. User specifications were found to provide a logical format for MIS design, largely consistent with normative views of decision making.

A survey of managers, using a questionnaire like those used by MIS designers, found that managers' information preferences varied with task conditions but not with their psychological and personal characteristics. Perhaps more importantly, the managers prescribed a defensible set of information requirements.

The preferred type of information varied with decision stage and decision familiarity. Person-centered sources of information (personal and interactive) were preferred for the diagnostic and option surfacing stages and for decision implementation. Non-person centered sources (report and analysis) were preferred for the detailing and evaluating steps. According to the managers, the unfamiliar decision called for a somewhat different set of information sources. Analytic information was still preferred for evaluation, and person-centered sources for implementation, but these preferences become more pronounced in unfamiliar terrain. There was a shift in preference from interactive to analytic when options must be identified in an unfamiliar situation. Person-centered information was preferred to specify needs for the unfamiliar decision. Analytic and interactive information was preferred to diagnose for the familiar decision. These views are generally consistent with the decision support literature.

Managers were also able to specify a consistent set of criteria to evaluate the product of an information system. There was considerable agreement on the criteria thought to be important. Precision and consistency were demanded. Clear steps and several sources were seen as desirable. Creativity and ability to handle subjective information were viewed as less important. This suggests that the managers surveyed preferred a traditional information system, one used for assessment and control purposes. An MIS was not viewed as a vehicle to support problem solving and creativity.

Cognitive factors do not appear to influence information preferences, suggesting that a cognitively tailored MIS is not needed and, indeed, may not be desirable. This finding simplifies the task of information system design because the MIS need not provide information contingently for specific classes of users. However, this finding does not support the prevalent “report-only” approach to MIS design. The cognitive style literature has made a contribution by pointing out different types of information that can be valuable in decision making. Rather than designing an MIS to support various styles, the information in an MIS should use the preferences of these styles to create a more balanced approach to information provision. The new generation of MIS should consider ways to provide analysis information to support personal and interactive sources by mechanisms that permit interaction and introspection, such as teleconferencing and case history profiles.

User characteristics had little impact on preferences. Educational background had no impact on preferences. However, both age and experience were found to increase an executive's appreciation of the value of each type of information. Preferences for information sources, in various stages of decision making with particular decision situation familiarity, were not related to user characteristics. As a result, the preferences articulated seem independent of the characteristics of the potential user.

The preferences uncovered in this study suggest an understanding of information needs consistent with the decision support literature, which supports gathering user preferences to specify information requirements in MIS design. This, coupled with the ability of users to state these preferences independent of their cognitive and other personal attributes, supports the user preference survey as a key step in the design of an MIS.

The research findings provide guidelines for both the MIS designer and managers. The ability of users to specify logical information requirements should aid the MIS designer in promoting this approach to skeptics. Managers should recognize that user specification has merit beyond the cooptative benefits that have been widely reported. However, both the innovation [e.g., 51] and the creativity [e.g., 15] literatures suggest that a computerized database approach to an MIS, which stresses a report-like form of information, is an overly narrow conception of MIS possibilities.

To enhance creativity and innovation future MIS designers could consider ways to support the use of personal and interactive information. For example, expert systems provide models that capture and classify case histories, indicating how the manager would deal with a situation. P information could be derived from reconciling the manager's introspection with the model. I information can be supported by source files and by provisions for the interactive use of MIS databases. Arrangements for database interrogation by a group is one approach that merits consideration.

These modifications to a traditional MIS are desirable because managers want P and I types of information in decision making. Ways to classify and store relevant analyses (inference based information) to square against reports (implicit inferences) may also merit consideration. These changes make an MIS similar to a library, with its extensive cross referencing, and flexible read-out capabilities. The MIS user, in reconciling information from these sources, obtains a more balanced view of a decision situation and gains creative ideas which may improve the decision ultimately selected. Research on these speculations is needed.

Increased attention to early decision stages (issue finding and options) and implementation seems needed because these stages are important and managers recognize this importance. However, the typical MIS does not provide information for the intentionality and option stages. Most MIS are designed for control and evaluation activities, which becomes the focus of information provision. This has led the MIS designers to stress a report format, focused on control and evaluation, ignoring the other decision stages. Research into the merits of MIS designs which provide P and I information sources and their capability to stimulate creativity in the early decision stages seems needed.
