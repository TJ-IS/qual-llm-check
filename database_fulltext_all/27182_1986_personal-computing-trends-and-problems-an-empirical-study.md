# Personal Computing Trends and Problems: An Empirical Study

By: Tor Guimaraes
Vasudevan Ramanujam
The Weatherhead School of Management
Case Western Reserve University
Cleveland, Ohio

## Abstract

This article reports the findings of a field study of personal computing in 173 U.S. companies. It identifies trends over the last five years in the use of personal computing as well as the corporate MIS departments' roles, policies, and practices with regard to personal computing. A prioritized checklist of personal computing problems shows lack of user education, lack of a company-wide perspective on the use of personal computers, and overwhelming demands on MIS departments at the top in terms of problem intensity.

Keywords: Personal computing, information management, information centers, MIS planning.

ACM Categories: K.6.1, K.6.2., K.6.4., K.8.0

## Introduction

Booz, Allen, & Hamilton, Inc. [2] estimated that the number of microcomputers in U.S. businesses rose from 2.6 million in 1982 to 4.6 million in 1984. An estimate by the Yankee Group, a Boston-based research organization, suggests that the number of personal computers reached 5.4 million in 1984, doubling from the previous year [10]. Booz, Allen & Hamilton also estimate that the number of microcomputers will reach 13 million by 1990. Clearly, it is no exaggeration that “the growth of end user computing” (of which personal computing appears to represent a fair share) “is one of the significant phenomena of the 1980’s in the information management field” [1, p. 35].

For many organizations this growth has been a mixed blessing. Along with the proliferation of personal computers has come a number of related problems. Corporate MIS managements in most organizations have responded in a reactive fashion $[1, 7]$ . Phrases such as “helter-skelter growth” $[9]$ and “the time bomb of incompatibility” $[7]$ reflect the chaotic state of personal computing management.

This article reports on a field study of personal computing trends and problems in a cross-section of U.S. businesses. Previous studies have provided snapshot views of the state of personal computing at specific points in time, usually using a case study approach [e.g., 7]; have compared personal computing with mainframe-based end-user computing, emphasizing differences in the types of users and applications [1]; or have examined the role of corporate MIS in managing end-user computing in general [1, 9] and personal computing in particular [4, 5, 6]. This study tracks trends in several aspects of personal computing over the last five years in addition to developing a checklist of personal computing problems. It is felt that the approach used in this study leads to a better understanding of the process of adoption and the evolution of personal computing as providing a clearer focus on the most crucial personal computing problems.

## The Field Study

Data for this project were initially sought from 400 firms randomly selected from the Forbes 500 list of 1984 [3]. A three page, structured questionnaire was used. One hundred and seventy-three companies responded, yielding a response rate of 43%. The higher than average level of response (average response rate is about 20% for this type of study) is believed to be due to the relevance of the topic as well as the brevity of the questionnaire. Demographics for the respondent firms are shown in Table 1. A diversity of firms in terms of sales, industry type, and size of MIS budgets is represented in the sample.

A comparison of the non-respondent firms and respondent firms confirms that no response bias is present. However, the sample is biased in one respect and this should be borne in mind while interpreting the results. All respondents represented the MIS function. While it would be desirable to obtain data from respondents in various user departments, resource considerations precluded the multiple-respondent option at the present stage of the research. The focus of the inquiry, namely the assessment of company-wide adoption of personal computing, suggested that corporate MIS departments were probably the best initial source of contact. Furthermore, it was the deliberate strategy of the researcher to obtain the MIS department's perspective on personal computing.

Respondents were asked to indicate, for each year from 1980 through 1984, (a) whether their organizations had personal computers, and (b) if so, what proportion of their organization's total computing was of the personal computing variety, (c) the level of control the MIS departments exercised over the acquisition of personal computers, and (d) which of a number of initiatives were adopted to support personal computing.

Concerns may be raised about the validity of retrospective data. Such data are subject to potentially unwitting distortion because of poor memory on the part of the respondents, as well as to willful distortion if adoption of personal computing is deemed to be a socially desirable trait. The use of retrospective data can be defended on three grounds. First, the data were not intended to be used in any statistical time series analysis where accuracy of recall might be a key issue, but were merely intended to capture broad trends for which even estimates should suffice. Second, biases arising due to social desirability reasons are unlikely to be systematic and would be expected to cancel out over the large number of respondents in the study. Third, no alternative exists for the collection of this type of data on a longitudinal basis at a reasonable cost since companies do not, as a rule, compile such data on an ongoing basis. Thus, respondents' judgements and estimates are the best one can hope for in studies of this type.

In addition to the areas previously mentioned, respondents were also asked to indicate the relative incidence of personal computing problems in their organizations. While several checklists of personal computing problems exist, assessment of their severity is not available in the literature. The checklist of personal computing problems used in this part of the questionnaire was developed by Guimaraes [5] through research and consulting in the personal computing area. In rating each problem respondents used a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (not concerned) to 5 (extremely concerned).

## Findings

## Trends

Since 1980, the proportion of companies in the sample using personal computers has increased dramatically, from 8% in 1980 to 100% in 1984. Thus, personal computing appears to have rapidly become a fact of life in U.S. businesses. While the number of companies using personal computing has been rising steadily, the proportion of organizational computing done on a personal computer is still relatively modest. As of 1984, an average of 9.4% of all computing was done on a personal computer, with a minimum of 1% and a maximum of 20%.

## MIS Department Involvement in Personal Computer Acquisition

There is strong evidence of increased MIS department involvement in personal computer acquisition. While in 1980 over half the sample companies claimed no MIS department involvement, by 1984 only 2% of the sample reflected this hands-off posture. Similarly, the proportion of organizations claiming MIS department control over the selection process has risen from a modest 15% in 1980 to 64% in 1984. It is interesting to note that in one out of three organizations, the MIS department exerts only an advisory role as far as personal computer acquisition is concerned. This proportion has remained more or less constant over the years. Analysis of this data also shows that in no organization has the MIS department become less involved in the acquisition process.

For 1980, as many as 32% of all responding MIS managers were unable to estimate the proportion of personal computing to total computing in their company. This percentage gradually declined for the next three years, and for 1984 all respondents were able to volunteer some estimate regarding the extent of use of personal computing in their organizations.

The level of MIS department control over personal computer acquisition within industry categories shows no significant associations for sales and MIS budget categories, but a significant association emerged across industry categories. Banking, insurance and other financial service organizations reported much higher levels of MIS department involvement. For 1984, 94% of the organizations in this category claimed that their MIS departments had control over personal computer acquisition. This proportion is in sharp contrast to the 56% applicable to the other industry categories as a group. While intriguing at first sight, this result is probably attributable to the fact that the MIS budgets of organizations in the financial services industry tend to be a relatively larger fraction of their corporate revenues. As a result, organizations in this industry have traditionally tended to be more control oriented as far as information systems management is concerned. This orientation also appears to manifest itself in their approach to personal computing.

## Support for Personal Computing

In 1980 and 1981 the only type of personal computing support activity performed by MIS departments was equipment demonstration. Even this type of support was not typical of all companies in the sample — only 15% provided even this minimal level. In his study of end-user computing, covering late 1982/early 1983, Benson [1] found a similar lack of policy and support from MIS managers for personal computing.

Since 1982, however, the situation appears to be rapidly changing. A variety of support mechanisms, including help desks, micromainframe communications, training programs, and personal computing libraries were in place in a majority of the sample companies by 1984. A somewhat smaller proportion of companies reported such further initiatives as user groups, local area networks, and company-wide planning for personal computing. The trend suggests that significant advances have been made in the last two or three years in taking the charging 'personal computing bull' by its proverbial horns. Despite this progress, strategic, corporate-wide, and long-term approaches to the management of personal computing still seem to be the exception rather than the rule. Just over one-third of the sample companies reported such planning in 1984.

Our data also show that the greater the level of acquisition control exercised by the MIS department, the greater the level of support provided to personal computer users. Initial enthusiasm over personal computing among users is sure to be dampened by technical problems unless users can rely on the technical support and expertise of their MIS peers. Such support appears to be more readily extended by those MIS departments that have a decided say in personal computer acquisition.

Controlling for size, MIS budget, and industry categories did not result in the identification of any patterns markedly different from the general trends reported for the overall sample.

## Personal Computing Problems

A number of problems have been identified in previous research $[2, 4, 5, 7]$ on personal computing. A detailed checklist of these problems has been developed over the years by Guimaraes $[4]$ . This checklist was used by the respondents in rating their current level of concern with problems within their own organizations. The averages and standard deviations of the ratings for all respondents are shown in the Appendix. A higher average score indicates a greater level of overall concern.

Indeed, the level of personal computing usage appears to be inundating MIS departments with requests for assistance in dealing with technical problems that users are apparently unable to tackle on their own. Security and data integrity problems are also rated as causing a high level of concern.

The most critical personal computing problem in the view of the respondents is lack of user education regarding a company-wide and long-term perspective about personal computing. With the increasing use of training programs for personal computing (see Table 2), this problem may become less critical in the years to come. However, evidence suggests that the nature of the training currently provided to personal computer users is technical and limited in scope. Its goal is to impart the basic skills necessary for using microcomputer software, such as spreadsheet packages or database management systems. Also, of the 123 companies (72%) that offered some kind of personal computing training in 1984, fully one-third had only just begun to offer such training and are thus relative newcomers to the training and education effort. Uncertainty exists in the minds of MIS managers as to whether training is really their job or the job of outside specialists.

Training and education of users are clearly crucial, but thorny issues remain to be resolved. Courses offered by MIS departments are being oversubscribed, and many users are on waiting lists. It is not surprising that independent consultants and trainers have taken up the slack and begun to offer short courses on such topics as spreadsheet programs and database management system use.

Almost every discussion on personal computing has emphasized the need to create greater awareness among users of the organization-wide impact of personal computing and the need for ensuring the integrity of both organizational data and applications. More attention to these issues is vitally needed.

The relatively low ranking by MIS managers of “lack of a company-wide plan for personal computing” (average score: 2.57) causes some concerns. However, there is considerable variation in the respondents' ratings (standard deviation 1.44). Nevertheless, the typical MIS managers' awareness of the need for such corporate-wide planning may need to be increased, since many of the problems they rate as serious could well be mitigated by better corporate planning. For example, problems 2, 4, 7, 8, 10, 11 and 15 in Table 3 are apt to be lessened by formal planning for personal computing. These problems basically raise issues of integration and coordination of activities across departments.

## Future Research Issues

What is the impact of corporate control, MIS department support, and the extent of personal computing usage on the incidence of personal computing problems? For example, it is reasonable to expect an inverse relationship between the level of MIS department support for personal computing and the incidence of major personal computing problems.

Research is underway relating the control and support variables to the level of problems experienced. For this purpose the various problems discussed in this paper have been classified into four major categories: integration, cost, integrity and security, and MIS department/user department relations. The framework of Figure 1 is being used to test specific interrelationships and hypotheses. Some preliminary results are reported in [8].

In this, as well as in most prior studies, there has been a primary emphasis on the perspectives of the MIS department. It is surely worthwhile to survey users, top management, and other interested groups in order to obtain a more balanced perspective of the management problems engendered by the proliferation of personal computing.

Since many problems appear to be related to the lack of clear policies on the part of top management and MIS management, greater attention is needed to the tools and techniques of strategic planning for personal computing.

The content and processes of effective training and education programs for users are further issues for systematic future research. Comparative research on more effective versus less effective personal computing implementations can yield important insights into how user awareness of personal computing problems can be increased.

## Conclusions

This paper has provided a panorama of key personal computing trends over the last five years. Although the proportion of total computing accounted for by personal computing is still not very large, the rate of growth is accelerating.

Corporate MIS departments initially resisted or dismissed personal computing as a viable alternative to mainframe computing, but are increasingly acknowledging the uses for this new technology. MIS managers need to be in the vanguard of the personal computing revolution, encouraging the steps many have taken to provide increasing levels of support to end users in their organizations.

A plethora of questions remain for future research to address. What measures are most effective in dealing with the problems of personal computing? How can MIS departments and user departments take more initiative in controlling these problems? How can users be trained and educated, and who is responsible for such developmental efforts?
