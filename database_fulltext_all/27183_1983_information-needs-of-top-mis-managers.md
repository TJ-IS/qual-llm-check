# Information Needs of Top MIS Managers

By: E.W. Martin

## Abstract

Despite the importance of the role of the Chief MIS Executive, little is known about the “Information Needs of Top MIS Managers.” This article uses a Critical Success Factors (CSF) approach to explore the information needs of these managers.

It has been widely speculated that top MIS Managers are remiss in their use of the technology they provide to others. This article also reports on a study of the formal information systems used personally by chief MIS executives and contrasts these results with their needs as revealed by the CSF analysis.

Keywords: Critical success factors, management of MIS, information requirements

ACM Categories: K.6.0, K.6.1

Despite the role of the chief (MIS) executive becoming increasingly important and prominent, very little is known about the personal information needs of top MIS managers. The purpose of this article is to explore the personal information needs of the chief MIS executive, to determine how well these needs are being met by the systems they use, and to find out whether top MIS managers are making effective use of the computer to satisfy their own information needs.

There is little doubt that the chief MIS executive is a crucial determinant of the success of the use of computer technology within a company. Top management certainty holds the Director of MIS responsible and there are reports that turnover in top MIS positions is quite high $[17, 18]$ . For the past several years, there has been a flood of articles in both the trade press and general management publications discussing the changing role of the chief information services executive, and describing the needed managerial capabilities of the future information executive $[2, 7, 8, 17, 18, 20]$ .

A number of studies of various characteristics of top information services executives and their roles have been published. Alternative managerial styles have been described by Wetherbe and Whitehead [23], and the balance between system professional and user community orientation has been reported by Taggart and Silbey [22]. Couger, Zawacki, and Oppermann studied the factors that motivate MIS managers [1]. Ives and Olson, in a study of the daily activities of MIS managers, found that these activities were quite similar to those of other high level managers and had a surprisingly low technological content [9]. This author has reported on the critical success factors of chief MIS executives [11].

Despite this interest in the chief information executive, little is known about the information needs of these managers. Although there are several articles on the evaluation of an MIS department that suggest information that might be helpful to the MIS manager as well as outside management [6, 12, 13], little has been written on the personal information needs of chief MIS executives.

There has, however, been some speculation that MIS executives are so busy supplying information services to others that they have neglected their

MIS Quarterly/September 1983 own information needs. Ives and Olson, in their study of the daily activities of MIS executives [9], noted that:

these managers rarely referred to computer based information systems. When they did, it was usually to reference reports generated from systems developed for other functional areas of the organization (e.g., accounting, personnel). There were few computer-generated reports related to information systems activities and even fewer signs of the so-called “decision support systems” currently in vogue. Like the shoemaker's children, information systems managers seem to be among the last to directly benefit from the technology they purvey.

This article reports on an exploratory study of the personal information needs of and the formal systems used by chief MIS executives of sizable organizations.

Since one objective is to determine how well the information needs of chief MIS executives are being satisfied by the formal systems they use, two things must be done:

1. determine the information needs of such managers and

2. determine what formal systems they are using and compare them with these needs.

Thus this article includes the results of two studies. The first study used the critical success factor (CSF) approach to explore the personal information needs of a group of chief MIS executives. The second study was conducted later and surveyed another group of chief MIS executives to determine what formal systems they personally used. The organizations that participated in these studies were located in the midwest, and are classified by industry in Table 1. The MIS organizations headed by the executives in this study ranged in size from just over fifty to around 800 employees.

In the following section the CSF approach to determining the information needs of executives will first be discussed. Then the results of the study of the CSFs and associated information needs of the group of chief MIS executives are presented. The study of the formal systems used by chief MIS executives is then reported, and the needs developed by the CSF analysis are related to the systems being used as revealed by the second study. Finally, the degree to which the computer is being used by these MIS executives is discussed.

## The CSF Approach

While a number of methods can be used to define the information needs of top level managers, this study uses the critical success factor approach suggested by John F. Rockart. According to Rockart [19]:

Critical success factors . . . are . . . the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will insure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where “things must go right” for the business to flourish. If results in these areas are not adequate, the organization’s efforts for the period will be less than desired.

As a result, the critical success factors are areas of activity that should receive constant and careful attention from management. The current status of performance in each area should be continually measured, and that information should be made available.

Rockart credits D.R. Daniel [3] with first expressing the concept of a critical success factor. Perhaps the first use of this concept in defining information needs was reported by King and Cleland in 1977 [10]. More recently Munro and Wheeler [16], have suggested that the corporate planning process be used to aid in the identification of senior and middle level manager's CSFs.

Rockart suggests that a manager's critical success factors provide an effective approach to determining significant information needs. For each CSF, the manager and an analyst determine information measures that can be used to monitor that critical area, and after suitable refinement these measures can be incorporated in reports that provide information that the manager needs for control of that critical area [19].

Although questions have been raised concerning the suitability of the CSF approach for determining a manager's information requirements [4, 5], one can argue that, given our historic lack of success in providing useful information to higher level management [15], an approach that elicits the enthusiastic participation of upper level management must be a step forward. The main reason for the use of the CSF approach in this study is because the chief MIS/DP executives involved were interested in this concept and willing to participate.

## The CSF Study

The subjects in the CSF study were fifteen chief MIS executives from industry and government. In the first stage of the study (which was reported in [11]), the following “generic” CSFs that appear to be widely shared by chief MIS executives were reported:

system development,

data processing operations,

human resource development,

management control of the MIS/DP organization,

relationships with the management of the parent organization,

support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization, and

management of change.

In the following section the second stage of this research is reported, where eight of these managers also reported a number of possible information measures appropriate to each of his personal critical success factors. Additional measures were added to the list during a group discussion among seven MIS managers (five of whom had contributed their information measures), three consultants, and five members of the Indiana University MIS faculty.

## Results of the Study of Information Needs

The following discussion organizes the information measures obtained from this process according to the related CSFs.

## System development

At the time the study was conducted, all the organizations involved were using some variation of the system life cycle model for managing system development. Within the broad area of system development, managers were concerned with project definition and selection, project management and control, and system evaluation.

The managers recognized the importance of project definition and selection, but the only measures they provided related to the existence of an effective process for defining and selecting projects. They did not produce appropriate measures of the quality of that process. In the area of project management and control, most of the managers wanted periodic reports by project, of progress compared to the planned schedule.

The area of system evaluation received a lot of attention, and included two distinct types of measures: those that evaluated the ongoing use and performance of systems, and those related to the “post-audit” phase of the life cycle. Measures of ongoing use included computer charges summarized by system and by location, user surveys of systems effectiveness, and reports of complaints and compliments. Post-audit related measures compared actual costs with planned costs, actual schedules with planned schedules, actual staffing with planned staffing, and actual benefits with planned benefits. Professional critiques, user critiques and system performance evaluations were also suggested.

## Data processing operations

Several performance measures were suggested for this area. For batch systems the managers were interested in the accuracy and timeliness of output: percent of reports on time, percent of reports in error, and percent of reruns. For online systems they were most concerned with system availability and reponse time.

Another area that concerned the managers was responsiveness to problems. There appear to be few formal systems in this area, but suggested measures included summaries of complaints and periodic user satisfaction surveys. Another approach involved logging problems and noting time of problem resolution so that reports of solution time by type of problem could be prepared.

Security was also of concern in the operations area. Most of the measures suggested were existence type measures — existence of security procedures, a comprehensive security plan, and a disaster plan. In terms of measures of effectiveness they mentioned reports of security audits and inspections, and reports of detected security breaches in physical access and data access.

## Human resource development

This area is concerned with the recruitment, training, and retention of highly qualified technical and managerial staff. There have been persistent shortages of qualified people for many MIS positions $[14, 21]$ , and most of the managers in this study were very concerned about this area. Ives and Olson $[9]$ reported that MIS managers devote a surprising amount of their time to acquiring and retaining qualified personnel. The managers were concerned with recruitment, training, turnover, morale, and with evaluation of the overall quality of the MIS staff.

In the area of recruitment, managers emphasized the need for a formal recruitment plan. Suggested measures included the number of unfilled positions, interviews, offers, acceptances, and the hit ratio (acceptances divided by offers).

In the area of training, managers again emphasized the need for a plan, and suggested the following measures — planned training vs. actual training to date, percent of effort devoted to training, and evaluations of the results of completed training.

Some MIS managers view personnel losses differently depending upon whether the person involved leaves the company or transfers to a user organization. Thus, some of them were interested in both an external and an internal turnover rate as well as analyses of “key” losses and turnover by cause. Suggested indicators of morale included frequency of grievances, absenteeism rates, and the recruitment hit rate.

The managers struggled to suggest ways of measuring the overall quality of the MIS staff. Some of the suggested measures included — review of educational profile of staff; analysis of composition with respect to experience, education, and capability; change in performance rankings; number and quality of appropriate outside professional contacts; professional reputation; reputation with knowledgeable users; complaints and compliments; performance in completing projects on time and within budget; and cost effectiveness and supportability of systems.

## Management control

Management control overlaps with the areas of system development and data processing operations, and the author will not repeat the measures that were discussed under those headings. Planning and control are closely related, so they are included together in this area.

The managers mentioned only a few information needs for planning. They were utilization of CPU, memory, disk/tape drives, and other hardware; status reports of system use by plant; and review of vendor product plans and industry trends for consistency with MIS long-range planning.

An important aspect of management control is management of the MIS department budget. Measures related to the budget were actual expenses compared to budget, expenses as a percentage of sales, monthly billings by user and by system, unit cost trends, and comparisons of rates charged internally with those charged by outside organizations.

## Relationships with the management of the parent organization

This area includes both relations with users and with top management of the company. Although the state of these relationships is usually easily felt, the managers suggested a surprising variety of measures in this area.

Measures concerning relations with users included measures of user involvement (number of users, amount of user time involved, quality of user involvement, and problems with user involvement), measures of the number and types of user contacts, and measures of the amount of user education being provided and used. Also, measures of user attitudes were suggested — complaints and compliments, results of user attitude surveys, changes in budgetary commitments, and number of “new customers.” Several viewed trends in purchases of outside DP services and acquisition of minicomputers by users as important measures of relationships with users.

Relationships with top management are usually directly observable by the Director of MIS. Nevertheless the managers suggested a number of measures of top management attitudes — concurrence with project and budget proposals and plans, unsolicited compliments and complaints, requests for assistance, and frequency of expense control, quality control, and cost effectiveness inquiries. The managers also suggested measures relating to the amount of communication with top management in meetings, presentations, one-on-one conversations, and the amount of personal involvement they had in corporate planning.

## Support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization

The managers were not able to suggest useful measures in this area. Their only suggestions related to the degree of involvement by top management in setting and reviewing MIS department objectives and priorities.

## Management of change

This area includes long range technological planning to assure that the organization takes advantage of advances in technology, and management of change so as to minimize the disruption caused by change. The managers noted the need for approved long-range system development plans and long-range hardware/software/network plans. Suggested measures included trends in costs, user productivity, design productivity, and degree of transparency to users.

## Analysis of the CSF Study

The above description of the information needs and possible measures was obtained by combining the ideas of a number of chief MIS executives. It makes no attempt to determine the relative importance of the individual items.

An examination of these descriptions reveals that the chief MIS managers in this study were interested in a wide variety of information. Some of this information could be readily produced by formal systems, but some of it is quite subjective and qualitative, and appears to be difficult to provide through formal systems. These needs are organized into the following categories ranging from highly formal to quite subjective.

Likely computerized or formal manual systems:

Project progress or status

Performance reports on computer operations

Personnel recordkeeping and assignment

Computer utilization

Billing or cost allocation systems or system usage

MIS department budget management

Unit cost trends

Operations problem analysis

Detected security breaches

Recruitment status

Training status

Possible decision support systems (could overlap with the above categories):

Staff planning and assignment

Budget development

Long-range planning

And in the CSF areas of relations with management of the parent organization, support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization, and management of change, most of the needed information appears to be qualitative or subjective.

## What Formal Systems Do MIS Managers Use?

The above study produced a set of information needs of chief MIS executives, only a portion of which is amenable to formal systems. The question addressed by the second study was what formal system do chief MIS managers actually use, and how do the systems they use compare with their needs as identified above? To answer this question, the author conducted a survey of seventeen chief MIS executives, five of whom had participated in the CSF study. In this second study these executives were asked to list and briefly describe the formal systems that they personally used, and to indicate whether they were manual or computerized systems. The managers were not asked for an exhaustive list of reports they got, but rather to report only those systems "that are so important to you that you would not forget them." The seventeen managers in this study reported that they used a total of 108 such systems, for an average of 6.4 systems per manager with a range from three to ten.

In the analysis above of the results of the information needs revealed by the CSF method, eleven areas are listed in which computerized or formal manual systems should be expected. The column headed “Percent of Managers Reporting System” in Table 2 shows the degree to which these seventeen MIS executives personally utilized systems in each of these predicted areas.

As Table 2 shows, all of the chief MIS executives personally used formal systems in the areas of project management and the MIS department budget. About two-thirds of these executives used performance reports on computer operations and personnel recordkeeping and assignment systems. About one-third of them used computer utilization, operations problems analysis, and billing/cost allocation/system usage systems. Usage of formal systems in the other four areas was quite light.

In addition, a number of systems were reported that did not fit into the above classification scheme. They are shown in Table 3. Fifty-three percent of the managers reported that they personally used corporate performance reports such as corporate sales reports, corporate financial reports, and corporate staff reports describing business conditions, problems, plans, and strategies. There has been much interest in the historical transition of the head of the Data Processing/MIS organization from a technical manager of a support function to a member of the senior management team. This study provided some very subjective evidence of where this group of chief MIS executives was located on that continuum. An examination of the descriptions of the systems they reported gives the distinct impression that most of them were operating at a relatively high level. The level of summarization and the coverage of many of the internal reports indicate a policy level interest rather than a concern with current operations, and one-third of them did not report any personal use of operations performance reports. Furthermore, as was mentioned before, more than half of them mentioned the personal use of corporate performance reports.

## Comparison of the Two Studies

How do the systems used by chief MIS executives that were reported in the second study compare with the systems they appear to need as defined by the CSF analysis reported in the first study? First, the CSF study defined seven generic CSFs that appear to be widely shared by chief MIS executives, and therefore are areas in which chief MIS executives need information. The second part of that study attempted to define specific measures that could be applied in each of these seven areas. After examining these suggested measures it was concluded that formal systems are likely for some aspects of four of the seven CSFs — system development, data processing operations, human resource development, and management control. On the other hand the measures proposed in the other three CSF areas appeared to be quite qualitative or subjective, so it might be concluded that formal systems are not as likely to be helpful in the following CSF areas — relations with management of the parent organization, support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization, and management of change.

Examining the results of the second study as expressed in Table 2 it was found that 100% of the managers personally used formal systems in the CSF areas of system development and management control (DP Department budget, computer utilization and billing/cost allocation/system usage reports). A high percentage of them used systems in the area of human resource development (since 70% used personnel record-keeping and assignment systems, and several used systems related to recruitment and training). The data processing operations area was also well covered, with 65% using performance reports, and 35% using operation problems analysis systems.

Surprisingly, Table 3 shows a higher than predicted use of systems in two of the remaining three CSF areas. 53% of the chief MIS executives reported that they personally used corporate performance reports that provide information relative to the objectives and priorities of the parent organization. Also, the management of change CSF was represented by systems in the area of capacity planning (19%), change control (12%), and technology forecasts (6%). Thus, relations with management of the parent organization was the only one of the seven CSFs in which substantial support from formal systems was not reported.

Why did the CSF analysis fail to predict the systems that were widely used in the CSF areas that appeared to be too subjective and qualitative for formal systems? One possible answer was the small sample size [8] of the managers that suggested measures. Another possibility is that managers are more clever in obtaining information that is important to them than they are in speculating about how to measure something as an abstract exercise. Also, the information obtained in these areas may not be very useful in measuring the degree of success in the area. For example, corporate reports may not measure the degree to which the MIS area is supporting the objectives and priorities of the parent organization, but they do provide the director of MIS with crucial information concerning what the objectives and priorities may be. Finally, two groups of chief MIS executives at different points are compared, and it would be quite surprising if there were perfect agreement between the results of the two studies.

These two studies do not enable the author to conclude that these chief MIS executives are getting all the information they need from formal systems, or that the information that they are getting is as useful as it should be. But together these studies indicate that these chief MIS executives are personally obtaining some information from formal systems in all but one of their major critical areas. In fact, the second study shows that they are using formal systems in two areas that were not deemed to be likely according to the measures analysis from the first study. Consequently, it appears that these MIS managers are reasonably well served by their formal systems. At least there is no indication from these studies that chief MIS executives neglect their own information needs or that they do not personally use the concepts that they promote to other managers in the organization.

## Use of the Computer

The degree to which the systems used by these top MIS managers utilized computer technology is presented in the righthand columns of Tables 2 and 3, which show the percentage of the reported systems that were completely or partially computerized. It is easy to explain why the performance reports on computer operations, computer utilization, and billing/cost allocation/system usage systems would show a 100 percent use of the computer, for some of the basic data in each of these areas could only be collected by the computer software.

Of the 108 systems reported by the chief MIS executives, 79 (73 percent) utilized the computer for all or part of the processing, but fifteen of the 79 systems that used the computer also involved manual processing. Only 27 percent of the reported systems were exclusively manual.

## Summary and Conclusions

Two different studies were used to identify the information needs of chief MIS executives. First, the CSF approach was used to obtain a picture of the overall information needs of these managers, including qualitative as well as quantitative information. The CSF approach, while it has its limitations, produces very specific information requirements when applied to the individual manager. But in this study the CSF approach was applied to a group of managers, and thus provided a broad overview of the information needs of a group of chief MIS executives which can be helpful to MIS managers who wish to evaluate their own personal information systems and perhaps detect opportunities for enhancing them.

The second study, a survey of the formal information systems that were personally used by a group of chief MIS executives, enabled the author to narrow down those formal systems that are widely used. It also allowed the degree to which MIS managers are using formal systems and computers to satisfy their own information needs to be assessed.

Among the major limitations of the above studies were the size and composition of the samples. The sample in the first study included fifteen MIS managers when determining the CSFs, and eight managers when obtaining the information measures. Seventeen managers participated in the study of the formal systems that were personally used by chief MIS/DP managers. Neither of these samples was random, for they were obtained through the cooperation of the members of the MIS Advisory Council of the Indiana University School of Business. Thus the results may or may not be representative of the general population of chief MIS executives.

Within these limitations, the measure analysis from CSF study showed that chief MIS executives need a broad spectrum of information, a substantial portion of which is qualitative and subjective. Such areas include judgments on the quality of project definition and selection, the status of staff morale, the quality of the MIS staff, the quality of the planning process, top management attitudes, the degree of top management involvement, and the degree to which change is appropriately managed. Also, the measured analysis indicates that these managers should be using formal systems for some aspects of system development, data processing operations, personnel management, and budgetary planning and control.

From the second study it can be concluded that within the context of their CSFs chief MIS managers appear to be making quite extensive use of formal information systems. As predicted above all of the managers personally used formal systems in the CSF areas of system development and management control, and there was very heavy use in the CSF areas of data processing operations and human resource development. Furthermore, the chief MIS executives were also using formal systems in two CSF areas that were not anticipated from the measures analysis — support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization and management of change.

Finally, the accusation that MIS managers are lax in utilizing their own technology appears to be a bum rap. Not only do they appear to be well off in regard to their use of formal systems, but also almost three-fourths of these formal systems utilize the computer, which appears to be a very large proportion of computer use for such high level managers.

## About the Author

E.W. Martin is a member of the MIS faculty at the Indiana University School of Business. He has been Chairman of the Quantitative Business Analysis Department, Chairman of the Operations and Systems Management Department, and Associate Dean for Academic Affairs. He has also been Director of the Indiana University Research Computing Center. He is the author or co-author of four textbooks on Mathematics, Data Processing and Computers, and is a member of the American Institute for Decision Sciences and the Institute of Management Sciences.
