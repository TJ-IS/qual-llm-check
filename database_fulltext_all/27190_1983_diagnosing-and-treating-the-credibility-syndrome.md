# Diagnosing and Treating the Credibility Syndrome

By: William J. Doll
Mesbah U. Ahmed

## Abstract

The credibility syndrome is a special case of MIS development failure with a distinctive set of symptoms and causes. In credibility problems, the directors of data processing — their management approaches and systems development philosophies — play an important role.

Keywords: MIS, MIS management, credibility syndrome, executive steering committee, systems development process, system planning objectives, application selection criteria

ACM Categories: K.6.4, K.6.0

The information systems department faces credibility problems when it is repeatedly unable to meet user expectations for developing successful applications on schedule and within budget. Completed projects which do not fulfill user expectations concerning what they are going to get or what they are going to have to do to get it, erode user confidence. Repeated project failures, delays, and cost overruns destroy the credibility of the systems department. This loss of credibility may lead to additional performance problems in the systems department.

Credibility problems reduce the status and influence of the systems staff. This loss of status and influence may create further difficulties in gaining management cooperation, responsiveness, and involvement and in securing funding for necessary hardware. Management becomes less willing to work with systems personnel and less willing to automate. This unwillingness to automate (i.e., to get burned again) may constrain the development and performance improvement activities of other departments whose operations are highly dependent on information systems.

A systems department with credibility problems may continue to have reduced status and influence in a company for many years. Once lost, restoring user confidence may take many years of concerted effort focusing on creating many “small successes” before attempting more difficult projects.

The purpose of this article is to examine this credibility syndrome (i.e., to better understand its causes, its characteristics, and its consequences). Although there is a growing body of literature on the reasons for the success or failure of information systems projects $[9, 14, 17]$ , the dynamics of the impact of repeated failures (i.e., eroding user confidence) on the systems department as a whole has not been described. The factors which cause individual projects to fail may be different than those which lead to a general lack of credibility of the systems department as a whole. In credibility problems, the directors of data processing — their management approaches and systems development philosophies — may play a more important role.

This article focuses on credibility problems at the level of the data processing (DP) department as a whole. First, the importance of the problem and some myths about its causes are explored. Next, the relationship between the management practices used to plan and develop information systems and the credibility problem is explored by contrasting the managerial approaches in firms with and without credibility problems. Finally, the implications of these findings for diagnosing and treating credibility problems are discussed.

## The Credibility Syndrome

When a systems department loses credibility, the problem is much more serious than the failure of any individual project. Although credibility problems may not be one of the major problems facing the average systems department, the loss of credibility can be a severe problem for particular firms who are having this difficulty. Systems departments have experienced credibility problems since the first widespread application of computers to business problems in the late 1950s and 1960s. However, the set of related symptoms which characterize the credibility syndrome has usually been considered just another case of MIS development failure. This grouping of credibility problems with MIS project failures has led to three myths about the causes of credibility problems:

Access Myth: In system departments with credibility problems, the MIS director has difficulty gaining access to top management.

Funding Myth: The lack of adequate funding for people, software, and hardware causes credibility problems.

User Involvement Myth: Credibility problems occur where the MIS director does not recognize the importance of user involvement in systems design and implementation.

To separate myth from reality and to identify the symptoms and causes of this syndrome, thirty-three organizations in Northwestern Ohio were studied. The sample represented a variety of industry groups and firm size categories (see Table 1). Information and insights about the firms and their systems development activities were obtained by structured interviews conducted by the researchers with the director of information systems in each organization. In addition, research instruments, Likert type scales, were administered to the information systems managers to obtain the more quantitative data reported in this article. The sample was select rather than random. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Statistics are presented for exploratory and descriptive purposes rather than hypothesis testing.

Although lack of access to top management is characteristic of many firms with MIS development problems, this access problem is not characteristic of the credibility syndrome. The MIS directors in firms with credibility problems perceived access to top management as a slightly less important problem than more successful firms. This suggests that the credibility syndrome may not be just another case of MIS development failure. It may be more appropriate to treat credibility problems as a separate syndrome.

With the possible exception of hardware, the funding myth also appears to be false. The problems posed by limited resources (i.e., people, software, and hardware) do not appear to be more serious in firms with credibility problems.

In examining the MIS directors' perceptions of the major problems facing their systems departments, the data suggest that the access and funding myths appear to be false. Systems departments with credibility problems do not perceive access to top management or securing adequate funding as more serious problems than firms without credibility problems. The user involvement myth is examined later in this article. The data does suggest that the credibility problem may be related to difficulty with managerial responsibilities such as planning development activities and creating user understanding of the systems development process. This indicates that credibility problems may be a separate syndrome rather than just another case of MIS development failure. In dealing with credibility problems, the management approach and systems development philosophy of the MIS director may be crucial. An examination of the information systems planning and development practices of firms with credibility problems may provide insights into causes of this problem. Also, comparing the management approaches of firms with credibility problems with those without such problems may help identify effective management practices.

## The Relationship Between Management Practices And The Credibility Syndrome

The information system managers were asked a number of questions about the management practices used in their organizations to plan and develop information systems. These questions pertained to the organization's: (1) framework for systems planning, (2) criteria for application selection, (3) system planning objectives, (4) executive steering committee and its operation, and (5) the management practices used in the system development process to insure user understanding, involvement, and control of logical design decisions.

Table 3 describes how the percentage of firms responding “yes” to questions concerning their framework for system planning differed between firms with high vs. low credibility problems. Firms with credibility problems are less likely (35.7%) to have a written overall plan for systems development than firms without such problems (52.6%). Since these “less successful” firms tend to view the lack of a planning framework as a more important problem and appear to have no more problems than their more successful counterparts in gaining the access to top management necessary to develop such plans, it appears that the system managers of these organizations may lack the necessary managerial planning skills.

One of the key steps in establishing a framework for system planning is developing a set of criteria for deciding which applications to implement first. Eight of the nine firms who did not have agreed-upon criteria were in the high credibility problem category. Firms with credibility problems are significantly (p < .002) less likely (42.9%) than their more successful counterparts (94.7%) to have mutually agreed upon criteria for application selection. Firms with credibility problems are almost as likely (57.1% vs. 57.9%) to enjoy a top management commitment to stable long-term funding for systems development activity. Apparently, how these resources are managed rather than the stability of support distinguishes successful firms from those with credibility problems.

Although systems managers with credibility problems usually have not reached agreement with top management on a set of criteria which should be used to decide which projects to implement first, they were able to express their opinions concerning the criteria actually used. Table 4 contrasts the application selection criteria used in organizations in both the high and low credibility problem categories. The firms with high credibility problems ranked “probability of success” as significantly more important (p < .1), and “natural sequence of information flows” as significantly less important (p < .01). Current problems and crisis situations replaced profit or effectiveness contribution as the most important criteria for application selection in firms with credibility problems. The firms without credibility problems ranked profit or effectiveness criteria first, timing (i.e., current problems, critical needs, and crisis situations) second, and natural sequence as third. In contrast, firms with credibility problems typically ranked timing first, profit second, and probability of success as third. In these firms with credibility problems, natural sequence of information flows dropped to fifth in the ranking.

To examine the user involvement myth, the system planning objectives of the two categories of firms (low vs. high credibility) are compared in Table 5. In firms with credibility problems, the planning process is viewed primarily as a method of getting meaningful user involvement in the design and implementation of systems. The focus on user involvement as the primary objective is significant at p < .1. In contrast, firms without credibility problems ranked project implementation sequence as the objective of first priority in planning. This suggests that the user involvement myth is false. The MIS directors of firms with credibility problems recognize the importance of user involvement. However, they may not know how to manage the systems development process to achieve this objective. This emphasis on user involvement as a planning objective is particularly interesting given the management practices used to plan and develop information systems in these firms with credibility problems.

## The desirable practices include:

1. insisting that systems are documented as they are developed for review and control purposes rather than after they are implemented,

2. developing the detailed logical design report in non-technical language as user documentation of the system,

3. emphasizing to users that their review of the detailed design is where benefits are designed into the system,

4. insisting that people who use the systems are involved in their design,

5. making it clear to the systems analyst that they are to present several reasonable alternatives and require user management to decide which alternative to select, and

6. requiring user managers to review and to sign-off (approve) systems development reports at each stage of the system life cycle.

These results suggest that credibility problems may be caused by the lack of management of the system development process to insure user understanding, involvement, and control of the logical design.

The management of user-analyst interaction during the system development process appears to have potential for treating credibility problems. The key is making sure that management rather than system staff personnel make the decisions concerning the logical design of the systems they will use. In some of the cases studied, there was the tendency to use the executive steering committee to make design decisions which should have been made by the manager of the operating function who will use the system. Ironically, the executive steering committee got involved in the specifics of gross design decisions because of the perceived importance of these systems for company operations. As the analyst team became comfortable in working with the executive steering committee, the input of the people who were to actually use the system was neglected and their approval was considered unnecessary.

## Summary and Conclusions

Although the credibility problem has its origin in the reasons for the success or failure of individual information system projects, the repeated project failures, delays, and cost overruns destroy the credibility of the systems department and reduce its influence and status in the organization. This loss of credibility compounds the problems of getting appropriate management involvement. As a result, firms with credibility problems develop a distinctive set of symptoms which merit consideration as a separate syndrome. Credibility problems have different causes than project failures. In this syndrome, the directors of data processing — their management approaches and systems development philosophies — appear to play an important role.

The symptoms of the credibility problem include:

1. an increased perception of the importance of a planning framework, yet the curious inability to develop such plans;

2. the increased perception of user expectations and understanding as a problem, yet the failure to involve operating management in a meaningful way in making decisions concerning the logical design of the systems they will be responsible for operating;

3. a decreased perception of the importance of personnel staffing and development and other problems (except user expectations, planning, and hardware) for the success of the systems department;

4. the reluctance of systems managers to view themselves as responsible for developing an overall written plan, educating users, and managing the process of user-analyst interaction during the systems life cycle (in other words, the major problems facing the systems department are not perceived as the system manager's responsibility;

5. given the importance placed on planning problems, the curious failure to develop mutually agreed upon criteria for application selection and the emphasis on current problems and crisis situations rather than the natural sequence of information flows for deciding what to do first;

6. the increasing importance placed on "probability of success" as a factor in application selection;

7. the tendency to view system planning objectives in terms of getting user involvement and resources rather than as a means of working on projects in a rational sequence, creating an integrated database, and developing or securing software to help users do their work;

8. the tendency of executive steering committees, where present, to function more like lower level project steering committees rather than high level policy making groups; and

9. the dramatic failure to use management practices in the systems development process which insures user management understanding, involvement, and control of logical design decisions.

These symptoms reflect the reduced status and influence of the systems staff where credibility problems have eroded user confidence. Management becomes less willing to work with systems personnel and getting user involvement becomes the primary system planning objective. There is a lack of planning and the systems department is forced to "put out fires" or select small "low-risk" projects in non-critical areas as a way of slowly rebuilding user confidence. Prescriptions for treating the credibility problem described above should focus on improving the overall planning process and establishing user involvement and control of decision making during the systems development process.

Having an effective framework for system planning requires that the systems department conduct feasibility studies (before crisis situations develop), develop a pool of projects which might be implemented, and reach mutual agreement with higher level management concerning a set of criteria for deciding which applications to implement first. Developing a pool of project areas where feasibility studies have been done increases the system staff's understanding of the business problems facing the organization and improves understanding of how information systems can contribute to these problems. Such studies also help in developing a written overall plan for systems development which (1) covers all major functional areas of the business, and (2) clarifies interrelationships between applications. In many of the firms with credibility problems, feasibility studies are only done after a crisis situation has already developed. In such crisis situations, the decision to go ahead with development has already been made and the feasibility issues receive little critical thinking. When failure occurs, it is seldom associated with the lack of a carefully conducted feasibility study.

The systems department with credibility problems needs to develop a proactive rather than a reactive planning posture. The pool of completed feasibility studies is a prerequisite for gaining meaningful top management involvement. However, the systems manager needs to involve top management in setting explicit application selection criteria and committing substantial systems development resources to projects with high profit or effectiveness contribution. The risk of project failure is an important consideration. However, risk should be controlled by managing the systems development process rather than selecting only low risk projects. In this proactive approach, the information systems manager needs to take a leadership role in guiding top management participation in an executive steering committee which focuses on setting priorities, allocating resources, and fitting feasibility studies into a slowly emerging written overall plan for systems development in the organization.

## About the Authors

William J. Doll is currently a Professor of Management at The University of Toledo and serves as a management consultant for area companies. Dr. Doll conducts seminars on Management Practices in Information Systems Development and EDP for the Non-EDP Manager. The author of many articles in academic and professional journals, including the Academy of Management Journal and the Journal of Systems Management, Dr. Doll has a doctoral degree in Business Administration from Kent State University and has worked as a senior management systems analyst on the corporate staff of Burroughs Corporation.

Mesbah U. Ahmed is an Associate Professor of Computer Systems and Production Management at The University of Toledo. He received his doctoral degree in Industrial Engineering from Texas Tech University in 1976. He is a member of ACM, AIDS, and TIMS. His past experience includes working as project engineer, systems analyst, and consultant.
