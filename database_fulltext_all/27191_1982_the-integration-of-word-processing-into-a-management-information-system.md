# The Integration of Word Processing Into a Management Information System

By: Raymond McLeod, Jr.
Donald H. Bender

## Abstract

Both the computing and word processing industries have grown to billion dollar size, but little real effort has been made to integrate them at the application level. The effort that has been expended to date has been directed at integrating word processing and data processing. What is needed is a serious look at the potential for integrating word processing into management information systems. One approach is to evaluate theoretical constructs of management information systems that indicate areas of potential word processing applications. Henry Mintzberg's concept of managerial roles and information flows is one such construct. The authors use Mintzberg's theory as a basis for a word processing and management information system integration that is being followed in a life insurance company. The company had recognized the logic of integrating word processing and data processing, but the potential for also involving information processing in the long range plan became clear only after an interpretation of the Mintzberg theory in light of word processing opportunities. The experiences of this company should be of value to others engaged in loosely-tied word processing, data processing, and management information system development programs.

Keywords: Word processing, information theory, management roles, hard information, soft information, Mintzberg, multifunctional work station, action oriented processing, multifunctional management information station

## Introduction

The year 1964 was a pivotal one in computing history. The third generation of computer technology was introduced and the concept of a management information system (MIS) was promoted by computer vendors as a way to employ the new breed of equipment.

Another introduction in 1964 has enjoyed a different evolution. This is the concept of word processing (WP), which came into being with the IBM MT/ST (Magnetic Tape Selectric Typewriter). IBM's management gave the MT/ST to the Electric Typewriter Division rather than the Data Processing Division, and that decision put word processing on a track separate from data and information processing. Word processing has grown in popularity over the years to the present stage where annual sales exceed \$10 billion. However, the growth has been due primarily to the enthusiasm for word processing applications alone — not for word processing as a part of data or information processing.

Now all of this is changing. Word processing is merging with data processing $[1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9]$ . Word processing equipment is taking over some jobs run previously on computing equipment — jobs such as preparation of direct mail letters and narrative for phototypesetting. Additionally, the same equipment is being employed for both word and data processing — microcomputers in small firms, for example. In larger firms, word processing software enables a computer to perform word processing functions. It is difficult to regard word processing and data processing separately when they are sharing the same piece of equipment.

The ultimate integration is not hardware — but applications. This integration can include management information and decision support applications as well as those of a data processing nature. However, an overall implementation plan, well-grounded in MIS fundamentals, is needed to enable word processing to achieve an integral position in the firm's MIS — not merely in the data processing system. For purposes of this discussion, the term data processing is used to describe primarily large volume data handling tasks on the operational level of the firm, keyed to transaction processing. The MIS identifies those resources dedicated to the creation of informational outputs for managers on all levels.

This article outlines a plan for implementing an integrated WP/MIS. The initial focus will be on a theoretical construct that is intended to provide a basis for the integration. Next, a case situation will illustrate how attention to the MIS concepts can expand upon and improve a long range development program that had been initially data, rather than information, oriented. In conclusion, an implementation plan is suggested.

## Theoretical Foundation

The recommended approach to the design of management information systems is to begin with descriptions of what managers do, how they use information, and what information they need. If a design plan leapfrogs these basic issues, the chances of success are reduced. This starting point can be established through direct contact by the system designer with the manager. As the designer seeks to understand the manager's information needs, understanding the responsibilities of the manager and the role of information in meeting those responsibilities first must be done. Theoretical constructs can provide a framework upon which the designer can build in gaining the required understanding.

## Mintzberg's managerial roles

A well-articulated model of managerial roles and information flows has been developed by Henry Mintzberg [7]. According to Mintzberg, the manager does more than plan, organize, staff, direct, and control — Henri Fayol's classical management functions. Mintzberg says that managers have difficulty in pigeonholing their work into those categories. Instead, he sees the manager playing ten roles, grouped into three categories. Figure 1 illustrates the grouping.

By virtue of formal authority, the manager gains status that enables the development of interpersonal roles. These roles generate access to information that enables the manager to perform certain informational roles. The informational roles, in turn, facilitate certain decisional roles. As a figurehead, the manager is the spokesman for the firm to its environmental members. The leadership role stresses the relationship of the manager with the staff, while the liaison role recognizes peer relationships. These interpersonal contacts make it possible for the manager to emerge as the “nerve center” of the organizational unit. The informational roles of monitor, disseminator, and spokesman are quite significant in Mintzberg’s view. One of his studies revealed that chief executives spent 40% of their contact time on activities devoted exclusively to information transmission, and that 70% of their incoming mail was informational rather than requests for action [7]. The decisional roles cast the manager as an entrepreneur, seeking to improve the unit, as a disturbance handler who performs much as an orchestra conductor in keeping all of the resources in tune, as an allocator of resources, and as a negotiator.

The manner in which the liaison, monitor, disseminator, and spokesman activities comprise an information network is illustrated in Figure 2. The manager acts as the central hub in this network, scanning the environment and soliciting information from liaison contacts. The information, including that received without solicitation, is monitored to determine its relevance to the organizational unit. As a disseminator, the manager shares information with subordinates and peers, and as a spokesman, the manager shares with superiors and the members of the environment.

## The role of the MIS

Although managers receive information from computer based information systems, “soft” information — verbal contact, mail, telephone calls, gossip, and so forth — is also important. The term “hard information” can be used to describe output from the formalized computer based system. Good managers will develop both of these information sources — hard and soft — in the process of establishing themselves as information hubs or nerve centers.

Figure 3 illustrates how the computer based MIS can fit into the overall information flow, integrating both hard and soft information. The manager receives soft information from the environment, superiors, peers, and subordinates. This is a two-way information flow with the manager also participating as disseminator and spokesman — the manager returns information favors in kind. In addition to personal contacts, the manager can receive hard information from the computer based information processor (both solicited and unsolicited) relating to activities of the four information interfaces. The hard information describes performance levels of both superior and subordinate organizational units, as well as peer units. For example, a sales manager can be informed on performance of the entire marketing department, other units within the department, and subunits within the sales operation.

## The role of word processing

One approach to integration of word processing into the MIS would be to focus on the soft information flows in Figure 3. Such an approach would ignore the possibility that word processing can handle some of the hard flows better than the computer based information processor. A better approach would be to use the model in Figure 2 as a starting point and evaluate word processing for each of the pictured flows. In performing this evaluation, it should be recognized that it is not the task of word processing to electronically capture all of the flows that have escaped the MIS, but to capture those that lend themselves to word processing.

Figure 4 isolates those managerial information flows that are good candidates for word processing. The liaison role is not included because of the informal nature of primarily face-to-face personal contacts. The roles that offer promise as word processing applications are those of monitor, disseminator, and spokesman. Overlaid on the flows are several examples of the types of communications that can be accomplished with word processing.

A firm just embarking on a word processing implementation project could use a model such as the one in Figure 4 as a guide for achieving a WP/MIS integration. The model identifies three major units (environment, superiors, and subordinates) that send information to and receive information from the manager. The model also shows how the three informational roles are linked to these sending/receiving units by information flows.

The Mintzberg theory is well-suited as an integrating framework because:

\- it recognizes the importance of information to the managerial process,

\- it regards information related activity as a prerequisite to decision making activity, and

\- it encompasses all information flows — hard and soft.

A WP/MIS integration plan using the Mintzberg theory as a starting point would be expected to improve the performance of the manager in both the informational and decisional roles. The theory provides the normative model. The task, then, becomes the implementation of the model in the form of blended WP/MIS technology and methodology.

## A Word Processing/MIS Case Study

Many firms have been developing both MIS and word processing applications for years, and have made real accomplishments in both areas. The missing ingredient has been an integration plan. A close look at one of these firms and how an application of theory changed its planning for word processing can provide a reference point for other firms considering a similar project.

## Government Personnel Mutual Life Insurance Company

Government Personnel Mutual Life Insurance Company of San Antonio (GPM), Texas was organized in 1934 by Colonel Peter J. Hennessey, a retired military officer, who felt that military personnel needed life insurance protection at standard rates without a war clause. Today GPM enjoys assets of approximately \$170 million, annual premium income of almost \$20 million, and annual dividends to policyholders exceeding \$4 million. GPM receives a high return on assets relative to many insurance companies due mainly to the company's ability to efficiently handle inforce business. GPM sales are modest by comparison, but emphasis has been on quality sales (business that remains inforce on a premium paying basis).

## GPM's data processing history

GPM's data processing history can be traced back to 1960 when a disk-oriented IBM 305 RAMAC was installed after three years of conversion effort. In 1964 an IBM 1401 was installed so that a tape oriented insurance applications package, 62 CFO (Consolidated Functions Ordinary), could be used. In 1969 GPM returned to direct access storage with an IBM System/360.

In 1977, GPM developed its corporate plan for data processing in support of the company's long range planning process, in which it made a commitment to convert to terminals and online computer services. After more than a year of planning, preparation, and training, GPM replaced the 360 with an IBM 370/145 and installed IBM Virtual Machines (VM/370) with the Conversational Monitor System (CMS). The 370/145 was only an interim measure, however, and the preferred IBM 4341 mainframe was installed in 1981.

## Management information systems development

Inspired by a paper presented at an American Management Association course by consultant A. John Ward in 1974, GPM management realized that a good planning system must be based on a good information system. Ward stressed that “planning is a decision making process, and information supports that process” [11]. Figure 5 positions the MIS in the planning and decision making process as seen by Ward. The resource plans define where and how much of the company’s resources are allocated to the operating units, and the operations plans define the behavior of the organization.

The success of the long range planning program has been due, in part, to the “systems viewpoint” described by Ward and embraced by GPM. Much attention has been devoted to the systems analysis work in developing the plan, and to the organized, structured form of the plan. The plan is regarded as a system of state, and the planning process is seen as a dynamic, interactive, looping activity — a closed loop system.

The long range planning system provides a good example of the integration of theory, the systems approach to decision making, and Mintzberg's managerial roles in the solution of the strategic level planning problem. Figure 6 is a model prepared by GPM showing how the informational and decisional roles lead to the attainment of operations and resource plans. Although valuable in itself, an additional significance of the long range planning system is the clarity it has provided to the importance of information in decision making. Furthermore, GPM has recognized the use of the computer as more than a data processor — as an information system.

## Word processing systems development

GPM early recognized the merits of using computing equipment to produce correspondence. For more than ten years the company used an IBM 1403 printer to produce 30,000 letters per month, mostly for direct mail advertising. While there were real benefits of this technology, there were also some problems:

\- The upper and lower case print chain or train produced a print quality inferior to that of typical typewriter copy.

\- Continuous form perforations left objectionable ragged edges on letterheads.

\- Entering, testing, and producing computer letters was a lengthy process and the system could not accommodate all correspondence.

The development of word processing hardware and software caused GPM to consider alternatives to overcome the problems inherent in computer generated correspondence. Two basic options were considered:

1. Stand-alone word processing hardware. While there are many good stand-alone systems, the mini-computer that accommodates as many as sixty terminals appeared to be practical for a large home office such as GPM. At the time the options were being evaluated, only one vendor product provided the flexibility of communication with the host computer, which was considered a necessity to prevent duplication of databases and equipment. However, the cost of that system was prohibitive.

2. Word processor software with terminals connected directly to the host computer. Besides being more economical for GPM, this alternative:

\- eliminates duplication of terminals and other hardware,

\- eliminates storage duplication for policy holder data; for example, the policyholder's name and address are already in the host computer database,

\- eliminates the labor needed to key information that is already available,

\- eliminates the training needed for the operation of two different terminals, and

\- offers the opportunity to use the same equipment for many purposes in addition to word processing. This flexibility is considered an important contribution to economy and overall efficiency.

While the use of software and the conventional terminal in the local environment of the GPM home office was a good choice, GPM is planning experiments in remote areas using the micro terminal that has independent word processing capabilities. Agents have correspondence and processing needs that are unique to their functions. However, communication between the micro and the corporate host computer is also seen as a necessity in an eventual electronic mail system and to allow agents to obtain information from the host computer that could not be provided by the micro computer system alone.

After studying several word processing software packages, GPM selected the University of Waterloo's commercial package, SCRIPT. GPM was attracted to SCRIPT because of its good cost/performance ratio. Specifically, SCRIPT offers an efficiency that enables it to produce reports and correspondence faster than a conventional typewriter. The following features of the package contribute to this efficiency:

\- Copy is stored on a disk and easily retrieved, changed, and refiled.

\- Both the coded copy (that includes embedded function codes) and the finished copy can be viewed on the terminal and changed prior to printing. This interactive display saves valuable time.

\- Much less keying is required to initiate correspondence. Macro-instructions and boilerplate copy stored on a disk to be embedded in the finished copy reduce the time required to produce initial drafts.

\- Data need not be manually transcribed from a master record status report since master file information can be directly accessed. This results in more personalized correspondence with fewer transcription errors.

SCRIPT solved the problem of the lengthy time required to produce the direct mail letters. The problems of objectionable appearance were solved by obtaining a daisy wheel printer and using continuous forms with pre-cut letterhead attached. Although the daisy wheel prints at only 55 characters per second, the quality is very good. The relatively slow speed was overcome by eventually installing four of the printers in different areas of the company, under control of the central computer. The combination of the printer, the special forms, and the ability of SCRIPT to justify margins and hyphenate words produces correspondence more attractive than conventional typewriter copy.

## Word processing systems implementation

Looking back on the word processing implementation, two points appear to GPM to be especially important:

1. Management, through the MIS Committee, gave strong support to the effort and appointed a project team to identify the functions where word processing was especially applicable. Valuable suggestions have been made by the project team, along with considerable voluntary input by users, that have enhanced the practical utility of the GPM word processing system.

2. A formal training program for all potential users was recognized as essential. GPM enlisted the assistance of an instructor affiliated with a local college who was experienced in the use of Waterloo SCRIPT, and two classes were held each week for a four week period. As part of the plan, two secretaries were selected to become "inhouse" experts in the use of SCRIPT. These specialists answered questions and worked out problems associated with the operation of the terminal and the use of the SCRIPT software. Several SCRIPT training classes have been held and the two secretaries are now solely responsible for SCRIPT training.

As with anything new, there is a learning curve. Management must be patient and allow adequate time for training. Once a user learns the word processing software codes and is as comfortable with the terminal as with a typewriter, the efficiency of the word processing approach far exceeds the old methods. However, in the early stages of training, the trainee is strongly tempted, sometimes under management pressure, to revert to the use of the familiar typewriter. Patience through the learning curve is most important.

## Integrating word processing and data processing

The GPM long range data processing plan included the objective of action oriented or “one stop” processing. A service function, such as a claim, is to be handled by one individual immediately doing all tasks relating to fulfilling the service. This consolidation of functions at single work stations is in contrast to the traditional assembly line method requiring several employees to handle separate phases of the service request and resulting in a lengthy processing cycle.

The company envisions the heart of action oriented processing to be an electronic work center, called the Multifunctional Work Station (MFWS), that would integrate word processing, the database, and a whole gamut of other electronic information storage, retrieval, and transmission technologies such as microform, electronic mail, and voice messaging. A person at the MFWS could have online access to all the data, information, and documents necessary to completely handle a service function. Neither the integrated hardware nor the software comprising the MFWS are presently available, but the word processing part of the plan has progressed well. Using SCRIPT, the company is generating production oriented documents such as memoranda, agent and policyholder correspondence, direct mail, and legal documents. Also produced are internal reports, program and work measurement documentation, rate manual narratives to interface with the typesetter, newsletters, corporate action plans, and handbooks.

Although management information was recognized as an important part of the 1977 data processing plan, word processing was initially seen to be a vehicle for fast and efficient handling of clerical production on the operational level. A migration model, illustrated in Figure 7, tracks the process that GPM is following in evolving from an assembly line (no consolidation) production method to its current state of a partial consolidation state (based on action oriented processing), progressing toward complete consolidation employing the more advanced components of the MFWS.

As envisioned in the 1977 plan, management systems represented only a midway point — a step beyond the action oriented processing. This is a data processing view of word processing that has since changed as a result of expansion and clarification of MIS and DSS concepts, and improved word processing capabilities.

## Decision support systems development

Success with the corporate long range planning system reinforced the MIS concept, which has subsequently been supplemented with the decision support system (DSS) concept as described by Gorry and Scott Morton in their MIS framework [3]. Figure 8 is an adaption of that framework showing how several decision systems are positioned in terms of management level and problem structure. According to Gorry and Scott Morton, firms first solve structured problems on the operational control level and then progress across and down the grid toward the unstructured problems on the strategic planning level. This has been the case at GPM. The systems that have been incorporated into the computer based DSS are preceded by an asterisk (\*).

In expanding DSS, GPM is following Ralph Sprague's recommendations in concentrating on one or two very important decision areas, and attempting to capture the steps taken by managers in the decision making process [10]. The systems are selected based on their contribution to achievement of the long range corporate plan. At GPM, the systems selected for initial attention are sales force productivity and sales force staffing (see Figure 8).

The beauty of the DSS effort is the present availability of the hardware and software needed to do the job. The initial GPM manager/DSS interface will include a conventional terminal equipped with an APL (A Programming Language) keyboard. GPM has realized significant success in using APL for information retrieval, and a training program is presently underway to introduce managers throughout the company to this powerful language. APL was selected as the language to support MIS/DSS development at the manager/user work station because it is viewed as likely to survive in the long term. APL requires less time to learn, and less time is required to design and modify programs than many other languages. When APL programs are designed in a modular fashion and the modules (functions) are logically labeled, documentation is good and can be represented in a highly structured fashion. Whereas some managers are using APL to design their own systems, other managers may need help. But anyone can execute operational APL programs.

APL should not be regarded as the key to a successful DSS. GPM has enjoyed considerable success with APL because it meets the needs of the GPM management team. Other firms, with different blends of managerial types and styles and information needs, will find a better match with another language. Although APL has the reputation of being unfriendly, GPM has not found it to be unproductive. GPM is primarily interested in increasing the productivity of the manager/user.

## Integration of word processing, MIS, and DSS

As in most companies, GPM's data processing, management information, word processing, and decision support systems began as separate courses of action. The intent now is to integrate them, not as a tightly coupled “total system,” but as a network of processes sharing data and information to provide an overall support activity to all levels of company employees. ## A plan for integrating word processing with MIS and DSS

Integration of word processing and MIS at GPM is being accomplished within the well-defined structures provided by the corporate long range plan and the plan for MIS. When such a framework exists, it is a relatively simple matter to prescribe the steps that can be taken to achieve the integration:

1. endorse the WP/MIS integration,

2. appoint the WP/MIS integration team,

3. survey WP hardware and software,

4. evaluate the survey results relative to existing computer and noncomputer based systems,

5. acquire the needed WP hardware and/or software,

6. establish a training program,

7. isolate key results areas (corporate objectives to be supported by WP/MIS),

8. establish strategy and action plans for integration of WP/MIS,

9. establish priorities.

Theory plays an important role in this progression. Initially the theory serves to focus management's attention on the need for the integration. At GPM, the theories of Mintzberg, Gorry and Scott Morton, Sprague, and others served this function. Later in the project, when it becomes necessary to establish priorities, the theory provides a basis. For example, support for the various managerial roles can be achieved in a series of steps.

Figure 10 illustrates a three step sequence in terms of both equipment and roles. The first step can be stand-alone word processing and computing equipment used in the batch mode. In this setting letters and memos can be prepared to support the manager in performing the spokesman role by establishing information flows with the environment. The second step integrates the word processing and computing equipment to add the ability to prepare messages, perform personal computing, and achieve action oriented processing. These abilities enable the manager to augment the spokesman role with those of monitor and disseminator. In this step, the emphasis is on vertical flow of information — from superior to manager to subordinate. GPM is presently at this stage. The third step will be taken when the necessary MFWS hardware and software are available. In this setting, emphasis will be on supporting the manager in performing the decisional roles, and the MFWS will become an MFMIS — Multifunctional Management Information Station.

The situation at GPM was ripe for application of theory. The corporate structure was there, along with the necessary resources and a history of innovative use of the computer. A plan now exists to integrate word processing and the MIS to support the manager in performing informational and decisional roles.

## Summary

One approach to recognizing the potential for word processing support in the MIS is to examine Mintzberg's managerial roles and to evaluate the extent to which the contemporary MIS facilitates the various information flows. Those flows that have not been exploited in typical MIS designs are good candidates for word processing. While word processing will not fill all voids, it can make a valuable contribution and should be considered.

Government Personnel Mutual Life has actively pursued progressive data processing, information processing, and word processing designs, and recognized the value of integrating word processing with data processing. Developments in MIS and DSS theory, combined with innovations in word processing hardware and software, prompted the company to recognize the potential value of word processing as a component of the MIS. A plan has been developed for the integration of word processing into the MIS, addressing information flows between management levels and between the firm and the environment. Other approaches to planning are possible, and may be better suited in other situations. More critical than the format of the plan is the realization by top management that word processing has the potential to handle some of the information flows that have proved troublesome for conventional computer equipment.

Raymond McLeod, Jr. is Associate Professor of Business Computing Science at Texas A & M University. He has worked for IBM, Recognition Equipment, and the management consulting firm, Lifson, Wilson, Ferguson, and Winick. He is co-author of Computerized Business Information Systems (Wiley) and author of Management Information Systems and Management Information Systems Case Book (SRA). His research interests include marketing the information product, design of academic MIS programs, and computer education for the hearing impaired. Dr. McLeod is a member of the American Marketing Association and the American Institute for Decision Sciences, and holds the Certificate in Data Processing.

Donald H. Bender is vice president and director of research for Government Personnel Mutual Life Insurance Company, a position that he has held since 1969. Mr. Bender holds the bachelor of business administration degree from St. Mary's University, and the master of science degree from Trinity University. His primary responsibilities deal with planning, coordinating, and installing computer systems, and with statistical research. As an active participant in life insurance industry affairs, he has submitted more than forty planning activity reports to the Life Office

Management Association, and has authored more than fifteen articles in publications such as the Journal of Applied Psychology, Journal of Machine Accounting, and the Journal of Information Management.
