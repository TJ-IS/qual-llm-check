# Successful Development Strategies for Business Application Systems\*

By: James D. McKeen

## Abstract

In a field study of thirty-two business application systems, the relationship between the time spent in various phases of the development life cycle and the outcome of the development was examined. Results indicate that systems which spent more time in the analysis phase required less time to code, resulted in greater user satisfaction, and were developed in agreement with established budgets and deadlines. These results suggest preferred strategies for the development of application systems and have implications for their successful management and control.

Keywords: Application system development, user satisfaction, development life cycle, implementation

ACM Categories: D.2.9, J.1, K.6.1

## Introduction

The ubiquitous nature of computers and computing in today's organizations belies the ability to develop application systems that are responsive to the informational needs of management. The means by which application systems are conceived, planned, developed, and implemented within organizations remains essentially an art form despite attempts to structure and control the process, the infusion of tools and techniques meant to explicate and standardize the process, and efforts to transform systems development into an engineering science. McLean [27] points out that the determination of a manager's information requirements for an application system is essentially a "black art." Yet, if the information requirements are only seventy percent complete or accurate, the resultant application system can at best be only seventy percent complete or accurate. Too often it is failure in these early, and often non-technical, stages of development that dooms many of today's application systems [16].

According to Basili and Zelkowitz [2], much of the difficulty with systems development stems from a lack of understanding of the general nature of the development process — a lack of understanding that retards the learning effects which should naturally accrue from years of experience with the development of application systems. As a case in point, many MIS textbooks, in presenting the system life cycle, depict the stages of development accompanied by a percentage breakdown of effort. These presentations are seldom derived from any empirical basis and often amount to little more than “armchair speculation.”

To address this situation and to increase present understanding of the nature of application systems development, this research analyzed a sample of application systems in search of answers to the following questions:

1. What percentage of total effort is consumed by each phase in the development of application systems?

2. To what degree does this pattern of effort consumption vary?

3. Can these variations in effort consumption, assuming they exist, be attributed to inherent characteristics of the application systems being developed?

4. Do successful application systems follow a different development pattern than less successful systems?

5. Finally, is the pattern of development related to the outcome of development in such a way that preferred strategies for development are suggested?

## The Life Cycle as a Framework for Analysis

The dominant organizing framework for application system development is the life cycle concept $[3, 4, 10, 20]$ . This methodology apportions the total developmental effort into identifiable stages — each stage representing a distinct activity characterized by a starting point, an ending point, and deliverables in concert with an express purpose. The engineering counterpart for this methodology is referred to as “Work Breakdown Structure” $[18]$ defined as “an enumeration of all work activities in hierarchic refinements of detail which organizes work to be done into short, manageable tasks with quantifiable inputs, outputs, schedules, and assigned responsibilities” $[42]$ . The roots of both methodologies can be traced to the elemental notions of general systems theory $[5]$ .

The process of systems development can be analyzed using this life cycle framework. By recording the relative consumption of resources during each component phase, a descriptive profile of the development process emerges. Couger [9] conducted a survey of fifty organizations in 1965 and again in 1972 comparing the costs associated with life cycle phases between first and third generation systems. He showed that the development process, as revealed by the distribution of resources, changed substantially during this period toward increased emphasis on the early phases of development. Of particular importance is the implication that inherent system attributes of scope and sophistication would not only increase costs, as would normally be expected, but would alter the distribution of resources.

Other researchers [31, 34, 35, 45] have adopted a view of the development process based on a formal representation of manpower utilization rates over the development life cycle. It has been found that these rates are adequately expressed by Rayleigh-Norden equations which predict manpower utilized over time, given a knowledge of the total cumulative manpower used in development, and the development time of the system. Despite this “macro” approach yielding good approximations to the actual manpower utilization patterns over time for some systems [8], it lacks specificity when used to analyze the development process. According to Graver [17], an approach which focuses on the component phases of the development process and their interactions provides a more detailed description of the nature of system development and should lead to greater understanding.

The usefulness of the life cycle framework as an investigative tool for the analysis of the systems development process has been demonstrated in the literature. Its application in this regard, however, has been predominately limited to extremely large development projects (some examples are provided by Graver [17]). There exists some scepticism as to whether or not the prescriptions arising from this literature can be applied to other development environments, and specifically to smaller business application systems. Evidence provided by Moore [28] would suggest that these prescriptions are not readily transferable. Of the key developmental differences between MSS (management support systems) and CSS (computer support systems) identified by Moore, size as measured by the mean number of programmer man-months committed and by the mean duration of the project in months proved to be a significant factor. In comparing various software cost models, Boehm and Wolverton [8] found that some existing models seemed to work well for some classes of systems and not very well for others. They found that the Boeing model [6] appeared to estimate small projects reasonably well but gave extreme overestimates on large projects, while the Putnam and Wolverton model [34] did just the reverse. This finding corroborates Moore's conclusion that system size is an important discriminating factor and casts considerable doubt on the generalizability of research results between these two environments.

This study follows this previous research by its adoption of the life cycle focus as the means to investigate the process of systems development. The identifiable phases within the life cycle provide the basis for analysis. This study diverges from previous research, however, with its focus on typical business application systems, as opposed to much larger software developments, and its attempt to relate development patterns to measurable outcomes of the development process.

## Settings and Methods

Thirty-two application systems were randomly selected from five different organizations (two manufacturing firms, a merchandising firm, a life insurance firm, and a leasing and rental agency). The selection was made by the researcher with no prior knowledge concerning the nature of the system, the development personnel or users involved with the system, or the status or quality of the system. The sample systems were selected from a much larger set of “qualified systems.” Systems qualified for inclusion in the study if they were developed 1) by internal systems personnel, 2) for users outside the systems department, 3) in support of a particular business function, 4) completed within the last year, and 5) passed through multiple phases of development. This selection mechanism served to exclude systems software, such as operating systems, utilities, or general support programs, as well as “minor maintenance” projects which tend to involve only one or two phases of development. All systems had reached the stage of being installed; some had been successfully incorporated into their organizations while others had never been used. The sample therefore represents the class of typical “bread and butter” applications necessary to keep most businesses operational on a day-to-day basis. This class of systems was selected for study for two reasons; it represents the majority of computer based systems in most organizations, and it constitutes a “best case” in the sense that most companies have had many years experience in the development of this class of systems. Some of the characteristics of the systems that make up the sample appear in Table 1.

For each system, two questionnaires were filled out in the presence of the researcher — one by the project leader and one by the primary user of the system. The project leader provided information relating to the system and its development including a description of the system, its objectives, the type of project control exercised, the nature of user participation, characteristics of the project team, the complexity of the system, the programming languages and techniques used, the design characteristics, and the status of the system. The primary user described the eventual usage of the system, general satisfaction with the system, agreement with original objectives, and any implementation problems that were encountered with the system. The administration of these separate questionnaires circumvented the problem described elsewhere [15] of having the primary user evaluate both the process and outcome of development.

The actual time spent by systems personnel in the development of each of these systems, as extracted from the project management reporting systems used by each organization, was allocated among five life cycle phases:

Analysis — all initial work through complete functional requirements (i.e., “what the system will do”).

Design — the translation of functional requirements into complete system specifications (i.e., “how the system will do it”).

Coding — the preparation of programming instructions including unit testing and program documentation.

Testing — activity to ensure the system operates in accordance with the specifications established during analysis and design (i.e., “system testing”).

Implement — activity which places the system into full operation within the organizational setting including user training and the development of procedure manuals. The term “implement” was chosen because of its wide acceptance by practitioners. This term is not to be confused with “implementation” which typically denotes the overall process of effecting change in organizations.

Each participant organization maintained a time reporting system that recorded all hours worked by systems personnel allocated by project and by activity within a project. Data corresponding to each application system in the study was extracted from these time reporting systems by the researcher. Then, the actual hours were allocated among the five life cycle phases being used in the study. This exercise was one of aggregation as most companies recorded hours expended in terms of activities that were components of the phase. The majority of the aggregation of activities occurred in the analysis phase of the development model. The other four phases tended to involve a one-to-one mapping as agreement among existing company definitions and the five-phase model was substantial.

The data thus obtained should be of high quality since each organization recorded its development data according to well-established definitions and procedures. Data were recorded weekly in terms that are directly applicable to the framework used in this analysis. In each organization, control measures were taken to ensure that all individuals filed their weekly time reports, that all hours were accounted for, and that only valid accounting codes (including project and activity codes) were used. These procedures prevented project managers from arbitrarily shuffling hours, except their own, amongst projects.

Despite these precautions, errors can and do exist within any project reporting system. These errors are of three types — the inappropriate allocation of overhead among projects, the misuse of activity definitions, and the conscious transfer of time from one project to another. According to Reed [36], the most predominant type of error is the misallocation of overhead. Both the first type of error and the third type of error cease to be a problem if systems personnel are assigned to a single project, which fortunately was the case for the majority of the systems that were studied. The second and third types of error may be caused intentionally and are therefore more difficult to detect. Of these two, the second is much less critical, as far as this research is concerned, than the third type of error. The time reporting system in each of the participant organizations had prescribed mechanisms for correctly allocating overhead among projects. The degree of compliance with these procedures on the part of systems personnel is undetermined. If allocations were made consistently over the development of an application system, the validity of the data would not be impaired. It is hoped that this is the case.

The pattern of effort expenditure by phase for each system represents a development profile. These development profiles constitute a descriptive aid which reflects the nature of the process of development. In order to compare development profiles of different systems, the actual hours consumed during each phase were expressed as a percentage of the total expenditure of effort for the entire development. As an example, one system in the study required 612 hours of analysis, 192 hours of design, 986 hours of coding, 253 hours of testing, and 46 hours to implement totaling 2,093 hours. Figure 1 shows the development profile for this particular application system.

Three variables were used to assess the outcome of development — cost and manhour budget compliance, agreement with projected deadlines, and end user's satisfaction with the product. The ability to develop a product in agreement with user needs which arrives on schedule and within budget is considered to constitute successful development [32, 33]. The development of an application system in violation of either established budgets or user needs (or both) is considered to indicate some degree of failure in the development process.

All budget estimates (cost, deadline, and total hours) were those established by the project leader at the completion of analysis and design since at this point in the development of a system, the planning function is complete and the developer should be able to make the best estimate of the resources necessary to bring the application system to fruition. All measures of budget compliance were based on:

$$
\frac {\left| \text { Estimated } - \text { Actual } \right|}{\text { Actual }} \times 1 0 0
$$

i.e., the “error” in development as a percentage of the actual figure disregarding the direction of the difference.

The importance of measuring user satisfaction derives from its behavior as a surrogate measure for the organizational acceptance of computer based systems. It has been found that user satisfaction and appreciation is correlated to information system utilization [40] and to systems success [28]. Powers and Dickson [32] concluded that user satisfaction is the most critical criterion in measuring system success and failure. Several measures of user satisfaction have been used [13, 24, 29, 30, 32, 41]. In preference to creating another instrument to measure user satisfaction, it was decided to use an instrument already developed and tested (see the Appendix). This instrument was chosen because it was designed for a similar study of project management and included factors relating to implementation problem assessment, overall system satisfaction, organizational acceptance, and the satisfaction of specific project objectives.

## Results

## The development profiles

An analysis of the effort consumed by phase for the entire sample of systems is presented in Table 2.

There are a number of noteworthy features of this data. First, coding is the dominant activity with regard to the consumption of human resources. The 42% average is far in excess of most recommended levels for coding [3, 10, 39, 46]. Second, the testing phase does not even approximate the importance attached to it by the "40-20-40" rule (i.e., 40% analysis and design,

20% coding, 40% testing, [7, 12]), supporting the contention that this rule may not apply to application system development [17, 28]. Third, the level of variation within each phase is substantial. This finding corroborates other research [17] that concluded “the degree of variation within each activity was so great that the development of independent resource-estimating relationships for separate activities does not offer any promise.”

The distribution of effort consumed by phases for all systems is shown in Figure 2. Using straight lines to connect the means provides an indication of the average development profile. This graphical representation allows easy visual comparison between individual systems or between groups of systems, and will be used for this purpose subsequently.

## The development outcomes

Table 3 compares the budget performance of the systems in terms of the completion time deadline, cost budget, and total manhours of effort.

Thus, on average, systems were developed within 22 percent of the estimated deadline, within 28 percent of the estimated cost, and within 25 percent of the estimated number of manhours. It is instructive to examine the direction of the variation (see Figure 3).

In virtually every instance, the projected deadline was missed. If the middle-most fifty percent of the observations (i.e., those encompassed by the first and third quartiles) are considered to be a good representation of the range of budget compliance, the emergent trend is that of underestimation. Based on this sample, it is not unfair to characterize the development process as one that produces systems which arrive late, cost more, and involve greater effort than was envisioned.

## The relationships between development profiles and development outcomes

The fourth question posed in the study — “Do successful application systems follow a different development process than less successful systems?” — was addressed through an analysis of the relationship between development profiles and development outcomes. If the answer is in the affirmative, the description of these development patterns might suggest a preferred process for application system development.

The process of examining these relationships is not straightforward. A combination of two methods was used. The first method involved the derivation of correlation coefficients between the percentage of effort consumed by individual phases and measures of the process outcomes for all application systems. Table 5 contains the results. One noticeable feature of these data is the absence of any significant relationships between the degree of time budget compliance and any of the individual (or combined) development phases. This result may be partially explained because established completion deadlines are subject to some “manipulation.” For instance, in two cases it was determined that the requesting users of the application systems purposefully decided to delay development until some time in the future. The specific reasons for the postponements were undetermined but the delay resulted in drastic misrepresentations of the degree of time budget compliance. It is also possible for developers to change the development process to align it with the established deadline through the use of overtime or extra personnel. Similar comments do not apply to either of the other budget compliance measures. Because of the time reporting systems used in the participant organizations (as explained under Settings and Methods), it would have been difficult to arbitrarily adjust the actual manhours of development effort, and therefore the cost, in an attempt to comply with projected deadlines.

The ability to develop a system in conformance with the budgeted manhours for development was greater for those systems where relatively more effort was devoted to analysis and design. There is also evidence of a significant negative relationship between the coding phase and the manhour budget performance. It can be speculated that the analysis and design phase and the coding phase are not independent and may reflect an operative cause and effect relationship. Investigations of this hypothesis by others $[17, 25, 26, 43, 44]$ have indeed found substantial inter-phase dependencies.

The extent of the agreement between the cost and manhour budget compliance measures is significant. The reason for this is because cost estimates are derived from estimated manhours for development. As a result, comments regarding the relationship between the development phases and the degree of manhour budget compliance apply equally well to the degree of cost budget compliance. The subsequent discussion, therefore, will be restricted to two outcome measures — user satisfaction and manhour budget compliance.

The second method used to analyze the relationships between development profiles and development outcomes distinguished “more successful” application systems from “less successful” application systems in order to contrast the (average) development profiles belonging to each group. Those systems falling in the top or fourth, quartile when ranked by development outcome were classified as “more successful” systems. Conversely, those systems falling in the bottom, or first, quartile were classified as “less successful” systems. This approach was used in preference to dichotomizing the data (i.e., dividing at the median) because it provides a stronger test of the hypothesis of no difference between groups. All between-group comparisons were made using t-tests for differences in means.

The average development profile for the “successfully” developed systems, using high manhour compliance as the measure of “success,” is strikingly different than the corresponding profile for the “less successfully” developed systems (see Figure 4).

In terms of the probability levels that are indicated by alphas in the figure, successful systems committed significantly greater resources to the analysis phase with a corresponding savings of resources during the coding phase. No differences between these two profiles were seen to occur with the design, test, or implement phases of development. The results of the between-group comparisons using user satisfaction as the indication of successful development are presented in Figure 5. Here a similar pattern emerges — increased emphasis on analysis with decreased effort consumption during coding. In addition, those systems yielding high-user satisfaction devoted significantly less effort to the design phase but greater effort to the implementation phase of development.

## Verification of results

As with all research, the applicability and transferability of the results of this study rest with the strength of the research design. A cross-sectional study that attempts to examine changes in a phenomenon over time risks confusing inherent characteristics of the investigative unit (i.e., application systems) with those of the change process. This research is a case in point. For example, it is possible that some systems, because of their unique characteristics, require only minimal design but a lot of coding with very little effort to implement. In such cases, the development profiles may be inherent characteristics of the systems rather than reflecting the process of development.

This study captured many characteristics of the sample systems and the environments in which they were developed. If it were so that the development profiles were characteristic of these systems and not reflections of the development process, it would be expected that of these many factors pertaining to these systems and their environments some would be related to the resultant profiles. In order to test this proposition, correlations were examined between factors (pertaining to the systems, the underlying task addressed by the development team, the nature of the user involvement, the specific development techniques used, and the experience of the project leader and user(s) who participated in the development of the system) and the effort consumed by phases (see Table 6).

An examination of the results reveals no support for the proposition that the development profiles are inherent characteristics of systems. No factor is correlated with more than one phase, and only one factor exhibits a correlation of significance less than one percent. Many of the relationships found to be statistically significant are spurious. For instance, the use of structured programming techniques was found to exhibit an inverse relationship with the effort consumed by the analysis phase! Based on this analysis and with the proviso that important factors have not been omitted, it can be concluded that the development profiles as reported in this research do in fact constitute a valid expression of the development process.

## Implications of the Results

The crucial role played by the analysis stage of systems development is borne out by this research. With the completion of analysis, virtually all key decisions concerning the system, as the users see it, have been made. It is often the case that a formal document, with contractual overtones, is signed at this point signifying the user's agreement with the system as planned — the “freezing point” in common systems parlance.

The importance attached to this developmental phase pervades the OR/MS/MIS implementation literature. For example, the bulk of recent implementation theory which is based on a normative model of social change first expounded by Lewin [23], Schein [37], and Schein and Bennis [38], elaborated by Kolb and Frohman [22], and tested by Sorensen and Zand [40], and Ginzberg [13, 14], argues that failure to resolve these critical issues arising during analysis often dictates the ultimate success or failure of the development process. Further, Ginzberg [16] has shown that the analysis stage of development can be used for early diagnosis of potential MIS implementation failure. His argument contends that the completion of the analysis phase provides the logical vantage point for diagnosis — no more than 25 percent of total resources have been consumed, yet all critical decisions regarding the eventual system are in place.

In light of this evidence, the results of this investigation serve to reinforce the critical nature of the analysis phase of development. It was found that projects which spent more time in the analysis phase were more successful regardless of the outcome measure used. Both user satisfaction and the ability to develop the system in conformance with the budgeted manhours were enhanced for those systems placing greater emphasis on the analysis effort. It can be reasoned that the expenditure of additional effort during the analysis phase enhances the likelihood that key developmental issues are identified and resolved. These issues pertain to the system's goals [13], the system's scope [19], and the system's general orientation to the business problem [11], as cited elsewhere [16]. Failure to address these issues during analysis has been shown to foster unrealistic user expectations leading to eventual user dissatisfaction [16].

While the results of this study are not claimed to substantiate the argument of unresolved key issues leading to inappropriate user expectations, leading in turn to user dissatisfaction and “failure” of the implementation process, the two are certainly complementary. It would follow that the analysis phase of development should be both thorough and exhaustive.

User satisfaction with the product of systems development was found to vary inversely with the design effort and the coding effort while varying directly with the analysis and implementation phases (see Table 5 and Figure 5). One interpretation of these results might be that the analysis, testing, and implementing phases involve, and in fact, depend on, high user participation [1, 3, 10, 47]. Conversely, the design and coding phases represent a period of development with minimal user involvement during which system personnel work somewhat independently. With the exception of the occasional progress report during this period, the user may temporarily relinquish involvement and control of the project. Given this situation, emphasis on design and coding, in the form of an increasing consumption of total resources, can be understood to have a dampening effect on the user's degree of satisfaction. The reverse argument would also follow from this — that is, emphasis placed on analysis, testing, and implementing would have a buoyant effect on the user's satisfaction. This result may simply underscore the need for effective and meaningful user participation over the entire development life cycle.

The design phase is the only phase which is at odds with the attainment of both high user satisfaction and high manhour budget compliance (see Figures 4 and 5). As such it seems to represent somewhat of a conundrum for the system developer. An increase in user satisfaction accompanies a reduction in percentage of total effort devoted to the design phase. This same reduction in design, however, can be seen to work against high budget compliance. Kling [21] explains this situation and proposes a means for its solution:

Computing personnel are usually hired for technical competence, and promoted for technical and fiscal success. The latter includes meeting deadlines and staying within the budget allocated. The explicit satisfaction of users with the particular application packages they have developed in the past is, at best, usually a secondary consideration. Nor is user satisfaction systematically monitored and evaluated like a budget. It is unlikely that many systems will be designed for the actual needs of many actual users, without a set of incentives that reward those members of the computing staff who value service as much as technical sophistication.

This research augments Kling's observations. He contends that the user satisfaction dimension has simply been ignored and can be encouraged by the implementation of an appropriate incentive system. An implication of this study is that the introduction of such a system may, in fact, prove dysfunctional. User satisfaction appears to be improved for systems spending relatively more effort in the analysis phase at the expense of the design phase. Any reduction in the design phase, however, is not conducive to high budget compliance. This suggests a careful reorientation of the development process in preference to the unilateral imposition of additional measures and their accompanied incentives.

Certainly the systems development process leaves ample room for improvement because systems typically arrive late, cost more, and involve greater effort than planned. Fortunately, this study has demonstrated that development outcomes are responsive to controllable aspects of the development process. Inherent characteristics of application systems and their environments — the uncontrollable elements, at least in the short run — were incapable of explaining the variation among development profiles. The fact that development outcomes are related to controllable elements of the process suggests potential strategies for the successful development of application systems. Such strategies would be designed to change the process of development for application systems to conform more closely to the “successful development profiles” established in this study (see Figures 4 and 5). These strategies might include:

— implementing procedures to ensure that development teams perform adequate analysis before progressing with the subsequent phases of development. In practice, this is not easily accomplished because of the difficulty in determining when the analysis phase is actually finished. One mechanism is the institution of check points, forms, and deliverables.

— implementing procedures to ensure meaningful and ongoing user participation throughout the entire development life cycle. This can often be accomplished with parallel activities such as assigning users the responsibility for user training while the system designers attend to technical aspects such as programming.

— implementing procedures to ensure that system developers strike a balance between technical and fiscal success. Adopting an organization for the MIS department that permanently assigns analysts to specific areas of the company may encourage the valuation of service as much as technical sophistication.

— implementing procedures to ensure that the final phase of the life cycle receives sufficient attention to ensure a smooth system installation. Installation usually provides the first chance for users to physically interact with a new system. This is the point where confidence and credibility are established (or lost, as the case may be). There should be no surprises for users. Their active involvement throughout development should ensure this.

The test of these strategies and others, via the incorporation of appropriate measures and controls within the realm of project management, will prove interesting.
