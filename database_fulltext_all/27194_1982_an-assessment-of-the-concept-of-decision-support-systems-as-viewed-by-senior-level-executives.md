# An Assessment of the Concept of Decision Support Systems as Viewed by Senior-Level Executives\*

By: Maryam Alavi

## Abstract

This article describes a study performed to (1) investigate senior executives' perceptions of decision support systems concepts, and (2) identify executives' needs and desired benefits related to decision support systems. A series of in-depth interviews was conducted with senior-level industrial executives. The findings suggest three guidelines for the design of effective decision support systems: (1) executive users should be directly involved in the development and evaluation of the initial design concept, (2) if possible, a prototype should be built before developing the full-scale system, and (3) the decision support system should provide capabilities for complexity coping, conflict resolution, and uncertainty reduction.

Keywords: Decision support systems, executives' requirements for decision support

ACM Categories: 3.50

## Introduction

The concept of decision support refers to the use of computer based technology to assist managers in complex and unstructured tasks. By definition, Decision Support Systems (DSS) are computer systems designed to mesh with executives' existing activities and needs while extending their capabilities $[2]$ . Recent developments in computer and information technologies and new products in the commercial software and hardware marketplace have provided many opportunities for enhancing the decision making effectiveness of senior executives. Examples of new technological developments include simulation and modeling languages with graphics capabilities; online access to large regional databases containing economic, demographic, and marketing data; and relational database management systems which permit the maximum flexibility in formulating unanticipated or spontaneous inquiries $[7]$ .

Are decision support systems driven by “technology push” or is there a “demand pull” for these systems? In other words does top management perceive a concrete need and utility for decision support systems? The purpose of this article is to describe a study performed to (1) investigate senior executives’ perceptions of DSS concept, and (2) identify executives’ needs and desired benefits related to decision support systems. A series of indepth interviews was conducted with senior-level industrial executives. The results provide significant insight into the nature of executives’ demand for decision support systems and offer several practical suggestions for the design of effective decision support systems.

## Indepth Interviews with Executives

Indepth interviews (also referred to as focus interviews) are personal and one-to-one interviews where the interviewer uses probing techniques in order to draw out, fully develop, and capture the ideas and reactions of the respondents. In this study, eight senior executives participated in interviews of this type. $^{1}$ Each held the formal title of either chief executive officer, president, director, or regional vice president and had been in their current position for an average of seven years. They came from relatively diverse backgrounds in terms of former functional responsibilities within their firms. Accounting, finance, marketing, and engineering were relatively equally represented in the combined backgrounds of the participants. The respondents came from the banking, chemical products, insurance, manufacturing, and research and development industries.

No prior knowledge or familiarity with computers and decision support systems was assumed for the executives. A week prior to each interview, a short overview article $^{2}$ on decision support systems was mailed to the participant. At the time of the interview, a brief verbal description of decision support systems was given. This verbal description was similar to the short overview article that the executives had received prior to the interview. Any questions that the executives had in regard to the decision support concept were answered. At the onset each participant was told that the interview questions had no right or wrong answers, and that the primary objective was to obtain individual opinions and attitudes toward decision support systems. Next the respondent was asked to take a few minutes and write down an explicit description of a specific, critical decision. The participant was also asked to reflect on his decision making behavior in general. The purpose of this step was twofold: (1) to get the executive to consider somewhat explicitly what is typically involved in making decisions, and (2) to enable him to concentrate on a specific decision which may subsequently become the target of his efforts during the interview process. $^{3}$

Two probing techniques were used to focus and control the interaction following a question.

1. Supplementing the question by a neutral phrase such as “How do you mean?” or “What do you think about that?” to indicate interest and to communicate to the respondent that more information is desired. Sometimes by pausing and waiting after a response the respondent was encouraged to answer further.

2. Summarizing the response in order to convey both understanding and acceptance.

These probing techniques served two primary purposes: to motivate the respondent and to focus the responses on interview objectives.

The executives who participated in the interviews were interested in the topic of discussion and were highly motivated during the interviews. In fact, although 1½ hour long appointments were scheduled, some interviews took as long as 2½ hours.

## Interview Findings

The interviews resulted in some findings that have important implications for development of successful decision support systems. First, the executives interviewed expressed concern about using “seat-of-the-pants” decision making approaches in today’s dynamic and uncertain decision environments. Second, the executives in general seemed to have positive attitudes toward decision support systems. They viewed the concept as potentially very useful and showed a positive predisposition toward adoption of these systems. There were, however, some concerns about adoption and usage risks of decision support systems. These concerns were primarily expressed in terms of potential low currency of information provided by decision support systems and a mis-match between executives’ needs and system capabilities. The thrust of these observations is summarized in the following two sections on the executives’ perceptions of DSS concept and nature of executives’ demand for DSS.

## Executives' perceptions of DSS concepts

An executive's perceptions and attitudes toward decision support systems constitute one determinant of the behavior toward such a system. Positive attitudes promote system adoption and usage, while negative attitudes inhibit adoption and usage.

During the interviews, the executives were asked to respond to attitudinal and perceptual items relating to the adoption, usage, and benefits/shortcomings of decision support systems. Executives generally responded favorably to the concept of decision support systems. The executives that were interviewed had no previous exposure and experience with decision support systems, and therefore were responding to the concept rather than an actual system they had used.

Two interpretive statements emerged from the discussions. First, decision support systems were associated with “better” and improved decision making. Executives felt that these systems would aid management in maintaining a higher level of objectivity in decision making. They also believed that a decision support system would provide more complete analysis of decision situations than otherwise might be possible.

There were, however, three concerns with regard to adoption and usage risks of decision support systems. First, some executives felt that a decision support system might require the use of decision making techniques and approaches that would be awkward given their characteristics and personal styles. As one executive commented, "I should not be expected to go to computer classes and become a computer jock in order to be able to use the system." Second, currency of information provided by the system was perceived as a potential problem. Executives were concerned that information in the system might not be timely enough to reflect last minute changes in the decision environment. Hence there would be the potential risk of using "old" information for decision making. Third, there were some reservations that decision support systems may not fit management needs. Executives indicated that they should be convinced of the match between their requirements and system capabilities. They wanted to try a decision support system in their actual decision making environment for a period of time before finalizing purchase and installation. As one executive remarked, “...it would be like buying a car; I would want to test drive it before buying.”

In order to meaningfully define expected benefits and communicate with the executives participating in the interviews, a list of potential benefits of decision support systems was developed by the researcher. During the interviews, each executive was asked to evaluate each benefit in terms of his perception of its usefulness. Two benefits were identified as having the highest perceived usefulness: information processing and retrieval followed by help with evaluating decision alternatives. Table 1 lists the expected benefits of decision support systems rank ordered by their degree of perceived usefulness. The last two items on the list, those with the lowest perceived usefulness, are “manage executive time by scheduling daily activities” and “increase decision confidence.” It appears that although time pressure was perceived as a difficulty in executives’ decision environments, executives were well-adjusted to it and viewed it as an inherent aspect of their jobs.

The executives' perceived needs for decision support systems were measured on Porter-type scales $^{4}$ (illustrated in Figure 1). The Wilcoxon Matched-Pairs Signed-Ranks test was chosen as the best alternative for analyzing this data. The results of the Wilcoxon test identified six out of ten areas (shown in Table 3) where executives perceived a significant difference between the manner in which they currently make decisions and the manner in which they thought that they should be able to make decisions. $^{5}$ These findings show that respondents perceived a need for improvement in their decision making behavior and therefore identify possible applications for decision support systems.

Perceived difficulties in decision environments and the executives' perceived needs for change in their current decision making approaches (displayed in Tables 2 and 3 respectively) identify requirements for decision support. Examination and comparison of perceived difficulties and needs for change in the decision making process reveals that (1) there is a close correspondence between perceived difficulties and expressed needs, and (2) the difficulties relate to conditions of complexity, conflict, or uncertainty in decision making environments. Complexity in decision environments refers to multiplicity of decision elements. It is represented by situations in which the environment is very large, highly heterogeneous or interconnected. Conflict in a decision environment may arise when (1) there exist multiple parties, (2) the actions of one party can affect the outcomes of others, and (3) the parties have different preference ordering on the outcomes. Uncertainty refers to lack of appropriate information. The manager may be uncertain about the nature of the problem, the alternative actions, the outcomes of the alternatives, or the environmental events that may influence the outcomes. This implies that experienced executives understand their decision environments well enough to be able to effectively participate in defining their requirements and evaluating alternative DSS solutions. In fact, the executives' knowledge and understanding of the decision environment is critical and necessary input to the definition stage $^{6}$ of the systems design process $[1, 3, 5]$ . To develop DSS that are perceived as useful by the executives and compatible with their decision making styles and requirements, the executive users should be directly involved in the definition and evaluation of the DSS. It is only through the active and meaningful participation of the executive users in the design process that the designers can develop an understanding and appreciation for the executives' decision environments and requirements. By capitalizing on this mutual understanding, it is possible for the designers to effectively communicate with the executive users and reduce the risk of asking the wrong questions and solving the wrong problems.

Interviews with the executives also showed that they are concerned about the adoption and usage risks of decision support systems. Specifically there were three concerns: (1) a decision support system might require the use of techniques and approaches that are incompatible with the executive's characteristic and personal style, (2) low currency of information provided by the system and the possibility of using "old" information for decision making, and (3) a mismatch between the executive requirements and the system capabilities. Based on these concerns, the executives expressed a desire to try a decision support system in their actual decision making environment for a period of time before finalizing the adoption decision. A practical and feasible way to provide the required “trialability” is through development of a DSS prototype. A DSS prototype in this context is an initial system which is quickly installed with as little expenditure of resources as possible. $^{7}$ The prototype demonstrates the potential capabilities and the essential features of the full-scale DSS. The DSS prototype provides trialability and serves to reduce the adoption apprehension and risk.

Perceived needs and difficulties in the executive decision environment seem to primarily relate to conditions of uncertainty, complexity, and conflict (demonstrated in Table 4). These findings imply that an important aspect of design of DSS is creative application of the decision support techniques (e.g., graphics, mathematical models, forecasting techniques) for purposes of complexity coping, conflict resolution, and uncertainty reduction. Decision support strategies for uncertainty reduction and coping aim primarily at reducing the manager's perception of uncertainty, modeling the uncertainty, and reducing uncertainty by processing more complete information. Decision Support strategies aimed at complexity reduction primarily draw upon an understanding of human information processing and decision making behavior. For example, factoring (grouping numerous items into a smaller, more comprehensive set) is applied to large systems where the interactions among subsystems are weak but not negligible. By reducing the complexity of the decision problem through this technique, the information processing requirements of the decision task are brought closer to the information processing capacity of the decision maker. Decision support strategies for conflict management primarily stem from game theory and behavioral science literature [4, 6].

## Summary

Decision support systems are potentially important and beneficial innovations. This innovation seems to be driven by “demand-pull” (i.e., response to concrete and perceived need for decision support) and not “technology push.” To design effective and marketable systems the nature of “demand pull” should be fully established and understood by the designers.

The expense of a decision support system is justified only if it can be effectively used as a management tool. Progress and new developments in computer and information technologies alone are not enough to ensure the success of decision support systems. Technology still must be assembled into systems that are compatible with executive decision environments and provide support that they feel is valuable. Explicit recognition of executive user requirements and the decision environment is a first step toward creation of effective and useful decision support systems.
