## Abstract

Knowledge utilization among MIS researchers is examined via an analysis of 9,911 references appearing in 532 MIS journal articles published in fifteen journals during the 1970-1979 period. Quantitative indicators of references per article, type of publication referenced, elapsed time between citing and cited publications, and cross disciplinary references are used to categorize knowledge utilization by researchers in the MIS discipline and to analyze changes in reference patterns over the ten year period and differences between academician and practitioner authored works.

Keywords: Management information systems, citation analysis, knowledge utilization

ACM Categories: A.m

## Introduction

The Management Information Systems (MIS) field has experienced dynamic growth. New managerial practices (e.g., systems development methods) are developed almost at rates approaching those of advancing technologies. MIS developments have drawn on a number of underlying disciplines, including management science, computer science, management accounting, and behavioral science. Researchers and practitioners have been attracted to MIS from these diverse fields, fields which must be effectively integrated if MIS is to be successfully researched, practiced, and taught. However, there has been virtually no cumulative tradition in the MIS field which builds on prior research efforts [12]. In addition, the MIS practitioner's attitude toward MIS academicians is generally negative, even though an underlying base of knowledge is desperately needed to support information systems practice [7]. Many of these problems can be characterized in terms of knowledge utilization.

Knowledge utilization pertains to the dissemination and use of research results by researchers in subsequent investigations, and by practitioners in applications. This study examines one aspect of knowledge utilization — the interaction of MIS researchers with prior research efforts — based on references attached to MIS journal articles. Bibliographic references attached to journal articles indicate utilization of prior research efforts in shaping concepts and understanding $[16]$ , and provide a powerful tool for examining knowledge utilized.

This investigation sought the answer to the following questions concerning knowledge utilization within MIS research:

1. To what extent does the MIS discipline rely on the accumulation of previously published knowledge?

2. What are the principal sources of information utilized by the MIS researcher?

3. How long is the delay between the time an MIS article is published and the time it is referenced in other publications?

4. To what extent do MIS researchers draw on the works of the various disciplines that serve as the foundation of MIS (e.g., computer science, management science)?

5. To what extent have the answers to questions 1-4 changed as we moved through the second decade of MIS?

6. Do questions 1-4 have different answers for authors with academic affiliations than they do for practitioner affiliated authors?

The analysis of references utilized by authors of MIS journal articles provides quantitative indicators for four aspects of knowledge utilization, as shown in Table 1. These indicators, which have all been employed in previous work, are discussed in detail in later sections of the article.

## Research Methodology

Knowledge utilization is examined here by use of references attached to journal articles on the subject of management information systems. The formal methodology is reported in some detail elsewhere [9], and is summarized briefly here.

Fifteen journals that commonly publish MIS related articles were selected. The journals selected are shown in Table 2. The tables of contents of these journals were then examined for the years 1970 through 1979. A selection methodology was employed to identify 532 MIS articles for the publication period examined. Table 2 indicates the number and percentage of MIS articles published.

The bibliographic references attached to each MIS article were then recorded, multiple references in an article to the same work were counted once, different works by a cited author were each counted separately. Bibliographic references suggesting what one ought to read, rather than actual sources, were not included. Table 2 summarizes the reference characteristics of each source journal in terms of the total references, the mean references per article, and the percentage of self references to the journal (i.e., references to works published in earlier issues of the same journal).

## References per article

## Meaning

The number of references per article quantifies the amount of interaction with prior research efforts and can be used to characterize the type of article. Fifteen to twenty references per article characterize research papers exhibiting a high degree of interaction with prior research efforts $[13, 18]$ . Articles having less than ten references are considered scarcely anchored and those with more than twenty-two are typically survey or tutorial papers $[2]$ .

## Results

For each journal, Table 2 characterizes the average number of references per article over the publication time period examined. The tutorial nature of Computing Surveys articles is clearly evident. The practitioner orientation of Harvard Business Review and Journal of Accountancy articles is reflected in a low number of references per article. Articles in the Communications of the ACM, Decision Science, Management Science, and Transactions on Database Systems represent scholarly works with respect to the norm for references. Overall, the mean number of references per MIS article was 18.6.

The percent of self references, shown in Table 2, indicates that $40\%$ of MIS authors publishing in the Harvard Business Review reference articles that previously appeared in the Harvard Business Review. To a lesser extent, MIS authors publishing in the IBM Systems Journal $(16\%)$ , the Accounting Review $(13\%)$ , and Management Science $(12\%)$ , also rely on the respective journal as a source of information. Self referencing, which generally averages $20\%$ of a journal's references [8] reflects a bias toward interaction with prior research efforts [11]. For the most part, the percentage of journal self references in MIS journal articles is considerably lower than the norm. This may reflect the relative immaturity of the MIS discipline and the lack of citable articles published in MIS journals.

## Changes

Price [19] suggests that as a field matures it will exhibit an increased number of references per article. This proposition has been supported by a study of eight social science journals covering a span of fifteen years [17]. Average references increased from 8.4 to 15.2 per article, though great variations in reference practices were noted among the eight journals. In general, referencing is increasing in all fields and established fields (e.g., hard sciences) have the highest average reference frequency [20]. The average references per MIS article have increased from 16.1 (1970-1972) to 22.1 (1977-1979). Based on the proposition of Price, these results reflect the maturation of the MIS field.

## Academic Versus Practitioner Authors

A comparison of references per article for academic versus practitioner authored articles shows a noticeable difference. Academic authors averaged 21.9 references per article, well over the fifteen to twenty references characterizing well anchored research efforts. However, practitioner authored articles contained an average of only 13.5 references, suggesting that they are generally less well anchored. In contrast, Culnan [5] found no differences between articles by academicians and practitioners appearing in National Computer Conference proceedings.

## Type of publication reference

## Meaning

Interaction with prior research efforts is generally accomplished through the “stock of knowledge” channels constituting a formal communication system for scientific information within a discipline. The more a diffusion channel, or type of publication, is presented in the references, the more the research community is expressing the fact that the diffusion channel is a useful carrier of information $[8]$ . In most fields, journal articles are the primary mode of interaction with prior research and represent the leading edge of research, whereas books serve more to collate and report established knowledge $[15]$ .

## Results

The types of publication referenced by MIS researchers are shown in Table 3. For the 1970-1979 time period, MIS researchers have primarily relied on journal articles (40.4%), and conferences (14.4%) as sources of information.

The twenty journals acting as most useful sources of information over the ten year period are presented in Table 4. Almost one third of the references were to four journals, and eleven journals accounted for nearly one half of the journal references. The data shown in Table 4 is biased, however, toward established journals that have published throughout the ten year period. The analysis of references attached to MIS articles published in 1979 reduces the bias. The data for 1979 MIS articles shows new journals such as the MIS Quarterly and Information and Management moving up significantly as useful sources of information [10]. The results support Bradford's [4] hypothesis that the significant articles in a given subject concentrate heavily in a relatively small core of journals.

The fifteen articles most frequently referenced by MIS researchers, listed in Table 5, generally represent the most influential documents. The threshold for defining important articles appears to be a citation rate of three to four times a year, a number which only a few percent of all papers ever achieve [8, 18]. The list of most frequently cited articles is, of course, biased toward articles that have been in print long enough to be cited (i.e., all were published prior to 1975).

## Changes

A graphical analysis of the modes of interaction over the 1970-1979 time period, depicted in Figure 1, suggests that the importance of journals as a source of information remained approximately the same for 1970 to 1979. Journals accounted for $41.6\%$ of the references in the 1970-1972 period and $41.3\%$ of the references in the 1977-1979 period. The importance of books has been decreasing. Books averaged $27.7\%$ of the references in the 1970-1972 time period compared with $23.7\%$ for 1977-1979. This might be taken as an indicator of the maturing MIS field based on the propositions of Price [18]. The graphical analysis also suggests an increasing role of conference proceedings as a source of information. Conferences averaged $10.5\%$ of the references in the 1970-72 time period, but accounted for an average of $15.5\%$ in the 1977-1979 period.

## Academic Versus Practitioner Authors

The mode of interaction with prior research has previously been shown to differ between researchers in academic versus practitioner positions. Culnan [5] found that computer science practitioners tend to cite conference proceedings more and books and technical reports less, than computer science academicians based on references attached to articles in a National Computer Conference proceedings. Culnan did not find significant differences between academics and practitioners in the distribution of references to different core journals.

The data in Table 3 suggest differences in information sources for MIS researchers in academic versus practitioner positions. MIS practitioners tend to make less use of journals and books while utilizing conference proceedings, technical reports, and working papers more than academicians. These results are in agreement with those of Culnan [5] concerning conference proceedings and books, but differ in terms of journals and technical reports/working papers. This may reflect a difference between the MIS and computer science disciplines or it may be attributed to the fact that Culnan only considered articles published in a conference proceedings rather than journal articles.

MIS academicians and practitioners vary considerably in information sources drawn from journals. Not surprisingly, Management Science was much more frequently used by academicians while practitioners favored Datamation. Interestingly, Communications of the ACM was a more popular reference source with practitioners, despite the frequent claim that this ACM publication is too academically oriented for the practitioner audience.

Elapsed time between citing and cited publication dates

## Meaning

In discussing the elapsed time between citing and cited publication dates Line and Sandison [13] make a broad distinction between use of information sources which is publication date dependent (“updating” use) and publication date independent (“basic” use). In “updating” use of documents, the researcher scans recent material for awareness and to keep up with new breakthroughs. “Basic” use is more intensive and usually restricted to a specific area of interest. This involves retrospective search of older material, and also overlaps with updating use of documents. The growth in the literature, estimated by the number of items available over time, affects both types of document usage patterns [13].

Elapsed time is, for a particular citation, the time that has transpired since publication of the cited articles. Median elapsed time represents the typical delay between publication dates of all citing articles and the date of publication of the original work. A high proportion of recent references, defined by elapsed time of less than five years, indicates the presence of an active research front and a greater degree of “updating” usage patterns. This is called the immediacy effect, and can be used to characterize a discipline as no science, soft science, and hard science as the age of cited material increases [19].

The median elapsed time has also been used to indicate the decline over time of the validity or utility of information. One possible reason for this declining utility is that in a fast growing field or in one with rapidly advancing technology, older work may be superseded or incorporated more quickly so that an average item can expect a fairly short life $[13]$ . It may also be that researchers in a field characterized by rapid technological change may not have time to search the literature and therefore rely more on recent reference materials.

## Results

Figure 2 depicts the median and distribution of elapsed time for each type of publication referenced in the MIS articles. The two major modes of interaction with prior research efforts (journals 40.4% of the references and books 24.3%), have median elapsed times through the communication system of 3.9 years and 5.2 years, respectively. The median elapsed times of conference proceedings (2.3 years) and technical reports and working papers (2.9 years) were considerably shorter.

## Changes

The median elapsed time between citing and cited publication dates did not change significantly over the 1970-1979 time period. These results suggest that the research front in the MIS field continues to reflect a substantial “immediacy effect” as a result of rapid growth, a relative newness, and the absence of a cumulative tradition.

## Academic Versus Practitioner Author

Shorter median elapsed times of references indicate faster access times to information sources and quicker knowledge utilization. The claimed advantages of an academic affiliation (e.g., more time for “updating” use of information sources, greater awareness of recently issued books, and university working papers) suggest that academicians should have shorter median elapsed times than practitioners. However, the data in Figure 2 shows that practitioners have shorter median elapsed times for references to journals, books, and conference proceedings. Perhaps practitioners may not have enough time for searches of older material, limiting references to recent material.

## Cross disciplinary reference patterns

## Meaning

Interaction with prior research efforts can be classified by content by grouping journals into discipline areas and then analyzing cross disciplinary journal reference patterns. However, categorizing journals to characterize the content of a journal reference has several limitations. Journals may not be easily classified into discipline areas and articles in a journal (especially interdisciplinary journals) may be in several disciplines. Given these limitations, cross disciplinary journal reference patterns can be used to indicate the primary “reference disciplines” for the MIS field. If the journals of one field, say behavioral science, are frequently cited by MIS articles, then this is a fair approximation of the extent to which behavioral concepts are being accepted and utilized by MIS researchers.

## Results

The analysis of cross disciplinary patterns is presented in Table 7. Relationships are expressed as percentages of “references to journals in subfield x” from “MIS journal articles in subfield y.” For example, 501 (58.3%) of the references attached to MIS articles appearing in MIS journals are to other MIS journals. This interdisciplinary interaction with prior research efforts is common to MIS articles appearing in other subfields. The highest concentrations of reference patterns occur in identical row-column intersections.

An examination of total journal references also reflects the fact that there are notable interdisciplinary tendencies of MIS researchers. These include:

\- MIS researchers interact to a substantial extent with prior research efforts in computer science, management, and management science in support of their research.

\- Prior research efforts in accounting and behavioral science were referenced very little by MIS researchers.

\- Authors of articles appearing in accounting and management journals tend to ignore the other MIS related disciplines, especially the MIS journals, in their sources of information.

## Changes

The changes in cross disciplinary reference patterns suggest that the computer science and management science disciplines have increased in importance for MIS researchers. The comparison between the 1970-1972 and the 1977-1979 periods is shown in the upper half of Table 8. One explanation is that the body of MIS literature is becoming less fragmented, since the percentage of references to the other category, representing journal titles in other disciplines, decreases substantially.

## Academic Versus Practitioner Authors

Interpersonal barriers, in terms of occupational differences between academicians and practitioners, may foster higher intradisciplinary concentrations of reference patterns and unusual interdisciplinary tendencies of MIS researchers. As shown in the lower half of Table 8, MIS academics cite the accounting, behavioral science, management, and management science literature to a greater extent than MIS practitioners. MIS practitioners utilize the MIS and computer science journals as primary sources of information.

## Summary

References attached to journal articles can be analyzed to provide a map of the information flow within a knowledge system. This article has analyzed knowledge utilization among MIS researchers. The more significant findings are summarized below.

Amount of Interaction — An indicator of the amount of interaction with prior research efforts is the number of references per article. The results for most of the journals used as baseline publications suggest that MIS researchers have been well anchored to prior research efforts, especially in comparison to other fields. Practitioner authors, however, are less well anchored than MIS academicians. The number of references per article is increasing, which is indicative of a maturing MIS discipline and the increasing size of the MIS literature.

Mode of Interaction — MIS researchers employed journal articles as the primary mode of interaction with prior research efforts (40.4% of the references), followed by books (24.4%) and conferences (14.4%). The most important journals, journal articles, books, and conferences acting as sources of information were identified. Conference proceedings have been growing in importance, books are assuming less importance, and journals have remained approximately the same as sources of information for MIS researchers. MIS practitioners tend to make less use of journals and books, while utilizing conference proceedings, technical reports, and working papers more than MIS academicians. MIS academicians and practitioners vary considerably in information sources drawn from journals.

Efficiency of Mode of Interaction — An indicator of the efficiency of these modes of interaction with prior research efforts is the elapsed time between cited and citing publication dates. For MIS researchers, conferences were the most efficient mode of interacting with prior research efforts (2.3 years median elapsed time), followed by technical reports and working papers (2.9 years), journal articles (3.9 years), and books (5.2 years). The high proportion of recent references indicates an active research front and a greater degree of “updating” usage patterns, and reflects the newness of the field and an absence of a cumulative tradition. The median elapsed times did not change significantly over the ten year period, suggesting that no major changes have occurred to influence the efficiency of these modes of interaction, MIS practitioners has shorter median elapsed time to journals, books, and conference proceedings, which may reflect faster knowledge utilization and/or utilization that is limited to only current material.

Content of Interaction — Cross disciplinary journal reference patterns, as an indicator of the content of interaction, suggest that computer science (19.6% of the journal references), management (15.4%), and management science (12.0%) have been the primary reference disciplines for MIS researchers, and have increased in importance over the ten year period. Journals on accounting (5.8%) and behavioral science (5.1%) were not important sources of information, although MIS academicians cited them to a significantly greater degree than MIS practitioners. MIS practitioners utilize the MIS and computer science journals as primary sources of information. In general, MIS researchers publishing in a particular field primarily interact with prior research in the same field.

Some of the problems in MIS research pertaining to knowledge utilization raised in the introduction of this article, can be addressed in light of these findings. There is a positive trend for MIS research to be well anchored to prior research efforts, especially among MIS academicians, implying that attempts are being made to build a cumulative tradition. Although the body of literature is becoming less fragmented, at least in terms of the journal literature, interdisciplinary barriers exist. Behavioral science has not been a significant reference discipline, especially for MIS practitioners. This may reflect the technical backgrounds of many MIS practitioners and suggests that work published in behavioral science journals, as well as that appearing in accounting, management science, and management outlets, is not effectively reaching the MIS practitioner. For MIS research to have an impact on practice, researchers must change either their publication outlets or the reading habits of this important target audience.

Further, researchers publishing MIS works in management, and particularly accounting journals, have tended to substantively overlook literature published in MIS journals. Hopefully, these separate “islands” of MIS research will eventually unify as researchers in these disciplines “discover” the parallel interdisciplinary research activities.

Other suggestions for improving knowledge utilization among MIS researchers focus on the modes of interaction with prior research efforts. Journals represent the most important mode of interaction. An analysis of the journal communication system for MIS research by Hamilton and Ives [10] provides the basis for personal decisions and institutional policies that can make MIS research results more available to the academic and practitioner communities.

Conferences, in comparison to journals, provide a more efficient mode of interaction with prior research efforts. However, there is no single professional society that contains a majority of MIS academicians. A similar dilemma exists for MIS practitioners. Technical reports and working papers provide an efficient mode of interaction with prior research efforts, but they generally reach a limited audience.

## Conclusion

References attached to MIS articles published in the last decade have been used to study knowledge utilization by MIS researchers. Changes in critical indicators over time reveal a maturation process within the MIS literature but also highlight certain significant barriers to the efficient flow of knowledge. Practitioners seem to over-rely on technical (i.e., computer science) and MIS publications, while researchers, particularly those publishing in accounting journals, appear reluctant to pursue related work published in various extradisciplinary journals.

The emergence of a well defined MIS discipline, accompanied by one or more prestigious journals, may eventually resolve this issue. In the meantime, practitioners and academicians alike need to recognize the interdisciplinary nature of the MIS field and reappraise their personal reading and researching strategies.
